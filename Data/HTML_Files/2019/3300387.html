Teachers’ Expected and Perceived Gains of Participation in Classroom Based Design Activities

Peter Börjesson , Computer Science and Engineering, Sweden, peter.borjesson@cse.gu.se
Wolmet Barendregt , Department of Applied IT, Sweden, wolmet.barendregt@ait.gu.se
Eva Eriksson , Aarhus University, Sweden, evae@cc.au.dk

Tilde Bekker , Eindhoven University of Technology, Netherlands, m.m.bekker@tue.nl

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300387
CHI '19: Proceedings of CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Glasgow, Scotland UK, May 2019

This paper explores teachers’ expected and perceived gains from classroom participation in design projects. The results indicate that teachers hope the experience will be fun for the children, and that it will increase both children's and their own knowledge about technology. Although they consider learning goals important, these do not necessarily have to be communicated to the children, since the teachers experience that the children are learning several skills anyway. However, early involvement in the definition of learning goals could make participation more beneficial. The teachers also see several gains from partication for themselves, especially related to using a design approach in the classroom. We discuss the implications of these finding and suggest a way to increase the user gains for both children and teachers by considering the opportunity to use classroom participation as a way to support teachers’ competence development, thereby fulfilling the promise of mutual learning as advocated in Participatory Design.

1 INTRODUCTION

While the inclusion of children in the design process has long been advocated in the Child-Computer Design (CCI) field in order to create products that are more suitable to children's needs, recent research, especially in the are of Participatory Design (PD), has focused more on the direct gains for children by participation in such a design process. McNally et al. [ 17 ] for example surveyed and interviewed children and parents after their participation in two PD projects and found that they perceived several gains related to their participation, such as collaboration, communication, design process knowledge, and confidence. In a similar vain, Schepers et al. [ 21 ] investigated fun as a direct gain for children participating in PD, arguing that this gain can lead to additional user gains, such as developing self-esteem.

While McNally et al. focused on understanding perceived gains for both children and parents, and Schepers et al. focused on children's perceived fun after an activity, teachers’ perceived gains from classroom participation are also important. Furthermore, since teachers act as gatekeepers for design activities within a school context, their expectations of the user gains of participating in a design activity are equally important [ 15 ]. Both the expected and the perceived gains of classroom participation may relate to what they perceive as their own, as well as the children's gains. This paper thus addresses this gap and focuses on teachers’ expectations and reasons for participation in a design project, as well as their perceived gains from participation in classroom based design activities, both for themselves and for their pupils.

2 BACKGROUND

User Gains of Participation

The HCI literature is rich in studies on how users experience various technology prototypes, however, as pointed out by Dindler et al. [ 7 ], there is a lack of studies investigating how people experience participating in design processes, and especially what they gain from their participation. A design process might in some cases be disruptive, but also be an opportunity to learn for the people participating. How a design process was experienced and what was gained is most often described from the designers’ perspective, for instance in the study by Gerber and Carroll [ 9 ]. In participatory design, a range of studies has started to appear addressing people's experiences in design processes, such as individual gains [ 4 , 5 ] and community gains [ 13 ], the degree of participation [ 8 ], or the perceived value [ 15 ]. Similarly, McNally et al. [ 17 ] investigated the sustained benefits for children in participatory design projects by interviewing both child alumni and their parents, and Schepers et al. [ 21 ] distilled user gains for vulnerable children through an analysis of different kinds of field data from a long-term project. According to Robertson and Wagner [ 20 ] “a PD project approach should include an evaluation of the design results and the design process by participants. More specifically, participants should assess to what extent the design process offers opportunities for genuine participation.” This paper, focusing on teachers’ perceived gains of classroom participation, responds to the call by Dindler et al. [ 7 ] to further examine design projects from a participant perspective. Specifically, it answers to Garde et al.’s [ 8 , p. 79] notion that “to confirm that participants actually benefit in PD projects, they must be queried directly in a structured and meaningful way”.

Teachers’ Perceived Gains

Participation of users in the design process is a corner stone in participatory design (PD). One approach to participation is that of mutual learning, i.e., where the designer and the users learn from each other in the design process [ 6 ]. This means that the designer has to both teach and learn in the development process. Robertson et al. argue that mutual learning is supported by embedding the design process in the practices of the participants [ 19 ]. Bratteteig argues that a successful process of mutual learning can give rise to new possibilities for design, but also to use principles from pedagogy when planning the teaching and defining the learning objectives [ 6 ]. This is in line with our argument [ 2 ] that when working with children in the classroom we should extend the who-what-when-where-how framework for structuring co-design sessions with children by Mazzone [ 16 ], by asking ‘why’ the participants would want to participate. We thus reasoned that teachers would expect and perceive higher gains from classroom participation if learning opportunities for their pupils would be made more explicit. Therefore, in [ 2 ] we proposed a three-step process, where the first step would be for the designers (alone or together with the teachers) to determine what the possible learning goals for the child participants could be for a separate activity and/or for the whole project. These learning goals could be related to: specific content (e.g. science), general skills (e.g. presenting), or design skills (e.g. brainstorming). In line with the recommendation by Iivarii and Kinnula [ 11 ], these learning goals should then be communicated to the children and the design activities should be aligned with the learning goals. Finally, moments of reflection should be incorporated, e.g., by planning discussion sessions, diaries, etc.

In this paper, we thus focus on teachers’ expected and perceived user gains of participation in a design process, both for themselves and their pupils. In this design process the designers actively try to work with creating explicit learning opportunities for the children. We aim to answer the following three research questions:

  1. What user gains of participation do the teachers foresee for themselves and their pupils?
  2. What user gains of the participation do the teachers actually perceive for themselves and their pupils?
  3. What changes to the design process could make the participation more beneficial to the teachers?

3 METHOD

The design projects in which the teachers participated with their classes, were run by students from a an Interaction Design master level course on the design of children's technology. The design projects were performed by 36 students divided into 8 project groups. Besides a number of lectures on how to involve children in design activities, the students had received and discussed our previous paper [ 2 ]. They also received regular supervision during the planning and execution of their design activities. In their project, the students had to plan for at least three visits to the school to perform design activities with the children, and had to follow an ethical procedure including getting informed consent from the children's caregivers through the school.

Data gathering

The data for this paper come from seven of the nine teachers who had volunteered to participate in a design project with their classes and who had agreed to share their experiences with us. Five teachers worked at a preschool, and two teachers worked at a primary school. Five teachers were interviewed in person or over Skype, while two of the seven teachers filled out a questionnaire containing the same overarching questions as the interview. The live interviews lasted between 33 minutes and 1 hour and 14 minutes (average 55 minutes). All teachers were women. The relation between the teachers and the design students groups is given in Figure 1 . The kind of technology the groups intended to design with the children is also given in this table. In order to avoid confusion, we will say ‘designers’ or ‘design groups’ when referring to the design student groups in the remainder of this paper. Figure 2 shows the learning goals for the children as the designers had intended and aimed to support during the different design activities. They were often related to the initial design concept, e.g. to learn about emotions or logical thinking.

The interviews were performed after the final visit of the designers to the school (or the teachers received a written questionnaire with similar questions). Besides a short discussion of the teachers’ background, we used the following questions related to our three research questions to guide the interviews with the possibility for the interviewer to ask deeper questions as part of the main question:

  • What were your reasons for participating in the project? Is it important to you that the designers work with learning goals for the children?
  • Can you describe what happened during the design sessions? Did the designers create and communicate learning goals, did they allow for reflection? What did you perceive as the benefits of your participation for you and the children?
  • Are there any other ways that could have made the participation more beneficial for you or the children?

As a support during the interviews a visual outline of the project process including the three visits of the designers to the school was used for the teacher and interviewer to base their discussion around and to make additional notes. For each of the overarching research questions we performed a thematic analysis clustering similar answers into themes.

4 RESULTS

The results presented below are arranged according to the three overarching research questions. An overview of all results can be found in the Discussion and Conclusion section at the end of this paper.

Expected User Gains

There were several user gains that the teachers expected from participation in the design projects. The expected gains mentioned by each teacher are given in Figure 3 .

The most important reason to participate was that the teachers had an interest in technology themselves and thought that their pupils shared this interest. Some of the teachers considered themselves novices and were specifically interested in learning more about technology and how to utilize digitization, while others explained that both they themselves, the children, and sometimes even the parents had a profound interest in technology.

The second most important reason to participate was that the teachers thought that collaborations could give children a useful and fun experience.

If teachers had considered to refrain from participation, this was usually not related to a direct concern for a lack of possible user gains, such as the activity potentially being boring for the children, or wasting time on activities without a clearly defined learning outcome. Rather, some teachers were concerned about finding time for the activities in their already busy schedules (A and C). Only one teacher (E) mentioned a practical concern with getting informed consent, which was partly related to the obligation to convince parents of the potential user gains for the children:

They [the parents] need to sign this, to give their consent. Because if they don't, we can't use it. We need to explain in a good way to the parents so that they too will find [the project] relevant and interesting

The answers to the previous question indicated that the teachers indeed thought that the experience of participating in a design project could be useful for children. However, the teachers did not explicitly mention the expected learning opportunities for their pupils as catered for by the designers as an important consideration. Since the designers had been coached to define learning goals for the children during the design activities according to the suggestions in the paper by Barendregt et al. [ 2 ] we still wanted to know whether the teachers thought such an approach would influence their expected user gains.

Most teachers answered that it was indeed important to them that the children would learn something from their participation, but that the learning goals may not have to be explicitly defined from the beginning by the designers. Instead, the technology itself and the experience of taking part could also provide learning opportunities later on, either after and/or in-between the design activities (C). Using an argument commonly used for considering educational games, one of the primary school teachers (A) even said that it was probably more important that the activity was fun for the children than that it should have learning goals immediately connected to it. According to this teacher, learning opportunities would only be picked up by the children if they were motivated first:

I feel like a bad teacher if I say enjoyment above everything else, but I will say it again. If the children enjoy it they will learn something from it, that's a fact. If they have lots of learning opportunities but it's not fun, they are going to forget it as soon as they walk out the door. They won't care about it. They need to care, it needs to be meaningful, it needs to be something that they can internalize. If they really enjoy it, they will come to me afterwards with their own ideas and we can create activities around what they experienced in the design activities.

Perceived User Gains

The teachers mentioned several perceived user gains from the participation, both for the children and for themselves (see Figure 4 ).

In line with the findings by Iivari and Kinnula [ 12 ] the teachers thought that the children had acquired some content knowledge, such as programming. In addition, they had practiced general skills, such as e.g., collaboration, negotiation, language, and communication. As teacher C stated:

The children had a chance to test their understanding of programming and learn different concepts in the activities. However, it was also a social experience so the children had to collaborate and negotiate, and in addition communicate with an adult, so that the adult understood their intentions. It would have been totally different if the children had used the game on their own, it would probably have been more focus on logical thinking and problem solving

One of the teachers (B) said the children had also learned about a new profession, that there are people who study and work with making games, and that they can help with and affect the design of these games. The children had also understood that they can incorporate popular culture into different school subjects and that this could provide learning opportunities for themselves and their peers. The teacher explained that the reason she mostly brought up these sort of skills was because the acquisition of content knowledge was almost taken for granted. Another teacher (C) also pointed out that some learning gains can arise in the design activity somewhat unexpectedly, without having been planned as a learning goal. In her case, as most of the designers only spoke English, the teacher described that children were eager to learn new words to be able to communicate with the designers and that many of the children had talked at home to their parents about their ‘English friends’.

One teacher (G) said that the learning goals for the game the designers developed came directly from the curriculum. In addition, the learning goals also included other aspects, such as the importance of making the children understand that they could contribute to the design process and be producers and not only consumers of technology, as is one of the main values of PD.

Although the teachers did not think the designers had made the learning goals very explicit, teacher B, who had worked with three design groups, stated that the designers had a good approach during the activities that supported children's learning, e.g., the designers always explained what they were going to do together with the children, or there was a clear recurring theme in the activities.

One teacher (C) explained that she was able to use questions and interests that the design activities had raised in her own teaching in-between design activities. At these occasions she could also help the children reflect on what they had learned.

I work actively in talking with the children about what they experienced during the day. When talking about the design activities the children often said that they had programmed, and some of them went to get their ‘algorithm bead bracelets’ and thus connected what they did in the design activity to what they had learned before.

Besides mentioning the learning opportunities for the children, the teachers gave a variety of examples about what they themselves had learned or gained from taking part in the project. One teacher (B) who also functions as the school librarian said that the interest in the library had increased among the children since the collaboration with the university. The designers started a lot of processes at the library that the teacher could now utilize in her daily work. She said the children who took part in the project could now be engaged as an expert group to involve in the further development of these processes at the library. Another teacher (D) mentioned that is was great to understand more about the process around designing technology:

I was amazed to see what design students could do in just three weeks and that their end product was something that we could actually use in the classroom. From their first meeting with the children and then to see it in an actual design.

What most teachers emphasized was however what they learned by taking a step back and observe others work and interact with the children.

In only three short visits I saw pupils who were able to express themselves, to work and be active in another way than in their regular classes. I saw children who are usually not that focused, but were now engaged and took initiative. Because that's how it is, the traditional form of school is not for everyone.

One of the teachers (D) also said that it was amazing to see what others can bring to the table with a whole different agenda, and that it was great to see how the designers could elicit different emotions from children with different scenarios. She said that she is usually responsible for the teaching in collaborative efforts, especially with teacher students, but that this time it was the other way around:

I'm not sure if we gave the group anything, they gave us way more than we gave them this time, I didn't teach them anything.

Similarly, another teacher (A) also learned new ways of teaching and working with the children:

I actually learned how to generate ideas in this story format, where they [the designers] asked the children what place in the world they liked best, and then from there moved a character from one environment to another and created this story. I actually recently held a writing workshop [based on the designers’ method] in the library for children ages 8-12 where I did just that, but with just paper, not on a tabletop computer.

Improvements to Increase User Gains

Improving User Gains for the Children. When asked about ways to make participation the most beneficial for them and the children, the teachers stated that defining learning goals for the children was generally a good idea that should be continued. However, they thought that in some cases the designers had mainly used those learning goals (e.g. storytelling, collaboration), to communicate with principals and teachers in the beginning of the project to gain access to the school or the classroom, but after that, they were not discussed in more detail. Several of the teachers therefore thought that it would be better if the learning goals were created in closer collaboration with the teacher. According to three teachers (B,C,D), since designers and teachers have different competencies they could complement each other, letting the designers have an initial idea of the possible learning goals, but elaborating on this idea together.

One teacher suggested that the designers should meet with the teacher before they met the children, both to discuss learning goals, but also to understand what is happening in the classroom. Indeed, several of the design groups that were able to involve the children in some free play to introduce themselves as part of their start-up meeting with the teachers, said that this was a very valuable experience. After this first encounter with the children, they also received the consent forms from almost all children in time for the first design session, something that other groups sometimes struggled with to achieve.

Although most teachers did not experience that the designers had consciously communicated the learning goals to the children during the activities, teacher C said that this was indeed a missed opportunity:

It's good that the children become aware of the expectations we have in a situation connected to learning. To explain that we are going to work with programming, and to use the correct terms and concepts. It is also important to make visible for them that ‘now we have worked with mathematics’ or ‘imagine how many words we learned today’, and that you connect the learning to their everyday lives

Iivari and Kinnula [ 12 ] also mention that learning goals should be explained to the children. However, not all teachers in our study agreed with that, or about who should communicate them. One teacher (A) thought that the design group should communicate the learning goals because this may be more engaging to the children as it is something out of the ordinary. Another teacher (C) thought that the teacher could be supportive in communicating the learning goals, and a third teacher (D) actually thought that learning goals should not be communicated to the children at all, since it was no common practice at the school to do so.

Another point of improvement was suggested by teacher G. She suggested that the designers could have an introduction activity with the children to get to know each other better during the first visit, and not just observe:

The first visit when the students came to observe was less rewarding. The children were very excited about the visit from the students but as they were just there to observe, the children got a bit disappointed. Perhaps next time the students should hold an activity to ‘lighten the mood’.

Several teachers also pointed out the need for more time for reflection about the learning goals. Two teachers (A,B) suggested that the designers could come back one more time to engage in a half-hour discussion with the children. When asked if the school would have time for additional visits one of the teachers (A) said that schools never have time, so they will make time.

Improving User Gains for the Teachers. Teacher E thought that it would have been especially beneficial for her to participate in the last activity when the designers evaluated their final product with the children. Unfortunately, the teacher was not involved in this activity, and therefore felt that this was a missed opportunity.

Two of the teachers (A,D) felt that it would have been beneficial for them if the designers had explained more about what they intended to do during an activity, but also what the designers themselves hoped to gain from a certain activity.

One of the teachers (E) said that it would have been useful if the collaboration had resulted in something, not necessarily a technological artifact, that she could use to build further upon in her own teaching at a later stage.

Finally, the teachers mentioned several impediments to user gains that could be be improved in further collaborations. First of all, one teacher (A) thought it was better if the designers worked with smaller groups of children at a time, making it easier to prevent the children from drifting off. Another teacher (C) thought it would have been beneficial if the designers would have explained to her what was expected of her and how the timing of the activity would be so she could better prepare the children.

5 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

In the subsequent sections we will discuss the relevance of our findings for designers and researchers working with both teachers and children in a school context. Finally, we will also address some of the limitations of this work.

Teachers’ Expected User Gains

Agreeing to participate is related to the user gains that the teachers expect for themselves and their pupils. Our results clearly showed that teachers were motivated to participate because they expected that they and their pupils would be able to learn more about technology. This finding could be related to the fact that several of the teachers were recruited through an educational Maker-project Facebook group in which the university is a project partner, consisting of teachers having expressed an interest in the use of technology in the classroom. However, this finding should also be seen in the light of current educational policy changes in Sweden. The changes, mainly aim to enhance and clarify 21st-century skills and digital competence in the policy documents, and has lead to changes in the national curricula, introducing programming in schools as an integrated part in several subjects, from the autumn of 2018 [ 18 ]. Many teachers in almost all subjects and on all levels are now in need of further education. However, as digitization and programming are not a part of the national teacher training yet, there are high demands for professional competence development. It is thus likely that many of the teachers saw their and their pupils’ participation as a way to address this need.

Teacher's Perceived Gains for the Children

While the teachers saw learning about technology as a possible gain for their pupils, they did not think it was very important that the designers explicitly defined learning goals for the children in order for them to decide whether to participate. The main consideration about whether to participate was if they could find enough time to plan the design activities. As a consequence, several teachers played a rather passive role during the design activities, merely facilitating the designers to manage the classroom interactions. While some teachers had noticed the designers mentioning learning goals to them, they were not very aware that the designers tried to communicate learning goals to the children, or allowed for reflection on those learning goals. One possible reason was that several teachers were not heavily involved in the design activities themselves, due to other obligations. However, when asked explicitly, the teachers were generally positive about the idea of designers considering learning goals because it matched their thoughts on good learning approaches.

Even though the teachers had not paid much attention to the designers’ attempts to work with learning goals, they did experience several learning gains for their pupils. These learning gains were in some cases complementary to or different from those explicitly targeted by the designers, such as learning about a new profession, or communicating in English. Similar to McNally's [ 17 ] results, none of the teachers mentioned content knowledge as a learning gain for the children. One of the reasons was that the teachers already implied that this would happen. Another reason could be that very few groups had content knowledge as a learning goal. However, this also indicates that content knowledge may be hard to target for designers during a design activity. Targeting general skills as well as focusing on issues ”in the heart of [the] PD agenda, i.e., participation, design, and technology” [ 12 ] may be a more natural approach in many cases, although we still think it may be possible to target e.g., math skills when designing a math game.

Opportunities for Teachers’ Professional Development

Although the teachers had expected to learn more about technology, several teachers perceived user gains related to being able to observe the designers and seeing the advantages of applying this kind of working with the children. This is in line with the findings by Kinnula et al. [ 15 ], who stated that ’[f]or teachers, the value co-created was related to their own learning and professional development’. In some cases, the teachers even expressed a disappointment that they would not be able to continue the design work because the designers did not leave the design materials in the classroom at the end of the project. They also thought that it would have been beneficial if more mutual learning could have taken place between themselves and the designers when defining the learning goals and planning the activities.

Taking all of this together, it seems that an unexpected user gain for the teachers was the opportunity to learn more about how to use designerly approaches in their teaching. In Europe, many teachers have indeed become interested in applying new forms of teaching to address 21st-century skills, such as creativity and innovation, critical thinking and problem solving, and communication and collaboration [ 1 ]. Design-Based Learning approaches have therefore received increased attention, and the teachers in this study showed a similar interest. However, in order to effectively use Design-Based Learning approaches, teachers need to “adopt a coaching-role rather than a traditional teaching role and the teaching materials usually need to be developed by the teachers themselves” [ 3 ] (p.532).

If teachers could use classroom participation in a PD project as a way to practically develop some Design-Based Learning activities, become confident about developing future Design-Based Learning activities by themselves, and train on adjusting their teaching role, their user gains could be improved considerably. In that sense, classroom participation in design projects could work as a form of continuous professional development for the teachers. This could partly mitigate the teachers’ concerns about finding time to incorporate the design activities, but also strengthen their involvement in defining relevant learning goals and planning the design activities. As Hjort et al. [ 10 ] have shown by analysing the results of a dedicated course for teachers to work with design processes and digital fabrication, “combining literature with peer collaboration and the development of a hands-on design process [makes it] possible to enrich teachers’ capabilities and to boost their confidence in taking on the challenge of supporting K-12 students in working through the complexities of the design process and in developing design literacy.” However, since our study shows that teachers may not expect this particular user gain, it is important that the designers emphasise this opportunity to the teachers from the beginning, and plan the activities accordingly.

Limitations

The main limitation of this paper is that the design projects were performed by design students rather than professional designers. It is of course possible that the students, in spite of the active coaching by the university teachers to incorporate and communicate learning goals and adapt the activities to these learning goals, were generally not very good at bringing this into practice. As students already struggle with many of the components of doing interaction design with children during a short project course, it is possible that paying attention to learning goals was not their main focus. However, our interviews with the students afterwards (which are not incorporated here due to space limits) indicated that they were indeed quite conscious about the learning goals and could describe them clearly. Another possible reason is thus that the teachers had not been prepared to observe this way of working, and did not have time to pay attention to it. In future projects, it might be advisable to tell the teachers in advance that the intention is to (collaboratively) formulate learning goals for the children. It would also be very useful if professional designers and researchers would try to incorporate learning goals for children in their design process and report on their experiences. If possible, it would also be useful to directly inquire the children about their expected as well as perceived user gains of this approach. Something similar was done by Kinnula et al. [ 14 ] who analysed the roles in which children were successful in value creation and how this was related to children's motivation.

A note also has to be made about the teachers who participated in the design projects. Five of them were pre-school teachers, and since the national pre-school curriculum in Sweden does not put much focus on having explicit learning goals, it is likely that the teachers would not do so either. It is even possible that the reason that most teachers showing an interest in participation came from pre-schools, was that they are less bound by learning goals in the curriculum. Indeed, the two teachers working in primary school (A and B) where the ones who underlined that more time for reflection on the learning goals should be incorporated, even if this would take more time. Several of our interactions with teachers for older age groups in other projects have also revolved around their need to make children's participation in design activities relevant in relation to the curriculum.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we argue that teachers expect and experience several user gains of participating in classroom based design activities, both for themselves and for their pupils. The main gains they expect from their participation are that they and their pupils will learn more about technology, which confirms Kinnula et al's question whether ”learning new skills and competences is a typical experience in other contexts as well’[ 15 ]. They also hope that it will be a fun experience for the children. Explicitly creating design activities that will be fun for the children, as also mentioned by Schepers et al. [ 21 ], is thus a fruitful approach. However, as teachers are the gatekeepers for classroom participation, it may also be useful to explain to them that having fun is one of the main goals. Furthermore, it may be necessary to consider more explicitly in what way children and teachers may learn about technology through the design activities. Learning about technology could be defined as one of the explicit learning goals.

Finally, since teachers are often unable to invest much time in participating in classroom based design activities, a solution we suggest is to develop the design activities into professional development activities for the teachers. By considering the possibility to use classroom participation as a way to develop teachers’ Design-Based learning competencies, it may be possible to tighten the collaboration with the teachers. This may result in more natural mutual learning regarding the definition, communication of and reflection on learning goals. In this way, even children's gains from participation in a design project with their teachers may eventually go beyond those related to the experience itself; they may also gain from having teachers who are able to apply Design-Based Learning approaches when teaching them.
