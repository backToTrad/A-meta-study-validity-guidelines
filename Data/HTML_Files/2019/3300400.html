ThermalBracelet : Exploring Thermal Haptic Feedback Around the Wrist

Roshan L Peiris , Keio University, Japan, roshan@kmd.keio.ac.jp
Yuan-Ling Feng , Keio University, Japan, xxfylxx@kmd.keio.ac.jp
Liwei Chan , Computer Science, Taiwan, liwei.name@gmail.com
Kouta Minamizawa , Keio University, Japan, kouta@kmd.keio.ac.jp

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300400
CHI '19: Proceedings of CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Glasgow, Scotland UK, May 2019

Smartwatches enable the wrist to be used as an ideal location to provide always-available haptic notifications as they are constantly worn with direct contact with the skin. With the wrist straps, the haptic feedback can be extended to the full space around the wrist to provide more spatial and enhanced feedback. With ThermalBracelet, we investigate thermal feedback as a haptic feedback modality around the wrist. We present three studies that lead to the development of a smartwatch-integratable thermal bracelet that stimulates six locations around the wrist. Our initial evaluation reports on the selection of the thermal module configurations. Secondly, with the selected six-module configuration, we explore its usability in real-world scenarios such as walking and reading. Thirdly, we investigate its capability of providing spatio temporal feedback while engaged in distracting tasks. Finally we present application scenarios that demonstrates its usability.

1 INTRODUCTION

Wrist-worn smart devices (e.g. smartwatches) have lead to a widely explored area of research on haptic feedback displays on the wrist. Smartwatches always stay in constant contact with the skin, making the wrist an ideal location for providing always-available haptic feedback. In addition, these devices are typically worn around the wrist, opening up space to enhance the feedback space around the wrist to provide spatial haptic information.

Our cutaneous senses on the skin allow us to detect various stimuli such as touch, vibrations and temperature [ 28 ]. Thus, recent research has explored a variety of haptic modalities for providing feedback on the wrist. Vibrotactile stimuli have been extensively explored with arrangements such as single tactons [ 48 ] and matrix arrangements as watch-back tactile displays [ 20 , 22 , 25 , 31 , 33 , 41 , 46 , 48 ]. Furthermore, vibrotactile actuators have been placed around the wrist as a spatial tactile display [ 14 ]. Recent research has also looked into other types of haptic modalities such as pressure [ 12 ], clenching [ 8 ], skin stretching [ 5 ] and dragging [ 15 ] and wind [ 21 , 43 ].

In addition to the above modalities, thermal feedback has been explored on sites such as thenar eminence (palm area at the base of the thumb) [ 10 , 29 ], and forearm [ 49 ]. In addition to these sites, the wrist presents a highly sensitive site for thermal haptic feedback [ 44 ]. In recent works, Singhal et.al,  [ 44 ] explored providing icon-like thermal feedback patterns on the volar side (bottom side) of the wrist with a single actuator. In ‘Hot & Tight’  [ 45 ], the authors explored providing thermal and squeeze cues on the dorsal side (top) of the wrist. However, previous research is limited in its exploration of thermal haptic perception around the wrist. Considering the advantages of the high thermal sensitivity of the wrist, we identify a clear opportunity to explore providing thermal haptic feedback with an increased number of thermal actuators. With such a device, we investigate the perception characteristics of thermal haptic feedback around the wrist in realistic contexts to convey enhanced information.

With this paper we present the following contributions: We present an optimal layout for thermal haptic feedback around the wrist based on a detailed evaluation with different configurations of thermal actuators and different types of thermal feedback around the wrist; Based on the selected six-module configuration from the above study, we evaluate it in semi-realistic contexts such as walking and compare with vibrotactile feedback under the same conditions; We evaluate the perception of spatio temporal feedback in semi-realistic situations to provide more detailed information and compare with vibrotactile feedback under the same conditions; We present the ThermalBracelet integrated with a smartwatch and present few application scenarios.

2 RELATED WORKS

Vibrotactile stimulation has been the broad choice for many wrist based haptic displays [ 6 , 9 , 27 , 32 ]. Mostly, this is due to the vibrotactile actuators’ small and lightweight form factor that offers easy integration as wearable devices [ 43 ]. Single vibrotactile actuators have been investigated to provide information through rhythmic actuation such as MorseCode [ 48 ]. In BuzzWear [ 23 ] , the authors used a 3 vibrotactile actuator layout to provide information. Azadi et.al. [ 3 ] and Brewster et.al. [ 4 ] investigated the design parameters such as frequency and amplitude to explore the expressivity of a single tacton. Other researchers explored having matrix type [ 20 , 22 , 25 , 31 , 33 , 41 , 46 , 48 ] actuator layouts to present more detailed information. For example, EdgeVib [ 26 ] explored 2x2 and 3x3 vibrotactile arrays on the back of a smartwatch to provide detailed information such as alphanumeric characters. Furthermore,  [ 14 ] presented comparing the use a 4-actuator vs 8-actuator layout arranged around the wrist for providing guidance in a non-visual context.

Pressure is another popular modality for wrist based haptic actuation. In HapticClench [ 8 ], the authors present a new shape memory alloy based mechanism that provided squeezing pressure feedback. In addition, authors have used motor based actuators in [ 7 ] and pneumatic actuation in [ 38 ] to provide pressure based haptic actuation on the wrist.

As another modality, in Skin Drag Displays [ 15 ] the authors used a wrist based wearable actuator that dragged on the the skin surface to provide higher resolution information. In  [ 5 ] and  [ 25 ] authors used skin stretching and pulling as a haptic feedback mechanism for the wrist.

Recent works have also begun to explore multimodal feedback on the wrist. In  [ 43 ] , the authors present using a 2x2 array of wind and vibration based multimodal watch-back haptic display. In this work, the authors explore the perception of different combinations of wind and vibration on the wrist. In  [ 45 ], the authors compared vibrotactile, pressure and thermal feedback based haptic feedback for cues recognition. In this work, the authors used two thermal modules placed on the top of the wrist to identify the accuracy of recognition of different cues.

Thermal Haptic Feedback

In terms of thermal feedback, more sensitive areas such as the face [ 35 ], neck [ 39 ], and ears [ 1 ] have been explored for thermal feedback perception. However, in addition to these sites, thermal haptic feedback has also been focusing on the wrist area. In the human hand, thenar eminence and the wrist are two more sensitive regions for thermal cues [ 44 ]. As such, thermal haptic feedback has primarily focused on the thenar eminence [ 10 , 29 ]. In [ 10 ], the authors present the thermal perception in different environmental conditions with actuators placed on the thenar eminence. In Hiya-Atsu [ 29 ], a temperature augmented computer mouse was used for a spatial navigation task. In this work too the module was aimed at directly contacting the skin of the thenar eminence region. Heat-Nav [ 49 ] took a different approach with three thermal modules placed on the forearm for a navigation task. [ 36 ] looked at placing two thermal modules on either side of the wrist to simulate a thermal stimulus that would “pass through” the wrist. In this brief work, the authors examined the timing of the stimuli that would increase this sensation.

However, recent works started exploring thermal haptic feedback on the top [ 45 ] and bottom of the wrist [ 44 ]. In [ 44 ] authors explore the expressivity of the thermal haptic feedback by exploring different actuation patterns. In ThermoMessage [ 24 ], the authors present a single actuator based wristband and discuss the usability of thermal feedback for peripheral expressions. RainSense [ 37 ] is another recent work that uses a thermal feedback module on the wrist to communicate weather information.

As presented, while most work with thermal haptic feedback on the wrist focused on a single or two actuators placed on the wrist, Jeong et.al.  [ 18 ] did a brief explorations of four actuators on the wrist. In this work, the authors present two studies by looking into the perception around the wrist for different thermal feedback and, exploring the perception of temporal feedback. While this work is similar to our approach in some aspects, we are interested in exploring more deeper into the perception with different configurations of the thermal actuators around the wrist. In addition, the article offers only limited details of the study and results and we could not find any other works that explored multiple thermal actuators around the wrist. Therefore we identify an opportunity to explore providing thermal haptic feedback around the wrist.

3 ThermalBracelet Wristband Prototype

Selection of Thermal Modules

We used Peltier elements (thermoelectric modules) as the main thermal actuator for this work. Peltier elements are capable of providing fast changes in temperature to provide active heating or cooling stimuli. Through preliminary explorations, we selected thermal modules that were designed based on  [ 30 ] (Figure 1 (A)). Another primary reason to select this module was having all the thermal modules, temperature sensors, and the vibrotactile module embedded in a compact form factor that allowed easy wearable usage. These modules are of 32 x 16 x 7 mm size and consists of four peltier modules of which two diagonal peltier modules are used for heating and the other two diagonal modules are used for cooling [ 30 ]. In this manner, using the thermal summation principle [ 19 ], the modules are able to deliver fast thermal cues to the users [ 42 ]. The module also embeds a ForceReactor AF vibrotactile actuator [ 30 ]. Currently, we use the vibrotactile module only for comparative explorations in this work (Study-2 and Study-3).

Interim Prototype System

Figure 2 depicts the interim prototype of the thermal wrist-band. The prototype was built with the intentions of facilitating the evaluations described in the later sections, thus, was required to be mobile and easily re-configurable.

All modules were controlled using a Proportional, Integral, Derivative (PID) controller to provide accurate temperature control. Each module was driven through an H-Bridge driver module 1 that was controlled by an Arduino Mega microcontroller 2 . In addition, we implemented a temporary 8-channel audio driver system to drive each vibrotactile module that uses a PAM8012 3 D-Class Amplifier that was controlled by the same Arduino Mega microcontroller. The audio driver is built temporarily as it is only used for comparative purpose in Study-2 and Study-3 (described in later sections).

Each module draws approximately 880mA at 5V during a single stimulus. We used a commercially available powerbank 4 as the main power supply that is capable of providing up to 2A at 5V from each of its USB ports. As we are not considering simultaneous stimuli for the current scope of the research, the powerbank is capable of providing adequate power for the prototype even in mobile situations.

Currently, we use a laptop to issue the commands to the prototype. The laptop connects to ArduinoMega controller of the prototype through a wired serial port connection at a 115200 baudrate speed. The laptop runs an Open Sound Control 5 based server script implemented on the Processing 6 platform that exchanges commands with the microcontroller. The laptop also connects to the vibrotactile driver modules through its audio interface to provide the necessary audio signals.

For the purpose of the study, we implemented a temporary wristband as shown in Figure 2 -inset. To accommodate a variety of wrist sizes, we attached the modules to a velcro based wristband as shown in the image. The point of attachment incorporated a thin foam layer to mechanically isolate the vibrations from each vibrotactile module (Figure 1 (A)). This arrangement allowed us to rearrange the modules to be equally distributed around the wrist based on the user's wrist size.

4 STUDY 1: THERMAL PERCEPTION AROUND THE WRIST

The main goal of this study was to explore the perception limits of thermal haptic feedback around the wrist with multiple actuators and select a suitable configuration to provide feedback. While Jeong et.al. [ 18 ] studied an early feasibility with four actuators, the design details and results are limited in the article. Hence, in Study 1 we explore different configurations and thermal levels to identify the optimal arrangement for providing thermal haptic feedback around the wrist.

We used three main configurations with four, six and eight thermal modules placed around the wrist (Figure 3 ). With consideration for practical implementation, we did not consider any number of modules beyond eight modules as they would not comfortably fit smaller wrist sizes. As the stimuli, we explored the feasibility of providing hot and cold stimuli as the different thermal levels. For the scope of this study we used ± 3°Cs −1 (plus/minus three Celsius per second -Figure 1 (B)) stimuli for 1 second (on for 1s and then switched off) as such stimuli go beyond the detection threshold (Just-noticeable difference) and ensure the thermal stimuli is perceived [ 19 , 50 ]. All stimuli started from the natural skin temperature (for all participants in this research the starting natural skin temperature range was 32.6°C to 33.8°C).

Study Design. We designed a within-subjects evaluation for Study 1 that consisted of two independent variables: Configuration (Four, Six or Eight Modules) and Thermal Level (Hot or Cold). The dependent variable was the accuracy of the perceived location and thermal level of the stimuli. In each configuration, each location was stimulated with either hot or cold. The configurations were counterbalanced using a Latin Square method and the locations and thermal levels were randomized. Each stimulus was repeated 3 times. Each participant faced 108 total trials (8x2x3 + 6x2x3 + 4x2x3).

Participants. We recruited 12 participants (5 female) aged 22-34 (M: 25.6, SD 4.0) for the study from the author's institution. 11 participants were right handed and all wore watches on their left hand. The average wrist size was 20.33 cm and the average skin temperature on the wrist was 33.2°C. The average room temperature was set to 24°C.

Procedure.

After the initial introduction to the system, the participant's wrist was measured and the thermal modules were adjusted to ensure equal distribution of the modules on the wristband. Next, the participants wore the wristband on their left hand. After wearing each configuration, the participants were provided with a demonstration of the sample stimuli. In the initial phase, this also allowed the participants to familiarize themselves with the study software. The study software was written in Processing and displayed buttons to select the perceived location, thermal level and logged the participants’ response details. The participants used their right hand to use the mouse to report the results.

As the task, after receiving the stimulation, the participants selected the location of the perceived stimulation (Figure 4 ). Next, we provided a 15-second interval in between each trial to ensure the temperature returning to the starting skin temperature and also to avoid thermal adaptation effect of the skin [ 19 ]. After the interval, once the participant clicked ”Next”, the stimuli was provided at a random time within five seconds. In addition, we had an informal interview with the participants after completing experiment. The experiment took approximately 1-hour per participant.

Results and Discussion of Study 1

The location wise mean perceived accuracy results of Study 1 are depicted in Figure 5 (A) with the overall mean, mean cold, and mean hot perceived accuracies indicated in Figure 5 (C). The results were further analyzed using a two-way repeated measure ANOVA tests. A significant main effect was found on Configuration ( F 2,22=24.933, p < 0.0001), and on Thermal Level ( F 1, 11=39.998, p < 0.0001). Post-hoc pairwise comparison with Bonferroni correction between each pairs of the three configurations revealed 8-6: p < 0.0001; 8-4: p < 0.0001; 6-4: p < 0.05. There was no interaction effect found. Therefore, as observed through the results and analysis, we identify that both four and six module configurations under the cold condition performed best in the Study 1.

Overall, cold stimuli performed better in all configurations (Figure 5 ). This result follows the observations of previous research where cold stimuli have been found to be perceived much more accurately than hot stimuli on the face [ 35 ]. In addition, neurophysiological observations show that the skin contains more cold receptors than hot receptors which could indicate greater sensitivity to cold stimuli than hot stimuli  [ 19 , 47 ]. Furthermore, it should be noted that this work was carried out in 24°C room temperature in an indoor environment in summer-like weather conditions. As previous research has indicated, the environmental temperature could have an effect on the perception of the stimuli. However, based on the post-experiment informal interview, six participants used words such as ”crsip”, ”sharp”, ”clear” to describe the cold stimuli feedback as compared to the hot stimuli. Many participants indicated that they were not fond of the hot stimuli. This could also be due to the hot stimuli being closer to the pain threshold as the starting skin temperature was approximately 33.2°C.

The confusion matrices depicted in Figure 5 (B) indicate that the majority of the confusions for cold stimuli occurred just one point away from the correct location. However, for the hot condition, the confusions are spread out more. During the post-interview, three participants mentioned that the modules placed on the edge of the wrist (e.g. modules 2 & 8 in the eight configuration and 2 & 6 in the six configuration-Figure 3 ) were particularly uncomfortable during hot stimuli causing difficulties in perceiving the location accurately. These comments can also be observed in the Figure 5 (A). This could be due to the uneven muscle/skin density around the wrist for some users and, in addition, as the skin contains more cold receptors than hot receptors [ 2 ]. As such, based on these results and observations, we concluded that hot stimuli are unsuitable for the next studies in our research.

Based on these observations we decided to select the six module configuration over the four and eight module configurations. We chose the six module configuration over the four module configuration primarily because the six module configuration offers more options to provide more information as it has more actuators. Although the cold condition in the four module configuration had an almost 100% accuracy, we feel that the six configuration has more to offer as explored through the next studies.

5 STUDY 2: THERMAL FEEDBACK IN A MOBILE SCENARIO

The objective of this study was to evaluate the thermal haptic feedback around the wrist in a semi-realistic scenario such as while walking. In addition, we investigate the performance of thermal as a feedback modality in comparison to vibrotactile as a feedback modality around the wrist. Vibrotactile feedback on the wrist has been utilized in research works especially for providing directions or guidance [ 14 ]. In addition, many commercially available smartwatches today have already integrated vibrotactile based methods for providing notifications. As such, in this study we evaluate the performance of around the wrist thermal and vibrotactile feedback in a semi-realistic walking condition.

Apparatus. For the purpose of this study, we used the 6-module configuration based thermal wristband described in the previous section. As the thermal stimuli, we only consider cold-thermal feedback since it reported a mean perceived location accuracy of 94.91%. The vibrotactile modules (Figure 1 (A)) were actuated at 170 Hz. Each stimulus was provided for 1s similar to the thermal stimuli. This frequency was selected based on the resonant frequency of the modules and also previous works that used similar frequencies to provide tactile feedback on the wrist [ 43 ].

Study Design. We designed a within-subjects evaluation for Study 2 that consisted of the Feedback Modality (thermal or vibrotactile) as the main independent variable. The dependent variables were the accuracy of the perceived location and the stimulus detection time. The condition was randomized for each participant and the stimulated locations were randomized within each condition. Each stimulus was repeated 3 times resulting in 36 total trials per participant (2 modalities x 6 locations x 3 repetitions). After each condition, the participants completed a NASA-TLX questionnaire [ 11 ].

Participants. We recruited 20 participants (8 female) aged 21-38 (M:27.4, SD 5.2) for the study from the author's institution. All but two participants were right handed and all wore their watches on their left hand. The average wrist size was 19.8 cm and the average skin temperature on the wrist was 33.6°C. The average room temperature was set to 24°C.

Procedure.

After an introduction to the thermal wristband system, the participants received a demonstration of the thermal and vibrotactile stimuli (before each condition) in a known order allowing them to familiarize themselves with the task procedure and the study software. In addition, the experimenter verified the reported perceived location and if the stimuli were not clear for the participant. This also helped us ensure that there was no vibrations propagating from one actuator to the wristband. Next, the participants were instructed to walk back and forth along a corridor approximately 3m wide (Figure 6 ) and approximately 30m long at a comfortable pace while holding a tablet which displayed the user study software along with instructions and tasks. The average walking speed was 2.77 kmh − 1 . The study software was written in Processing and ported to an Android Tablet (Huawei MediaPad T3 7 ) and displayed an interface of the actuator locations for the participant to select. In addition, similar to Study 1, we introduced a 15-second delay in between each stimuli. Between two stimuli, we displayed a text for the participant to read. The reading task was aimed at preventing the participant from focusing on the next stimulation. The participant was instructed to tap on the tablet as soon as the stimulation and location was identified. This initial tapping was recorded as the stimulus detection time. Next, the reading text was removed where the participant could report the perceived location using the selection interface. During the full study period of each condition, participants kept walking at their own pace and were allowed to take any breaks if needed. The full experiment took approximately 35min per participant.

Results and Discussion of Study 2

The results of Study 2 are depicted in Figure 7 . Results were further analyzed with a paired t-test which revealed significant differences between vibrotactile and thermal conditions for the mean perceived location accuracy ( t (20) = −3.88; p < .001). While the thermal stimuli were perceived slightly faster (M: 1.45s, SD: 0.69s) as opposed to vibrotactile stimuli (M: 1.49s S: 0.58s), the stimulus detection time did not reveal any significant differences between the two feedback modalities.

Vibrotactile feedback had a lower accuracy level at 77.78% (SD 2.13). A main challenge for providing vibrotactile feedback with multiple actuators around the wrist is that they should be mechanically isolated from the main body of the structure that they are attached to. Otherwise, the whole structure tends to vibrate during the stimuli. We ensured to address this point in our prototype design (Figure 1 (A)). In addition, we ensured this point during the demonstration of stimuli session at the beginning of the study, that, all sample vibrotactile and thermal stimuli are perceived at each different location. However, as three participants indicated, in few occasions, the hand movements while walking made it more difficult to recognize the location of the the stimuli as they felt that a whole portion of the wrist band was vibrating. This aspect could be the reason for the wider points of confusion as observed in the confusion matrix for the vibrotactile feedback condition in this study (Figure 7 B).

In comparison with the Study 1, the mean perceived location accuracy of cold thermal stimuli in the six module configuration slightly decreased from 94.91% to 89.17% (SD 1.36). Such a decrease can be expected due to the increased demand in physical and mental loads. As depicted in the cognitive load results (Figure 7 C), the mental and physical demand and the effort were reported relatively high. In addition, the confusion matrices indicated that the points of confusion have slightly increased to higher levels (more than one-level difference). Some participants mentioned that, although they could identify the location of the stimuli, in certain cases, the hand orientations could have an effect in how they report the results. Similar results have been observed in other previous works such as PokeRing [ 17 ] where, under semi-realistic conditions such as walking and standing, the perceived accuracy of poke feedback around the finger dropped as compared to the original conditions. However, in this research, the accuracy for the thermal condition was still maintained at a relatively high level at 89.17% indicating that, this thermal configuration has the potential to be effective in mobile situations.

6 STUDY 3: SPATIO TEMPORAL FEEDBACK WITH THERMAL WRISTBAND

Many current smart watches and previous research [ 45 ] utilize multiple sequential haptic stimuli usually on the top of the wrist to provide haptic notifications and alerts. Therefore, the motivation of this study is to explore the possibility of expanding haptic notifications around the wrist with spatio temporal stimuli. The starting location and the number of follow-on stimuli could enable more “enriched information” (such as which app, and how many notifications from that app) to be presented. In addition, we investigated the performance of thermal in comparison to vibrotactile stimuli.

Apparatus. As the apparatus for this study, we used the same configuration and setup described in Study 2. In a pilot study, we identified that when the interval between stimuli was 0s, the participants felt a continuous moving sensation of the stimuli. Therefore, in designing the stimuli, we selected the inter stimulus interval to be 0s, that is, once the first stimuli was over (after 1s), the next stimuli would start immediately. In addition, as a design decision we limited the maximum number of stimuli to four stimuli since, beyond that, the full “notification” would take approximately 5-6 seconds which would require a longer attention span.

Study Design. We designed a within-subjects evaluation for Study 3 that consisted of three main independent variables: stimuli start location ( Location ), the number of consecutive spatio temporal stimuli ( Number ) and the modality. As the dependent variable, we recorded the perceived starting location and the subsequent perceived locations of the stimuli. Therefore each trial could provide 2-4 stimuli starting from one of the each six locations. Figure 8 depicts an example of the possible stimuli for location 2. The conditions and the direction of the stimuli (clockwise, counterclockwise) were randomized. Each trial was repeated 3 times resulting in a total of 108 trials per participant (2 modalities x 6 locations x 3 spatio temporal stimuli x 3 repetitions). Following each condition, the participants completed the NASA-TLX questionnaire[ 11 ].

Participants. We recruited 15 participants (5 female) aged 22-35 (M:26.8, SD 4.1) for the study from the author's institution. All participants were right handed and all wore their watches on their left hand. The average wrist size was 19.78 cm and the average skin temperature on the wrist was 33.56°C. The average room temperature was set to 24°C.

Procedure. After the initial introduction, participants wore the thermal wristband prototype and experienced examples of possible kinds of spatio temporal stimuli. Next, during the task, we provided the participants with a game (Online Tetris Game 8 ) to play as the distraction task. At random times in 15 second intervals, the stimuli were provided. Upon perceiving the stimuli, the participants reported the perceived starting location and the subsequent perceived locations of the stimuli using a similar study evaluation software as described in Study 1. Each study took approximately 45 mins.

Results and Discussion of Study 3

The results are shown in Figure 9 . The direction (clockwise, anti-clockwise) of the stimuli were detected correctly. Overall, the thermal haptic feedback results indicated higher accuracy for the perceived stimuli. We initially ran individual 2-way repeated measures ANOVAs on thermal and vibrotactile haptic feedback respectively. The result reported that there was no significant difference on Start Location and Number of Stimuli in both thermal and vibrotactile feedback. To gain further insight into overall performance between the two type of feedback, we aggregated the data across Start Location and Number of Stimuli into the condition of thermal and vibrotactile feedback. A paired t-test showed that thermal feedback is more accurate (85.43%, SD:4.25) than vibrotactile feedback (73.7%, SD:8.91) in delivering spatial temporal patterns (t(14)=4.896, p < 0.0001).

There were no significant differences observed in the cognitive loads (Figure 9 (C)). However, similar to the previous study, the participants mentioned that while some could understand the region, identifying the exact location was challenging. Previous research has also indicated that the suitability of vibrotactile stimuli could be dependent on the context and environment [ 13 ]. As such, we observe that in this task, with the introduced gaming distraction, the perceived location accuracy significantly decreased for vibrotactile feedback.

Furthermore, another reason as mentioned by the participants due to the constant and discrete nature of the vibrotactile stimuli as opposed to the thermal stimuli. A key observation by the participants was that the vibrotactile stimuli felt more ”discrete” in nature. This is due to difference in the type of stimuli. In the context of this work, for thermal haptic feedback, we used a 3°Cs −1 stimuli for 1s. This would mean that the temperature would gradually change for 1s at that rate. Although this gradual change is felt immediately [ 19 ], the participants mentioned that this change felt more continuous/analog in nature. In contrast, the vibrotactile stimuli felt more discrete in nature.

7 DISCUSSION

The goal of this research was to explore the potential of providing thermal haptic feedback around the wrist and to explore its feasibility for use in wearable semi-realistic scenarios. As such, we designed three experiments that looked into the optimal configuration of placing the thermal haptic feedback modules around the wrist, explored the chosen configuration in semi-realistic scenarios with comparison to vibrotactile feedback and, finally, explored the potential of providing spatio temporal feedback in comparison to vibrotactile feedback.

As the results indicate that, the mean perceived accuracy for thermal haptic feedback worked best with cold thermal feedback in the four (99%) and six modules (95%) placed equally spaced around the wrist. These results are in contrast to the previous work that indicated that hot stimuli was perceived better with four thermal modules around the wrist [ 18 ]. Unfortunately, this work is limited in its details and discussion and therefore we are unable to discuss the differences effectively. However, as discussed before, our results are aligned with previous research that indicate that perception of cold thermal stimuli is better than hot stimuli [ 19 ]. Based on the results from our first study, we selected the six module configuration as it offers more room for exploration with a higher number of modules. Our next study revealed that in a semi-realistic condition such as while walking, the mean perceived accuracy for thermal haptic feedback is better around the wrist (89%) than vibrotactile stimuli (78%). This is indicative of the feasibility of providing thermal haptic feedback on the wrist. Finally, our third study indicated that thermal haptic feedback can be suitable for providing spatio temporal stimuli during a semi-realistic distracted task (playing a game).

In addition, from the Study 2 and Study 3, the thermal stimuli performed better compared to vibrotactile stimuli. In previous research that compares different haptic modalities on the wrist, Hot & Tight [ 45 ] indicated that thermal feedback was poorly recognized as opposed to vibortactile and pressure cues. However, it should be noted that in this previous work, the focus was on recognizing different patterns of thermal, vibrotactile and pressure cues on the top of the wrist. In our work, we focus the perceived spatial location accuracy. As a suggestion, this could be viewed as an indicator that distribution of thermal stimuli around the wrist could work better than stimulating the same location with different patterns (this notion is out of scope for this paper and is not verified in this work).

Applications of ThermalBracelet

We implemented a proof-of-concept mobile and wearable ThermalBracelet prototype using the six module configuration. Figure 10 indicates a few potential application scenarios of ThermalBracelet . As the ThermalBracelet  has been integrated with a smartwatch, we identify that scenarios such as haptic guidance and haptic notifications are capable with this device. In the guidance scenario, for example, clockwise or counter-clockwise spatial temporal stimuli could indicate for going upstairs or downstairs at a lift lobby, and other spatial stimuli could indicate finer details about the target location. In the notification scenario, we identify that different spatial temporal stimuli could indicate information from different applications such as two notifications from calendar app or one notification from a messaging app . In addition, in the Weather-feel app, we notify the user about weather conditions (cold thermal feedback for rainy or cold day and hot thermal feedback for a warm or sunny day, etc) by using various thermal stimuli similar to RainSense [ 37 ], and LiquidReality [ 34 ].

8 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

A main limitation of this work is that the explored operational parameters of the stimuli are limited. For example, thermal actuation can offer many different parameters such as starting temperature, temperature change rate, stimuli duration etc. However, this study offers an initial exploration into thermal haptic feedback around the wrist with the mentioned set of parameters that were selected based on previous research. As a part of our next steps in this direction, we hope to explore more actuation parameters. For example, using small rates of temperature range and shortening the stimuli duration could benefit the power requirements.

In this work, we did not consider simultaneous stimuli conditions (stimulating two locations simultaneously). As indicated in previous research, simultaneous stimuli could be difficult to recognize due to the thermal summation effect [ 19 ]. However, as explored in vibrotactile research, phantom sensations with simultaneous stimuli have been utilized to increase the resolution of the haptic display [ 14 , 16 ]. This is another direction we wish to look towards in the future with thermal haptics displays on the wrist.

Currently, although we explore thermal and vibrotactile stimuli separately for comparisons, we do not consider multimodal feedback that could potentially enhance the space for more information. Multimodal feedback has shown potential in various other contexts such as in  [ 43 ] where wind and vibration stimuli were combined and, in [ 39 ] where thermal, wind and even smell [ 40 ] stimuli have been combined for interesting virtual reality applications. As such, multimodal stimuli is an application space we certainly wish to explore in the future.

As we indicated in our studies, all our studies had a minimum of 15s interval in between different trials. The primary reason for this was to avoid the thermal adaptation effect [ 19 ]. In addition, this interval also helped cool down our thermal modules as when peltier modules are actuated over a longer period of time, they heat up and loose the ability to cool effectively. In recent research [ 10 , 45 ], to address this issue, sizable heatsinks have been used. However, our modules consist of a small heatsink integrating inside the module. As such, with our stimuli mechanism (with the intervals), the over heating issue was addressed.

9 CONCLUSION

In this work we present an investigation of using thermal haptic feedback around the wrist. We conducted three experiements to identify the characteristics and the feasibility of using thermal haptic feedback around the wrist. Firstly, we compared three thermal module configurations with hot and cold stimuli (four, six and eight module configurations), out of which the six module configuration was selected. Next we used this configuration in a semi realistic walking scenario and compared its performance against vibrotactile feedback. Finally, we explore the capability of providing spatio temporal thermal feedback in comparison with vibrotactile feedback in a distracted scenario. The results indicate that for both walking and distracted scenarios, the thermal feedback was perceived with higher accuracy than the vibrotactile feedback. As such, we conclude that thermal haptic feedback has the potential to be used in mobile and real world scenarios for haptic feedback around the wrist.
