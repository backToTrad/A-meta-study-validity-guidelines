Only one item left? Heuristic Information Trumps Calorie Count When Supporting Healthy Snacking Under Low Self-Control

Daniel Reinhardt , Julius-Maximilians-Universität Würzburg, Germany, daniel.reinhardt@uni-wuerzburg.de
Jörn Hurtienne , Julius-Maximilians-Universität, Germany, joern.hurtienne@uni-wuerzburg.de

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300708
CHI '19: Proceedings of CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Glasgow, Scotland UK, May 1997

Pursuing the goal of a healthy diet may be challenging, especially when self-control resources are low. Yet many persuasive user interfaces fostering healthy choices are designed for situations with ample self-control, e.g. showing nutritional information to support reflective decision making. In this paper we propose that under low self-control, persuasive user interfaces need to rely on simple heuristic decision making to be successful. We report an experiment that tested this assumption in a 2 (low vs. high self-control) x 2 (calorie vs. heuristic information) design. The results reveal a significant interaction effect. Participants with low self-control resources chose the healthy snack more often when snacks were labelled with heuristic information than when they were labelled with calorie information. Both strategies were about equally successful for participants with high self-control. Exploiting situations of low self-control with heuristic information is a new and promising approach to designing persuasive technology for healthy eating.

1 INTRODUCTION

Pursuing the goal of a healthy diet may be challenging, especially when self-control resources are low. A healthy diet is mostly focused on reducing energy intake while promoting fruit, vegetable and fiber intake [ 5 ]. Self-control refers to the ability to alter or override impulsive responses (i.e. inhibition) and regulate thoughts and behavior [ 4 , 47 ].

Recent research has shown that self-control relies on a limited universal resource, akin to a strength or energy [ 26 ]. Repeatedly exerting self-control (i.e. regardless of activity) will use up the resource, impairing subsequent self-control success, a state known as ego depletion 1 .

Ego depletion might explain the findings of Instacart, an online grocery store, showing that people tend to order healthy food in the mornings and less healthy food in the evenings [ 51 ]. It seems that after an exhausting working day, people prefer to treat themselves; and they may have trouble to select the healthier choice when snacking. In the face of growing numbers of obesity in the developed nations and the accompanying health issues like cardiovascular diseases and type 2 diabetes, we ask how persuasive technology can support people in making healthy eating choices. The goal of persuasive technology is to leave people the freedom to choose for themselves and still provide nudges that make the healthy choices more attractive and more frequently chosen [ 20 , 21 , 52 ]. Designers of e.g. snack vending machines, dietary recommender apps or food ordering websites play a major part in this even when making simple decisions as to what information is to be displayed or highlighted on the screen.

Yet many persuasive user interfaces fostering healthy food choices are designed for situations with ample self-control. Displaying the calorie count of food items, for example, is a common strategy [ 42 , 50 , 58 ]. The idea is that by providing nutritional information, users gain the possibility to reflect on their proclivities and then would consciously choose the healthy alternative. Often, however, the healthy alternative is less attractive than the unhealthy one, thus creating a dilemma between the goal for healthy eating and appreciating the food. These dilemmas need a high amount of self-control to be resolved in favor of the healthy alternative. At the end of the exhausting working day, the reservoir of available self-control is often depleted (i.e. state of ego depletion) and the willingness and ability to reflect upon nutritional information is often not given, so that this strategy may be unsuccessful [ 3 , 13 , 46 , 47 ]. A prognosis, which is in line with dual process frameworks [see 8 ], since if self-control resource depletion hinder the processing of calorie information, the sensitivity to argument quality due to limitations in willingness and mental capacity is also reduced [ 15 ].

In this paper we propose and investigate a different approach for displaying snack information that works on a more automatic and impulsive route of decision making by using well-known influence heuristics like scarcity and social proof [ 10 , 11 ]. The prediction thus is that in situations of high self-control displaying the calorie count of snacks will lead to more healthy snacking decisions, whereas in situations of low self-control, displaying information based on influence heuristics will lead to better decisions. Before reporting on the study, we give a brief overview of the concepts of self-control, influence heuristics and prior work on healthy snacking.

2 THEORY AND PRIOR WORK

Persuasive technology is used to support people in their wish to change their behavior towards the positive [ 20 , 21 , 58 ]. Such technologies use a diversity of strategies, often based on psychological theories of information processing and decision making. On the one hand, human thinking is guided by conscious and reflective processes, on the other hand by unconscious, intuitive and automatic processes [ 14 , 23 , 34 ]. Both types of information processing work together when producing behavior. Automatic processes will create a judgment first that can then be modified by reflective processes [ 28 ]. Persuasive strategies that build upon reflective processes are strategies of direct persuasion, whereas strategies building upon automatic processes are strategies of indirect persuasion [ 41 ].

Strategies of direct persuasion, e.g. those that aim at presenting all decision-relevant information, often fail. People, instead of deliberating each alternative in the light of the relevant information, often automatically apply heuristics in their decision making. Heuristics are simplified patterns of judgment that in most situations lead to satisfying results, but under specific circumstances can lead to erroneous decisions. They are likely to be chosen in situations where the motivation and ability to use reflective processes are low, whilst the target behavior (e.g., goal to eat healthily) is still active, but no motivation and ability can be mustered to behave according to the goal [ 7 , 20 , 44 ].

According to B. J. Fogg, a pioneer in the field of persuasive technology, successful persuasion is dependent on three factors: the motivation of a user to perform a target action (e.g., show healthy behavior), his ability to do so and triggers to perform the action made by the system. Designing a persuasive technology requires increasing motivation or ability or both, while also assuring that the desired action is triggered at the appropriate time [ 21 ]. With heuristics, designers of persuasive technology have available a potent instrument to design triggers. These triggers should function independently from the ability and motivation of the user according to the heuristic-systematic model of Chaiken [ 7 ].

One of these heuristics is the social proof heuristic [ 10 , 11 ]. The social proof heuristic proposes that people tend to assume the behavior of others, especially in ambiguous situations. For example, although it is not explicitly an indicator of good food, we tend to prefer restaurants that are highly frequented by others and tend to avoid those that appear always empty. Another heuristic is the scarcity heuristic. Information or goods seem to be more valuable when they are less available [ 10 , 11 ]. If something is in short supply, everyone else must have wanted it, so it must be good. In addition, if something is in short supply, we are about to lose the freedom of acquiring it. This potential loss of freedom is driving us to take action.

Regarding healthy nutrition choices, indirect strategies of persuasion were found to be more successful than direct strategies of persuasion [ 36 ]. Research also finds that calorie information may not be the best approach to ensure a healthier diet [ 24 ]. Yet, it is astounding that so many user interfaces designed to support healthy eating still rely on direct strategies by presenting nutritional information and require elaborate dietary regimes [ 9 , 30 ].

Self-control theory might be the key to understanding what can make persuasive design strategies for healthy eating more successful. According to the theory, self-control is a limited universal resource that can be used up by exercising it [ 2 , 3 , 28 ]. After exhausting the resource, a condition called ego depletion, the resource must be renewed and cannot be used for further acts of self-control. As self-control exertion relies on one universal resource, it can be depleted by several means (e.g., affect suppression, Stroop Task; for a review refer to [ 26 ]). Conscious and reflective decision making, dissolving conflicts and inhibiting behavioral tendencies are difficult to achieve in a condition of ego depletion, because they all draw on self-control [ 2 , 19 , 26 , 48 ]. In a state of ego depletion, people are vulnerable to external stimuli and temptations because reflective processes cannot intervene [ 6 , 28 , 29 , 46 , 47 ]. In addition, ego depletion increases the tendency to rely on impulsive decision making on the grounds of heuristics [ 15 , 32 , 33 ]. Thus, under low self-control, persuasive technology would be more successful when it appealed more to impulsive than reflective decision making.

Research has already transferred these ideas to the context of healthy snacking (although not to the design of persuasive technology). Vohs and Heatherton [ 54 ] compared dieters and non-dieters and found that the high amount of self-control the dieters exerted over the day makes them more likely to end up in a state of ego depletion. As a consequence, dieters are more likely to be tempted by ice cream at the end of the day than are non-dieters. Salmon et al. [ 47 ] could show that non-dieters in the stage of ego depletion were more likely to choose unhealthy snacks than when not in the state of ego depletion. When heuristic information, in this case social proof information (i.e. the number of people who chose a snack) favored the healthy option, then people in the state of ego depletion chose the healthy option more often than when no heuristic information was given. Thus, presenting heuristic information, although not related to nutritional value, can be very effective in supporting people to choose the healthier option in situations under low self-control.

The goal of the present study is to extend this research to directly compare a strategy of direct persuasion (i.e. displaying calorie information, as most applications do today) with a strategy of indirect persuasion (i.e. displaying social proof or scarcity information) under situations of high and low self-control. Under low self-control we expect that social proof information will be more effective for healthy snack choices than calorie information. Under high self-control we expect the inverse: calorie information will be more effective for healthy snack choices than social proof information. The study is embedded in a context of persuasive technology (i.e. the design of a snack vending machine) aimed at testing these assumptions under ecologically valid conditions. We decided to use social proof and scarcity as heuristics. Although social proof underpins all influence heuristics [ 10 , 11 ], With scarcity we expressed social proof in a different format to draw on a wider variation of stimuli.

3 METHOD

To balance the external and internal validity of the study we carefully designed a controlled experiment that let us test the above hypotheses. In order to make the situation as realistic as possible, we tried to ensure that snacking is a relevant concern to our participants that comes with relevant consequences (participants were slightly hungry and received one of their chosen snacks at the end). We checked for their goal of healthy eating and made sure that the food choices presented real dilemmas. According to a broad body of research, participants are more likely to experience a self-control dilemma when they have to resist a temptation that endangers their long-term goal to eat healthily [ 17 , 18 , 19 , 55 ].

Participants and Design

Ninety-four students (71 female) were recruited from a German university campus. The distribution of participants was intentionally skewed towards women, because women generally tend to have stronger goals of healthy eating than men [ 53 ]. We explicitly recruited participants stating a healthy eating goal. Most were students of media communication (63 students) and human-computer interaction (30 students). One student did not state his course of study. Their age ranged from 18 to 26 years ( M = 20.64, SD = 1.65). Participants were reimbursed for their time in form of partial course credit and a snack of their choice. All participants were naive to the subject of investigation. The study used as 2 (self-control: low vs. high) x 2 (persuasion: heuristic information vs. calorie information) mixed design, with the former as between-subjects and the latter as within-subjects factor.

Procedure

Participants were told that the aim of the study was to explore current snack preferences among students to adjust the range of snacks available at the vending machines on the university campus. The participants were told, that this would be necessary because many snacks in the vending machines have to be disposed when nobody buys them. To enhance the probability that participants were interested in snacks, they were instructed to refrain from eating and drinking (except water) for at least two hours prior to the experiment [ 53 ]. Upon arrival at the laboratory, participants sat down in front of a computer, and completed an online demographic questionnaire implemented in LimeSurvey [ 49 ], which also included some filler questions (e.g., How often do you use snack vending machines on the campus?) to increase the credibility of the cover story and to enhance the personal relevance of the study. After finishing the questionnaire, participants were told that they would get one of their favorite snacks as a gift after finishing the study. They were shown a collection of environmentally friendly snack bags and were told that each snack is only available in limited quantities. The cover story, their feeling of hunger and the personal relevance of snacking thus helped to increase the possibility of encountering a self-control dilemma and served as a good explanation why the experiment could not be done online.

Then, the participants were randomly allocated to the low or high self-control condition. This meant that they performed the E-Crossing task. Although every act of self-control can be used to diminish the self-control resource, we selected the E-Crossing task, which is a proven way to diminish self-control, easily applied, and has been successfully applied in the health context [ 47 ]. The E-Crossing task is the most established depletion task in ego depletion research according to a meta-analysis [ 26 ]. After the E-Crossing task, participants rated their affect on the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS) and their current level of hunger. Subsequently, the participants completed 12 food choice tasks, where they choose between two snacks (i.e. healthy and unhealthy). Six trials included no self-control dilemma and functioned as practice trials. The other six trials included a self-control dilemma and were expected to consume self-control resources (see Food Choice Task below). In half of their six decisions the food was accompanied by an influence heuristic. In the other half the food was accompanied by calorie information (see Figure 2). Finally, the participants filled out a further questionnaire to assess their goal to eat healthily. Then they received one of the snacks they favored in the food choice task, were thanked and debriefed. We did not log the final snack pick of the participants, because this decision was not augmented by direct or indirect persuasion.

Material

E-Crossing Task for Varying Available Self-Control. To vary self-control, we used the E-Crossing task [ 26 , 47 ]. First, participants in both the low and high self-control conditions were first instructed to cross out any occurrence of the letter e in a text presented using LimeSurvey [ 49 ]. This part took five minutes and established a behavioral pattern (habit) that needed to be either maintained or suppressed in the second part of the task to vary self-control. The second part contained the experimental manipulation. Participants in the high self-control condition just repeated the first part of the task using the same rule they had already learned. The participants in the low self-control condition were instructed to cross out each e in the text, except when either another vowel followed the e in the same word (e.g., heat) or when the e was two spaces removed from a consonant (e.g., towel). Previous research has shown that applying complex rules for e-crossing reliably drains self-regulatory resources through response inhibition and, thus, lowers self-control compared to the condition in which participants followed the simple e-crossing rule in both parts [ 26 ].

Replication studies that failed to show ego depletion effects [ 25 , 38 ] used an early version of E-Crossing or an another task, which did not include a habit forming period (i.e. participants have to cross out every e to establish a habit, which they then need to override). A broad body of research, however, suggests that the inhibition of a habit is important to reliably create a state of ego depletion. Inhibition is the main determinant of ego depletion and thus versions of the E-Crossing task including inhibition are more demanding [ 15 , 32 , 33 ].

Heuristics and Calories Presented as Picture Captions. In the influence heuristic condition, either the scarcity or the social proof heuristic was used. In terms of social proof, a label was placed beneath each picture of the food pair, which allegedly represented the ratio of choices of students who previously participated in the study. To provide statistical information about a relevant reference group is a common way to operationalize social proof [ 10 , 11 ]. Instead of using exact percentages we used the terms many people choose this and a few people choose this (see Figure 2) to indicate which food was chosen by the higher or lower number of people. This we thought would allow for more flexibility when applying the heuristic in practice. Compared to the social proof heuristic the scarcity heuristic [ 10 , 11 ] does not require an explicit reference group. In particular, we indicated scarcity through stock information on the labels beneath the snack pictures. Scarce options were labeled last item; the other options were coupled with a random stock number between 5 and 15 (see Figure 2).

The distribution of the random numbers was counter-balanced among all participants. In the condition without heuristics, calorie information was placed under the pictures of the food pairs see [ 36 ]. Calorie information was expressed per normal serving and came from an online food database (see Figure 2).

The Food Choice Task

The Food Choice Task consisted of six practice trials and six trials representing self-control dilemmas. In each trial, participants were confronted with a food pair consisting of a healthy and an unhealthy snack. These product pairs were determined in a pilot study similar to [ 47 ] (see below).

During the actual experiment, participants indicated which of the two snacks they would prefer at the moment. Due to possible sequence effects, the same snack pairs could not be presented both in the heuristic and the calorie information condition, which is why an incomplete balanced block design was used for stimuli presentation. Since our dependent measure was the number of healthy food choices and thus individual comparisons between single snacks were not of interest to us, comparing blocks was adequate. The rationale of these kind of designs can be found in [ 47 , 53 ]. Figure 3 shows an example sequence of the presented snack pairs.

Initially, the six practice snack pairs were presented via an online survey in two blocks (heuristic and calorie information). The order of the blocks was randomized among all participants. In the practice trials, four different versions of the snack pairs were used in the heuristic condition: social proof in favor of the healthy snack, scarcity in favor of the healthy snack, social proof in favor of the unhealthy snack and scarcity in favor of the unhealthy snack. The two latter versions were used additionally to disguise the actual study objective (that required favoring the healthy choice). The two latter versions were used additionally to disguise the actual study objective (that required favoring the healthy choice).

All versions of the snack pairs occurred in the same block equally often across all participants. In the calorie information condition, calorie information was used as mentioned before.

Next, the six self-control dilemma snack pairs were presented to the participants. Care has been taken to ensure that not two heuristic or two calorie blocks were displayed successively. The heuristic conditions always favored the healthy choice. Both heuristic versions occurred equally often across all participants. The calorie information in the calorie information condition was displayed as in the practice trials (and naturally favored the healthy choice).

In this way, each participant saw all twelve snack pairs. The randomization of the blocks and the allocation of the snack pairs to the different blocks by creating the correct picture captions were done in LimeSurvey [ 49 ]. Thus, neither the experimenter nor the participant knew about the relation between stimuli and presentation what makes this study a double-blind study.

Pilot Study

In order to confront participants with real self-control dilemmas, the snack pairs should represent a trade-off between the goal to eat healthy and the goal to enjoy tasty snacks [ 47 , 53 ]. This means that one of the snacks has to be tempting and unhealthy, whereas the other has to be healthy and not very palatable. Inspired by [ 47 ] we conducted an online study using a separate sample of 39 participants (25 women) with an average age of 21.69 years ( SD = 2.75).

Pictures of 36 snacks were shown to the participants in a randomized order and they evaluated their healthiness (i.e., How healthy do you think this snack is?) and their temptation (i.e., How much do you want to eat this snack right now?) on 7-point Likert scales ranging from 1 (not at all) to 7 (very much) [ 47 , 51 ]. The snacks were chosen on the basis of the survey Snack Attack: What Consumers are Reaching for Around the World by the Nielsen Company [ 12 ].

On this basis, six snack pairs were chosen that significantly differed on the healthiness and temptation dimension: chocolate cookie vs. flapjack, muffin vs. trail mix, apple crisps vs. pumpkin seeds, sandwich cookie vs. dates, chocolate lentils vs. wine gums, hazelnut slice vs. almonds. In these pairs, which are likely to cause a self-control dilemma, the healthy snack is rated healthier and less tempting than the unhealthy snack.

In addition, six snack pairs for the practice trials (i.e., these snacks have to differ significantly on the healthiness dimension, but not on the temptation dimension) were chosen out of the remaining snacks: chocolate bar vs. cashew nuts, sour sugar-coated fruit gums vs. dried mango, cereal bar vs. rice cake, candy bar vs. peanuts (natural), jelly babies vs. crisps, chewing pellet vs. rice cracker (see Table 1 and 2 in the supplementary material for all means and standard deviations).

Control Variables

Current level of hunger, gender, affect and the goal to eat healthy were measured, because they might affect food choices. The level of hunger was measured in the same way as in [ 47 ] with one item, (How hungry do you feel at this moment?) on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 7 (very much).

In contrast to [ 47 ] we used the broadly available PANAS to measure to measure positive and negative affect [ 57 ]. Scores can range from 10 to 50, with higher scores representing higher levels of affect (either positive or negative).

Finally, the goal to eat healthily was measured with the same item as in [ 47 ], To what extent do you have the goal to eat healthily? on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 7 (very much).

4 RESULTS

Descriptive Data and Randomization Check

Participants reported a moderate level of hunger ( M = 4.43, SD = 1.47) and reported fairly high goals of healthy eating ( M = 5.02, SD = .98). Regarding affect as measured with PANAS, a mean score of 33.3 ( SD ± 7.2) can be used as a benchmark value for positive affect and a mean score of 17.4 ( SD ± 6.2) as a benchmark value for negative affect [ 57 ]. On this basis, participants reported a small positive affect ( M = 22.39, SD = 5.67) and an average-sized negative affect ( M = 15.13, SD = 4.70) after the e-crossing task.

Five separate ANOVAs with hunger ( F = .72, p = .40), healthy eating goal ( F = .102, p = .75), gender ( F = .01, p = .92), positive affect ( F = .097, p = .76) and negative affect ( F = .081, p = .78) as dependent variables revealed no significant differences between the self-control conditions. This suggests that the random allocation of participants across conditions was successful.

Overall, 27.48 % of all food choices were healthy. The average amount of healthy food choices in the heuristic condition was .89 ( SD = .84), and .77 ( SD = .82) in the calorie condition on a scale ranging from one to six possible healthy choices. However, a t-test revealed no significant differences, t (93) = -1.17, p = .250, d = .12.

As the collected control variables (i.e. level of hunger, gender, affect and healthy eating goal) may have had an influence on healthy food choices, we checked for correlations with these variables, but with no significant relationships (all p < .05). Due to this result, additional ANCOVAs with control variables were not performed on the data.

Effects of Ego Depletion and Persuasion on Food Choice

To test the effect of the factors self-control, persuasion, and their interaction on healthy choices, a mixed ANOVA was performed. Self-control functioned as between-subject factors and persuasion as within-subjects factor. There were no main effects of self-control and persuasion on the number of healthy choices made, all F s < 1.

As expected, the interaction was significant, F (1, 92) = 5.76, p < .05, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ = .06. For participants low in self-control, the number of healthy choices was higher in the heuristic ( M = 1.00, SD = .82), compared with the calorie information condition ( M = .58, SD = .71), F (1,92) = 6.76, p < .05, , $\eta _{p}^{2}$ = .07. Participants high in self-control show no significant differences between the heuristic ( M = .81, SD = .85) and the calorie information condition ( M = .91, SD = .88), F (1,92) = .43, p = .510, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ = .01.

Furthermore, when presented with calorie information, participants who were high in self-control made descriptively more healthy snack choices than participants low in self-control, though this difference did not reach statistical significance, F (1,92) = 3.87, p = .052, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ = .04. When presented with heuristic information, participants who were high in self-control made descriptively less healthy snack choices than participants low in self-control, but this difference was not statistically significant, F (1,92) = 1.13, p = .291, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ = .01 (see Figure 4).

5 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

The elaboration likelihood model [ 44 ] and the heuristic-systematic model [ 7 ] suggest that to effectively influence behavior, persuasive messages should be elaborated on [ 47 ]. Self-tracking technologies in the health domain typically rely on strategies of information and rational choice, which are requiring enough willingness and mental capacities to make well-deliberated decisions. However, health behavior is often determined by impulsive influences [ 47 ], because people frequently cannot muster enough motivation and mental capacity to behave according to to their healthy goal, although this goal is still active.

In this study, we investigated whether calorie information or heuristic information are more effective under conditions of low and high self-control. The prediction was that calorie information that requires conscious and reflective decision-making processes would be less effective in a state of low self-control (i.e. ego depletion) than heuristic information that is automatically processed. This prediction was confirmed. Further it was predicted that calorie information would be more effective in a state of high self-control than information heuristics. The data do not support this hypothesis; both strategies were about equally successful under high self-control. Our study thus shows that not only in the situation of ego depletion, heuristics help to let people attain their goals. Such an approach is directly relevant to develop more useful and effective health technologies that are not abandoned after their novelty effect wears off [e.g., 43 , 16 , 31 ].

The results support previous findings that show that the value of calorie information is limited [ 24 ] and that indirect persuasion strategies using heuristics may be more successful than direct strategies based on nutritional information [ 36 ]. More specifically, it shows that previous findings regarding the effectiveness of heuristics under low self-control [ 47 ] can be transported to interacting with screen-based displays. This study, for the first time directly pitted heuristic information against calorie information and shows its superiority under conditions of low self-control. But even in conditions of high self-control heuristics seem as effective as calorie information.

The results are of high practical importance. They show that previous strategies of persuasive technologies that track and display calorie information are, at best, only the first half of the story. Calorie information only appeals to individuals in situations with high amounts of self-control. The second half of the story is that designing for unconscious and automatic information processing is necessary when self-control is low. The findings of this study have also shown that it is possible to be effective with only minimal and quite vague heuristic information (many people choose this instead of giving exact numbers) based on social proof and scarcity heuristics. Because we did not log the heuristics separately, we cannot give more details to their differential effectiveness and future studies should examine the distinctive effectiveness of different heuristics.

Using indirect strategies of persuasion based on heuristics might not only be more effective but also less effortful than approaches geared toward strengthening the self-control muscle. One proposal made in [ 35 ] involved the Chocolate Machine. Placed at a study desk, the machine releases a chocolate ball onto the desktop - about every 40 to 60 minutes. The user can either eat the chocolate or put it back into the machine, which is registered by a counter. The intervention proved effective: repeatedly resisting the chocolate led to higher persistence in another self-control task: solving anagrams. Such self-control trainings with technology are well-suited for prevention that pays off when people are confronted with self-control dilemmas. Meta-analyses, however, show that especially in the dietary domain such strategies are only moderately successful [ 27 , 40 ]. Again, the explanation could be that most health-relevant decisions are made in a state of low self-control [ 1 , 56 ]. Once in that state, self-control trainings are not effective anymore.

To avoid erroneous decision making and to support people in achieving their long-term health goals as much as possible, persuasive technology, therefore, should be able to assess how much self-control is available to the user. We envision systems that can adaptively change what information to display according to how much self-control a user is able to summon for a task. Sensing user states is common in ubiquitous and context-aware computing and approaches that sense self-control/ego depletion are already being developed. Earlier work found, for example, that self-control can be unobtrusively and continuously measured via the entropy of cursor movements [ 45 ]. However, less well developed are approaches that try to understand what these data mean and, in particular, how to design system responses based on these understandings. Here, our paper shows that, depending on the inferred self-control state of the user, different system response strategies will be successful and that appealing to the heuristic mind should always be considered, even when dealing with situations under high self-control. In the domain of healthy snacking, the paper concretely shows which strategies can be chosen and also demonstrate how these strategies can apply to other domains (see Figure 1).

To arrive at fully functioning systems, however, further work is needed. Although care has been taken to simulate real snack choices as much as possible in our study, it is desirable to obtain field data on how heuristic information influence healthy snacking in the wild. Old sales tricks like scarcity and social-proof information have shown to be successful in this study. The effects of other heuristics need to be investigated, too, e.g. the decoy and default effect [ 36 ] many more e.g. [ 11 , 29 , 37 ].

Finally, using the heuristic route of persuasion may also benefit other choices in healthy behavior, including exercise, smoking, drug and alcohol consumption as well as life choices with respect to impulsive buying, cutting smartphone use, or fighting procrastination (see Figure 1 for some examples). Combining heuristic with fact-based decision making supported by persuasive technology could help us to fulfill many goals to self-develop and lead better lives. Designers, however, need to be clear about the ethical implications: heuristics are a double-edged sword that can also be used as means of manipulation. As with every persuasive technology, individuals should not be forced in their choices, but instead always have the possibility to consciously choose another option [ 52 ].

FOOTNOTE

1 Ego depletion has come under scrutiny with the publication of some replication failures [ 25 ] and latest meta analyses [ 22 , 39 ] decide that the available evidence is inconclusive. Lurquin and Miyake [ 39 ], however, suggest that due to the high variation in definitions and operationalisations in the literature it is best practice to state a clear operational definition of self-control, to use an empirically validated task to diminish self-control in empirical work, and to make unambiguous, falsifiable predictions in theoretical work. We decided to follow this advice here.
