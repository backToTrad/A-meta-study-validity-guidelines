Apprise: Supporting the Critical-Agency of Victims of Human Trafficking in Thailand

Hannah Thinyane , United Nations University, Institute on Computing and Society, Macao, hannah@unu.edu
Karthik Bhat , United Nations University, Institute on Computing and Society, Macao, krtk.bhat@gmail.com

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300385
CHI '19: Proceedings of CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Glasgow, Scotland UK, May 2019

Human trafficking and forced labor are global issues affecting millions of people around the world. This paper describes an initiative that we are currently undertaking to understand the role technology can play to support the critical-agency of migrant workers in these situations of severe exploitation. Building on five consultations with more than 170 direct and indirect stakeholders in Thailand, the paper presents the co-design, development, and evaluation of Apprise, a mobile app to support the identification of victims of human trafficking using a Value Sensitive Design approach. It also provides a critical reflection on the use of digital technology in the initial screening of potential victims of human trafficking, to understand in what ways Apprise can support the critical agency of migrant workers in vulnerable situations.

1 Introduction

Sopon is a Cambodian fisher who moved to Thailand in search of employment to support his family back home. His work hours are very long – too long in fact, and the conditions are harsh. The pay is very small, about 10% of what Sopon agreed to, but the skipper has promised him the rest as a lump sum at the end of his one-year contract. Half way into his contract, the fisher is informed of a death in his family and asks to be allowed to travel home to attend the funeral. The skipper refuses. Sopon asks if he can cut his contract short, and receive the outstanding payment, but again the skipper refuses. The skipper proposes that the fisher can leave and forfeit his payment, which by this stage equates to 90% of his pay over six months, plus his share of the catch. The cost is too high so Sopon misses the funeral and keeps on working. To make an example of the fisher, the skipper makes sure that he can't leave the boat when it is in port, forcing him to stay on board. While he would never admit it, Sopon is a good worker and the skipper doesn't want him to leave as he'd have to find and train up a replacement.

One day before leaving port, labor inspectors conduct a routine inspection. They call Sopon and five of his co-workers aside to ask about their working conditions. The labor inspector has a translator with him, and the translator is fluent in a few languages from neighboring Myanmar, but only has a basic understanding of Khmer. After months of being kept on board, Sopon has had enough. Despite being surrounded by the five other fishers, he gathers the courage to try to tell the inspector about his situation and ask for help. The other fishers understand what Sopon is saying but remain silent. They don't want the skipper to hear that they were involved at all, because they too are owed the remainder of their pay. Through the broken communication, the inspector understands that there is a problem, but the key signs he knows to look for – signs of physical violence, and document retention – are not evident. He makes a note in his file and sends Sopon and the other fishers back to the skipper, giving the boat clearance to set sail.

We begin this paper with a scenario that we have created by piecing together the stories from different stakeholders that we have consulted with over the past two years, to reveal the real problems that are faced while trying to identify victims of trafficking in Thailand. While this example depicts a common situation within the fishing sector, our discussions with stakeholders in other sectors revealed different patterns of exploitation, across a wide range of sectors including seafood processing, manufacturing, sexual exploitation, construction, and forced begging.

Thinyane [ 51 ] describes an initial study that she undertook to understand how victims are currently identified across different sectors in Thailand. That work uncovered a two-step process of identifying workers who are in vulnerable situations, and who may want and need help to exit that situation: initial screening in the field; followed by off-site, in-depth questioning to understand the severity of the exploitation. Often this second step is delayed, to provide victims with time to recover (both physically and mentally) and be ready to take part in the extended interview, consisting of over 200 open-ended questions. She asked participants, consisting of frontline responders (FLRs), trafficking survivors, Government officials, non-Governmental Organizations (NGO) and inter-Governmental Organizations (IGO), how they believed technology could support them in identifying potential victims, and their responses were wide-ranging, from networks of web-cams to monitor for exploitation, to mobile phone-based lie detectors. One suggestion that participants raised across each of the different focus groups, was to help them to communicate with potential victims, to understand how vulnerable their situation was, and if in fact they wanted to leave it. This paper describes the co-design and development of Apprise, the system we created to support FLRs in this initial screening phase of victim identification.

In this paper, we contribute to the growing body of HCI literature on design considerations for migrant and vulnerable populations. We build on Brown and Grinter's designing for transient use [ 6 ] by developing design considerations while designing for non-recurring once-off use. Our contributions include: i) describing the design requirements for vulnerable migrant workers in Thailand as surfaced by the different stakeholders at our consultations, engendering future research; ii) discussing the common issues that would influence victims to correctly self-identify or intentionally not self-identify based on our findings; and iii) reflect on the role of technology to support workers’ critical-agency when in vulnerable situations.

The next section provides some background and context on the issues of forced labor and human trafficking, and the current situation in Thailand. This is then followed by a discussion of related work, and the theoretical framing that underpins our research. We then present the design of Apprise followed by a discussion on the use of technology to support workers’ critical-agency.

2 Forced labor and Human trafficking

In Convention 29, the International Labor Organization (ILO) defines forced labor as “work or service which is exacted from any person under the menace of any penalty and for which the person has not offered himself voluntarily” [ 20 ]. This convention is part of ILO's fundamental principles and rights at work, including other core principles such as: freedom of association and the right to collective bargaining; abolition of child labor; and the elimination of discrimination of employment and occupation. As part of ILO's ‘core eight’, this convention is binding for all member countries of the ILO, implying that stakeholders can appeal to regulators in their area to increase labor enforcement.

We use Skȓivánková’s continuum of exploitation ( Figure 1 ) which shows work environments ranging from ‘decent work’ (as defined by International Labor Organization [ 12 ]) at one end to ‘forced labor’ at the other [ 46 ]. Between the two points are situations that do not comply with ILO's definition of decent work and reveal labor law violations (to some degree or another). These range from more benign forms of labor violations such as discrimination and payment under minimum wage on the left, to forced labor on the right. As the arrow in Figure 1 illustrates, the human trafficking process is a series of exploitative acts that moves the worker towards a situation of forced labor. While we can understand the human trafficking process as the means to an end of forced labor, popular media, practitioners and academics often use these two terms interchangeably.

The human trafficking discourse uses the terms ‘survivor’ and ‘victim’ to invoke specific narratives, meanings, and reactions, each of which have racial, gender, and class undertones [ 27 ] Proponents of the term “survivor”, on the one hand, indicate that this term locates “the problem from the individual psyche to the social sphere where it rightfully belongs, … to empower victims to act constructively on [their] own behalf and thus make the transition from passive victim to active survivor” [ 2 ]. Mahdavi notes that “individuals often find and exercise agency even within the structures that seek to limit them” and the word ‘survivor’ recognizes that individuals can be active agents in their lives during and after their exploitative experience [ 27 ]. The term ‘victim’ on the other hand implies a crime with a perpetrator, allowing anti-trafficking actors to identify the traffickers as the only offenders requiring punitive action, and victims requiring protection [ 15 , 48 ]. At the same time, being identified as a victim of trafficking by government or law enforcement, is a requirement for migrant workers to remain in the destination country, or to gain protection rather than to face criminalization [ 15 , 38 , 48 ]. Accordingly, not being identified as victims, may cause individuals to be charged for alleged crimes, including prostitution or illegal entry [ 48 , 57 ].

In this work we cautiously use the term victim, uncomfortable with the connotations of lack of agency that it can insinuate, but because our work feeds into legal definitions of victimhood and the access to complaint mechanisms, protection, and support that it implies.

2.1 Victim identification in Thailand

Thailand is hailed as a land of opportunities by its neighboring countries by virtue of being one of the largest economies in the Mekong sub-region [ 39 ]. Consequently, Thailand has seen a large influx of migrant workers from its neighboring countries as well as internal migrants from its own rural areas to its urban and semi-urban areas to meet a demand for cheap labor [ 39 ]. This mismatch in the labor market is a conducive environment for exploitative working conditions.

The extent of the problem related to human trafficking and forced labor is clouded by the lack of reliable data on the number of victims, and by the very nature of this “illicit, clandestine and underground economy” [ 56 ]. These exploitative work situations are able to exist due to a number of reasons including: private or inaccessible work environments; and poor regulation and enforcement of labor standards across the labor market. In Thailand, state actors (local and federal police, labor inspectors, Royal Thai Navy) work with non-state partners including social workers, inter-governmental organizations, and non-governmental organizations to assess working conditions, to help potential victims become aware of and gain access to social services, complaint mechanism, and support (for example emergency shelters, legal representation). State actors are also tasked with the role of ensuring that cases of labor exploitation are firstly identified and then dealt with (typically using penalties for exploiters and recompense for victims). In this paper, we refer to this broad cross section of stakeholders as FLRs.

Our work brings together these FLRs with survivors of labor exploitation, to co-develop a standard practice that can be used to support the initial screen of workers for signs of exploitation.

3 Related Work

As noted by Zimmerman, Hossain and Watts [ 58 ], the situations that victims find themselves in are similar to other vulnerable populations, including migrant laborers [ 1 ]; irregular migrants, refugees and asylum seekers [ 44 ]; and victims / survivors of torture or abuse [ 8 ]. Given these similarities, and the nascent research in this area, it makes sense to consider ICT studies undertaken with this broader set of vulnerable populations.

Our review of literature extends to prior work on designing technology for marginalized populations, including migrants, and people engaged in sex work where it is stigmatized. We also examine works that study low-resource populations. We further present existing work that discuss aspects of literacy and communication barriers in designing technology, focusing more on those that do so in the developing world. We finally provide a commentary on Sen's capability approach which forms the theoretical framework of our work.

3.1 Designing with marginalized populations

The field of HCI has focused extensively on designing with marginalized populations, with works at the intersection of HCI and ICTD focusing on plurality perspective and the importance of marginalized voices [ 5 ] and empowerment of marginalized populations [ 32 , 34 ]. The specific issues of migration and human trafficking has also been studied in the context of its interrelationship with the internet and online technology in addressing or exacerbating the problem [ 25 , 26 ]. There are a number of studies that examine the role of technology in the lives of marginalized migrants. Almohamed and Vyas [ 3 ] examine refugee and asylum seekers’ experiences in their new country and the challenges they face in the course of their social inclusion. They seek to establish some design foundations for ICT solutions for these populations by taking into consideration their challenges and experience with technology. In [ 52 ], authors study the use of WeChat among dual migrant Chinese workers in Macau, to understand how social media technology is being used by these populations. With in-depth interviews, they study the challenges faced by these populations while being migrant workers in Macau, and how they use technology like WeChat to overcome these challenges, supporting their acculturation. Talhouk et. al. [ 50 ] studied community radio usage by Syrian refugees in Lebanon and its impact on their health education and the community dynamics with local healthcare providers. Brown and Grinter [ 7 ] studied how technology could help refugees’ vulnerabilities while resettling into the USA. They describe issues of language and culture the refugees faced in this process, and how Rivrtran, a tool to enable communication between these refugees and American families, could bridge those social gaps. All of these studies surface challenges faced by migrant populations in their migration destination and exemplify a strong argument for involving vulnerable populations in the ideation and design of technology, to identify and then co-develop solutions to address the challenges that they face.

Designing technology solutions for local and global scale problems in low-resource environments has been the focus of HCI research in the recent past. As described by Dell and Kumar [ 14 ], this is a trend that has only increased in the past few years. Further, the authors show that with the increasing accessibility and affordability of mobile technology, the focus of HCI research in this area has been more towards designing for mobile technology, and less towards designing new hardware for different contexts. Parikh and Lazowska [ 35 ] also identify a similar opportunity of smartphone technology to “… bootstrap computing in the developing world”.

Sex work is, across various countries, a stigmatized profession. Extensive past research has studied sex work with medical [ 18 ] [ 55 ] and human rights [ 13 ] focuses. Little work, however, has attempted to study sex workers’ use of technology. Sambasivan et. al. [ 40 ] built a broadcast-calling system in collaboration with a local NGO to provide at-risk urban sex workers with relevant information while considering unique design constraints pertinent to that population. Strohmayer et. al. [ 49 ] explore the social injustice relating to sex workers, and how technology could ameliorate some of these injustices. They stress the importance of addressing sex work and sex worker rights as a part of contemporary HCI studies.

3.2 Designing for low literacy

A major design consideration when developing technology for low-resource environments is that of low literacy. Migrants and semi-skilled workers, who comprise most of the victims of human trafficking, face similar language-based challenges in using technology [ 45 ]. There has been a lot of work that has been undertaken in the past two decades, aimed at developing interfaces for illiterate and semi-literate users [ 24 ]. Researchers have taken various approaches to designing for low literacy: the most successful of which have been visual and speech-based technology. Medhi et. al. [ 28 ] study and compare different alternatives for textual interfaces for novice and low-literate users. They show that graphical, spoken, or live-operator interfaces suit those populations much better than textual interfaces. Other studies [ 9 ] show that graphical interfaces, more so those with large visible buttons, perform well with these populations. Various speech-based approaches have been attempted too. Kazakos et. al. [ 23 ] present design learnings from deploying an IVR system called “Sehat ki Vaani” to disseminate health-related information in low-resource areas of North India. Vashistha et. al. [ 54 ] designed and studied the usage of a voice-based forum for cultural content that was curated by members of the community and used by the people of the community.

These interfaces are often either full iconic devices or with audio capabilities. These systems often employ a “training wheels concept”, supporting layered designs and scaffolding techniques which are useful when an interface is designed to support the “… graceful evolutionary paths for learners” [ 43 ]. These techniques can be useful to support learning in situations such as Brown and Grinter's “transient use” [ 7 ] or more long term situations, but the question becomes how to design for immediate usability, where a system will only be used once by a typical user. Scaffolding techniques add ‘bulk’ or ‘overhead’ to an interface, in order to support the user's learning. They also take precious screen real estate, and more importantly user's time. Our work focuses on this idea of short-term once-off usage by users.

Working with these at-risk populations involves design constraints specific to their different contexts. Our research is conducted in a context where target users are in constrained spaces under the constant surveillance of their employer/exploiter (fishing boats, seafood processing industry), time-constrained situations and where the nature of risk inhibits open communication unless with known and trusted individuals (sexual exploitation). Sambasivan et al. [ 40 ] in their work designing technology for urban sex workers in India describe their design constraints like times of calling, privacy, and desired invisibility of the target population owing to stigmatization. They also explain their research ethics in working with these populations. These design constraints align well with that of our research as we explain in the future section. Our research examines this once-off usage of a technological artifact by users with a reduced situational agency with the goal of enhancing their critical agency.

3.3 Capability Approach

In his book Development as Freedom , Sen presents his ‘capability approach’ to development, built on the concept of ‘development as freedom’, arguing that the expansion of human freedom should be viewed “as the primary end and the principle means of development” [ 41 ]. He defines development as “the ability – the substantive freedom – of people to lead the lives they have reason to value and to enhance the real choices they have” [ 41 ], in doing so removing “unfreedoms” such as “poverty as well as tyranny, poor economic opportunities as well as systematic deprivation, neglect of public facilities as well as intolerance or over-activity of repressive states”[41].

An important concept in this influential framework is “agency”, which Sen defines as “the realization of goals and values she has reasons to pursue, whether or not they are connected with her own well-being” [ 42 ]. Importantly, agency includes both the achievement of goals as well as the freedom to achieve them. This freedom of agency is constrained by a number of factors, including social, political and economic opportunities. In his definition, Sen distinguishes between well-being and agency, providing a space for the discussion of the pursuit of a goal that may in turn result in the sacrifice of health, friends, and in some cases, the individual's own life [ 10 ].

Drawing on the Marxist concept of ‘ false consciousness’ Sen introduces the concept of adaptive preferences as “a process in which persistently disadvantaged people accommodate themselves to that disadvantage” [ 53 ]. Although Sen acknowledges the impact of persistent disadvantage, he provides little analysis or guidance on how this could be operationalized. Roberts argues that methods from critical feminism and critical pedagogy are required to “understand the ideological processes and institutional mechanisms that structure subjectivities and material (dis)advantage” [ 53 ]. In his thorough discussion of Sen's later works, Roberts highlights the importance Sen places on individuals having “the freedom and ability to critically evaluate their own circumstances in order to be able to determine the lives that they have reason to value” [ 53 ], pointing to Sen's similar concepts of “adaptive preferences” [ 27 ], “objective illusions” [ 4 ], and “culturally influenced norms and values” [ 16 ]. Roberts builds on and extends Sen's term, providing his own definition of critical-agency 1 as “people's critical understanding of the (dis)advantage that they experience as well as their agency in acting together to overcome it” [ 53 ]. In these situations of (dis)advantage, Peters suggests that this “… does not imply impaired moral autonomy, absence of agency-capability, and thus absence of judgment. What may, in terms of manifest behavior, seem like submission may hide more subtle strategies of resistance” [ 36 ].

Within this research we aim to understand how migrant workers could use digital technology to support their critical-agency when in exploitative work situations, that is, to understand the vulnerability of the situation that they are in and if they decide, to act to change that situation. Our research builds on this prior work both in providing us a strong theoretical understanding of working with our systemically and involuntarily marginalized populations and in directly informing design decisions in designing for their low literacy while cognizant of socio-cultural barriers to interaction. It uses the overarching lens of the capability approach to ground the design in empowering the critical-agency of the marginalized populations.

4 Research Method

This research takes a Value Sensitive Design (VSD) approach to system design. VSD is “a theoretically grounded approach to the design of technology that accounts for human values in a principled and systematic manner throughout the design process” [ 17 ]. VSD stems from an understanding that all software contains the biases and assumptions of its designers and creators. Instead, VSD proactively integrates “…ethical reflection in the state of design of architectures, requirements, specifications, standards, protocols, incentive structures and institutional arrangements” [ 19 ]. We use VSD's three component framework, consisting of conceptual, empirical, and technical investigations that “are applied iteratively and integratively, with new investigations building on results from earlier ones”[30].

Prior to beginning this study, we obtained research approval from the UNU-CS HSRP Board (#201703-01). After approval was obtained, we began our research with a desk-based conceptual investigation, to identify the values and potential tensions that have been reported by academics, practitioners, and policy makers within victim identification in Thailand. Through this process we identified our context of use, and the key actors involved in the process that were reported in literature. From there, we undertook a series of five stakeholder consultations over an 11 month period, from April 2017 – March 2018 (with two to three months between each consultation). A high-level overview of these consultations can be found in Table 1 , which presents a summary of the number of participants as well as their background. The remainder of this section describes the purpose of and methods used in each of the consultations.

4.1 Consultation One

The first consultation consisted of a series of six focus groups across four cities in Thailand with a wide variety of participants, including: migrant workers, who had been exploited, rescued and were now recovering at an NGO; local and regional NGOs; inter-governmental organizations with mandates in migration and/or trafficking; and inspectors from Department of Special Investigations, Ministry of Justice ((MOJ)- who are responsible for investigating federal crimes such as human trafficking in Thailand). In the initial consultation series, recruitment of participants was undertaken on behalf of the researchers by United Nations Action for Cooperation against Trafficking in Persons (UN-ACT), Bangkok Office. Using Puri et al.’s [ 37 ] terminology, they acted as the mediating agency between the researchers and the local stakeholders. They had the local connections to be able to suggest a broad range of direct and indirect stakeholders to participate in the focus groups.

We arranged the focus groups with the social-hierarchical structures present in mind, to support stakeholders to participate as freely as possible. Within each of the groups, we asked about their current practices and problems in identifying victims of human trafficking; and their current access to and use of technology. We then shifted focus in the groups to brainstorm how the different stakeholders believed the technology that they had access to could be used to help them in their process of identifying victims. Thinyane [ 51 ] provides full details of these focus group sessions.

To summarize, these focus groups identified the initial screening phase of victim identification as critical to support them to help more migrant workers. The values of human welfare, privacy and autonomy were identified as critical to support the frontline responders’ work, with value tensions [ 30 ] arising between the different stakeholders in terms of accountability vs. privacy / trust. Stakeholders also identified key problems in victim identification in terms of communication, training, privacy, and trust. These four issues will be used in Section 5 to frame the discussion around the design implications for the system.

At the end of this consultation, we identified the potential for a mobile app that is downloaded onto the frontline responder's phone, that is ultimately a tool in the potential victims’ hands. The app would contain screening questionnaires for different sectors of work (fishing, processing and manufacturing, sexual exploitation, forced begging), with audio questions translated into the most common languages among migrant workers’ communities. Based on the responses to the questions, the app would advise the migrant worker and the frontline responder of the vulnerability of their situation and allow the worker to signal for help to exit the situation if they choose.

4.2 Consultations Two to Four

Our second, third, and fourth consultations iterated over the same process. We would develop a low-fidelity prototype, based on the feedback from the previous consultation, and then use it to stimulate discussion through a value dams and flows method [ 30 ]. This method removes design / policy options from consideration that are opposed by even a small number of stakeholders (dams), while at the same time prioritizing features, designs or policies that are approved of by the majority. The importance of value dams in this process is critical, as it ensures that the voice of minority groups is heard and valued. When dams and flow are identified and were at odds with one another, we explored different values-oriented design tradeoffs to reach some kind of decision. Across each decision, the human welfare, privacy and autonomy of potential victims were used as the deciding factors to inform these decisions. By using digital wireframes and mockups, we could quickly respond to feedback from the different stakeholders. As well as being used to determine the design of the app, we used value dams and flows to understand what questions to ask, what data to collect, and what data to share (and with whom), similarly to the process described in [ 11 ].

The difference between these three consultations was the audience: consultation two was only MOJ; consultation three was predominantly NGOs and exploited migrant workers, consultation four was predominantly state actors from MOJ, Ministry of Social Development and Human Security (MSDHS), Ministry of Labour (MOL), Royal Thai Navy (RTN), and Department of Fisheries (DOF). Each of these ministries and bodies have some role in victim identification, whether in rescue operations (MSDHS for local police, MOJ for federal police), labor inspectors (MOL for exploitation on land; RTN for exploitation at sea). By consultation four, we had developed a high fidelity prototype based on the feedback from the previous consultations, as well as some complex value scenarios to support discussions around the systemic effects of this potential new technology [ 31 ]. While many of the participants were not direct stakeholders, we believed their expertise from this broad cross-section of stakeholders was integral in the design of the system, given their combined knowledge of the anti-trafficking support and victim assistance frameworks that currently exist.

4.3 Consultation Five

The fifth consultation consisted of a series of observations and focus group discussions, with a subset of the NGOs who had shown interest in being involved in a pre-pilot session, throughout the previous four consultations. We observed NGOs as they used the app in some of their outreach activities, with a total of 22 initial screening observations across fishing, seafood processing, and sexual exploitation sectors. One author maintained extensive interview notes that were later verified by the other authors. We open coded these notes in multiple iterations to find emerging themes. We then merged these themes with those that emerged from the earlier consultations. We then conducted a thematic analysis of the notes with the combined set of themes.

The next section presents the design requirements surfaced across the five stakeholder consultations, according to the issues of communication, training, trust and privacy that were identified in the initial consultation. We highlight the unique requirements and design considerations that go into designing for these marginalized populations in explaining our design choices.

5 Design and Evaluation

We begin this section with an introduction to Apprise, to ground the discussions and provide an overview of the system functionality. Apprise was designed in multiple iterations with features surfacing and being discarded using the value dams and flows method of VSD. Various design constraints were discussed and the important ones that influenced the final design of the application are explained in this section. We finish this section with a description on the design and evaluation of the questionnaires that are presented to the potential victims by the FLRs.

5.1 System functionality

Apprise is a mobile application that supports communication between FLRs and workers. While it is installed on the FLRs phone, it is ultimately a tool in the potential victims’ hands. Once the FLR has logged in to the system, it shows the sector specific questionnaires that the FLR has access to. The FLR selects a list and hands the phone, as well as a set of headphones on to the worker. The worker puts on the headphones and selects the language that they would like to use ( Figure 2(a) ). At this point, an introductory video is played, describing: the purpose of the interview, illustrating how to use the interface ( Figure 2(b) ), and asking for consent to continue. If the worker agrees, the phone cycles through each question in the list ( Figure 2(c) ). The last question in each list asks if the worker would like help to leave their situation. At this point, the app calculates the vulnerability of the situation (more details of this will be described in Section 5.5) and, combined with their response to the question about leaving informs the worker with the script: “based on your responses, it seems that [you are in a vulnerable situation | you are in a difficult situation | there are no signs of exploitation] and you said you want to [stay | leave]”. After providing this information, it asks the worker to confirm if they want to stay (or leave). After storing this response, a prompt is played, asking the worker to hand the phone back to the FLR. At this point the vulnerability rating, and their expressed desire to leave their situation is displayed ( Figure 2(d) ) for the FLR, followed by a screen to store additional notes about the interview. At the end of the interview, the information is stored on the phone. The next time the FLR logs in and has network reception, the screening responses are uploaded to their account, to support post-hoc analysis of screenings.

5.2 Communication and literacy

Across each of the different consultations, participants highlighted the importance of supporting multilingual communication to allow FLRs to assess the vulnerability of their situation. As the vignette illustrated in the introduction to this paper, FLRs often come across workers who do not speak the same language as themselves or their translator. As well as having translation problems, participants described low literacy levels in the target population. We therefore designed Apprise to have an audio-based pre-screening questionnaire in multiple languages. These languages were decided during the later stakeholder consultations, based on the demographics that FLRs suggested they come across in their work. As explained in Section 3, we chose to use a combined audio-visual interface with minimal text to tackle this problem. Part of our initial consultation was to understand how migrant workers would most intuitively be able to signal their preferred language. Apprise is required to support translations into many languages, including those that are used in multiple countries, and those that are not the primary language of a country. We used brainstorming sessions with five trafficking survivors, as well as 23 NGO workers in the first consultation to inform our initial design decisions for Apprise. These designs were then refined over the subsequent consultations.

In our initial consultations, we asked participants how they would represent the different languages that workers speak. While some indirect stakeholders suggested that participants would be able to find their home country on a map, direct stakeholders noted that this would not be the case. They instead spoke of the pride that workers have in belonging to a clan or tribe and suggested that tribal flags be used (where available), and national flags where a country only has one official language. Figure 2(a) shows the final interface for language selection. When a flag is selected, the name of the language is played in that language, and a button is displayed at the bottom of the screen to continue.

In our evaluations, the language-selection screen was very well received by the users who, irrespective of literacy, were easily able to select their languages. There was an interesting dynamic with selecting the Burmese language, with many stakeholders in our design sessions arguing for the old Burma flag (as opposed to the Myanmar flag), saying Burmese migrants gravitated to that more than the Myanmar flag. In our field observations in the fifth consultation, we noted an almost unanimous disappointment. We learnt that despite the ethnic conflict represented by the flag change, the Burmese diaspora still identify with the Myanmar flag more than the Burma flag.

A similar process was undertaken to identify how to indicate ‘yes’, ‘no’, and ‘repeat’ as responses to the questions. As we were aware of the impact of designing culturally inappropriate icons (for example, see [ 29 ]) we used a combination of two approaches. Firstly, we presented a mock-up interface with a red ‘no’ option, green ‘yes’ button, and black ‘repeat’ button (similar to that in Figure 2(c) ). We asked participants what each button meant, and all were able to correctly understand what the yes and no options were. When we asked about the repeat button, one participant struggled to identify it. We provided paper and pencils and worked together to sketch new icons that made more sense. After ten minutes, all sketches that we came up with tended to look identical to the one initially shown. We went through a list of 100 ‘repeat’ icons found online to see if there were any that made more sense to the participant. After going through the list, the participant said that now they had seen others, the symbol made sense. Despite the agreement, we believed that this showed a possible place for misunderstanding to occur within the interface. We suggested that an introduction to the buttons in the interface be included in the initial introductory video and the migrant workers said that would be very helpful. The ‘I don't know’ response was added after consultation three, in response to concerns from indirect stakeholders that this option was not available. This button was initially designed to draw on the traffic-light metaphor by coloring it yellow and situating in between the other two buttons (see Figure 2(c) ). This final interface was evaluated in consultations four and five, and participants found it easy to understand.

Across the different consultations, participants were impressed with the way Apprise supported their communication. FLRs felt it would integrate into their regular workflows and processes easily and would provide the added benefit of being able to communicate with populations that they previously could not serve. We observed survivors of exploitation, as well as potential victims using the application, navigating through the different screens and answering questions without requiring support.

5.3 Trust

When asked about their current identification practices, participants across the first four consultations continually referred back to a lack of trust in the intentions of each of the different direct stakeholders. Participants noted that it was due to the mental and physical abuse that workers had often suffered, with one participant in the first consultation emphasizing that “ trauma is traumatizing” . At the same time participants mentioned a lack of trust in the motives of migrant workers, some stating that they were “ wasting our time ”, and “ just not pleased with their boss ” or “ just want to get off a boat ”. Participants mentioned cases of corruption that they had heard of or experienced, and a lack of trust in “ the system ”. Finally, a number of participants mentioned a lack of trust in translators. Some referred to cases where “ the translator is someone from the trafficker's side ” while others mentioned that translators may show racist or unsympathetic views towards other ethnic groups from their home country, which colors the accuracy of their translations.

Apprise was designed to ensure that once questions were translated and verified, the translator was not needed in this initial screen phase of the interview. In the fifth consultation, we piloted the app with NGOs that had worked in their respective sectors and with their target populations for a long time, and therefore are those trustworthy faces that potential victims would feel most comfortable with. The NGOs used Apprise to bridge any communication gap that exists with workers. After the initial screening sessions, NGOs asked workers if they preferred to speak face to face with them, or if they preferred using the app, and in all but one occasion (16 of 17, 94%), workers suggested they preferred the app. One worker explained this in more detail, saying “ I don't have to look you in the eyes when answering these private questions if I use the app ”. Some felt that if a person was asking those questions, they wouldn't want to answer as “ You've to think about the fact that we don't even know each other. So I'd rather answer on the phone ”. Some also said that they would be shy answering these questions to real people, and a phone was an easier medium to express themselves. As will be explained more in the next section, the interface was designed to hide any sensitive responses from onlookers and people in the vicinity.

5.4 Privacy and safety

Privacy during an interview with potential victims was a crucial factor that was brought up by the participants in each of the first four consultations. As was illustrated in the vignette, interviews are conducted in uncontrolled environments, where the worker is surrounded by other actors whose allegiances are unknown. Participants noted that workers often lie about the severity of their situation due to fear of retaliation. The participants also suggested that there was a possibility of the workers being trained to answer questions in a specific way so as not to implicate their employer. In order to encourage honest responses, ensuring privacy while answering the questionnaire is essential. To support the worker's privacy, Apprise checks for headphones when undertaking initial screening sessions. We have checks in place to alert the frontline responder to insert headphones before handing the phone over to the migrant worker. While enhancing audibility of the audio information, this also ensures that the questions are not overheard by anyone in the vicinity. While responses are visible, as button clicks, an onlooker cannot tell what question was asked. Further, Apprise does not display any text while in the hands of the worker. Some consultations did suggest having text transcripts of the questions. This however was discarded in a value dam process, as other participants felt the enhanced accessibility was not sufficient to warrant the consequent loss in privacy of the question-answer process.

In consultation five, an observation was made of a local NGO approaching a group of sex workers to test out the Apprise app. While she was speaking with the workers, an author observed a person, ostensibly their boss, approach the group, peer into the screen, linger for a while attempting to make sense of the interaction, and then watch from a distance until the interaction ended. While we are unable to state what happened after we completed the observation, this illustrated for us the effectiveness of the design to ensure that the user interactions with the application are private during the initial screening process.

A design option that was suggested in the first three consultations was to allow FLRs to take photos of workers after completing the interviews. Direct stakeholders suggested that this would assist them in following up with interviewees at a future time. This suggestion was discussed across consultations two to four, but many indirect stakeholders suggested that this put the users in danger of retaliation in the event that these pictures, and therefore identifiable information, somehow made its way into the wrong hands. Viewing this as a value dam, this feature was not integrated into the final version of Apprise.

5.5 Training

If we consider the continuum of exploitation presented in Figure 1 , it is apparent that the end points (decent work, forced labor) are easy to identify. The more subtle or well-hidden cases become more difficult to identify, being based on specific details of Labor Law and the changing patterns of exploiters. Without a standardized rubric, participants noted that the identification process often lead to cases being missed. As illustrated in the vignette, Sopon had access to his identification documents and there were no signs of physical abuse, so the labor inspector misdiagnosed the case. There are two components that work together to support the perceived lack of training in the field: questionnaires, and vulnerability rating.

Across each of the consultations, we have had two specific focuses: iterative evaluation and critique of the Apprise app itself (that we have been discussing so far in this section), and iterative evaluation and critique of the questionnaires. Four key sectors of exploitation were identified in the initial consultation, and specific lists of questions were developed for each: processing and manufacturing, at port / at sea, sexual exploitation, and forced begging. We added one generic list of eight questions that could be used in any time-critical scenario, with questions that were not aligned to any specific labor law, but were based on internationally agreed upon indicators of exploitation. Through the different consultations, we were able to combine the perspectives of legal experts, subject matter experts, and those at implementation level who have more hands-on experience of the practices of exploitation that are currently being used by traffickers. The iterative process aimed to capture the language, meaning and intention accurately, while being simple enough to be understood by a target population that may not have received a formal education. As an outcome of the consultation series, each question was classified against ILOs 11 indicators of forced labor and assigned a weight, category and subcategory [ 21 ]. We aimed to ensure that categorization was based on participants’ insights, which represent the current practices of exploitation and realities faced by workers, rather than a (comparatively) dry and slow-moving legal definition.

By using an algorithm to calculate the vulnerability of the respondent's situation, we aimed to make the identification process less subjective, as many respondents had requested in our initial consultation, and to overcome the perceived lack of understanding of the legal indicators of human trafficking. ILOs Hard to See Harder to Count methodology [ 22 ] was used and adapted to support the calculation of a vulnerability rating, to determine the vulnerability of the situation that the respondent was in based on their responses, classifying cases as: highly vulnerable, vulnerable, difficult, or no signs of vulnerability. The algorithm was validated by Thai human rights lawyers who ensured that the categorization offered by the algorithm mapped not only to international law (e.g. the original ILO methodology), but also to Thai labor law. These lawyers also verified the calculation by comparing the results generated by the algorithm to existing cases that they had represented.

Figure 2(d) shows the vulnerability rating, and the categories that were flagged in the workers’ responses. In consultation five, a number of the FLRs noted that they use this screen as a simple way to prioritize cases, and to understand how to direct their further investigation. For example, if presented with the ratings in Figure 2(d) , the FLR said they use the high rankings in ‘withholding wages’ and ‘physical / sexual violence’ as a prompt to request to see pay stubs from the employer, to check for visible signs of physical abuse, and to refer the worker for medical help / counselling. As we had mentioned in the design constraints, the stakeholders had surfaced the issue of a lack of standardized knowledge of what contributes to identifying somebody as a victim of trafficking. Our legal stakeholders were impressed with the algorithm used to calculate the vulnerability score saying “ The advantage of devices and technology is [the] accuracy [of the] result. When [results were] calculated, [they were] very good !”.

6 Discussion

We begin this discussion by returning to Sen's initial definition of agency as “the freedom and ability to critically evaluate their own circumstances in order to be able to determine the lives that they have reason to value” [ 53 ]. Across the different consultations we kept returning to these two ideas: supporting an understanding of the current situation, and providing a means to seek help. This section discusses these two issues separately, as well as the implications of (mis)use of collecting information.

6.1 Understanding their current situation

In our initial consultation, a number of participants mentioned that often victims don't realize the severity of the situation they are in, with one stating “ … [the victims] need someone to help them think logically, especially when traffickers are feeding them the opposite ”. She expanded on this further saying that a predefined and vetted list of simple questions could provide the logical reminder to the victim that may be missing in their situation. This insight maps to Sen's concept of adaptive preferences where persistently disadvantaged people accommodate their disadvantage. As mentioned in Section 3.3, Sen highlights the importance of individuals being able to critically evaluate their circumstances [ 52 ]. Applying this to labor exploitation, we believe that while the list of questions is important, the vulnerability rating was the most critical component to support workers to understand their situation. Apprise provides interviewees with two instances to request help: before and after being told of the severity of their situation. We believe that providing workers with this information, and providing them a safe, discreet option to seek help to leave their current situation enhances their critical agency.

There certainly are challenges that are created by the introduction of a vulnerability rating. Designing an algorithm to objectively assess the vulnerability of an individual's situation is complex given the wide variety of other factors influencing this vulnerability. Consequently, there is a likelihood of false positives (individuals who are not in vulnerable situations being identified as vulnerable), and false negatives (individuals who are in vulnerable situations identified as not being in vulnerable situations). Given the complexity of the problem of exploitation, and the range of indicators that can be used to accurately identify vulnerability (for example, the 200-question exhaustive interview conducted by Thai officials) coupled with the time-constrained nature of Apprise's usage, we designed Apprise to classify borderline cases as vulnerable cases requiring further interview.

From the initial consultation, both direct and indirect stakeholders have warned that as soon as this technology is released, exploiters will learn the “correct” responses and force workers to respond in that way. We have kept this in mind while developing Apprise, and have built in different techniques to circumvent users from “gaming the system”: we use a combination of positively and negatively worded questions; we have lists of “equivalents” (each of which can be positively or negatively worded themselves) that are randomly chosen at the beginning of each interview; and we support new questions being added by administrators and pushed to devices.

6.2 A means to seek help

In some situations, a workers’ responses may indicate that they are in a vulnerable situation, but they may also signal that they wish to stay. Linking back to Sen's concept of agency, this is a key example of a worker sacrificing their own wellbeing, in pursuit of another goal. We designed Apprise to highlight whether workers signaled a wish to stay or remain in their work environment, regardless of the vulnerability rating (see Figure 2(d) ). Direct stakeholders in consultations one to four noted several reasons why workers that have been identified as in a vulnerable situation, may choose to remain. These include: lack of employment opportunities in their home country, family pressure, and shame associated with identifying as exploited and returning home. They felt that continuing to work within their exploitative environment was better for them than exiting it. While our recommendations for best practice in the use of Apprise highlight the importance of respecting the workers decision, the different stakeholders have different legal responsibilities. In consultation five, NGOs mentioned that they would respect the workers decision, but may try to convince workers to leave by providing information on the protection, skills development, and legal support that they can provide. Labor inspectors and Thai Navy however have a duty of care and are legally bound to remove a worker from their work situation if it is a case human trafficking. We asked these state actors if they have ever come across a situation where someone asks to stay in a trafficking situation, and they said they had never heard of such a case. What they did suggest is that they find cases where a worker would like to leave their work environment, even when there are no signs of exploitation. In these cases, both the state and non-state stakeholders agreed that they would provide support for workers to change employers (to some degree or another). In some industries in Thailand, work permits tie a worker to an employer, but there are current discussions on changing this stipulation on work permits.

It is important at this point to distinguish Apprise from a helpline. In the initial consultation described in the introduction to this paper, one of the suggestions made by stakeholders was to develop a helpline. Superficially, this sounded like an interesting option. If successfully managed and advertised, a helpline would have far greater reach and accessibility than a group (however large) of FLRs. It could potentially be accessible from any location and at any time of the day. This in itself seems very alluring, and we considered taking this approach. However, when we considered the ethical implications of providing potential victims with information about the severity of their situation without being able to provide a means of exiting that situation , we decided that it did not support their agency (or wellbeing). Apprise instead targets a small subset of this vulnerable population, who are able to physically interact with persons in a position to help, but are unable to communicate and/or request for help.

6.3 (Mis)use of data generated by Apprise

In Section 6.1, we discussed the (mis)use of Apprise, and in this section we broaden that discussion to include the (mis)use of data generated by Apprise. After completing an interview, the participants responses, GPS location (where available), timestamp, language of interview, and FLR notes are stored on the mobile device, to be uploaded to the server on the next login. As mentioned in Section 5.4, in consultations two to four we had discussed the implications of allowing identifiable information (photos, names, phone numbers) to be associated with initial screening responses. On the one hand, this would support FLRs to follow up on cases, and to identify changes in work conditions for each worker. On the other hand, the added information would increase the risk of retaliation, should the information fall into the wrong hands. After much debate it was decided to not include any photos, names, or phone numbers, but to keep GPS locations as they could only say where an interview took place, rather than be used to identify interviewees.

Once uploaded to the server, registered users can access results of their own screenings. Each organization's administrator can set the sharing permissions for their organization, enforcing: sharing between all FLRs in the organization, sharing within each department in the organization, or sharing only to the administrator. This provides flexibility to suit the different requirements of the FLRs, but also maintains a level of transparency and accountability, at least to the organization's administrator. When viewing screening responses, only the FLR who interviewed participants is able to view the exact location of the interview, with a drop in GPS accuracy introduced for any other user within the organization.

One benefit of storing responses is that it has the potential to support post-hoc analysis, and the identification of sector-specific patterns of exploitation. This information could be used to inform protection and prevention efforts undertaken by stakeholders. An obvious potential drawback of centralizing and storing responses is the potential for misuse of data, and subsequent reprisals for workers. Practitioners [ 33 ] and inter-governmental organizations [ 47 ] have noted the deep-rooted connections between human trafficking and corruption, so from a critical-agency perspective we are interested in understanding how technology such as Apprise could be misused and further disadvantage workers. As such we are currently undertaking a comprehensive, critical evaluation on the potential misuse of the system.

7 Conclusion

In this paper, we have presented the co-design of Apprise, a tool to support the critical-agency of victims of human trafficking in Thailand, using a Value Sensitive Design approach. We described the design constraints and choices that were surfaced over the course of five consultations with more than 170 direct and indirect stakeholders involved in identifying victims of human trafficking in Thailand. Here, we detailed the constraints of communication, trust, privacy and safety, and training followed by an analysis of how Apprise addresses these constraints. We then discussed the role Apprise plays in empowering exploited migrant workers with knowledge of their current situation, and its implications on their critical-agency.
