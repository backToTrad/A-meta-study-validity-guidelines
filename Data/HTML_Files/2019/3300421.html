CHI '19: Proceedings of CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Glasgow, Scotland UK, May 2019

Research predicts that 196 million people will be diagnosed with Age-Related Macular Degeneration (AMD) by 2020. People who experience AMD and other vision loss face barriers that affect their Quality of Life (QoL). People experience only modest improvement from technologies (e.g., screen readers, CCTV), tools (e.g., magnifying glasses, tactile buttons), and human help (e.g., friends, blindness organizations). Further, there are issues to accessing these resources based on one's place of residence. To explore these challenges and determine design implications to support people who have experienced vision loss (PVL), we conducted a qualitative semi-structured interview study exploring QoL with 10 PVL. We uncovered themes of supporting creative work, recognizing the impact of one's living in a non-urban setting on QoL, and increasing efficiency at accomplishing tasks. We motivate the inclusion of PVL in the design process because they learned skills while sighted and are now low vision or blind.

1 Introduction

People who experience significant and uncorrectable vision loss face psychosocial and accessibility barriers that affect their quality of life (QoL) [ 29 ]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that 285 million people are visually impaired [ 39 ]. Because there is a higher prevalence of vision impairment as people age [ 40 ], overall prevalence will likely increase as the general population ages. According to the Census Bureau, 20.9% of the US population will be ≥65 years by 2050, compared to 13.1% in 2010 [ 6 ]. A recent article projected that 196 million people worldwide will be diagnosed with age-related macular degeneration (AMD) by 2020 [ 34 ]. While AMD, glaucoma, and similar conditions do not lead to total blindness, they can negatively impact one's mental health [ 4 , 20 , 24 , 30 ] and one's ability to complete daily life activities [ 14 , 20 , 31 , 32 ]. For example, patients with glaucoma reported lower on the emotional/well-being subscale [ 11 ] and on the mental and general health scales [ 27 ] than those with no visual impairment. Further, WHO listed increased rehabilitation for people with visual impairments as a primary focus for this decade [ 39 ]. Both the medical [ 14 ] and technical [ 31 , 32 , 36 ] literature suggest assistance for people with vision loss (PVL). However, high-quality assistance (e.g., high- and low-tech tools, transportation) may not be available to people with fewer financial resources or living in less-populated areas.

Even with vision loss services, there are gaps in QoL. Hinds et al. reported that after providing vision loss services (e.g., diagnosis, low vision aid training, in-home training), PVL concerns about completing daily life activities and loneliness remained unchanged [ 16 ]. With similar service offerings (i.e., assessment and provision of low vision aids along with in-home training), Lamoureux et al. measured significant – albeit modest – improvements on the Impact of Vision Impairment subscales for “reading and accessing information in daily life” and “emotional well-being” [ 22 ].

To explore the potential of mainstream technologies to enhance QoL for PVL we conducted a semi-structured interview study with 10 PVL. The participants experienced vision loss within the last 10 years. We focused participant recruitment on small metro areas since the intersection of recent vision loss for PVL outside large metro areas has been understudied in our field. Participants may have been familiar with their vision loss for ≥10 years, but they all experienced additional vision loss within the last 10 years that affected their QoL. We discussed the effects of vision loss on aspects of wellness [ 41 ] along with the solutions they employ (e.g., computing, tools, people, organizations). Further, we conducted a systematic online search of accessibility tools in Google Shopping to determine what tools are marketed for PVL.

We make two contributions: 1) identification of themes that emerge with PVL, including abandoning creative work, impact of one's place of residence on QoL, and being less efficient with tasks, and 2) design considerations to inform the design of technologies for PVL.

2 Methodology

We conducted two investigations. First, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 10 people who experienced vision loss within the last 10 years. Second, we conducted a systematic web search of assistive devices for PVL to determine what solutions exist. We describe our methods for those two investigations.

2.1 Interviews

We conducted semi-structured interviews with PVL to inquire about how their loss affected QoL and how they address accessibility barriers (whether technical or non-technical).

2.1.1 Participants. We recruited 10 participants who experienced recent vision loss. We recruited participants through a university email list and blindness-related organizations (ages 21-89, mean=65). Inclusion criteria included that participants had a change in their vision that affected QoL within the last 10 years, but they could have been aware of their vision loss for a longer time. Four participants reported that they were low vision or legally blind, while six mentioned their vision loss conditions (see Table 1 ). Seven participants live in small metropolitan counties of less than 250,000 in population [ 38 ].

2.1.2 Procedure. In the interviews, we inquired about how vision loss affected their QoL along seven dimensions of wellness: social, emotional, spiritual, environmental, occupational, intellectual, and physical [ 41 ]. While there are several validated low vision QoL scales (e.g., [ 8 , 21 , 23 , 33 ]), we instead selected open prompts to ensure diversity in participant responses. We asked about their solutions to accessibility barriers, including but not limited to computing devices, tools such as magnifying glasses, or human help. We asked about how vision loss affected their usage of high tech devices including computers and mobile devices. We provided two story prompts: when they were able to address an accessibility barrier and when they had to quit an activity altogether.

2.1.3 Interview Analysis. We audio recorded and transcribed the interviews. After all interviews were complete, we employed cross-case analysis [ 9 ], where two researchers independently read and found themes in the interviews. The researchers met to agree on the list of themes. We randomly selected three interviews and the two researchers used the new set of themes on those interviews. The two researchers reviewed their work and updated the themes. One researcher then independently coded the remaining interviews.

2.2 Online Search of Assistive Technologies

To understand the market of tools for PVL, we conducted a systematic search on Google Shopping. We selected Google Shopping because it indexes a wide range of stores and provides an overview of products marketed to PVL. We also chose Google Shopping (over Amazon, for example) because the product URLs remain consistent over time to ensure longevity of our findings. In addition, we could have multiple researchers curate and access products on a shared “Short List” simultaneously to ease product curation logistics. While PVL may be better served through assistive technology specialist shops, we were interested in the perspective of PVL with recent vision loss who, in particular in small metro areas, may not have easy access to such shops, and whose support network may not include people aware of such shops.

In the US state where nine of the participants lived, there is only one assistive technology shop, with very limited hours, in a location that is more than 100km away from most of our participants. We checked the list of assistive technologies listed on the shop's website and all their offerings appeared on the list we compiled from Google Shopping.

We compiled two lists of vocabulary terms that pertain to vision loss and technology ( Table 2 ). We paired every vision loss term with every technology term as a phrase and performed an online search with the paired phrases. Two researchers performed the online search and added the products to a single Wish List. This process took 21 days, and we compiled 2072 items. We extracted the items from the Wish List page by scraping the HTML and sorted the items by commonly used product keywords. Two researchers reviewed the classified products by grouping them by product type ( Table 3 ).

3.1 Strategies to Tackle Accessibility Barriers

3.1.1 Assistance from People or Groups. All participants asked for human help, such as getting rides (P1, P3-4, and P6-7), walking with partners (P1, P5, and P10), identifying people or items while outside (P1-4, P7-10), and reading documents (P1-2, P10). These activities are also found in literature on help seeking [ 2 , 19 , 28 ] and collaborative accessibility [ 3 ]. Participants also mentioned getting assistance from blindness support organizations (P1, P5, and P7), mainstream public transit/Uber (P3-5) or accessibility ride services (P5, P10), and their doctor (P1, P3-4) or teacher (P8). These external services provide connection to helpful tools and transportation. However, we will discuss the impact of one's place of residence on their ability to acquire these services in Section 3.3.

3.1.2 High and Low Tech Strategies. Participants used mainstream technologies including computers with magnification (P4, P6-P9) or screenreaders (P1, P6, P9), Jitterbug phone (P1, P5, P10), and smartphones (P3-4, P7-9), which is in line with research that people with disabilities want to use mainstream devices [ 28 ]. While participants also encountered challenges with accessibility hardware and software, but we refer to a comprehensive study by Szpiro et al. [ 31 ] exploring these challenges with people who have low vision.

Participants mentioned several of the technologies found during our systematic search ( Table 5 ) but did not mention 55% of the curated items. This may be for several reasons: we did not address all items during the interviews, the search results related to protecting one's vision (e.g., Blue light blocking glasses, smartphone screen protector), or that the products may not have as much utility. For example, a separate smartphone magnifier does not have utility because the smartphone already has built-in magnification. While the systematic search helped us with triangulating vision-loss specific items that participants use, this list is not prescriptive.

3.1.3 Mind Strategies. Participants also employed solutions that required no tool at all, which we called mind strategies, and have also been reported by Zhao et al. [ 35 , 37 ]. For instance, P9 smiles at people while walking outside to connect with people: “I just smile [at] nearby people when they come to me. If I smile, they can know that ‘oh he knows me’ … If I don't smile, then they think ‘oh this guy's suffering from some depression … he doesn't recognize me.” Both P4 and P6 used memorization, whether it was stair counting, people's shapes, and voice, or presentation slides. Participants planned activities; P8 showed up early to events to be acquainted and P10 planned rides.

3.1.4 Unexpected Strategies. Four of our participants used ‘unexpected’ strategies by using devices in unintended ways or not to their fullest capacity, which has been found in Kane et al. 2009 [ 19 ]. These solutions may arise due to inaccessibility. For example, P6 addressed an accessibility barrier introduced by software that could not magnify “I have a handheld video reader that I'll actually hold up to the screen to try to read the screen, so I can figure out what I'm supposed to do next.”

PVL had lived with sight for the majority of their lives, so transitioning to a new paradigm of interaction requires training. This may not be of interest to all PVL. For instance, P1 uses NVDA screen reader on their computer, but uses the mouse to trigger the speech output instead of using keyboard shortcuts, not fully utilizing NVDA. “[My husband] said I'm moving the cursor all the time because I have to be on [the text] for [NVDA] to read it to me. He encouraged me to use key strokes rather than using the mouse, but I am so used to using the mouse I am still using that… I just didn't feel like I was up to it.”

The unintended uses have an impact on other stakeholders (e.g., family). Before P3 transitioned to a smartphone with magnification, to read text messages, their family had to modify their composition of text messages: “It was getting so bad with texting I forced my children to put the text in large type, so I could see it, and I couldn't make them do that… It was time for a new phone.” P5 created a workflow in several stages to enjoy an activity of bird watching: “I've asked [my spouse] to please take pictures of the birds and I can get the copy on paper and use the magnifying glass and then I can see the birds.”

3.1.5 Design Implications. Participants are employing a diverse set of solutions to tackle accessibility barriers, which is in line with related research. However, several participants were employing unexpected solutions, which afford the opportunity for future design. There is an interesting challenge to design for people who learned how to interact with technology (and the world) while sighted but are now adapting to a new method of perceiving the world. As an example, while NVDA and other screen readers work with mouse interaction, there are open questions for how to make this interaction paradigm more efficient (e.g., using a bubble cursor to help people gravitate to points of interest [ 7 ]). Other research opportunities include interacting with PVL and indirect stakeholders to determine how to reduce the number of steps needed to address an accessibility barrier (e.g., bird watching).

3.2 Impact of Vision Loss on Creative Work

3.2.1 Results. While participants utilized strategies to continue with critical tasks (e.g., reading), participants abandoned their creative professions and hobbies. Abandoning these activities has an impact on QoL. P2 was a graphic artist, and they describe their transition to abandoning art creation. “As my vision went I made a lot of changes in my art, because I didn't have the acuity anymore. So, I went from fairly technical stuff to watercolor. And I changed the size of my art quite a bit because I could no longer work small. I tended to use much stronger colors much more … because I couldn't see subtlety anymore. I gave it up all together now.” We also consider this a ‘mind strategy’ (Section 3.1.3) because P2 employed approaches to continue with art creation while they had a high enough level of vision. P10 also had to give up her creative occupation: “I had to give up my long-term occupation of hairdresser … that was so hard to give up all my patrons.” Cimarolli et al. [ 5 ] identified similar challenges with other creative activities, such as arts and crafts, writing, and sewing.

P8 mentioned having to isolate themselves in an art classroom: “I usually just make my own still life and work by myself if I have to get too close to something … I don't want to hinder anyone else's experience.” P10 had to give up on painting because they “haven't been able to find a compatible partner to paint with.”

P1 and P5 discussed how vision loss affected their creative hobbies. P5 used to garden, but had to give up that activity: “I absolutely love to garden but I can't do that anymore… My eye doctor has warned me to not be in the sun. So, you put on dark glasses and you're out in the garden. And it's hard to function so I miss that a lot, but it is what it is.” P1 described how their vision loss affected their ability to enjoy scenery: “When we travel I can't see the things that we're trying to see you know. We were farmers, we always looked at the crops, and I don't see them very well anymore… I miss a lot of that stuff.”

3.2.2 Design Implications. There is an opportunity to develop technical solutions that support the creation and consumption of creative work. Recently, Bornschein et al. [ 1 ] created a computer and tactile-based drawing system for people who are visually impaired. Reichinger et al. [ 26 ] created tactile reliefs of artwork that included sensors that would play audio so people could feel and learn about the art. Rector et al. [ 25 ] created a proxemic system for people with visual impairments to consume artwork by delivering audio information based on their location. Given that participants gave up creative activities, there are opportunities to further this space, including bridging the gap between end users and accessible mediums. Example solutions include tactile graphics or 3D printing (e.g., [ 17 ]) so PVL have other methods of engaging in the creative process through new artifacts.

3.3 Impact of Place of Residence on QoL

3.3.1 Results. Participants mentioned that vision loss constrained their social life to people nearby (P2-3, P10). P3 mentioned “I'm much more isolated now and my family lives in … a town about 75 miles away… It's hard to see them since I'm unable to drive.” Transportation quality affected P10 as well: “My ability to connect with [others] has lessened because of the transportation quality.” While larger cities have extensive public transportation to airports and trains, sparsely populated towns make it difficult to transit within and between towns.

Accessible shared ride services exist in several locations but are not ideal for people who live in smaller towns. For example, P5 mentioned the unreliability of shared accessible rides: “It's a van and it comes out of the county and it's a really good program, but since we live in the country they come out here 2 days a week so you have to make your appointment on the days they would come to [town].” P5 also reported that the accessible shared ride services do not accommodate all time requests because the service stated, “that's a busy time with our regular customers.”

Even navigation within a small town can be challenging. P1 mentions the challenge of going to the local grocery store: “I can't easily get [to the grocery store] on a bus, and I could get there in an Uber, but that is an expense.” P5 wants to increase their fitness by walking outdoors but reports that “it's hard to get 10000 steps … we don't have a very big house and we don't have sidewalks in [my town] so even in nice weather, [my spouse has] got to walk with me… So, I guess maybe we should move.”

Living in a larger city does not guarantee access. P9 lives in a larger city (≥1million) but does not have access to employment or technical solutions: “In [my country] it is really hard to get a job […] because they believe we can't work […] because they don't know this type of disease.” When discussing low-tech solutions during the interview, P9 reported “Even a simple magnifier we can't get it in [my country].”

3.3.2 Design Implications. We learned new insights from participants mostly from small metropolitan areas (≤250,000 people). Our findings are in line with research; a systematic search by Jutai et al. determined that mobility is one the most crucial activities, particularly for PVL [ 18 ]. However, out of all our themes, we had the most difficulty determining design implications because ‘solving’ the problem involves policy and infrastructure. Researchers should continue utilizing mainstream devices (e.g., [ 13 ]), so the solutions can reach a wider audience regardless of location. There is an opportunity to explore mainstream technologies, such as voice assistants, to connect people with one another, or to emulate services that are otherwise difficult to do in smaller towns (e.g., grocery shopping).

3.4 Impact of Vision Loss on Efficiency

3.4.1 Results. While discussing aspects of QoL, P3-P7 mentioned – without solicitation – reduced efficiency with their work (P3, P6), reading (P4, P6-7), or extracurricular activities (P5). Lack of efficiency forced P3 to retire early: “My productivity was so low compared to my coworkers, and the workplace never complained … But it got so low that I was so very unhappy with it … So, I took sort of like a 6-month period where I went half time and just transitioned out. ” P6 commented that they could not keep up with their job anymore: “I worked in IT and … I was in a manager position. I spent probably half my day in meetings and the other half the days either talking with a person or reading emails and reading on a computer. I was able to do it, but at such a reduced rate that I really couldn't keep up.”

P4 and P6 discussed the impact of their vision loss on their reading speed, compatible with prior findings by Hazel et al. that text-reading speed is correlated with vision-related QoL [ 15 ]. Participants transitioned to audio books as it became difficult to use their vision to read. While there are negative implications on speed, P4 describes why they want to use their vision to read , “Audiobooks are fine… I kind of resist going to them even though my reading speed is probably half what it used to be … you don't have a voice that's interpreting or anything like that.” P6 described audio slows them down due to irrelevant online content: “I would say at best my reading is maybe half to one third of what a normal sighted person can do. Partly because I guess what I'm reading on the internet - the narrator will read through all the ads and it will read through stuff that I don't want to read through.” P7 described operating at an “80% level” with their reading and writing skills.

The impact of vision loss on efficiency can also affect one's hobbies or entertainment. For example, P5 mentioned that their vision loss affected gameplay with friends: “All I do on the tablet is play words with friends … I have to make words and my college friend of over 50 years skunks me but that's ok. She is better than I am and she can see. It is just too time consuming to pull up the letters, try 7 times, and see if this is a word or that is a word.”

3.4.2 Design Implications. While there are technologies to assist with one's professions, reading, and communication, there are opportunities to explore how to increase one's efficiency while performing tasks. One possibility is to determine how to help people with skimming documents. Some examples that explore this efficiency are AttachMate, a system that uses Natural Language Processing to derive summaries of attachments in the workplace [ 12 ], or research into hearing multiple audio streams where one focuses on the most interesting voice [ 10 ]. P4 suggested a novel-computing device to help PVL use the vision they do have for reading instead of moving to audio: “you can train somebody to keep their reading going by learning to scan across things instead of maybe staring at word after word you know directly.”

4 Conclusion

While we learned interesting insights from interviews with 10 PVL mostly living in less urban areas, we acknowledge that there are limitations. First, because we centered our interviews on loss, not all qualified participants may have participated. Our participants were self-selecting. Second, while Google shopping provides one lens on marketed tools and technologies for PVL, it may not be a representative list. Therefore, we labeled the shopping products that participants reported, and we mentioned several mainstream devices in our results.

Our semi-structured interview study with 10 PVL confirms related research about the impacts on QoL and the diverse set of solutions employed to address accessibility barriers. Our uncovered themes provide new implications for the design of technologies to support creative work, bridge the barriers that come with one's place of residence, and increase efficiency with tasks such as reading. Further, there are opportunities to explore technology design working specifically with PVL, where they developed schemas as a person who is sighted, but now must relearn activities as low vision or blind. We hope the outcomes of this work will support researchers in accessibility who explore technology design for PVL.
