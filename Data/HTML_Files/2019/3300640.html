Interface Characteristics, Shared and Hybrid Control

CHI '19: Proceedings of CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Glasgow, Scotland UK, May 2019

The inclusion of IoT in office lighting allows people to have personal lighting control at their workplace. To design lighting control interfaces that fit people's everyday living, we need a better understanding of how people experience lighting interaction in the real world. Still, lighting control is often explored in controlled settings. This work presents a qualitative field study concerning the user experience of two control interfaces for a state-of-the-art lighting system of 400+ luminaires in a real-life office. In ten weeks, 43 people interacted 3937 times. The findings illustrate the effects of using a smartphone for lighting control, how people experience lighting control in shared situations, and issues with automatic system behavior. We define design considerations for interface characteristics, shared control, and hybrid control. The work contributes to making the potential benefits of interactive office lighting a reality.

1 INTRODUCTION

The inclusion of the Internet of Things (IoT) in office lighting systems allows people to have personal control over the lighting at their workplace. As offices are transforming into open-plan office floors with flex-working policy, there is a growing need for personalization [ 51 , 53 ]. Providing people with control over the lighting in the office can help with fulfilling this need [ 35 , 50 ] and can increase work comfort [ 46 ], especially since individuals differ in their lighting preferences [ 19 , 23 , 34 ]. Other potential benefits of personal lighting control include an increased level of appraisal of the work environment [ 52 ] and job satisfaction [ 49 ], and decreased energy consumption [ 1 , 57 ], which can impact a variety of stakeholders [ 55 ]. The high granularity and variety of parameters that modern lighting systems offer calls for a new interaction paradigm [ 30 , 41 ], primarily because they enable a new range of services and applications for a variety of actors that can input to the system at the same time – e.g., automatic demand-response from a smart grid, or closed-loop daylight adaptation [ 42 ].

To realize the potential benefits of IoT lighting, it is necessary to develop wild theories [ 44 ] based on people's experiences in the real world. Design knowledge derived from the lab is often difficult to map on the “messy human-computer interactions in the real world” [ 44 : 2 ], and isolating lighting control from the situated context can therefore result in isolated knowledge without considering contextual influences. However, research in the field is often far from ideal, because of the complexity of the real-life context. Consider Figure 1 as described by the following scenario (based on results from this study):

Sarah is working in the office. It's getting cloudy outside, so she decides to change the lighting. She opens the app on her phone, increases the light level on her desk, dims the light on the empty desks around her, and continues working. A little later, her colleague Walter enters and sits down at the desk across from her, in dimmed lighting. Then the lighting changes suddenly. Surprised Sarah looks up, then looks around. This is not the first time this happened. Did Walter just do that? Or did the sensor fail to see her again? She starts waving at it. No effect. She doesn't want to have to take her phone again and, most of all, doesn't want to disturb Walter. Disappointed she gets back to work.

To better understand what is happening in Sarah's office, we can apply different lenses when reading the scenario [ 17 ]. We can take a cognitive perspective by discussing Sarah's motivations, needs, and benefits when being able to control the lighting. Studies on lighting perception, work comfort, and well-being often apply such a lens [ 3 , 7 , 33 ] and show the potential benefits of detailed lighting control. However, how to make these benefits a reality is a less explored area. In this work we aim to contribute to that area and therefore apply the following lenses in this work. At first, we focus on the interface, of which its characteristics influence the effort and availability of lighting control [ 41 , 56 ]. Secondly, we take a system perspective, its intelligence and autonomous behavior [ 20 , 33 ]. Finally, we approach interactive lighting from a social perspective: offices are hardly ever occupied by one person only and other people present are known to influence lighting interaction [ 38 , 56 ]. Combining these perspectives in a holistic approach is both a strength and challenge for design studies in the wild.

In this paper, we aim to formulate design considerations for interactive lighting. We do this by reviewing available literature about designing interactive systems (with a particular focus on lighting) by applying our three lenses. In an attempt to bridge the gap between knowledge generated from lab and real-life application, we conduct a field study. We design two lighting control interfaces and deploy these in a real-life office: a smartphone app for personal lighting control in the office, and tablets with dedicated control for meeting rooms. We evaluate the lighting control experience with 23 office workers in semi-structured interviews. The participants’ accounts provide rich empirical data about people's everyday experiences with lighting control: practical problems of using a smartphone for lighting control, issues with inappropriate autonomous system behavior, and uncertainties regarding lighting control in shared spaces. Based on the results from this field study, we discuss considerations for designing interactive office lighting according to the three perspectives, looking at the interface, the social context, and system behavior. This paper contributes design-relevant knowledge for interactive office lighting systems to support designers in making the benefits of interactive lighting systems a reality.

2 RELATED WORK

Investigating a technology as complex as interactive lighting in a real-life office demands a holistic approach. We therefore reviewed related literature concerning designing interactive systems by taking three perspectives as mentioned in the introduction. First, we apply an interface perspective, discussing the effects of designed interface characteristics on people's experience of lighting control. Second, we take a social perspective and address the role of interactive objects in systems that are shared in use. Third, we review the role of the autonomous system behavior in hybrid control situations.

2.1 Interface Characteristics

Interface characteristics are known to influence people's experience with lighting control significantly. Van de Werff et al. evaluate three distinctly different lighting interfaces in the same workspace and present a relational overview of interface characteristics and their effects on lighting interaction [ 56 ]. Similarly, Brumitt and Cadiz found significant differences in participants’ liking and ease of use of three Wizard-of-Oz lighting interfaces differing in interaction modality and other features [ 6 ]. Meerbeek et al. [ 32 ] studied the user experience of automated blinds and found that the perception of control rather than the objective control is most important for user comfort.

Literature suggests that control availability “ ໿ determines for a large part whether or not people will interact with their lighting” [ 41 ]. The accessibility of the controls can be influenced by the interface's location, its distribution, mobility, and modality [ 56 ]. These aspects influence the necessary effort to interact, as an easy-accessible interface generally requires little effort to make a lighting adjustment. The experienced effort of taking out a phone and locating and opening an app is comparable to walking up to wall-mounted controls [ 56 ].

Previous studies discuss the definition and detail of the control parameters. Lighting has many parameters, ranging from technical lamp or lighting parameters to humanized context parameters [ 39 :149]. Lucero et al. showed that a metaphor for a technical parameter appeals to emotional skills and could, therefore, reduce complexity [ 29 ]. Similarly, Offermans et al. [ 41 ] used a contextual parameter like ‘cozy’ rather than ‘color temperature’ or ‘Kelvin’. An elaborate sequence of interactions can increase effort [ 56 ], while little control detail likely results in less optimal light settings [ 41 ]. Moreover, every parameter should have a fitting detail of control, or ‘degrees of freedom’ [ 41 ].

2.2 Social Context & Accountability: Shared Control

True personal lighting control in the workplace is often impossible as the lighting grid and office layout commonly do not match [ 27 ]. In this way, an input from one person is likely to influence someone else, making lighting control shared [ 38 ]. Magielse et al. found that interfaces can mediate different social mechanisms and they can make people aware of, and accountable for their actions [ 31 ]. Van de Werff et al. found that an interface's distribution and ownership can influence the “sharedness of an interaction” [ 56 ]: people experience interacting with a shared interface as a collaborative effort and involve others in the process. Consequently, Niemantsverdriet et al. call this shared use and found relations between interface characteristics and coordination of shared use – verbal communication, consideration, and accountability [ 38 ]. Chraibi et al. showed that people can coordinate consensus lighting control in the real world [ 9 ], indicating that designing for shared use of office lighting is a feasible strategy.

Awareness, or ‘an understanding of what is going on’ [ 16 : 36 ], plays a significant role in collaboration activities as people need awareness to act socially appropriate [ 18 , 24 , 58 ]. Many have stressed the importance of sharing information to allow people to coordinate actions in everyday situations [ 11 , 15 , 37 , 38 , 43 , 48 ]. It is thus crucial for designers to consider the information needs of people acting in that context [ 36 ]. This can be done by enforcing identity [ 2 ], for example by increasing the visibility of actions [ 18 ]: an interface positioned centrally in the space makes an intention to change the lighting visible to others [ 56 ]. Similarly, interactions like gestures or pointing are more visible compared to pressing a button on a phone [ 28 , 56 ]. All in all, awareness results in people feeling accountable for their actions and being held accountable by others [ 18 , 38 ], reducing the risk of antisocial behavior [ 2 ].

Communication plays a significant role in coordination. The interface can encourage that, by being positioned and assembled to support sharing of information [ 43 ]. Similarly, making a control interface visible to both the actor and the people affected can help to initiate a conversation [ 56 ]. Without this, people often fall back to interacting and waiting for a reaction (if any), or they refrain from interacting at all [ 38 ]. Furthermore, the parameter definition and the interface's expressivity [ 24 ] can help people with constructing a lighting vocabulary [ 5 , 38 ].

2.3 System Behavior & User Control: Hybrid Control

Related lighting research includes a variety of studies that evaluate automated lighting control strategies [ 1 , 9 ] or manual control strategies [ 8 ] individually. Hybrid control (a mix of an autonomous system and user control) is more challenging and rarely evaluated. In practice, human input is often an issue in automatic control strategies [ 26 , 45 , 47 ].

Offermans et al. show that people accept system initiative in limited conditions, for example when actions are always the same or when manual control is difficult [ 41 ]. Dey et al . [ 12 ] present an applicable framework for supporting context-aware systems, but they seem to portrait the system as a “nonproblematic and faithful mediator of that context” [ 2 ], while conflicts between an intelligent system and its users are not unusual. A system needs awareness to act appropriately in the social context, similar to people. Systems need sufficient contextual information (e.g., from presence sensors, or users) before changing lighting conditions. Bellotti and Edwards call this intelligibility [ 2 ] and stress the importance of a system informing its users about its contextual understanding. Van Essen et al. approach this challenge by supporting a dialogue between system and user [ 20 ]. They applied this principle in a variety of lighting interfaces [ 40 ] and suggest that users should always be able to exercise control over the system – e.g., by vetoing system actions [ 2 , 20 ]. System-initiated actions should fit socially responsible behavior, and the system must be accountable for its actions, similar to its users [ 13 ]. Yet, computer-mediated control is disembodied [ 25 ]: the system allows different actors (human or not) to change its state without a guarantee that they can be detected by others. This makes socially responsible behavior and embodiment inseparably linked [ 14 ].

3 THE TEST-BED OFFICE

The test-bed office is a 500m 2 renovated factory floor on the fifth floor with high ceilings and an industrial look ( Figure 2 ). Originally, the lighting system comprised suspended tube-based fluorescent lights providing 350 lx at 2700 K on each desk and loosely aligned with the office layout.

The system was replaced with a state-of-the-art IPv6-controlled lighting system. The original layout was kept as the fixtures were replaced with state-of-the-art luminaires, providing approximately 500 lx at 4000 K on each desk. The luminaires were connected over ethernet, and each luminaire had a unique addressable IP address. A server managed the lighting system, authorized user controls, and logged all user interactions. Groups of two to four luminaires were automatically switched through presence-sensing with an on-state, and a background and off-state with delay times of 15 minutes. Luminaires close to windows applied closed-loop daylight adaptation [ 42 ]. All light switches in the office were disabled. After the introduction of custom lighting control solutions (as presented later), automatic system behavior was disabled when user control was exercised and would resume after defined delay times when the sensors detected an absence.

The office is occupied daily by employees of a municipal health service with a variety of work occupations (e.g., doctors, administrative workers, team managers). Daily occupancy varies from half full to completely full. People's most common activities in the building include computer- and reading tasks, phone calls, brief consultations between colleagues, and staff meetings. The office provides 24 individual offices ( Figure 2A ), ten meeting rooms ( Figure 2B ), and 120 desks and several open discussion cubicles in the open office ( Figure 2C ).

The open office applies a flex-desk policy. However, most people have a strong preference for a desk, and they tend to sit together at a fixed location when they are part of a team. All desks are equipped with a PC, computer screen, keyboard, and mouse. Employees rarely bring a laptop to the office. Generally, people work individually at their desk with an occasional chat with their neighbors. They sometimes consult others briefly while sitting at one desk together, but usually they go to one of the cubicles in the open office if they might disturb others. Before the new lighting system was installed, people used lighting switches distributed over the office floor to control groups of about 20 luminaires. People usually switched the lighting on when being the first to enter that area, or off when being the last to leave. This brought some occasional issues, for example when someone entered an unlit area while someone else was sitting in the dark. It was unclear whether that person left the lights out on purpose.

The individual offices are small, closed-off cubicles (or ‘cockpits’ as people call them) with a glass door. They have a relatively low ceiling and one or two recessed lighting fixtures. They are high in demand and apply a first-come-first-serve principle. People go there when they want to be isolated from the sometimes-noisy open office, for example to finish a report or for a phone call. Before the new system, the lighting for an entire block of cockpits was controlled with a switch in the open office, near the block. In some situations, people left and switched off the lights while someone was still working in one of the cockpits.

The office contains ten meeting rooms of different sizes, offering various facilities. The rooms are high in demand need to be booked in advance. Generally, they are fully booked throughout the day for meetings and presentations, especially on busy days. All but one room are situated at the sides of the office with windows providing natural lighting. The biggest meeting room, the ‘library’, is centered in the east-wing and therefore has no direct daylight. It is commonly used for receiving guests and giving formal presentations. Apart from the library, all meeting rooms lack see-through windows or doors, making it sometimes difficult to assess the occupancy. With the old lighting system, each room was equipped with a cord next to the entrance to switch the lighting on and off, which people usually did when entering or leaving the room. Also, people sometimes switched off the lighting when passing an unoccupied room.

4 METHODOLOGY

We foresaw different lighting control needs for the meeting rooms as they were generally used for group activities. We therefore designed two lighting control interfaces: a smartphone application for local lighting control anywhere in the office, and tablets with lighting control dedicated to the meeting rooms.

4.1 Smartphone App

The smartphone app allowed for manual lighting control in the open office, meeting rooms and cockpits. It was available in the regular smartphone app stores. Stickers with QR-codes were added on all desks in the office and were linked to the luminaires above that desk in a database in the server. After opening the app and scanning a QR-code ( Figure 3A ), the app shows a control screen with continuous sliders to adjust the intensity of the luminaire(s) above the desk between 0% and 100% of the light output, with 1% intervals ( Figure 3B ). The number of sliders shown in the control screen depended on the number of lamps present at the user's location. In case of multiple lamps (usually four above a group of desks in the open office), the first slider controls the intensity of all lamps at that location. The second slider controls the one closest to the user's desk. In case of a single lamp (in most cockpits) it shows only one slider. When opened for the first time, the app displays a tutorial explaining the interface, its features, and how to adjust the lighting. The server kept all applications in sync: After a lighting adjustment, the server informs all apps at that location to update their control screen and reflect the current lighting conditions. The server and apps communicated with the lighting system over the available corporate and public Wi-Fi to make lighting control available for as many people as possible.

4.2 Room Control Tablets

Tablets with dedicated lighting controls were deployed in the meeting rooms (see Figure 4 ). The exact positions of the tablets are displayed in Figure 2 . The control screen shows buttons to apply predefined scenes (e.g., ‘on’, ‘discussion’, ‘presentation’, ‘off’) that can be dimmed with a continuous slider. The scenes were created and labeled according to common activities in the meeting rooms. A QR-code at the bottom can be scanned with the smartphone app, allowing for more detailed control.

4.3 Study design

Figure 5 shows the study timeline. Before the interfaces were introduce, we allowed participants to accustom to the new lighting system and the default automatic dimming behavior without the control interfaces. During this period, we collected baseline information to cancel out any effects unrelated to lighting control. We deployed the interfaces five weeks later, making them available to everyone in the office. Participants received weekly data sampling surveys and participated in an evaluation interview in week 5 and 6. After the interviews, the system with lighting control interfaces ran for another four weeks, resulting in a total study duration of ten weeks. The interfaces were not changed during the study, apart from minor updates to alleviate connection and stability issues.

4.3.1 Participants. All the people working in the office were invited to participate in the interviews. A total of 23 participants completed the interviews (±10% of all people in the office, 28-67 years, 19 female and 7 male). They spent 1-5 days a week in the office, had a variety of job types (e.g., doctor, secretary, team manager, facility manager), and did not have a fixed desk as they worked on their day-to-day jobs.

4.3.2 Measurements. The first two authors of the work conducted the 30-minute baseline and evaluation interviews in the test-bed office. During the baseline interviews, we collected data about people's everyday experiences in the office (e.g., age, flexible/fixed workplace, lighting appraisal), people could vent their experiences with the new lighting system unrelated to the study topic, and we demonstrated the interfaces. Both semi-structured interviews were guided by the same questionnaire to quantitatively capture people's experiences with control. This questionnaire included items like ‘Adjusting the lighting is effortful’, and ‘I feel in control’ that were scored using 7-point Likert scales. Participants scored their lighting appraisal on a scale from 1 to 10, which we used to evaluate whether lighting control can influence people's overall appraisal of the lighting in the office. All interviews were audio-recorded.

A weekly data-sampling survey was sent to the participants. It allowed them to reflect promptly on their experiences with the lighting system and the interfaces. If a participant did not use the interfaces, they were asked why. The surveys were used exclusively to sensitize participants for the evaluation interview. The server logged all interactions with the interfaces. Unfortunately, we were unable to collect data during the first week of the study due to technical issues.

4.4 Data and Analysis

The findings presented in this work are based on the qualitative data from the interviews. The accompanying quantitative results are used only to support the qualitative findings by demonstrating that the interfaces were used enough throughout the study period.

The data preparation and analysis were performed by the first two authors of the work. A total of 17.73 hours of audio recordings were transcribed. The data was subject to an inductive thematic analysis [ 4 ]. The quotes were kept in their original language and only translated for presenting them in this paper. We selected ±10% of the transcriptions (ranging in age, part-time/full-time employment, flex or fixed desk behavior) to jointly develop an initial coding structure. Initial codes included lighting needs & motivation, lighting appraisal, contextual influence, feeling of control , and coordination . Rich quotes were selected from the remaining transcripts by the first two authors individually, and were collaboratively grouped according to the preliminary structure.

In a successive analysis, the 486 quotes were clustered into four themes. The first theme considers people's general ideas and experiences with lighting control: Cognitive Aspects (120 quotes). The three remaining themes correspond with the three perspectives derived from related literature: Lighting Control Interface (127 quotes), System Behavior (133 quotes), and Social Aspects (106 quotes). In the next section, we present the four themes with accompanying participant quotes.

5 FINDINGS

We begin this section with a general overview of people's experiences with lighting control in the office during the study period. This is followed by a description of how people experienced lighting control with smartphones and tablets, the automatic system behavior, and how lighting control in the open office was experienced as shared.

5.1 General Use

Figure 6 shows a heatmap of all lighting adjustments made during the study. The logged data shows that 43 smartphone apps were used to make 2712 lighting adjustments (1579 in the open office, 307 in meeting rooms, 826 in cockpits). This results in an average of about 13 lighting adjustments for every desk in the open office, and 32 in a cockpit. The tablets accounted for 1225 adjustments in the meeting rooms. This demonstrates substantial use of the interfaces, convincing us of the validity of the qualitative data. The lighting appraisal median from the baseline was 8.0 (ranging from 6-10), and 8.5 (ranging from 8-10) during user control. Wilcoxon signed-rank test: T = 0, z = -2.754, p = .002, r = -.62, indicating a large effect [ 10 ].

People expressed several reasons for making a lighting adjustment. Motivations relating to the results of an action included fitting the lighting to activities and natural lighting, and to save energy: “If I was sitting alone, especially then I don't have to turn all the lights up, because that costs more energy” (p3). Motivations relating to the control experience itself included exploring the interface, playing around, helping others when they struggled or if they had no access to control: “Last week we had a training day in the library and [lighting control] did not work for the meeting chair […] He looked at me like: ‘Would you please help me?’, and of course I did.” (p17), and to have fun with colleagues as an occasional office joke: “[My colleague] did it as a joke. She […] walked to the coffee machine. And from there, in the distance, she could still turn [my light] on and off.” (p12).

The possibility of lighting control allowed people to improve places with unpleasant lighting conditions (i.e., without natural lighting) by adjusting the artificial lighting to their needs: “[The library] is a very dark space: it has two glass doors on the sides, but there is no sunlight. It has a dark atmosphere, and that's why I like that you can make the lights a bit brighter when you have to meet there.” (p17). Even more interesting, people used the interfaces to recreate the lighting of popular meeting rooms in other, less popular, rooms: “It is quite handy, because sometimes [the library] is occupied, and now I can recreate it somewhere else. So, […] I can mimic the room anywhere by adjusting the light.” (p13).

Many participants expected to use the interfaces at least once a day, while they used it less often in practice. Their most prominent reason was that the lighting quality was already significantly better with the new lighting system, which made control less urgent: “[The lighting] has improved a lot already, so you never really think about the light in your daily work.” (p11). Overall, the possibility of lighting control was appreciated, especially at exceptional situations (e.g., with little natural lighting or a special occasion) and in locations with multiple luminaires: “I think the cockpits with two luminaires are more convenient, as you can set those two lights differently to create an atmosphere” (p26). Moreover, as some people did not like to use the tablets in the meeting rooms, the smartphone app was a nice alternative: “Well, with that [tablet], I couldn't really get it to work. So, I always used my iPhone.” (p17).

Amongst the many reasons for not using the interfaces, people most often just did not think about adjusting the light. Seeing a QR-code sticker on their desk reminded people of the possibility of lighting control with the app. At the same time, the stickers were easily hidden by clutter. On top of that, some people interpreted the absence of a QR-code as no lighting control is possible here: “I overlooked that I can use [the app] in the open office. […] I didn't see the QR-codes there, so I thought ‘ah well’. Maybe they were hidden?” (p24).

5.2 Lighting control with a smartphone app

All participants owned a smartphone, either personally or supplied by the employer. Overall, they were quite happy with using their smartphone for lighting control: “I am a smartphone user, and I don't use [the app] because I need to, but because I like it. Which means it's a great app. It's simple and straightforward, just like an app should be. It has only a few options, well, that makes it clear” (p4). It wasn't extraordinary for any of the participants to use a smartphone for lighting control. For some it was even the norm, as they controlled the lighting at home with an app as well: “I have Hue lamps at home. So of course, I'm used to it, I think it's very nice to [have an app in the office].” (p17). Nevertheless, as described next, deploying lighting control on people's smartphones influenced the availability of control and effort .

Some participants had difficulties with setting up the app correctly, and needed help: “[IT] installed the app on my phone and said it was not working well, something needed to be done. So, I did not look at [the app] anymore.” (p14). After the app was installed correctly, some people experienced Wi-Fi connection problems (e.g., due to wrong, or outdated login credentials), making it impossible to use the app: “Yes, I had that in the beginning when installing that app that was pretty difficult, because sometimes I had no Wi-Fi connection and no connection [to the lighting system], so I asked [someone] to help.” (p7). This was highly discouraging to use lighting control in the future. Smartphones were sometimes unavailable if people forgot to bring one, or when it was broken: “At a certain moment my phone didn't work for a week. And then you notice immediately that such an app is not usable.” (p15). With that in mind, people also envisioned how it would be if they had lighting controls ready at hand: “If it just there, then I might think ‘oh well, you know, I might as well [interact]’, I would be more aware of it. Now [the app] is obviously in the phone. Just now I had to search for it, where is it again?” (p1). This illustrates how the availability of lighting control was affected by the phone.

The effort to make a lighting adjustment was also a direct result of the smartphone as a lighting controller. Whether interacting was worth the effort was weighed with the size of the lighting need: “…I would use that app if my need were stronger. I do so with other apps that I find useful. This effort is balanced with the need to change [the lighting].” (p1). On the one hand, it was sometimes too much effort to take one's phone, locate the app, and open it, especially since people were used to pushing a button or pulling a cord: “I have to take my smartphone, enter my pin code, and I have to open the app. That is little effort, but ∼ gestures pulling a cord and pressing a button ∼ is faster. So, […] yes, it costs effort.” (p7). On the other hand, it was sometimes worth the effort to use the phone app, instead of the tablet, when people wanted more detailed control: “∼About the phone∼ Because you can easily set [the light] yourself. Especially in the library it's nice that you just [use your phone to control] the lamps above your desk.” (p17).

5.3 User control and autonomous system behavior

The lighting dimmed automatically with daylight and presence sensors. In general, people were charmed by the energy-saving approach to switch the lights off if nobody was there. This presented useful information to estimate whether a meeting room was occupied, as the lighting was observable from the hallway. However, people also experienced problems with automatic behavior. Many reported that the system started dimming automatically while people were still present: “I've experienced it in a conference room. We couldn't get [the lighting] to switch on anymore. No matter how much we waved. So, we went looking for a different room.” (p13). Interestingly, the facility manager mentioned to use the apps to correct the system when it was misbehaving or providing an incorrect lighting output: “And also in those cockpits with two [lamps], it happens sometimes that one lamp is on and the other lamp is not. You can correct that nicely with that app.” (p16). Some participants felt less in control with the new system as it seemed to live a life on its own: “It happens too often that [the lighting] is switching off while we didn't order it to so at all. So, fair is fair, we were more in control in the old situation.” (p13). This discouraged future use of the app and was particularly frustrating when a light setting was just made: “It's is a pity that [an adjustment] is not permanent. […] I adjust the lighting. And after 10 minutes they switch to full brightness again. So, yeah, why did I do this?” (p4).

If the lighting changed suddenly, it was difficult for people to estimate why this happened: “It strikes me that [the light] sometimes suddenly becomes very bright. […] Is that because someone is waving? […] We were working and moving quite a lot, so I do not know if it has to do with that. My phone was just [on my desk], we didn't use it.” (p22). Moreover, an unexpected change might have been initiated by a colleague but it was difficult to identify the actor, especially in the open office. This was not always a bad experience as it could trigger conversations as well: “When I played around with the lighting, everyone was looking up thinking ‘Who is playing with the light this time?!’. This led to some nice conversations.” (p11).

5.4 Shared lighting control in the open office

The difference in average number of lighting adjustments for the cockpits and desks in the open office demonstrates that people experience lighting control significantly different in an individual situation, compared to a shared situation. As the lighting grid did not completely line up with the office layout, it was unavoidable to influence colleagues. In general, nobody wanted to disturb others with their lighting adjustment: “I never considered it. I just thought ‘Gosh, as long as I won't bother anyone’. And anyone could install that app on their phone, so if someone else would like to adjust the light then [they could do so themselves].” (p15). In general, people considered their lighting needs less important than the needs of others. They often refrained from interacting if there was only a small possibility that someone else was disturbed by an adjustment. Despite these concerns, most participants had no problems with being affected by others themselves: “I never heard that it is was annoying and I don't experience it that way myself. I think I wouldn't mind if a colleague would adjust the lighting.” (p3).

People considered several aspects before making an adjustment, e.g., their relationship with others, what work they are doing, how they might be impacted: “No, I wouldn't [adjust the lighting] if [my setting] would be very different [from the current setting]. And if there's someone I don't know, I wasn't going to disturb them. That's like opening a window in the winter. You just ask permission, yes or no, a kind of consultation. Moreover, they were working very hard.” (p7). Also, being there for a short period only was enough reason to refrain from adjusting the lighting: “…then I won't touch the light, because I was sitting there for only one hour, and there were some people around me who I didn't know so well and then I think ‘Well, then just leave it...’.” (p17). People also reflected on whether they knew the other's lighting preferences and mentioned to weigh these with their planned adjustment. Finally, knowing whether control was available to others also informed their actions: “Some colleagues that I am sitting with more often know [I can adjust the lighting]. They also set the lighting for themselves, so then it is all fine too.” (p17).

Generally, people liked to make lighting adjustments in the open office when being alone, as only then they could make the light setting exactly as they wanted: “It is nice if you work alone. That is usually on Friday afternoon, then I dim those lamps around me slightly and make the lamp above me I a bit brighter.” (p17). When more people were around, it made coordination needed and people felt obliged to consider the others during, and thus potentially needed to adjust their preference: “And now you must always do it in consensus, not that it causes big problems, but you're looking for an average that everyone can work with, and I can imagine situations where the one person wants a lot more light than the other.” (p11). In this way, lighting adjustments were made together, with all people in that area, either implicitly (Through considerations) or explicitly (by involving others). Another strategy was to act and wait for a potential reaction from others: “You come in and you think it could be a bit brighter or less, then you adjust it and see whether the others react. But sometimes you come in first and then you just decide. Then you see whether others who come in later also find a pleasant brightness.” (p9).

The shared nature of control did not only have negative effects. In some cases, the occasional office gag allowed people to have fun: “[My colleague] walked past our desk, quickly scanned [the QR-code], and walked on to the coffee machine. From there, in the distance, she could still switch [our light] on and off. I saw it happening, but the rest looked around thinking ‘Huh? What's happening?’. That was a nice practical joke at work.” (p12). This was only possible because the actor was able to act secretly, without being seen by the affected people: a direct result of using a smartphone for lighting control. Participants also mentioned helping others when they were struggling with lighting control, or when they were in ‘bad lighting’: “Sometimes, the lights in cockpits don't seem to work properly, and people sit in really pathetic light. Then I can correct it for them.” (p16).

Lighting control in the meeting rooms was also considered shared. People would generally only make a lighting adjustment when they were the first to enter: “… the first one entering often looks at what lighting is needed. But usually it will work itself out in the group.” (p9), and they generally wouldn't when entering later: “I actually rarely use [the tablet], because when I enter a meeting room [the lighting] is already adjusted, and you are with multiple people, so I never really notice [the lighting].” (p22). Whether someone controlled the lighting, was also influence by the role that a person had in the group. In general, a meeting chair was allowed to set the lighting as they liked: “Yes when I come in as chairman I think ‘This is nice and bright’. […] It just depends on which role you have.” (p24).

6.1 Interface Characteristics

Looking from the interface perspective, we see that the designed interface characteristics influenced lighting use significantly through the availability of controls and the definition of control that the interfaces offered.

6.1.1 Availability of Control. The availability of control played a significant role in people's lighting control experience. This availability can be designed by considering the interface location , it's ownership , and modality . We do not say that a high availability is always better. In some cases, it is undesirable to make control available to everyone (e.g., during a presentation it might make sense to make lighting control available to the presenter only). Although people can coordinate who takes control quite well, it can be interesting to explicitly design more or less control depending on the context.

The tablets made lighting control available to anyone, depending on the tablet's location. Tablets next to the entrance made control readily available when entering or leaving a room, while tablets near the presenting space were available to the meeting chair. This example shows how designers can consider the interface location to allow for more or less availability of control.

When lighting is control is only possible with an app on personally owned devices, people become responsible for the interface themselves, which is inaccessible if the device is unavailable – e.g., because the battery is dead, people forget to bring it, or Wi-Fi is not working. Moreover, the app in someone's smartphone makes lighting control available to the owner of the phone only. Thus, the interface's ownership can affect the availability of control.

People experienced taking out their phone, locating and opening the app and waiting for a successful connection, as effortful. This is in line with earlier findings [ 41 , 56 ]. Interfaces that go beyond the modality of the touchscreen can be more engaging to use [ 56 ]. Although our study does not evaluate any tangible or gestural interfaces, participants contemplated how interfaces ready-at-hand could trigger lighting control more often. This illustrates how the modality can influence control availability.

The interface location, ownership and modality contribute to the interface visibility, which makes the possibility to control perceivable, allowing people to adjust the lighting as they please. When the interface is less visible, it can help to place visible cues in context. In our case, the QR-codes on the desks triggered people to consider lighting control. Alternative localization strategies like VLC might benefit the user experience, but make control less visible and thus less available.

6.1.2 Control Definition. Our study illustrates the importance of carefully considering the parameter definitions for the interface. For example, the contextual labels of the scenes in the control tablets were appreciated as they helped people to envision the planned lighting adjustment. We want to emphasize that the labels, as contextually-defined parameter, fitted the needs of people in our test-bed office. In this way, the definition of parameters should be explicitly considered with the needs of the intended user in mind.

Participants emphasized the importance of fitting the control resolution to the noticeability of a lighting adjustment: small lighting adjustments were sometimes hardly noticeable (e.g., because of outside lighting, nearby light sources, or long transition times), thus the 1% intervals in lighting intensity were often superfluous. The combination of disembodied control and low noticeability makes tightly coupled feedback needed to reassure users of a successful action [ 54 ].

6.2 Shared Control

In line with results from earlier lab studies [ 38 , 56 ], participants experienced controlling the lighting system as shared, making design for shared control a valid approach for interactive office lighting. Next, we discuss how this implies that designers should consider people's awareness of the social context and should support coordination.

6.2.1 Inform Awareness. Our study illustrates how people require social information to assess whether a lighting adjustment is appropriate. People expressed explicit information needs for others’ activities, mood and lighting preferences, their level of acceptance to a change, and how long they would stay at their desk. It can be worthwhile to collect information needs in the context that one is designing for, to include those types of information in the design of the interface.

Enforcing identity through visibility of actions plays a significant role in lighting control. To illustrate, when people need to walk up to the interface, it can be a threshold to act, not only because the effort but also because their action is visible to anyone around. In our case, actions with the smartphone were hardly visible, making it unclear where a sudden change came from. This makes it difficult to hold each other accountable for undesirable behavior, resulting in the occasional practical joke.

6.2.2 Support Coordination. Informing awareness helps people to support coordinating shared control. Our findings illustrate how the visibility of the interface affected whether people engaged in a conversation when wanting to make a lighting adjustment. For example, it was easy to involve colleagues after walking up to a tablet, as everyone knew that someone wanted to adjust the lighting. A lack of information can cause problems, e.g., with a sudden change, it was difficult for people to identify the actor, making it hard to address someone. It appears to be caused by a lack of visibility of actions and the individual ownership of the interface.

Also, we like to emphasize the effect of the expressivity of an interface on communication. People mentioned to use the percentage of light intensity and scene labels to communicate their preferences to co-workers. We did not capture any explicit role of the interface during coordination, potentially because of the low visibility and expressivity of the actions with our interfaces. Nevertheless, earlier studies showed how shared, visible interfaces can play a key role in discussing preferences [ 38 ].

6.3 Hybrid Control

Participants in our study experienced quite some problems with the automatic behavior of the lighting system, and it influenced people's experience with lighting control. This suggests that it becomes partially the responsibility of interaction designers to realize a proper implementation of autonomous system behavior. Inspired by related literature, we propose two considerations for hybrid control: when and how the system can take the initiative, and how to inform users about the system's contextual understanding.

6.3.1 System Initiative. Our implementation strategy for manual control and automatic system behavior was well thought out. Unfortunately, the reliability of the presence sensors proofed to be a problem in practice. This made the system behave inappropriately. Although we did not evaluate any strategies to improve appropriateness of autonomous lighting adjustments, we do want to propose some considerations to do so.

It can be valuable to approach the interactions between system and user as a dialogue, where the user and system can engage in conversation to change the lighting [ 40 ]. In this way, the system can engage in a dialogue by asking for additional information from its users when it is uncertain whether an action is appropriate. For instance, in case of unreliable presence sensor data, the system can ask the person that made the last light adjustment to check whether people are still present.

Another noteworthy course could be to directly couple the probability of appropriate action to lighting parameters. For example, when a system is uncertain about switching off the lights it can use a longer transition time to slowly dim the lights, allowing people to intervene. The study clearly showed that people need such an override (e.g., the facility manager regularly ‘corrected’ the system when it was behaving incorrectly) and ‘undo’ functionality. These should be almost effortless, especially when someone wants to retrieve their carefully created light setting after inappropriate system behavior.

6.3.2 System Accountability. When the lighting system is an autonomous actor, it is essential to allow people to hold the system accountable for its actions, similar to all other users. This was difficult for participants in our study, as they had no idea why the system just changed the lighting. To address this problem, the system can inform people about its identity , contextual understanding and motivation for an action, especially in shared settings where a change can be initiated by an office worker, automatic energy saving, time-scheduled downtime, or the facility manager. Designers can also consider the priority of these actors in different situations. Arguably, the system sometimes has priority over office workers, e.g., during a demand response when energy needs to be conserved. The system informing its users about the prioritization can potentially increase people's acceptance to system behavior, as it helps them to understand the system's actions.

7 DISCUSSION

This paper presents considerations for interface characteristics, shared control, and hybrid control when designing interactive office lighting. These considerations originate from related literature and were further defined through a field study in which we evaluated control over an interactive lighting system in a real-life office. We do not wish to argue that our considerations are the only considerations for interactive lighting, nor that they are only applicable to interactive lighting in the office. Additionally, we foresee interesting cross-links between the three perspectives that can lead to additional considerations. Our main point is that designing interactive lighting can and should be approached from multiple perspectives as it is a multi-faceted application area.

It was a unique opportunity to conduct a field study of this size with a state-of-the-art lighting system in a real-life office. As the research was part of a multi-stakeholder project it was not up to the researchers to design the entire lighting system. Different parts of the system were designed by different stakeholders with goals apart from the research goals (e.g., luminaire placement and the automatic dimming to decrease energy consumption), making it not an ideal research context. However, it would not have been possible to conduct a field study of this size without the effort and expertise of the other stakeholders. Moreover, office lighting systems in the real world are almost always deployed by multiple stakeholders with a variety of goals for the lighting system. Arguably, the role of interaction designers in similar projects can no longer be focusing on interface design only and should go beyond a user-centered approach, inspiring a stakeholder-centered design approach. [ 21 , 22 ].

Although the real-life setting might not have been ideal, it allowed us to evaluate the user experience specific to real-life situations that is difficult to capture in a lab environment. Many choices for the system implementation prioritized feasibility over the best possible user experience. For that same reason and because lighting control was new for almost all people in the test-bed office, we chose to design a basic user interface. This made it robust and understandable. We believe this benefitted the study, and did not affect the results negatively. The study results suggest that the design of novel lighting control interfaces is an interesting direction, and our design considerations can support that work.

8 CONCLUSIONS

The goal of this work was formulating design-relevant knowledge for designing interactive lighting systems in the field. Based on our experiences with interactive lighting studies, we advocate a holistic approach and introduce three lenses that can be used to investigate lighting control in the field: looking at the interface, the lighting system, and the social aspects. We reviewed related literature through these lenses, looking for findings from lab and simulated field studies that we can recognize in the field. We designed and deployed two lighting interfaces in a real-life office and evaluated them with office workers. The results disclose consequences of using a smartphone for lighting control, how people experience lighting control in a shared context, and issues with system behavior.

We generalized the study findings into three considerations for designing interactive office lighting: First, we stimulate designers to carefully consider how interface characteristics can affect the lighting control experience through the availability of controls and the definition of control that the interfaces offer. Second, we challenge designers to develop autonomous systems that act appropriate to the social context, by carefully considering when system initiative is appropriate, and by informing people about the system's actions. Third, we want to stimulate designers to create interfaces that support shared control. As lighting interaction is often shared in nature it is essential to inform people's awareness, and support coordination. By considering these aspects, interaction designers can contribute to making the potential benefits of interactive office lighting a reality.
