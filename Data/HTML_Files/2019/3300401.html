HaptiVec: Presenting Haptic Feedback Vectors in Handheld Controllers using Embedded Tactile Pin Arrays

Daniel Kuan Yu Chen , Shanghai Jiao Tong University, China, leinadchen.dc@gmail.com
Jean-Baptiste Chossat , Shanghai Jiao Tong University, China, jbchossat@sjtu.edu.cn
Peter Shull , Shanghai Jiao Tong University, China, pshull@sjtu.edu.cn

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300401
CHI '19: Proceedings of CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Glasgow, Scotland UK, May 2019

HaptiVec is a new haptic feedback paradigm for handheld controllers which allows users to feel directional haptic pressure vectors on their fingers and hands while interacting with virtual environments. We embed a 3 by 5 tactile pin array (with an average pin spacing of 25 mm) into the handles of two custom VR type controllers. By presenting directional pressure vectors in eight cardinal directions (N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW) to users without prior training, they were able to distinguish the correct direction with an accuracy of at least 79%. We illustrate two applications where our device enhances virtual experiences over traditional vibrotactile feedback. In the first application, through the classic first-person shooter Doom, we demonstrate that users can receive directional pressure feedback corresponding to the direction of incident enemy projectiles. In the second application, we demonstrate how our controller can create a more immersive experience by allowing the user to feel their virtual climate by randomizing the directional vectors and presenting the user with ”haptic rain” which adapts with the intensity of the rainfall.

1 INTRODUCTION

Humans have a complex somatosensory system which allows for rich sensing and interaction with the physical world. Among the vast array of sensors which exists embedded in the human skin for sensing different stimuli such as temperature and pain, mechanoreceptors are responsible for detecting stimuli such as pressure, skin stretch, and vibrations [ 22 , 47 ]. Haptics is the science of experiencing and creating touch sensations [ 30 ], and has been an area of significant research spanning multiple disciplines from neuroscience to robotics. The ability to convey realistic three-dimensional haptic sensations using handheld devices has been a two-decade long pursuit. One field which drove much of the innovation has been the field of medical simulators for training of hands-on medical procedures and has proven to be highly effective in developing minimally invasive surgery skills [ 7 , 54 ]. Unfortunately, much of the advancements in haptic feedback in commercial systems have been limited to grounded devices like the Touch by 3D Systems (formerly known as the Phantom Omni from SensAble Technologies) [ 1 ], and thus limiting the applications in which feedback can be applied.

In recent years, with the explosion of commercial virtual reality systems like the Oculus and HTC Vive, and augmented reality systems like the HoloLens; the field of haptics has experienced a renaissance in which researchers are seeking to develop ungrounded haptic devices which can recreate realistic haptic feedback in a mobile context. Ungrounded haptic devices have several advantages over their more mature grounded counterparts, this includes being more mobile and having the potential to operate over larger workspaces [ 10 ]. Various commercial haptic devices have recently surfaced for VR interactions from haptic gloves like the Plexus to larger body grounded exoskeleton gloves like the HaptX; but handheld controllers have remained as the dominant interface due to the convenience of not needing to strap on additional accessories.

In the realm of handheld controller technology, very little advancements have been made, with most recent commercial handheld controllers (e.g. Sony Playstation VR, Oculus Rift, and HTC Vive) still relying on the presentation of basic vibrotactile stimuli.

In this paper, we develop a new type of controller, HaptiVec , which extends the presentation of haptic sensations in handheld controllers by embedding a 3 x 5 tactile pin array in the handle of two custom VR type controllers allowing the user to feel pressure sensations on their fingers and palms giving the ability to convey directional vectors in virtual environments. We first give a brief overview of the related work in this area, introduce the HaptiVec prototype, demonstrate its effectiveness in presenting directional haptic pressures in the eight cardinal directions with a user study, and then show two applications which create a more immersive virtual experience.

2 CONTRIBUTIONS

The contributions to the field of HCI from our work are as follows:

  1. HaptiVec , the first VR type controller with tactile pin arrays embedded for giving the user local pressure sensations on the fingers and hands.
  2. A user study illustrating that users can accurately identify directional pressure vectors in the eight cardinal directions (N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW).
  3. Demonstration of the directional haptic vectors in the classic first-person shooter Doom, where users can feel the direction of the incident enemy projectiles. This illustrates how users to obtain directional information via tactile sensory channels while operating a VR controller, opening up future applications of motion guidance and navigation in virtual environments.
  4. Demonstration of users feeling their virtual climate through the presentation of ”haptic rain”, and examples of how the frequency of actuation can adapted with the intensity of the sound rain. This illustrates how users can receive haptic information regarding their virtual environment, enhancing immersion in applications like gaming especially for those who are visually impaired.

3 RELATED WORK

This section reviews the main motivations and issues in the field of haptics with an emphasis on mobile, ungrounded, and handheld haptic devices, we then provide an overview of previous developments that have been made by other researchers in the field of HCI.

Haptic Feedback Modalities

Haptic feedback devices can be largely classified into two main categories, kinesthetic and cutaneous devices. Kinesthetic devices seek to give realistic feedback of three dimensional forces, however, as described by Newton's third law, a mechanical grounding is required for the device to transmit it's reaction force. Devices based on the gyroscopic effect [ 60 , 63 ] and asymmetric torques [ 2 ] have also been developed allowing users to feel ungrounded torques; these devices, despite being ungrounded, only give rotational sensations and also require heavy and bulky flywheels rendering them inappropriate for portable handheld haptic applications. Consequently, the majority of ungrounded devices only seek to present the cutaneous sensations of pressure, skin stretch and vibration which will be discussed here.

From the inception of mobile haptic devices, vibration feedback has been the standard due to its simplicity, small form factor, and low-cost actuators. The majority of vibration haptic devices target the Pacinian corpuscle mechanoreceptor which has a peak sensitivity of around 250 Hz [ 59 ]. Conveniently, the fingers show the highest sensitivity towards high frequency vibrotactile sensations which only require an indentation of 0.07 μ m at 200 Hz [ 62 ]. This could be, in part, one of the contributing reasons vibrotactile feedback has been so pervasive in handheld haptic devices.

Researchers have explored the use of simple binary on and off vibratory cues for different applications such as motion guidance [ 38 ] and navigation [ 51 ]. Vibratory feedback using arrays of vibration actuators taking advantage of spatio-temporal illusions and patterns have been introduced in wearable applications. Vibrotactile arrays have been integrated into wristbands [ 11 ], belts [ 57 , 58 ], and an ankle bracelet [ 13 ]. One major limitation of vibrotactile feedback is that it is not inherently directional, therefore conveying directional haptic information to the user requires the user to learn cues.

In recent years, researchers have experimented with asymmetric vibrations to give the sensation of a directional pulling stimulus using a single actuator. Culbertson et al. modeled asymmetric vibrations on the fingers and suggested that the perception of the directional pulling sensation was the result in the skin displacing in one direction more than the other at specific frequencies [ 20 ]. Various devices utilizing asymmetric vibrations for directional haptic feedback have been implemented resulting in high directional recognition accuracy rates [ 3 , 4 , 5 , 17 , 21 ]. Despite its ability to provide a directional skin stretch perception, the perception is extremely sensitive to actuator placement [ 21 ]. It has also been noted that continuous vibratory stimuli can be irritating [ 37 ] and also lead to desensitization [ 9 ].

Skin stretch, another fundamental cutaneous sensation the human skin is sensitive to has also been explored as a form of haptic feedback. Lateral skin stretch stimuli are detected mainly by the slow-adapting, type II mechanoreceptors [ 40 ]. Feedback using skin stretch has been attractive in haptic applications due to the fact that it is inherently directionally sensitive and thus more intuitive than learning vibrotactile cues [ 12 , 26 , 32 , 35 ]. Chinello et al. developed a wrist-worn skin stretch device as assessed its performance in motion guidance [ 15 ]. Guinan et al. embedded skin stretch actuators for the thumbs in a gaming controller for obtaining directional stretch cues [ 27 ]. Finger-tip skin stretch devices have also been thoroughly explored as a form of feedback modality [ 43 , 56 ]. Recently, a reactive grip motion controller by Tactical Haptics utilizing lateral skin stretch has surfaced as one of the first commercial devices to implement a method of feedback other than vibration.

As with skin stretch devices, a popular method of providing the user with pressure sensations is through the use of a moving platform against the finger pad using some kind of finger attachment [ 14 , 24 , 46 ]. The use of pin-arrays have been another avenue of presenting pressure sensations [ 31 , 52 ]. The perception of pressure is registered primarily by slow-adapting, type-I mechanoreceptors [ 25 ]. Tactile spatial acuity has been suggested to be influenced by the innervation density and the receptive field size of mechanoreceptors responsible for detecting the stimuli [ 44 ]. Therefore, although the human hand is very sensitive to vibratory stimuli, the large receptive field size and lower innervation density of Pacinian corpuscles [ 35 ] only allow the detection of stimuli down to spatial resolutions of 6 mm at the finger tips [ 50 ]. Comparatively, the two point discrimination threshold for a constant pressure stimuli at the fingertip is about 1 mm [ 55 ]. One major advantage pressure displays have over their vibrotactile counterparts is that their mechanical stimuli are typically constrained locally to within the bounds of the contact area whereas vibration waves propagate through the body resulting in possible stimulation of unintended locale [ 19 ]. The majority of tactile pin-arrays stimulate the finger pad by utilizing an array of electro-mechanically actuated pins. Kim et al. implemented pin-arrays into a glove, providing feedback to the fingers [ 39 ]. In the field of HCI, tactile pin arrays have been thoroughly explored. Examples include, inFORM [ 23 ], a large table-top tactile display which actuated pins for rendering shape and objects for a variety of interaction modes and Lumen [ 49 ], a smaller array of lower density with built in illuminated rods for UI interactions.

Handheld Controllers with Tactile Pin Arrays

Despite the diverse implementations of haptic modalities and devices, very few have been integrated with handheld controllers suitable for the consumer market. Choi et al. [ 18 ] developed the CLAW, a gun-like VR controller which allowed the rendering of grasping and touching interactions. Whitmire et al.’s [ 61 ] Haptic Revolver used a spinning wheel to render different haptic sensations on the finger. Han et al. [ 28 ] developed a gaming controller with custom attachable modules for different haptic interactions. Handhelds which altered the surface like GraspForm [ 29 ], Animotus and the Haptic Taco [ 53 ], have also been explored in shape rendering and navigation tasks respectively. Recently, researchers have explored integrating tactile pin arrays into handhelds to allow for haptic interactions in a wearable and mobile context. Benko et al.’s [ 8 ] TextureTouch embedded a 4 x 4 array into a VR type controller for rendering textures for the finger pad. Similarly, Ubi-Pen [ 41 ] is a pen stylus with a 3 x 3 pin array built it for texture rendering. The most similar to our HaptiVec controller is Jang et al.’s [ 34 ] Haptic Edge Display, where they embedded pin arrays to the right edge of a custom mobile phone case for different UI and haptic interactions.

HaptiVec introduces the concept of embedding tactile pin arrays into the handle of a VR type controller for rendering pressure vectors. Pressure sensations, although being also inherently non-directional like that of vibration. By embedding tactile pins inside a VR controller, not only will users feel pressure sensations locally on different areas of their hands and fingers but the pressure stimuli will be perceived to project out into the cardinal directions due to the users natural gripping form around the controller allowing for intuitive pressure vectors to be felt. We demonstrate applications where our device enhances haptic feedback in gaming and virtual environments.

4 HAPTVEC: DESIGN OF THE PROTOTYPE

The main body of the controller was 3D printed, having a cylindrical shape with a diameter of 55 mm and a height of 100 mm. Solenoid mounts and holes were created in the surface of the controller spanning 180° and arranged in 3 by 5 arrangement. The tactile pins have an average separation of 25 mm, although a person's hands and fingers have a much higher tactile acuity, there inherently exists a trade-off between having a high-density tactile pin array and also being able to embed the array into the handle of a VR controller due to size constraints. The proposed design was deemed by the authors to be the balance between having enough resolution to introduce haptic pressure vectors for different applications whilst still maintaining a form factor similar to that of current VR controllers. Each column was staggered in height with its adjacent neighboring column so that the solenoids mesh and fit more compactly inside the controller. The tactile pin arrays were made up of 15 solenoids in each controller. The solenoids (DC0410, Yong Ci Neng Co.) with dimensions $9.9\space x\space 9.9\space x\space 25.1\,\mathrm{mm}$ and a small cylindrical pin contact (1.5 mm in diameter), had a stroke length of 3.5 mm when actuated. Each solenoid drew approximately 0.57 A of current when supplied with 6 V DC. The solenoid produced a blocking force of 0.32 N as measured using an ATI Nano17 (SI-25-0.25) force transducer. Each pin array column was arranged 45° from their adjacent neighboring column, facing in one of cardinal directions (figure 1 ). An analog 2-axis thumb joystick was attached to the top of each controller for allowing user control during gaming applications. An Arduino micro was used to read the state of the buttons and joystick to mimic keyboard commands [ 42 ]. The tactile pin arrays from each controller was controlled by a power MOSFET switching matrix, where pins can be actuated by sending a logic HIGH on the corresponding row and column MOSFET gates. A computer running LabVIEW with a National Instruments USB-6343 data acquisition card was used to output the control signals to the solenoid driver. Figure 2 below shows the complete system block diagram.

The controllers were designed to resemble that of VR controllers but with tactile pin arrays embedded inside. During normal operation, the controllers were held in such a way so that the columns of each pin array on the left controller was facing in the cardinal directions: N, NE, E, SE, and S; and the right controller was facing N, NW, W, SW, and S, all with respect to the user's frame of reference (figure 3 ).

5 USER STUDY: RECOGNITION OF THE EIGHT CARDINAL DIRECTIONS

Using the HaptiVec controllers we developed, we sought to determine how accurately participants could identify the directions of the pressure vectors when each column of the pin arrays was actuated. Our hypothesis (stemming from the high tactile acuity of the hands) was that participants could identify the directional pressure vectors with an accuracy significantly greater than random chance of 20%. Ten healthy participants were recruited for this study (7 males and 3 females, 23-37 years of age). Only 2 participants had previous experience with our HaptiVec controller.

Experimental Set-Up

Participants were seated at a table at about chest height and given the two HaptiVec controllers (one for each hand). Participants were instructed to hold the controllers so that the tactile pins (which were visible to them initially), corresponded to the cardinal directions (figure 4 ). A diagram was placed in front of the participant for the whole duration of the experiment showing the different directions that they would need to identify (figure 5 ).

A C# program acting both as a TCP client and as a graphical user interface was developed. This program sent commands to the LabVIEW TCP server which in turn switched the columns of the tactile pin arrays in that particular direction on or off.

Procedure

In order for us to determine what the accuracy is for a single directional pressure vector, we separated the two North and South stimuli from both controllers as individual stimuli resulting in 10 different stimuli. The different stimuli that were presented in this study were: NL (North for left controller), NW, W, SW, SL (South for left controller), SR (South for right controller), SE, E, NE, NR (North for right controller). The participants were not given specific directions as to how to hold the controllers apart from that each of the 5 columns of the pins needed to be able to come in contact with their hands when actuated. The participants were given approximately 1 - 2 minutes to adjust their grip accordingly, while simultaneously experiencing what each of the vectors felt like with each column of the pin array randomly actuated. After the short trial session, participants placed their hands under the table so that the controller was completely out of sight. Participants also wore headphones which played white noise to block out any noises that would be made by the pins. A pseudo-random list of the 10 different stimuli was created for each participant, with 10 trials for each stimulus (100 stimuli presented in total). For each trial, a 1 second stimulus was given where all the pins from that particular column was actuated. The participants were allowed an additional cue if they couldn't feel the first stimulus. Participants verbalized their response, which was recorded by the person administering the experiment. There was a 3 - 5 second delay between each presentation of the stimuli depending on how long it took the participant to respond and for the operator to record the result. The entire experiment for each participant took between 5 - 10 minutes, and was completed in a single session.

Results

A confusion matrix was generated for each participant, showing their responses for each stimulus resulting in a final accuracy percentage for each direction (figure 6 ). The percentages for each direction was averaged across all the participants as shown in table 1 . From the results, we observe that participants could recognize directions with an average accuracy of at least 79%.

A single sample one tailed Wilcoxon sign rank test was performed on each of the ten directions with the H 0 = the median percentage is not greater than random chance of 20% (under the assumption that a participant would never mistake a stimulus on one hand for another). The Wilcoxon sign rank test (table 2 ) showed that participants identified each direction with a median accuracy significantly above chance (p < 0.05).

6 APPLICATION 1: DIRECTIONAL HAPTIC VECTORS IN DOOM

We demonstrate how our device can be used to create a more immersive experience in virtual environments like games. Using an open source version of Doom (Chocolate Doom 2 [ 16 ]), we show how developers can utilize our device to improve the gaming experience by giving directional pressure vectors showing the player the whereabouts the incident projectile that they are being hit with is coming from. Despite the advances in visual and auditory aspects of gaming we have seen in the last decade, haptic feedback in commercial systems still only give a binary vibration feedback through the controller when the player is hit with a projectile with no way of indicating the direction is it coming from. We feel our implementation allows the experience to be more realistic.

We modified the source code of Doom and integrated it with a TCP client for sending directional commands to the same LabVIEW TCP server used in the user study. When the player was hit with a projectile by an enemy in the game, the direction closest to the angle with which the player was hit (with respect to the player's coordinate frame) was output from the client to the server. The user was able to control the movement of the player avatar via the joystick and experience the haptic pressure vectors every time they were shot (figure 7 ).

After having a few users navigate Doom using HaptiVec controllers, we asked them for remarks and comments about their experiences. The participants’ comments were extremely insightful with all users stating how useful it was to have directional information given through haptic feedback. One participant commented that ”most games nowadays show directional information visually; some companies even develop realistic directional surround sound, but having directional information through your controller via haptic feedback extends that level of realism even further”. Another participant noted that he was looking forward to ”playing first person shooter games, and seeing how having directional haptic feedback would benefit a player's performance.”

7 APPLICATION 2: IMMERSIVE VIRTUAL ENVIRONMENTS: HAPTIC RAIN

The second application we set up to show HaptiVec controllers enhancing the experience in virtual environments was by writing a LabVIEW program which actuated the pins on the controller randomly to mimic the sensations one would feel when walking in a rainy climate. The frequency at which HaptiVec was randomly actuated was synchronized to the volume of the sound of the rainfall playing in the background.

We gave numerous users the opportunity to experience this by allowing them to hold the controllers while viewing different videos showing rainy climates and also navigating in rainy environments in commercial video games (figure 8 ), and asked them for their comments regarding their experience. Although our HaptiVec controllers weren't particularly dense in terms of the number of pins it contained, users throughly enjoyed being able to feel what felt like raindrops on their hands especially while navigating a commercial video game. Multiple users made similar comments like ”It really does feel like raindrops” and ”wow, it's really immersive”. One participant noted how he was looking forward to the future of gaming, ”being able to feel different environments through his handheld controller”.

8 DISCUSSION

The user study reinforced the hypothesis that our HaptiVec controllers were capable of rendering pressure vectors that users could identify with good general accuracy in the eight cardinal directions with minimal prior exposure and training. A few of the participants found difficulties in accurately identifying vectors from certain directions, this however was different for every participant. There are a few factors which we think contribute to this perceptual error. Firstly, we intentionally didn't give detailed instructions to the participants as to how to grip the controllers due to the fact that in realistic scenarios using handheld controllers, users would typically not be given placement instructions. The way that their hand was placed in relation to the controller therefore could have caused the pins to present varying amounts of pressure indentations on different areas of the hand. A few comments we received from participants of the study afterwards were that they felt like their hands weren't able to distribute their hands evenly on the surface of the controller for every pin to come in contact with their hands, partly due to their hand size relative to the size of the controller. Participants also noted that they sometimes found it hard to maintain the same grip for the entire duration of the experiment. It is interesting to note that the majority of errors in direction identification were due to the participant mistaking a certain cardinal direction with its adjacent neighbor. Participants also mentioned that they had to really concentrate on the task for the entire duration, cognitive fatigue could have also been a contributing factor to identification errors as well. Although the entire session for each participant never exceeded 10 minutes, splitting the experiment into multiple shorter sessions with breaks in between could have minimized any fatigue and also allowed the user to reorient their grip and hand placement back to the original starting positions. In this study, only one of the North or South pin array columns were actuated, intuitively it would seem actuating both North (NL and NR) and South (SL and SR) vectors for those directions respectively would make those directions even clearer; reducing if not completely eliminating any misperceived directions with their adjacent directions. Our statistical analysis comparing the accuracy rates between each direction yielded no statistically significant difference even when we only retained users that were right-handed in our analysis.

Previous devices for conveying directional information in the eight cardinal directions included a handheld haptic navigation device based on asymmetric vibrations [ 6 ]. Although the majority of participants who used this device could accurately perceive the N, S, E, and W cues, many had difficulty perceiving the NE, SE, SW, and NW cues. It was also mentioned that one participant was consistently perceiving the direction to be in the complete opposite direction. This is contrasted with our HaptiVec controller, where our results show that all eight directions seemed to have pretty high accuracy rates of at least 79%. Other researchers have explored the use of vibrotactile arrays on the forearms and torso [ 36 , 48 ], resulting in high accuracy rates for directional and instructional cues. The methods used for interfacing with the human body in these instances however, are usually with the users directly adhering or strapping the vibrotactile motors to their skin via a type of sleeve or vest. Little has been seen by embedding these same actuators into existing commercial handheld devices and obtaining the same effectiveness. One possible reason why this could be is that when embedded into a rigid handheld device, the vibrations propagate through the whole device rendering individual vibration cues to be extremely difficult to perceive.

Being our first prototype, there are a few hardware improvements which could be made for HaptiVec . The first improvement would be to reduce the form factor of the controllers even more to one more akin to current commercial VR controllers. This would allow users with smaller hands to grip the controller with greater ease and have greater skin coverage over the pins. The current design doesn't fully take advantage of the high tactile acuity that a human hand is capable of. Even though our pin spacing of 25 mm was sufficient for rendering pressure vectors in the eight cardinal directions, increasing the number and density of the pins would further enhance the angular resolution of the pressure vectors but also allow for greater sensations to be felt like shapes and objects etc. The solenoids that were used in HaptiVec , had all the same nominal electrical characteristics, however, minor differences between the solenoids most likely exist thus leading to differences in current draw and therefore the actuation force. Improvements can be made by creating a closed-loop system whereby the force for each pin can be controlled by monitoring the current being drawn or adding force sensors. It is also possible for our device to create static pressures without continuously powering the actuators if the solenoids were replaced by motors moving a tactor along a lead screw or having an in-built locking mechanism for keeping the pins in the extended position. Another disadvantage to using solenoids is also the heat which results from the high current draw. Future designs should take this into account, since the controller surface can start to heat up after actuating the solenoids for extended periods of time. Often times, trade-offs have to be made when selecting actuators because powerful and fast-moving actuators often times come at the expense of size and power consumption which are not ideal for portable systems. The controllers that we developed in this paper was completely wired and didn't support tracking like current VR controllers. In order for it to be suitable for VR tasks and gaming, wireless versions along with controller pose tracking would need to be implemented.

Future applications which could result from this work include seeing how suitable controllers like these are at real-time trajectory tracking for navigation and motion guidance tasks in an VR environment. It would also be interesting to observe whether or not the pressure feedback would help in virtual grasping and object manipulation tasks. An important factor which was not covered in this paper is how long it takes for users to identify and react to the directional pressure vectors, as this will give greater clarity to what applications are suitable for this type of feedback. Despite the advances made in visuals and graphics in different gaming and multimedia applications, persons whom are visually impaired have not been able to benefit from these advancements. Future studies should explore how the presence of directional pressure vectors on the hands and fingers allow users with visual impairment to be more immersed in virtual and gaming environments. In the future, libraries of different haptic sensations can be created [ 33 ], allowing developers to easy integrate the controllers with their games or videos.

9 CONCLUSION

In this paper we presented HaptiVec , a synergistic combination of tactile pin arrays with VR style handheld controllers for enhancing the immersion in gaming and virtual environments. We show that users were able to identify haptic pressure vectors in the eight cardinal directions without extensive prior exposure or training. We demonstrate the potential of our feedback paradigm of embedding tactile pin arrays in controllers through two applications. The first application showed how users can feel the direction of the incident enemy projectile in the classic first-person shooter Doom, which would've been a simple binary vibration in traditional gaming controllers. The second application showed how users could be more immersed with their visual scenery in videos, games and virtual environments through the example of ”haptic rain”. Since the beginning of gaming, handheld controllers have been the dominant input device despite all the other gloves and body-grounded exoskeletons that have been developed in recent years. In this paper, we sought to improve the haptic feedback of existing handheld devices currently used in VR systems. We hope that in the years to come, the presentation of haptic feedback will catch up to their visual and auditory counterpart, creating more realistic immersive experiences.
