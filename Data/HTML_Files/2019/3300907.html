CHI '19: Proceedings of CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Glasgow, Scotland UK, May 2019

Sound Forest is a music installation consisting of a room with light-emitting interactive strings, vibrating platforms and speakers, situated at the Swedish Museum of Performing Arts. In this paper we present an exploratory study focusing on evaluation of Sound Forest based on picture cards and interviews. Since Sound Forest should be accessible for everyone, regardless age or abilities, we invited children, teens and adults with physical and intellectual disabilities to take part in the evaluation. The main contribution of this work lies in its findings suggesting that multisensory platforms such as Sound Forest, providing whole-body vibrations, can be used to provide visitors of different ages and abilities with similar associations to musical experiences. Interviews also revealed positive responses to haptic feedback in this context. Participants of different ages used different strategies and bodily modes of interaction in Sound Forest, with activities ranging from running to synchronized music-making and collaborative play.

1 BACKGROUND

Sound Forest[ 5 ] is a large-scale Digital Musical Instrument (DMI) situated at the Swedish Museum of Performing Arts in Stockholm 1 . It is one of the museum's major attractions, visited by about 50 000 people in 2017. The instrument serves as a long-term music installation that can host a wide range of different musical material, depending on context and current exhibition. For example, Sound Forest could be used for pedagogical purposes, musical creation, collaborative music making, and so on. The installation consists of five light-emitting strings attached from the ceiling to the floor in a dedicated room measuring 10x5 meters (see Fig. 1 ). The two shorter walls in the Sound Forest room are covered by mirrors, creating infinite reflections of the five strings, resulting in visual effects reminding of an enchanted forest, see Fig. 2 . The room has an extended floor built on top of the original hardwood floor. Five platforms are cut out from this extended floor, see Fig. 3 . These platforms rest on vibration dampeners and thus, they behave similarly to floating floors; each platform vibrates freely if set in motion by two vibrational loudspeakers. In the center of each platform there is an opening to the hardware floor, where the strings are attached. In this way, the strings are decoupled from the resonating platforms.

The strings in Sound Forest consist of many thin intertwined fiber optic cables wired around a force-resistant woven thread. They can emit light of different color (RGBW) and intensity. The strings themselves do not make much sound, but their movements and plucking positions can be captured using contact microphones and infrared sensors. For each string, there are three speakers; one is located above the string and two are attached to the platform below it. When the strings are plucked, sounds are produced and emitted from the speakers located above the them. At the same time, tactile sound creates vibrations that can be felt through respective platform. The musical interaction with a string thus consists of an interactive loop involving sonic, light and vibration feedback as responses to gestures performed on respective string. The multimodal space in Sound Forest, i.e. the context of use, invites to playful and exploratory interactions. Photos of the installation without and with interacting visitors are displayed in Fig. 3 and 4 .

Sound Forest is a DMI providing truly multimodal feedback; it has the potential of stimulating several senses at once. The process of coordinating information from different sensory channels into a final percept is usually referred to as cross-modal interaction [ 10 ]. When the brain processes multisensory information it is faced with the challenge of correctly categorizing and interpreting many sensory stimulations at the same time. Incongruities can result in unexpected perceptual effects and illusions. Moreover, cross-modal stimuli can result in multisensory enhancement or depression, and multisensory integration can alter the salience of cross-modal events [ 33 ]. Interaction between modalities can also result in interaction effects (see e.g. [ 20 ] for an overview of interactions between the auditory and tactile modality). Correctly designed multimodal feedback can produce sensorial experiences going beyond what can be achieved with a unimodal stimulus. To conclude, the complexity involved with designing multisensory experiences for Sound Forest motivates practical evaluations with actual users.

As mentioned in [ 5 ], one of the main aims of the Sound Forest project was to create a DMI that facilitated intuitive musical interaction, allowing visitors to quickly start creating music either alone or collaboratively. Sound Forest was designed to enable users without any prior knowledge of musical instruments to express themselves through bodily interaction and engage in musical activities in a rewarding way. One important goal was that the DMI should be able to serve as a pedagogical tool allowing users to learn about concepts related to music making, but also for the instrument to facilitate long-term engagement. Participants should be encouraged to return to the museum for continued music exploration. Another important aspect in the design of Sound Forest was accessibility, i.e. that the instrument should be able to provide rich musical experiences for all museum visitors, regardless of age or abilities. A key concern in this context was to ensure that Sound Forest was not only easily accessible for persons with impairments, but that it also could provide rich experiences for these visitors. For this purpose, we designed the central vibrating platform to be larger than the others, in order to fit one or several wheelchairs (see Fig. 1 ). The fact that Sound Forest allows for multiple modes of interaction promotes inclusion in the sense that it enables many different user groups with different abilities and needs to take part in music making. For example, visitors who have reduced hearing can enjoy music in the form of vibrations perceived through their feet.

In a previous pilot study, we invited nine participants from different age groups (9-53 years) to interact with an earlier version of the light-emitting strings used in Sound Forest [ 30 ]. Results from observations highlighted the importance of designing for different levels of engagement while considering factors such as age and musical background. Several bodily modes of interaction were observed, the most common ones being plucking, shaking, striking using one finger or hand, pulling and muting the string. Some examples of less common strategies were boxing, dragging and twisting the string. In general, children were less careful compared to adults, hitting and striking the string with a lot of energy.

In our current study, we invited 16 children and teens as well as three adults with physical and intellectual disabilities to explore different compositions in Sound Forest. The purpose was to evaluate potential visitors’ musical experiences of interacting with different musical material. The youngest participant who took part in the study was two years old. In the context of music research, findings have suggested that perception of e.g. expressive features in music is an ability that must be nurtured and developed through education and listening experience [ 8 ]. However, children as young as preschoolers have been found to be astonishingly consistent in associating certain attributes (happiness, sadness, anger and fear) with musical excerpts [ 9 ]). Moreover, infants (5-24 months) have been found to engage in significantly more rhythmic movement to music than to speech [ 38 ]. These findings suggest that not only adults, but also children of an early age, are capable of perceiving musical qualities. Therefore, we also included very young children in our evaluation.

The study presented in this paper is novel in the sense that little prior work has focused on evaluations of large interactive multisensory music installations aimed at providing rich as well as accessible experiences to a wide range of users. To our knowledge, no previous paper has documented the evaluation of a large-scale music installation providing haptic feedback in the form of whole-body vibrations, especially not for users with reduced mobility and intellectual disabilities, or for very young children. In general, relatively little of the research on new interfaces for musical expression has focused on promoting inclusion in music making (see e.g. [ 11 ], for an overview). The main contribution of our current work lies in its findings suggesting that multisensory platforms such as Sound Forest can be used to provide visitors of different ages and abilities with similar associations to musical experiences. Moreover, findings suggest that picture cards can be used as a means of evaluating associations made in musical interaction, for a wide range of users. These results may be of interest for researchers in the field of HCI working on interactive, accessible, musical installations for museums or other public, as well as educational, contexts.

2 RELATED WORK

While there are several examples of DMIs and art installations focusing on interaction with strings (see e.g. [ 12 , 21 , 34 ] and Maria Chavez’ String Room 2 ), relatively few studies have reported visitors’ experiences when interacting with such instruments. As mentioned in [ 27 ], studies of interactive music installations are often characterized by very little, if any, evaluation of the player's experience.

The degree of formality in the evaluation of instruments for musical expression is a widely discussed topic. Traditional HCI methods, such as observations, interviews, questionnaires, data recording, input logging, as well as experimental evaluation methods, could effectively be used to evaluate interactive systems [ 1 ]. In fact, the premiere conference for instrument builders, New Interfaces for Musical Expression (NIME), started as a Special Interest Group at the ACM Conference on Computer Human Interaction. At the first NIME, the use of HCI evaluation methods was suggested [ 36 ], reaffirming the connection to the origins in the HCI field. In [ 36 ], Orio et al. presented a review of input device evaluation and design, along with a discussion on possible applications of such methods in design and evaluation of new interfaces for musical expression. A set of musical tasks was proposed for the purpose of evaluation. These musical tasks included generation of isolated tones, basic musical gestures (e.g. glissandi and trills), simple scales, phrases with different contours, continuous feature modulation, simple rhythms and synchronization of musical processes.

The field of HCI, in turn, has also expressed an interest in the perspectives of artistic practice [ 35 ], motivated in the ever-expansive domain of computing [ 2 , 31 ]. In the last decades, a wide range of evaluation frameworks has been suggested [ 15 , 18 , 19 , 29 , 32 , 37 ]. However, HCI methods might not provide the best methods to evaluate artwork, as the aims of interactive art could involve other aspects than those related to e.g. usability and accessibility. Following that direction, some critique the idea of positivist formalization of an aesthetic experience and argue that while a reductionist view might make evaluation easier, it loses sight of the singular experience and goals of musical interaction [ 17 , 25 ]. Evaluation of interactive art pieces may require an integration of art-criticism techniques and HCI methods [ 28 ].

In the current study, we used response cards with pictures as a method of measuring users’ experiences. This method was adopted since many of the participants were not able to read, and therefore were judged to have difficulties performing tasks such as e.g. rating a word on a continuous scale. Although children possess or develop some ability to compare numbers in the first years of life, comparing accurately in many situations can be challenging, especially in cases where one would think of numbers in terms of discrete or continuous quantities [ 6 ]. Solving problems using number lines is actually quite difficult for kids, perhaps because of confusion between the dual representation of number as points and distances [ 13 ]. Moreover, young children do not always have the confidence or vocabulary to express their opinions regarding an experience, and picture cards can therefore be used as a supplement to interview questions. Picture cards have previously been successfully used as a method of evaluation for young children in HCI (see e.g. [ 23 ]) as well as Music and Emotion research (see e.g. [ 14 ]). The method is, alongside think aloud and drawing, listed as one of the evaluation methods suitable for children with special needs in [ 16 ]. The use of response cards in groups has also been found to be beneficial in comparison to traditional methods such as hand raising, since it may raise participation, improve test achievements as well as reduce off-task behavior [ 26 ].

3 STUDY

In the study presented in this paper, we employed both qualitative and quantitative methods as a means of evaluating how different user groups perceived musical material in the interactive music installation Sound Forest. This exploratory study set out to approach the following questions: “Can an audience perceive/recognize the creative ideas of composers when interacting with the Sound Forest installation? More specifically, how well does the installation support the composers’ creative intentions?” We wanted to investigate if the Sound Forest platform could be both so restricted that it would be possible to evaluate and compare different works with the same instructions, while at the same time flexible enough to allow for freedom and diversity in the presented solutions. Moreover, we posed the following question about the tactile sound: “How did participants describe and perceive whole-body vibrations, and how did these sensations add to the overall experience for different user groups?” On a more general note, we were interested in whether platforms such as Sound Forest may be appropriate for education tasks as the ones mentioned above, since the installation will be in place for several years, and we have the possibility to engage students from both technical as well as artistic programs in future projects. Below follows a more detailed description of the methodologies used in our study.

Material

Music. Five students from KMH Royal College of Music in Stockholm composed and produced one piece each for Sound Forest. The music was written and produced as a part of a course in music production, held in November 2017. In this course, students are given the task of composing and producing a piece of music for an interactive environment. This year the course focused on the Sound Forest installation. As a core part of the task, the composers were expected to use sound, music and light to communicate a non-musical, high level property or keyword. Each composer therefore initially chose one or several words, e.g. “magic” and “rain forest”, as a starting point for their composition.

Once all composers had finished their pieces, they agreed on six pairs of words that reflected the range of keywords they had worked with. The six pairs were peace/stress, meadow/city, warm/cold, magic/real, soft/hard and wet/dry. We consider these words as an interpretation of properties along one dimension, rather than dichotomous categories. In order to translate these words to pictorial representations, we initially proposed a set of different pictures to the composers, whereafter they rated how well these pictures represented the six word pairs. The pictures with highest ratings were used in the evaluation study, see Fig. 5 . In order to get a reference level for each picture and composition, composers were asked to rate how well each picture represented their music. The composers selected points on a visual analogue scale with respective picture placed on opposite sides of the scale (labeled from 0 to 10 at the ends). These ratings are displayed in Tab. 1 .

In this study we wanted to use “adaptive music” based on preproduced audio files. Adaptive music is often used in computer games (see e.g. [ 7 ]) and refers to music adapting to interactions, variables and states in the game. In our study, the interactions with a string were used to select and start playback of predefined sounds. The composers specified how the sound should rhythmically synchronize with the rest of the composition. We used an adaptive music playback engine, iMusic [ 22 ] to control loading of samples, timing, filters and channel routing. The music was recorded and produced using a Digital Audio Workstation (DAW) and exported into separate audio files. All compositions had a musical background layer that was playing continuously, independently of the interaction taking place in the room. In addition to this background sound, there were interaction sounds designed for the five strings. Each string had three different sounds, which were triggered depending on how hard the string was plucked. In other words, the compositions contained maximum 15 different sounds for all strings, on top of the background layer. This concept invited the users to control the musical arrangement, as well as the intensity of the generated sounds. You can resemble this interaction to a musical conductor controlling the expressive outcome of a musical composition, or a DJ manipulating existing recorded music. The focus was not to play “the right notes”, rather, it was to be expressive through bodily interaction. Excerpts of the compositions are available as supplementary material.

Vibrations. In order for the composers to get acquainted with the technical system used for generating sounds, lights and haptic vibrations in Sound Forest, a workshop was initially held by the technicians who designed and developed the installation. The composers were also informed about the platform's frequency response. To use the vibration platforms to their full potential, the composers were recommended to separate the music into two separate files; one for the speaker placed above each string, and one for the two vibration speakers attached to the haptic platform. In other words, the composers were encouraged to design different sonic material for the vibration speakers and the loudspeakers. In this way, different frequency ranges, envelopes and temporal properties of the auditory versus haptic channel could be used as part of creating a sonic narrative.

The composers opted for different strategies when designing vibrotactile sounds for their pieces. We expected them to produce files with predominantly lower frequencies (below 100Hz) for the channels connected to the vibration speakers, and files with high range sounds for the channels connected to the top speakers. However, such a technique was not used to a very large extent. Therefore, when mixing the compositions in the Sound Forest room, we added a standard second-order resonant low-pass filter to the channels connected to the vibration speakers 3 . Frequency analysis of sound files for respective composition revealed that only one composition contained low frequencies for all vibration speakers. The majority of the compositions included low frequency sound for only one or a few strings/platforms. One composition did not include any low frequencies at all. Interestingly, one composer mentioned that some composers did not want to use the vibrating platforms so much in their compositions, as they were worried that the vibrations might be perceived as “unpleasant”.

Lights. All composers matched their music with specific colors for each of the five strings. They chose one color for each string to be used when it was not set in motion (i.e. when it was inactive) and one color for when the string moved with maximum amplitude. These two colors were interpolated in real time to represent the current amplitude of each string. A schematic representation of the different color selections for active versus inactive states of each string and composition is displayed in Fig. 6 .

Participants

In the current study we wanted to test Sound Forest with a subset of its main target groups; kids of different ages and persons with physical and intellectual disabilities. A total of 19 participants, divided into four groups, took part in the experiment:

  1. One woman and two men (20-40 years) with physical and intellectual disabilities, all using wheelchair.
  2. Seven children aged 2-4 years (3F, 4M, average age 45 months, Std.Err 9.6 months).
  3. Four 12-year-old girls.
  4. Five 16-year-old girls.

Participants were recruited from different organizations and schools in the Stockholm area: group 1 from the organization JAG 4 , group 2 from a preschool, group 3 from a private elementary school with a specific focus on music, and group 4 from a technical high school. We refer to these user groups as group PX, where X goes from 1 to 4, and participant number PX:Y, where Y is the participant number in group X. Since most of the participants were minors, parents gave written consent for their children to take part in the experiment and for the data collection.

For comparative purposes, we also performed a separate evaluation of the different compositions with a reference group, consisting of six 15-year-old girls from the music school Adolf Fredriks Musikklasser, in Stockholm. These participants only listened to excerpts of the compositions, and did not interact in the Sound Forest room.

Procedure

The study took place during two days in November 2017. The four groups visited Sound Forest for one hour each. Group 1 was accompanied by personal assistants and group 2 by preschool teachers, as well as four parents. Each session was organized in the following way: the group was welcomed by the test leader and a short introduction to Sound Forest was given, before entering the room. The background music for each composition was then activated, whereafter the participants were instructed to “enter the room and play on the strings”. Participants played on the strings for three minutes, while the test leader observed tendencies in behaviors, took notes and video recorded excerpts from each session. An exploratory observation method focusing on a wide variety of factors was used.

After each composition, participants were asked to exit the room and sit down by a table with bundles of response cards. The test leader instructed participants to “put the six cards on the table, with the sides that you think best represents the music, facing upwards” . Participants worked individually and made forced-choice selections between two sides of six separate response cards (see Fig. 5 ). The cards had one picture on each side. The test leader asked the participants to “choose the cards that fit best to the music” . As the task was performed in groups, participants could see each others card selections, however, most of them were very focused on arranging their own cards and did not look at other participants’ selections. The test leader photographed the card responses of each participant, see (see Fig. 7 ). The above-described process was repeated for each composition. All participants got the chance to interact with all five compositions, except for the participants in the first group, who only tried the first four compositions. 5 . On average, the card selection task took one minute.

One of the compositions especially emphasized the use of haptic feedback. After this piece (composition 2), a semi-structured interview was held with the participants, focusing on their experience of the tactile sound. The interviews were conducted on a group level. Audio from these sessions was recorded for transcription and analysis. The participants were asked to elaborate on the following questions 6 :

  1. Did the vibrations contribute to your overall experience, and if so, how did the vibrations contribute?
  2. Was the experience of vibrations positive or negative?
  3. In which part of the body could you feel the vibrations?

We subsequently performed a short follow-up experiment with a reference group. These participants listened individually to a mix-down of both background sounds and interaction sounds from each composition, using headphones. Interestingly, one of the participants had to stop the listening test (at composition 2), since she perceived the sounds to be “too scary” . However, all other participants completed the test, providing us with responses from a total of five individuals.

Analysis

Card Selection. To investigate patterns in probability of selecting one side of respective card for a specific composition, we modeled the data using Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMMs), employing the function glmer in the R package lme4 [ 3 ]. GLMMs incorporate random effects into the linear predictor of a generalized linear model, allowing for modeling of correlated data [ 24 ]. The purpose of incorporating random effects is to quantify the variation among units, such as e.g. individuals. The GLMM can handle non-normal data by using link functions and exponential family distributions such as the binomial distribution [ 4 ]. Since we have binomial data, we use a logistic model, specifying the glmer function to a binomial distribution. The simple logistic model has the following form:

\begin{equation} logit(Y) = log_n (odds)= ln(\frac{p}{1-p}) = \alpha + \beta X \end{equation}
(1)
Where Y is either 0 or 1, p is the probability of the outcome of a particular event (e.g. pick first side of a card), α is the Y intercept, β are the regression coefficients and X is a predictor (categorical, in our case). The logistic function of any number is given by taking the inverse-logit on both sides of Eq. 1 , giving us a formula that can be used to predict probability p of the occurrence of an outcome of interest (e.g. probability for side one vs two, in our case):
\begin{equation} p = P(Y_o|X=x_1) = \frac{e^{\alpha + \beta X}}{1+e^{\alpha + \beta X}} \end{equation}
(2)
Where Y o is the outcome of interest and x 1 is a specific value of X . In our case, we fit a model with the fixed effect “composition” and a varying-intercept participant (random) effect using the variable “participant number” . In other words, the outcome of the card selection was hypothesized to depend on composition and individual differences were hypothesized to be present. In addition, a model testing for group differences was also tested, but without significant results for any of the picture pairs. The sample size was too small to include higher order models involving random slopes for each participant. The formula defined for the glmer function is specified below:
\begin{equation} \texttt {y} = \texttt {f} + 1 | (\texttt {i}) \end{equation}
(3)
Where y is card selection output (0 or 1), f is the fixed effect of “composition” (factors 1-5, coded as 4 dummy variables) and participant number ( i ) models the random effect by subject. Model selection was based on Akaike's information criterion (AIC); the model with the lowest AIC value was opted for. P-values were obtained by comparing the full model with the effect in question against the model without the effect in question, using the ANOVA chi-square test function anova in R, specified to “Chisq” .

Interviews. Recordings of interviews were transcribed verbatim. Excerpts were then sorted using an emergent themes approach. The following categories of topics emerged from the analysis: (1) Descriptions of haptic sensations, (2) Positive and negative associations to haptic sensations and (3) Effects of combining sounds and vibrations.

Observations. In order to investigate patterns in behaviors across groups, two of the researchers in the team analyzed notes taken by evaluators during the experimental sessions. The same researchers also analyzed recorded video material. For each participants group, interesting quotes and interaction strategies were collected and categorized.

Interviews

One of the themes that emerged from the analysis of interview data was attempts to describe different haptic sensations . The following verbs were used to describe perceived sensations: itching, tickling, shaking, tingling, rushing [through the body] . The following bodily locations were mentioned in discussions on where the haptic sensations could be felt: in the feet (5 participants), in the heart (3 participants), in the head (3 participants), in the belly (2 participants), in the chest (1 participant) and in the legs (1 participant). The youngest children mainly described their experiences as tickling sensations felt through the feet. In other groups, the descriptions of different sensations varied among participants. Interestingly, some participants used metaphors to describe the haptic sensations. For example, P1:2 said that the vibrations felt like “bicycle pedaling” . Another participant (P3:3), mentioned that the experience was similar to “when you put on a pair of Virtual Reality-goggles, without sitting in one of those [vibrating] chairs...” . Yet another participant describe her perceived sensation as “something rushing through the entire body...like ’bom bom bom”’ . Moreover, participants in group 4 agreed that the vibrations could be felt “physically” in the feet but also “emotionally” in the heart.

Guided by the interview questions, participants also discussed positive or negative associations to haptic sensations . This also involved accounts on how pleasant different sensations were. Overall, the majority of the participants described the presence of haptic feedback as positive, something that “felt good” or was “fun” . Interestingly, one of the participants in group 1 was very positive to the experience overall, describing that he really enjoyed the vibrations very much. Some participants also made remarks about some unpleasant sensations. One participant in group 2, the group with young children, commented that he felt an itching sensation that he did not like “since it made him want to scratch his feet” . Another older participant (P3:4) described that “some sensations were unpleasant, since certain sounds were unpleasant” . Yet a third participant mentioned that one of the strings “was screaming at her” and that this made her feel uncomfortable.

Another theme brought up by several participants focused on the effects of combining sounds and vibrations . In general, these discussions were mostly present in the older groups. Several participants in group 3 described the haptic feedback added to the experience by making it “stronger” or more “powerful” . In addition, participants in several groups (P1:3, P3:3, P3:4) mentioned that they “associated the vibrations with the music” or that they could “feel the music” through vibrations. Two participants also described that the vibrations made the experience “richer” (P4:4) and contributed to making the experience “more unified” (P4:3). Interestingly, one participant mentioned that there was one occasion when Sound Forest was almost quiet, but that “the vibrations were so strong, [and therefore] it was still a cool experience” .

Observations

The observations done during the evaluation sessions indicated that most participants engaged in the interaction with the strings easily, with a lot of energy and laughter. However, different groups adopted quite different strategies when interacting with the strings. Group 1, the three participants with physical and intellectual disabilities, used their wheelchairs to approach the strings. They tended to stay by the first string that they had selected throughout a session, and spent most of the three minutes on exploring the sounds, vibrations and lights for that particular string. These participants did not interact much with others (apart from their assistants). Interestingly, they mostly focused on shaking the string back and forth, rather than on plucking or hitting it. One of the participants, an almost blind man, commented that he could see the light of the string when he held it against his eye, and that he could sense the vibrations coming through his wheelchair into his body. He also said that he wished that he had such a string to play with at home. Group 2, consisting of the youngest kids, enjoyed running between, and in circles, around the strings. As for group 1, these participants tended to hold on to, shake and hang onto the strings, rather than plucking them. These results confirm our previous findings reported in [ 30 ]. Group 3 and 4 (12 and 16 year old girls) behaved similarly when interacting with the strings. As opposed to groups 1 and 2, these participants focused mainly on plucking the strings. They also spent time exploring the range of different sounds produced by a particular string and collaborated with other participants, creating games or structures for synchronizing sounds. For example, they triggered several sounds simultaneously, and plucked the strings in specific, repeated, sequences.

5 DISCUSSION

In the exploratory study presented in this paper, we employed both qualitative and quantitative methods as a means of evaluating how different user groups perceived different musical material in Sound Forest. We were interested in if an audience can perceive/recognize the creative ideas of composers when interacting with the Sound Forest installation. More specifically, we wanted to know how well the installation supported the composers’ creative intentions. We investigated this research question using a pictorial response cards methodology. Results suggests that participants and composers agreed in the categorization of the compositions when using a set of picture cards, for some picture pairs and compositions, and that these effects were present across groups. Moreover, these findings were consistent with card selections made by a reference group (participants who only listened to the music compositions). However, the overall consistency between composer ratings and card selections of the reference group was greater than the consistency between the composer ratings and the visitors interacting with the installation. In addition, results indicated no significant effect of group belonging on card responses. In other words, we can not reject the hypothesis that there are no differences between the groups. To summarize, we can conclude that an audience can perceive some of the creative ideas of composers when interacting with the installation. However, it should be noted that this study was carried out on a relatively small set of participants.

Our second research question focused on if the Sound Forest platform could be both so restricted that it enabled evaluation and comparisons of different works, and at the same time be flexible enough to allow for freedom and diversity in the presented solutions. Based on our findings from the picture cards evaluation, we can conclude that some pieces, in particular piece 2 and 5, were judged to be most different by participants. This is an interesting finding, considering that composition 2 was the piece that had a high focus on haptic feedback with low-frequency range. Based on our results, we conclude that the Sound Forest platform can be used for evaluation and that it offers composers many degrees of freedom and possibilities. It is in fact possible that the composers were presented with too many possibilities; we noticed that only one composer actually explored the full potential of the installation in terms of providing haptic feedback.

Furthermore, one of our research questions focused on how participants described perception of whole-body vibrations, and how these sensations affected their overall musical experience when interacting in Sound Forest. The interviews performed in this study shed light on these issues. The vocabulary used for describing haptic sensations included words such as itching, tickling, shaking and tingling . Not surprisingly, the most frequently mentioned location where participants perceived the vibrations was the feet. Interestingly, the heart and the head were also mentioned, followed by belly, chest and legs. Overall, participants had mostly positive associations to vibrations. Some of the participants were really enthusiastic about perceiving music through tactile feedback. The tactile vibrations were said to enhance the musical experience overall, resulting in stronger and more powerful sensations. However, some participants described that certain haptic experiences (e.g. itching) were unpleasant.

In addition to the above mentioned research questions, we were also interested in whether platforms such as Sound Forest may be appropriate to be used for education tasks such as the one used in the current study, i.e. for students in Music Production focusing on interactivity and Adaptive Music. Sound Forest will be showcased for several years, and many different works, and student projects, could be set up using this system. Based on the findings presented in this work, we conclude that the Sound Forest platform could be used as platform for future studies. However, some adjustments should perhaps be done when it comes to transferring knowledge from those who developed the technical system to the composers. In particular, perhaps the functions of the haptic platforms should be more clearly described to future composers. In our current study, designing haptic feedback was obviously not a trivial task, as only one composer actually produced music that in a conscious manner made use of tactile sound. It may be that composers would benefit from spending more time exploring the full capabilities of the platforms.

Our observations clearly indicated that participants actively engaged and interacted with the strings, as well as each other. Many of the participants, especially the younger kids, interacted with a lot of energy. The majority appeared to really enjoy the music installation overall. This is an interesting finding, as it was not obvious that participants of all age groups, including those with special needs, would enjoy the interactive possibilities provided by Sound Forest. This indeed confirms observations made by the museum's pedagogical department.

We also observed that participants in different age groups approached the strings with different strategies and focused on several different bodily modes of interaction. For example, young children were less careful in their interaction, hanging from and dragging the strings, while the older children appeared to coordinate their music making, organizing the group to pluck different strings simultaneously or in specific repeated sequences. Possibly, the fact that different participant groups used different strategies when engaging with the music material in Sound Forest may suggest that group dynamics, as well as elements of play, can heavily influence the interactions and music making taking place in the room.

For the purpose of our study, using picture cards as a means of evaluating associations in musical interaction appears to work well. Most participants easily performed the picture selection task. However, those with physical disabilities required some assistance when it came to flipping and selecting cards. One critique that could be mentioned in this context is that the response card methodology in itself provides no information about why certain participants opted for one option rather than another. In order to limit the effects of personal preference for certain pictures, we tried to make the pictures as similar as possible, by presenting them in black and white. However, the fact that there is no means of evaluating the personal interpretation of a picture raises questions about internal validity of the results obtained in the current study. However, the card results were rather consistent across groups, suggesting that the interpretations were somewhat similar for most individuals. In future experiments, we propose the use of response cards together with other methods, such as think aloud, in order to fully reveal the participants associations to pictures, as well as their reason for card selection.

6 CONCLUSIONS

This paper presents an exploratory study on the evaluation of the interactive multisensory music installation Sound Forest, designed with the aim of being accessible while still providing rich experiences to a wide range of users. The study is novel in the sense that it documents the evaluation of a large-scale interactive music installation providing haptic feedback in the form of whole-body vibrations. The study also focused on how very young children, teens as well as adults with physical and intellectual disabilities perceived vibrations and musical sounds. The main contribution of this work lies in its findings indicating that multisensory platforms such as Sound Forest can be used to provide visitors of different ages and abilities with similar associations to musical experiences. Moreover, findings suggest that picture cards is a useful method in evaluation of associations made in musical interaction. Inclusive design of sonic interactions aimed for minority groups, as well as evaluations of such interactive systems, are still rather unexplored topics in the field of Sonic Interaction Design. With this paper, we hope to encourage future discussions on evaluation methods for inclusive interactive music contexts.
