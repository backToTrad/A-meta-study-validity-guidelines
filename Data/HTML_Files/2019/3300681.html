Face and Ecological Validity in Simulations: Lessons from Search-and-Rescue HRI

Lorin Dole , Cornell Tech, USA, lorindole@gmail.com
Wendy Ju , Cornell Tech, USA, wendyju@cornell.edu

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300681
CHI '19: Proceedings of CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Glasgow, Scotland UK, May 2018

In fields where in situ performance cannot be measured, ecological validity is difficult to estimate. Drawing on theory from social psychology and virtual reality, we argue that face validity can be a useful proxy for ecological validity. We provide illustrative examples of this relationship from work in search-and-rescue HRI, and conclude with some practical guidelines for the construction of immersive simulations in general.

1 INTRODUCTION

It seems impossible to imagine using a robot for search and rescue without knowing how it will interact with survivors of urban disasters, or dependents. Yet the field of search-and-rescue HRI has until very recently focused almost exclusively on operators instead of dependents—on the interface between the rescue team and the robot, to the exclusion of that between the robot and people in need. This focus is perhaps most visible at the annual RoboCup Rescue League competition, which offers prizes for Best-in-Class Autonomy, Best-in-Class Mobility, and Best-inClass Manipulation [ 1 ]; the social and psychological needs of dependents are completely absent from the competition's reward structure.

There are understandable reasons for this neglect of dependents: first is the lack of opportunities to study real dependents in the wild. Since 2001, ground robots have been deployed in at least 23 different disasters worldwide, but no live dependents were ever encountered [ 2 ]. The second reason has to do with physical or moral harm: even if real dependents were accessible for research purposes, it would be inhumane to assign them to different treatment conditions whose influence on survival or psychological wellbeing is yet unknown.

The response to these obstacles should not be neglect but ingenuity. Given that we cannot study dependents in the wild, using immersive simulations to study research participants who play the role of a dependent is the most promising alternative. Our challenge then becomes one of validity: how can we be confident that the conclusions we draw from studies involving simulations tell us anything useful about real-world encounters? In this paper, we chart an answer to this question by drawing on theory and practice from the fields of social psychology and virtual reality, which have had to grapple with highly similar if not identical challenges.

2 CONTRIBUTION

Researchers are constantly beset by challenges of validity, but this is especially true for studies that involve simulations; as shown by Parsons [ 3 ], Neisser's [ 4 ] critique of ecological validity in laboratory (i.e., simulation) studies set off a decades-long (and still ongoing) debate in cognitive and social psychology about the trade-offs between experimental control and generalizability. The main goal of this paper is therefore to describe and clarify a complex theoretical problem with which the CHI community may not be familiar, and to explicate relevant concepts and the relationships between them. (The prevailing trend in CHI has been to study crises in the wild [ 5 , 6 , 7 ]—a notable exception was the workshop by Bennett et al. [ 8 ]—while in HRI the the practical and moral issues described above support a focus on simulation and disaster-preparedness training [ 9 , 10 , 11 ].) In the interest of putting these concepts to practical use, we apply them to three studies from search-and-rescue HRI and offer a set of guidelines for the creation of immersive simulations in general. To ease the circulation of this new vocabulary, we also close in Section 9 with a concise glossary of key terms. A secondary goal of this paper is to support researchers who might need to unpack and address issues related to validity. We hope that such authors can use our paper as a conceptual resource, or point to it as a place where a discussion and perhaps resolution of certain validity issues can be found.

While we focus on the specific case of search-and-rescue HRI, our argument is relevant to any field in which simulations are used to circumvent practical or moral obstacles that make the collection of real-world data difficult or impossible, including HCI, VR, and others. For example, application areas like military and police training, space exploration, and surgery share with search and rescue the inability to collect real-world data outside of a scenario in which people's physical or mental wellbeing is at stake. Less severe but still fraught are areas like assistive robotics and education, in which the consequences of real-world testing can be negative but are less chronic. By contrast, our argument is least applicable to areas like entertainment and industry, whose exposure to such problems is relatively limited.

3 VALIDITY OF IMMERSIVE SIMULATIONS IN SEARCH-AND-RESCUE HRI

Simulations offer a solution to the practical and moral obstacles of studying dependents in the wild. Specifically, they mitigate the issues of impracticality [ 2 , 12 ], clinical equipoise [ 13 ], and informed consent [ 13 , 14 ]. But the use of simulations is not without problems of its own. For example, how can researchers be sure that results obtained from a simulation-based study correspond to those that would be obtained in the real-world scenario it is designed to simulate? When results are applicable in this way, a study is said to be ecologically valid. 1

Another important question for simulation studies is whether they appear to measure what they are purported to measure, regardless of whether they actually do. When a study seems to possess this kind of conceptual accuracy at face value—when it seems to fulfill its purpose at a purely intuitive level—it is said to have face validity. Other kinds of validity are of course important to simulation-based studies, but they are not our focus here.

In any field, one cannot tell for certain whether ecological or face validity has been achieved. To conclude as much would be to accept the null hypothesis, which is not allowed under the logic of classical hypothesis testing. Rather, each type of validity can only be strived toward. In fields like neuropsychology, for example, progress toward ecological validity can be quantified by correlating indices measured in the lab (e.g., one's scores on a test of executive functioning) with real-world performance (e.g., one's ability to prioritize their day-to-day tasks [ 15 ]). But in search-and-rescue HRI, such comparisons are made impossible by practical and moral obstacles like those discussed above. The closest we can get to this sort of quantitative evidence is to identify increases in factors believed to contribute to ecological validity, such as how present in a study's fictional scenario participants seemed or reported themselves to be [ 16 ]. In other words, researchers who study dependents in search-and-rescue HRI must use face validity as a proxy for ecological validity.

4 SOME USEFUL CONCEPTS FROM SOCIAL PSYCHOLOGY

What reasons do we have for believing that face validity is correlated with ecological validity—that it can serve as a proxy in this way? The only study that appears at first glance to have tested this relationship empirically is [ 17 ], but unfortunately its authors focused entirely on simulated driving. It would be helpful if simulations with high ecological validity also had high face validity, but evidence on this point is mixed. For example, time spent in a flight simulator alongside aircraft training is better than aircraft training alone, but whether the simulator includes motion cues surprisingly makes no difference [ 18 ]. To our knowledge, there is no conclusive empirical evidence either for or against this point.

We thus turn to theoretical reasons. For Kurt Lewin, one of the founding fathers of communication research, the answer has to do with the Gestalt concept of transposition . Transposition describes the maintenance of key structural conditions when changing from one situation to another [ 19 ]. For example, in Milgram's famous study of obedience [ 20 ], as well as its contemporary and modern replications [ 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 ], the researchers simulated a dynamic that is believed to exist in situations where people obey unconscionable orders because they “no longer view [themselves] as the efficient cause of [their] own actions” [ 25 ]. Intense or lasting pain was never inflicted on the ostensible victim or “learner” in these studies, but it allowed the researchers to learn much about the psychology of people in positions of power and servitude. Notwithstanding Milgram's excessively reductive explanation of the Holocaust in these terms [ 25 ], simulations like his afforded important insights about the real world because they emulated structural conditions present within real organizations—places in which power disparities are created and maintained by social titles (e.g., teacher, learner), spatial separation (e.g., separate rooms), associated objects (e.g., electroshock generator, electric chair), and ideological commitments (e.g., science as an end in itself [ 22 ]). Moreover, the ubiquity of these structural factors means that Milgram's conclusions ought to extend not only to scenarios involving electric shock in the service of memory and learning, but to any situation in which one group wields (il)legitimate power over another by similar means, be it labor relations, unhealthy marriages, racism, or sexism. When structural conditions are accurately transposed into the lab, that should increase belief in a study's ecological validity. 2

Another Gestalt concept, that of emergence, can help explain where face validity is present or absent. When a pattern or concept seems to emerge from a particular set of stimuli, it is said to be an emergent property of those stimuli [ 26 ]. Consider what happens when one is asked to determine whether democracy exists in a room full of congressmen and -women. One might observe the positions of the chairs, which are arranged such that people face one another instead of a single podium. One might listen to the way discussions in the room are conducted, which seems fair and reasonable. Neither of these observations alone determines the presence or absence of democracy, but the overall pattern that emerges might prompt one to declare that democracy can there be found. The more likely people are to make this same declaration—that is, the more democracy tends to emerge from the situation's structural conditions in the minds of different people—the more face valid the presence of democracy becomes.

This same evaluative process can be used to determine the face validity of simulation studies in search-and-rescue HRI. In response to sensory deprivation, a participant might experience greater sensitivity to auditory and tactile information, just like in an earthquake that kills the electricity. In response to the authoritative voice of a fake radio announcer, they might feel comforted by information and guidance just as they would when listening to a real emergency broadcast. And when approached by a robot while taking cover beneath a table, they might feel the same security and company as a dependent in a real robot-assisted disaster. The more participants respond in such ways, the more confident one becomes in the study's face validity. (These structural conditions and participant reactions were implemented and observed in the course of work on search-and-rescue HRI, which is discussed further in Section 6.)

Mundane and Experimental Realism

As these examples suggest, there is a correspondence between a simulation's realism and its face validity. There is a difference, however, between the kind of realism that prompts participants to take a simulation seriously and that which denotes a strong resemblance between the laboratory and the real world. The Handbook of Social Psychology calls these experimental realism and mundane realism , respectively [ 27 ]. Both kinds of realism are important. Experimental realism matters because people take real life seriously. If participants fail to take a simulation seriously, then it can hardly be claimed that the study's results apply to the real world. Mundane realism matters because similarity to the real world is often prerequisite to a situation being taken seriously. Indeed, the two kinds of realism are far from independent. Consider, for example, whether Milgram's participants would still have been so distressed if his studies had taken place next to a busy beach volleyball court rather than the “elegant Interaction Laboratory of Yale University.” Probably not.

Throughout the 1970s, social psychologists used simulations to study phenomena like group conflict and social influence. Generally speaking, these scholars privileged experimental realism over mundane realism [ 16 ]. The reason was that experimental realism allowed them to study psychological processes that mattered a great deal, despite the fact that they were fantastic or unlikely to happen in real life. For example, Jones & Sigall introduced what is known as the bogus pipeline technique, in which participants are hooked up to a lie detector, convinced that it works, and then questioned about their attitudes [ 28 ]. The fact that lie detectors do not work, and that few people would ever encounter one in real life, did not prevent the researchers from discovering how people behave when they do not believe it is possible to lie.

Experimental realism is beneficial to research on search-and-rescue HRI for the same reason. It is impossible to create a disaster simulation that has perfect mundane realism because to do so would cause harm to participants. Sheer terror is not a structural condition that can be ethically transposed into the lab. The opposite is true of other structural conditions like sensory deprivation, uncertainty, and perceived dependence on help from a third party. These can introduce enough mundane realism that participants begin to suspend disbelief in the simulation. (See Section 5 for a discussion of how to measure the degree to which a participant is “present” within a simulation in this way.) If they do, then experimental realism has been achieved. The upshot is that, even in the absence of any real danger, important conclusions can be drawn about how people think and behave when they are sensorially deprived, and uncertain, and feel dependent on outside help. In other words, one can draw conclusions that ought to apply wherever exist the structural conditions that have been transposed.

Experimental realism, then, is a measurable indication of a study's face validity, and should also suggest that at least a certain degree of mundane realism has been achieved. It is therefore also a useful measure for evaluating a study's ecological validity.

5 TOOLS FROM VR: MEASURING EXPERIMENTAL REALISM

The field of virtual reality (VR) has developed concepts that are useful for operationalizing realism. These are immersion and presence . Immersion, just like mundane realism, is a property of simulations. VR technologies that provide a wide field of vision, accurately and quickly track users in three-dimensional space, and offer stereoscopic vision are more immersive than VR technologies that lack these features [ 29 ]. Presence, just like experimental realism, is a property of users’ psychology. A user is said to be present in VR when they subjectively experience being inside the simulation as opposed to the real world,. The more immersive the VR, the more present users tend to feel inside it.

The same relationships hold for search-and-rescue HRI, although the simulations are physical rather than virtual. Instead of field-of-vision, user tracking, and stereoscopic vision, physical simulations are made immersive through the use of props, trained actors, and deception—techniques borrowed from the art of stagecraft [ 16 ]. The more immersive the physical simulation, the more present study participants tend to feel inside it.

How to measure a user's presence within a simulation is hotly contested. The most common type of measure is self-report. Five questionnaires have remained especially influential over time: the Slater-Usoh-Steed Questionnaire [ 30 ], Presence Questionnaire [ 31 ], I-Group Presence Questionnaire [ 32 ], ITC Sense of Presence Inventory [ 33 ], and Lombard & Ditton Questionnaire [ 34 ]. (For a review of these canonical measures see [ 35 ].) Although different in many respects, they are all committed to the idea that presence involves a sensation of “non-mediation.” Users are asked to gauge the extent to which they felt like they were “really there,” or to which it felt like “the avatar's body was my body.” Regardless of whether a simulation seems to transport the user somewhere else, place her inside a new body, bring distant objects close, or elicit responses that one would expect in real life, being present in VR involves feeling as if there were no technology mediating the experience [ 34 ].

Other measures of presence are behavioral or physiological. Behavioral measures involve users’ actions while they are inside VR. For example, imagine a virtual pit with a thin plank extending over it. Users who tip-toe or crouch while walking over the plank seem to be more present in the simulation than users who do not. Similarly, physiological measures involve tracking skin conductivity or heartrate. In the same example, users who sweat more or whose heartrates accelerate while walking over the plank seem more present in the simulation than users who do not exhibit these signs [ 36 ].

While self-report measures avoid experimenter bias, they are subject to participant bias and cannot easily be used to compare presence across media (e.g., VR versus real life). Conversely, behavioral measures avoid participant bias but are subject to experimenter bias and must be chosen carefully; dizziness, for example, is a poor indicator of presence in VR since it can also be caused by simulator sickness—a sensation caused when stereoscopic images do not update frequently enough to match the user's position and orientation in space. Physiological measures, just like behavioral ones, avoid participant bias but are subject to experimenter bias. Their validity is also especially difficult to gauge because skin conductivity and heart rate can indicate many things besides presence. Physiological measures are therefore best used in conjunction with self-report or behavioral measures, rather than alone. Indeed, all three types of measures can make up for each other's pitfalls.

Not only can these three approaches be used to measure presence in search-and-rescue HRI, but they are also clear indications of experimental realism. Participants can be asked to report on how present within a simulation they felt; researchers can watch for behaviors that seem to indicate a participant's presence or lack thereof; and physiological measures can be used to corroborate that information. Each type of measure also gets to the heart of what it means for a participant to take a simulation seriously. If a participant claims to have experienced a virtual body as her own, that means that her avatar's actions and appearance have real consequences for her thoughts and feelings about herself and others [ 37 ]. If researchers observe someone use fewer paper towels after she has been educated through VR about deforestation, that means she took it seriously enough for it to affect her behavior [ 38 ]. And if a participant sweats and experiences a rapid heartbeat while also claiming to have been present in a simulation, that ought to increase our belief in the idea that she took the simulation seriously [ 39 ]. Accordingly, researchers in fields like search-and-rescue HRI, who must rely on face validity—operationalized in terms of presence—as a proxy for ecological validity, ought to ensure that participants feel as present as possible within their simulations.

6 CREATING IMMERSIVE SIMULATIONS: THREE EXAMPLES FROM SEARCH-AND-RESCUE HRI

Between 2011 and 2015, three experiments were conducted with the goal of understanding how autonomous a robot should be in order to maximize positive outcomes for dependents. In each of the three experiments, participants were instructed to imagine that they had just experienced a large earthquake and must now await rescue while taking cover amongst the rubble. While awaiting rescue, participants were approached by a search-and-rescue robot [ 40 ] and interacted with it in separate thirteen-minute sessions. The simulations of the earthquake's aftermath varied across studies in terms of immersion, and we observed correspondingly different levels of participant presence across two of the studies.

To measure presence, we paid close attention to how engaged participants seemed during the semi-structured interactions. Refusing to take cover during and after the simulated earthquake, responding sarcastically to the robot, and disobeying instructions not to touch the robot were indications of low presence—they indicated a gap between the reality that participants perceived, on the one hand, and the reality that we had intended to create, on the other. Conversely, participants who did not exhibit such behavior were judged to be relatively more present in the simulation. 3

In the first study, each participant interacted with a digital representation of the robot while viewing a virtual simulation on his or her personal computer; because experimenters were not collocated with these participants, their level of presence could not be observed [ 45 ]. In the second study, each participant interacted with a physical robot while taking cover inside a rudimentary physical simulation that included realistic sound effects and a box-like Styrofoam enclosure with a blanket inside; in this study, approximately half of participants either failed to comply with safety instructions or deviated strongly from the semi-scripted interaction, thus suggesting low presence [ 46 ]. And in the third study, each participant interacted with the same physical robot while taking cover inside an elaborate physical simulation that included a compelling script, fake rubble, scattered office materials, realistic sound effects, and two well-trained confederate experimenters; in this study, the rate of noncompliance or deviation fell to less than five percent, thus suggesting higher presence [ 47 ].

Participants in the third study therefore found the scenario more face-valid than participants in the second study. This conclusion is also supported by interview data: while being debriefed after the third study, they were asked a question that was designed to help increase the immersion of future simulations. They were asked, “Was there anything during the interaction or build-up to it that seemed particularly unconvincing, that could have been done better?” Not a single participant said that they found something unconvincing. Many also said that they were impressed by the study's fidelity.

One cannot be certain whether the results of the third study will hold up in a real disaster scenario. However, relative to the results of the second study, its participants’ behavior and self-reports should increase one's confidence that they will.

7 PRACTICAL GUIDELINES

Here we distill from our theoretical discussion a set of practical guidelines for the creation of immersive simulations in general. To concretize them, we discuss each in terms of an HRI project from outside the subfield of search and rescue. Imagine a study in which each participant will interact with a robot in a laboratory setting intended to represent his or her own living room. The question is, how do you make this simulation as immersive as possible?

Answering this question in a productive way requires keeping several things in mind:

  1. Ecological validity is a goal but not a destination. It is important to remember the impossibility of declaring that ecological validity has been achieved; rather it can only be strived toward, and therefore every improvement to a simulation's immersion exists only relative to some previous iteration or alternative. Making a laboratory version of a participant's living room as immersive as possible would require pre-testing different strategies, iterating over the course of several studies, or ideally both of these approaches at once. In light of this impossibility, however, we as a community might agree on a threshold of ecological validity that we consider acceptable. In our opinion, that threshold should be somewhere around 80%. Moreover, we believe that researchers should try to declare before running a study how they will identify participants with low presence, and either exclude these participants from their analyses, include presence as a covariate, or attempt to experimentally manipulate it.
  2. Transpose key structural conditions from the real world into the lab. Doing so will produce mundane and likely experimental realism, by causing the emergence of theoretically relevant concepts in the minds of participants. In the living room example, promising candidates might include smells, objects, ambient sounds, or the presence of other people or pets. Promising structural conditions can be identified through familiarity with the real-world situation(s) of interest, relevant theory, and empirical comparisons like the one we conducted between the studies in Section 6.
  3. Avoid the introduction of unwanted between- subjects variables. If one simply asked each participant to bring a homey object from their living room, it is true that all of them would likely feel more present in the simulation than if they had brought none at all. But this would come at the cost of some participants being better able to relax, perhaps because they brought an ottoman on which to rest their feet, while others might be more distracted because they brought a home stereo system. Variables like arousal and attention have far-reaching consequences, and should therefore be kept as consistent as possible between participants. One possibility, then, would be to have each participant bring a homey object of a particular category, like a photo. Another would be to recruit couples and have them participate as dyads.
  4. Measure participants’ presence in the simulation. This is the upshot of our entire argument. It is participants’ presence in a simulation that indexes its face validity, which in turn can be used as a proxy for its ecological validity. Note, however, that one should only be content with using face validity as a proxy if real-world data are difficult or impossible to collect. When dealing with regular simulations like the living room example, one should also estimate ecological validity by correlating laboratory and naturalistic data. Measuring presence also allows one to identify any less immersive aspects of the simulation, and to gauge their improvement across iterations. Ideally, presence should be measured using a combination of participants’ self-report, behavior, and physiology.

8 CONCLUSION

In fields where in situ performance cannot be measured, ecological validity is difficult to estimate. Face validity—which is associated with what the fields of social psychology and virtual reality call experimental realism and presence, respectively—can be a useful proxy. When participants take a simulation seriously, one should feel more confident that the study's results will be applicable to the real world. In this paper, we laid out the theory behind that intuition, and provided practical guidelines that researchers from all parts of the CHI community can use to create more immersive simulations. Armed with relevant concepts, their origins, and the relationships between them, we hope the CHI community feels empowered to discuss face and ecological validity in these terms.

9 GLOSSARY

We offer the following definitions of key terms from our argument, in order to ease their adoption and circulation. Note that “laboratory setting” is synonymous with “simulation.”

  • The degree to which a study or particular aspect thereof appears to accomplish its intended aims, regardless of whether it actually does.
  • The degree to which results obtained in a laboratory setting correspond to those obtained in a naturalistic one.
  • The maintenance of structural conditions from one situation to another.
  • The impression of a concept from a set of stimuli.
  • The degree of similarity between a laboratory setting and the real world.
  • The degree to which participants in a laboratory setting perceive and behave within it as if it were real.
  • The degree to which a simulation causes users to feel present within it.
  • The degree to which users in a simulation feel and respond to it as if their experience or some aspect thereof (e.g., an environment, avatar, or interaction) were unmediated.
  • Measures that require participants to report on something verbally or in writing (e.g., attitudes or justifications).
  • Measures that involve the observation, recording, and/or coding of participants’ behaviors (e.g., walking speed or gaze direction).
  • Measures that involve the observation, recording, and/or coding of participants’ physiological state (e.g., skin-conductance or pupil dilation).
