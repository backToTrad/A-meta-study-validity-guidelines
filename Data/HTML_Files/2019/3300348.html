More than the Sum of Makers
The Complex Dynamics of Diverse Practices at Maker Faire

CHI '19: Proceedings of CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Glasgow, Scotland UK, May 2019

Human Computer Interaction has developed great interest in the Maker Movement. Previous work has explored it from various perspectives, focusing either on its potentials or issues. As these are however only fragmented portrayals, this paper aims to take a broader perspective and interconnect some of the fragments. We conducted a qualitative study in the context of two Maker Faires to gain a better understanding of the complex dynamics that makers operate in. We captured the voices of different stakeholders and explored how their respective agendas relate to each other. The findings illustrate how the event is co-created at the nexus of different technological, social and economic interests while leaving space for diverse practices. The paper contributes a first focused analysis of Maker Faire, probes it as a site for research and discusses how holistic perspectives on the Maker Movement could create new research opportunities.

1 INTRODUCTION

The growing availability of Do-It-Yourself (DIY) fabrication technologies has not only enabled designers to rapidly create and test physical prototypes, it has also spotlighted a new technology user group to study – the ’makers’ [ 2 ]. As Human Computer Interaction (HCI) has developed a research interest in the Maker Movement, previous work has often looked at it as a vibrant subculture of DIY-tech pioneers, fascinated by their creativity [e.g. 52 , 58 ]. Many potentials have been highlighted [e.g. 23 , 44 , 52 ]. Simultaneously, several critical voices have drawn attention to issues around the lack of demographic diversity among makers [ 10 , 54 ], practices of commodification [ 5 ], and a sense of technosolutionism [ 26 ]. Each of these accounts discuss important characteristics of the Maker Movement. Yet altogether, they have also produced a range of rather fragmented portrayals of the same sociotechnical construct. Given that the Maker Movement is evolving within a nexus of technological, social and economic interests, we argue that it is time to interconnect these fragments and develop more holistic accounts. In particular, we believe it is important to set both the idealised and criticised aspects of the Maker Movement into relation with each other and to acknowledge the multitude of skill sets, interests and agendas as well as the complex diversity of individuals, groups and organisations involved in Making.

This paper aims to make a start at putting together some of the puzzle pieces from the literature and public discourse by studying them in action. It describes a qualitative study of two Maker Faires in different countries that captured the voices of various stakeholders and highlights the respective agendas of exhibiting makers, event organisers and visitors and how these relate to each other. Besides offering a first analysis of diverse agendas and the multitude of reasons to engage in Maker Faire, the paper also contributes to previous CHI-work by probing Maker Faire as an interesting yet challenging site for HCI research. By illustrating the complex dynamics between individual agency and aggregated structures that makers (or those wanting to become makers) operate in, our intention is to provide a tangible example of developing more holistic perspectives and to discuss new opportunities for research on maker cultures.

2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK

The Maker Movement refers to a trend intertwining a general DIY ethos with technologies for prototyping and small-scale fabrication. It is a branded term with its origin connected to MAKE magazine [ 29 ] (published by O'Reilly media since 2005). Calling itself the ’bible for makers’ , it takes pride in having named the community as ”makers”[ 30 ]. In 2006 the Make Media corporation started organising Maker Faire, describing it as ’the Greatest Show on Earth, an incredible celebration of makers and a showcase of innovative-in-the making. Maker Faire is the best evidence of the power and reach of the Maker Movement – all around the globe.’ [ 30 ] According to their published data more than 240 Maker Faires took place around the world in 2017.

2.1 Different Interpretations of the Maker Movement

Just as O'Reilly Media has a traditional focus on technology, MAKE magazine has also been very focused on tools and has maintained close connections to the tech industry. MAKE readers will most likely think of pocket-size computers, electronics toolkits, 3D printers and laser cutters when referring to maker technologies. Yet, there is also space for less fashionable tools such as sewing machines, soldering irons and anything else that helps ’lay people’ to create their own ’stuff’. The DIY-spirit of the Maker Movement is not new. Gauntlett for instance revisited its long history exemplified in the Arts & Crafts Movement, the 1960s alternative culture around the Whole Earth Catalog and the 1990s lo-fi punk DIY zine culture [ 17 ]. What is new in the Maker Movement for the DIY-minded individual is the range of tools available that involve both traditional and high-tech ones.

Despite its focus on the high-tech side of things, Make Media's slogan is ’All of Us Are Makers’ which implies a social mission around community building: ’Tinkerers, educators, parents, and professionals are included, because we are all Makers. [...] As people learn to develop projects, they become innovators, makers of change. We exist to help more people participate so that they can acquire the tools and expertise necessary to make their ideas become real’ [ 30 ]. Such a mission statement could be interpreted in many ways including seeing great sociotechnical potential in building a platform for makers, smart marketing for an emerging consumer market or expressing internalised technosolutionism.

What Make Media has initiated with strong ties to their brand, has further evolved at the intersection of economic, technological and social interests of a growing number of stakeholders – including those from industry [ 31 ], politics [ 9 ] and research institutions [ 19 ]. The public discourse surrounding the Maker Movement has thus received it either euphorically or sceptically, being mixed between celebrating it as a ’new industrial revolution’ [ 4 , 12 ], interpreting it as yet another arena for a gendered valorisation of practices [ 8 ] or doubting its capacity for disruption [ 24 , 35 ]. Likewise, HCI research around the Maker Movement has been similarly split into enthusiastic and sceptical camps [ 26 ] with some authors highlighting potentials [e.g. 1 , 22 , 23 , 34 , 36 , 52 ] and others expressing critical concern over the naïve idea of guaranteed empowerment through Making [eg. 2 , 3 , 14 , 32 , 44 , 47 , 55 , 56 ].

2.2 Maker Faire in the HCI Literature

While reviewing HCI-related publications about the Maker Movement in the ACM Digital Library, we identified 25 papers mentioning the keyword ’Maker Faire’ [ 5 , 6 , 11 , 13 , 14 , 20 , 21 , 26 , 27 , 32 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 46 , 48 , 49 , 51 , 52 , 57 , 58 , 60 ]. Despite this body of work being limited (and not systematically collated), it gives an impression of how HCI researchers perceive Maker Faire and which roles they give it in their work.

2.2.1 Descriptions of Maker Faire. In the collated literature, Maker Faire is described as a ’large science and engineering fair’ [ 6 ] or a ’public exhibition of designs’ [ 42 ] which were created in makerspaces [ 41 ]. Some authors characterized it specifically as a family event [ 38 ] or highlighted its branded origin as ’an extension of the MAKE and CRAFT magazine community’ [ 11 ], while others depicted it more as a celebration to grow the popularity of DIY/Making [ 13 , 21 , 32 , 58 , 60 ]. In general, Maker Faire is thought to be the most ’popular avenue’ of DIY/Making [ 51 ] (besides makerspaces). Its scale [ 6 , 13 ] and capacity for networking [ 48 , 49 , 52 , 57 ] seem to make it a good platform for makers to ’show off’ [ 41 , 51 , 52 ], present their work [ 27 , 39 ] and promote their agendas [ 14 ].

None of this literature is specifically about analysing a Maker Faire event or its audience. Apart from a study with teachers dealing with a School Maker Faire as something that can be designed in itself [ 38 ] and a brief insight into Vancouver's maker community connecting via the local Mini Faire [ 49 ], none of the papers placed any particular focus on it and most referred to Maker Faire only in a casual mention. Most authors tended to simply use Maker Faire as an operational component in their research or referred to it as part of their argumentation.

2.2.2 Using Maker Faire as Part of the Research. We identified four different ways in which Maker Faire was used as an operational part of research. These comprised using it as a site for (i) experimenting with research ideas, activities, or designs [ 20 , 39 , 48 ], (ii) recruiting suitable participants for a study [ 20 , 39 , 48 , 57 ], (iii) disseminating project outcomes [ 11 , 20 , 37 , 48 ], or (iv) conducting fieldwork and collecting data [ 27 , 38 , 39 , 49 , 52 ]. In some papers referring to Maker Faire as a site where a study was conducted, it was not entirely clear how the event was used. In some cases, it seemed that attending or volunteering at a Maker Faire was a way to sensitize the researcher for the specific dynamic of the local maker culture, yet this was not much reflected upon.

2.2.3 Argumentations based on Maker Faire. In terms of using Maker Faire for purposes of argumentation, references were often made for giving visual examples that would illustrate and support the respective statement the authors were trying to make. Lindtner et al. [ 26 ] for instance outlined the emergence of maker-related events in China and how the Maker Faire 2014 tagline ”Innovate with China” was representative of attempts to ’reframe Chinese manufacturing in the global imaginary’ . Such illustrative examples could relate to observations made at Maker Faire events or in the media. Commenting on these observations could then support either claims of impact (eg. Maker Faire helping to create more demand in schools for project-based learning around STEM [ 6 ]) or critique (eg. Maker Faire's controversial decision to accept funding from DARPA [ 52 ]). In any case, the practice of using Maker Faire as an example indicates the underlying assumption that it is representative for the general discourse surrounding the wider Maker Movement. Sun et al. [ 51 ] criticised the commercialisation of the Maker Movement by mentioning the sponsorship of Maker Faire as an example of how corporations and industry participate in and shape Making culture. Other authors critiqued that the Maker Movement lacks diversity and that it reifies privilege through stating that Maker Faire is attracting a homogenous audience [ 40 ], is designed to appeal to white men and the wealthy [ 21 ], and thus is made by Maker Faire officials for people just like themselves [ 5 ].

2.3 Fragmented Portrayals

Based on the insights that Maker Faire is already used as a site for HCI research activities and argumentation, we argue that there is a need for specific critical reflection on Maker Faire. Not doing so comes at a risk. As Lindtner et al. state: ’When we understand the projects and materials that appear in MAKE Magazine or at Maker Faires as exemplars of making, this is not an innocent description’ [ 26 , p.1399]. In this case, it led the authors to apply a post-colonial lens on the Maker Movement and consequently present maker cultures in Taiwan and China as a counter-perspective to dominant Western accounts. This is however again just one way of critically reflecting upon representations of the Maker Movement. While their focus is chosen for good reasons, it remains limited and provides another partial portrayal of the Maker Movement. More generally, we see a tendency in the literature to produce or incorporate such partial notions rather than trying to set them into relation with each other. We argue that such a practice is problematic because the Maker Movement emerged at the intersection of manifold technological, economic and social interests. There is still a need for developing a more holistic understanding that interconnects the different fragments. We suggest that a nuanced elaboration of Maker Faire and the dynamics between different participating stakeholders could make a good start for the endeavour of exploring the wider Maker Movement's structures more holistically.

3 METHOD

In alignment with literature seeing Maker Faire as one popular platform for the Maker Movement, we conducted a study of two Maker Faire events with the intention of developing a wider account of maker cultures as a sociotechnical construct and to highlight the complexity within which people engage in Making. Our particular inquiry focus was placed on the different kinds of people participating in such events. To spotlight the particular dynamics between them, we chose an inductive qualitative study approach and a set of research methods to capture the multiplicity of perspectives.

3.1 Maker Faire as a Site of Inquiry

We chose to run our study at Maker Faire because we understand it to be one platform for people to participate in the wider Maker Movement and because Maker Faire events are to our knowledge less studied than makerspaces [e.g. 45 , 54 , 55 ]. We assumed that the event character of Maker Faire would attract a wider audience than a single makerspace, involving not only makers but also other stakeholders such as companies producing maker tools or people who are not yet makers but interested to become so. It was important to us to see a picture of the event as full as possible including all the hobbyist enthusiasm, corporate interests and flaws of exclusion we had learned about in the literature.

Our study focused on two Maker Faire events that took place in different European cities in late spring 2018. All research data was collected directly at or online in relation to the events. Maker Faire UK (MFU) was organised at the Science Museum of an English city for the 9 th year in a row. Maker Faire Vienna (MFV) was held in a refurbished factory hall in the outskirts of the Austrian capital, organised by a group of people associated with the biggest local makerspace and an independent computing research center. MFV is less established than MFU taking place only for the 3 rd year, yet in terms of exhibitors listed in the program it was double the size and attracted 12,000 visitors over the weekend.

The visitor number is only an estimate reported by the organisers based on ticket sales and we are aware that no demographic data on visitors and exhibitors have been collected and assessed in any systematic way. Our study does not try to compensate this lack of such demographic data, rather we try to elicit rich qualitative insights that allow us to set different experiences in relation to each other. Being aware of the fragmented portrayals of the Maker Movement in the HCI literature and public discourse, we were curious how these characteristics would be reflected at the Maker Faire events. We chose an inductive research approach to explore how the idealisms and criticisms would come into action. So rather than narrowing down on a single research question, our study was guided by a series of interests such as: Who is interested in Maker Faire and in which ways do they participate (or not)? What agendas do they have for participating (or not)? What is important to whom - and for which reasons? How do these perspectives relate to each other (or not)? Specifically, we were interested if the organisers were actually catering for the needs of exhibitors and visitors, if the exhibitors would show what visitors were interested in, and if the visitors would come with an aspiration to be/become makers themselves and how this would affect their interactions with exhibitors and organisers.

3.2 Data Collection

Our study involved direct participation in both Maker Faires which helped us to collect data, sensitise our analysis and shape our understanding of the results. This is of importance given that our qualitative inquiry takes a constructivist approach which is concerned with demonstrating validity through the themes emerging from the data rather than through the quantity of unbiased data. The first and second author participated at both Maker Faire events by organising a maker stall that was part exhibition and part hands-on activity. They were assisted by six more individuals helping to present the exhibition and to facilitate the hands-on activity.

The exhibition showcased some outcomes of our previous or ongoing collaborations with different community groups as part of a long-term participatory action research project. These comprised (i) a touch-interactive quilt that was created with a charity supporting sex workers [ 32 , 50 ], (ii) 3D-printed assistive gadgets for power-wheelchairs designed by makers with disabilities and (iii) mixed-media objects created in a local Men Shed (a community workshop for men of all ages). We invited our collaborators to present their projects at the Maker Faire events themselves. None of them had previously attended a Maker Faire. Yet, three individuals perceived taking part as exhibitors being an exciting opportunity to present their skills and ideas to the public. Furthermore, in the participatory action research spirit of our previous interactions positioning us all as co-researchers, the exhibiting participants also served as informants and provided valuable reflections from their participant observations.

We also designed a hands-on activity about crafting electronics. This activity involved creating an electronic button (based on a switch or capacitive touch sensor design) from a wide range of tinkering materials we provided for free. This let us invite the Maker Faire audience to engage with us in an active way. After building the button the participants had the chance to test their creations with a Raspberry Pi powered ’Maker Guestbook’ (see bottom right of figure 1 ), an interactive documentation system. To take part in our study visitors would choose a card with a question printed on, write down an answer, connect their self-built button to the Maker Guestbook and press it to take a photo of everything. We could have chosen a more light-weight procedure for surveying but in this way the activity became a creative research method [ 16 , 18 ] where participants could reflect longer on their answers and they were rewarded for their effort with a (hopefully inspiring) present to take away. We included 30 questions of three different categories regarding (i) the participants’ maker identities, values, aspirations and purposes (eg. What difference has Making made for you?), (ii) the support they needed for pursuing their Making (eg. Imagine your worst making nightmare. What does it look like?), and (iii) their reflections on Maker Faire itself (eg. What have you learned from attending Maker Faire?). The question cards were colour-coded accordingly. Participants could choose a question out of the 30 options and many had a browse through them before deciding which one they felt most comfortable to answer.

The data collection went beyond just the visitors’ reflections and our own participant observations as exhibitors. It also consisted of many additional written, audio-recorded or photographic accounts produced before, during or after the Maker Faire events. The official event websites and social media accounts provided information about how the organisers framed their event and which kinds of maker projects were showcased. The descriptions of the exhibitor stalls in the program were written by the participating makers themselves and shed light on their respective practices and agendas. Accounts from professional and amateur media complemented our collection of different first-hand voices. These included reports for public service TV or Make Magazine, interviews recorded for community radio and reviews in form of blogposts and podcasts. Our own participant observations as exhibitors were captured in form of written reflections and audio-recorded group discussions – either way this was guided by a set of prompts involving questions such as ’What kind of people did you talk to mostly?’ or ’How did our stall fit into Maker Faire?’. Four exhibitors (out of our group of eight people) were able to attend both events and to compare them. The other exhibitors reflected on their experiences with the one event they took part in.

3.3 Analysis

Using spreadsheets and text editing software, we processed the collected data in the same way for both events to assure the sets were as consistent and comparable as possible. In this way, the analysis could tease out differences specific to the local context. Furthermore, the data were collected with the intention to capture the voices of different people being involved in the same events. For the purposes of the paper, we categorised people as visitors, exhibitors or organisers. As we highlight throughout the paper though, each of these superficially specified groups represent the aggregation of highly diverse individuals with different interests to take part in a Maker Faire while sharing the same ’role’.

We used inductive thematic analysis [ 7 ] to dissect each data set and used constant comparison triangulating the emerging themes across the data which enabled us to highlight the particular dynamic between the different perspectives. The first author started with open coding on the transcribed answer cards from the visitors (for both events), creating a first set of themes which we grouped into categories. The same was done with the data collected for the organisers and exhibitors, with new codes however refining and extending previously identified themes. Eventually, by continuing to compare the themes, we reviewed the categories and re-grouped them if these reflected aspects of the literature. The results of the analysis provided an overview of the different agendas of those who came together at the same Maker Faire events and how these related to each other.

4 FINDINGS: STAKEHOLDER VOICES

This section is dedicated to highlight some important findings in relation to the different stakeholders. Table 1 provides an overview on the data sets we used to capture the voices of Maker Faire visitors, event organisers and exhibiting makers.

4.1 Organisers

To capture the voices of the Maker Faire event organisers we collected texts from the official event websites and social media appearances, hence data embodying the ways in which they like to present Maker Faire to the public.

4.1.1 Who organises Maker Faire? Both Maker Faires had a professional online presence with websites and social media accounts containing much information, yet it was not easy to find out who exactly the organisers were. In the case of MFU the general contact email address indicates a strong affiliation with the museum venue that hosted the event. This suggests that organising a Maker Faire could be used as a strategy to sell more museum tickets than on a usual day. On the other hand, the MFV organisers were operating independently from the venue (having switched location after the first year). In an interview with an organising member of the MFV team, the event was referred as a large collaboration between many different parties: ’Innoc [an independent research center] and Happy Lab [Austrian fablab] collaborate a lot with the founders of Maker Faire. Maker Faire originates actually from the USA and was initiated by the publishers of Make Magazine. We work together with the German edition of Maker Faire.’ [MFV, media] While identifying certain specific groups this answer still leaves much room for interpretation about their respective motivations for organising such an event. Given that organisers sell tickets for their event, it is safe to assume that there is a certain interest in making profit, yet other additional motivations are also likely. For instance, it could be a prestigious opportunity for leaving a mark in the local maker community, networking and building social capital for the own institution.

4.1.2 Being part of a Global Franchise. Maker Faire is a brand and a franchise. Event organisers need to apply and pay for a licence which in return lets them use Maker Faire logos and visual identity [ 28 ]. Both MFU and MFV did so and used the branding, yet with their own tweaks to it. MFV for example inserted the silhouettes of two famous landmarks into their logo. In both cases, there was strong emphasis on the local context and how Maker Faire fits into it. MFU for example introduced the event venue as ’an award-winning visitor attraction, welcoming around 250,000 visitors annually. [Its] public engagement programme attracts a broad audience for exhibitions and special events, and the education team deliver the biggest schools’ science workshop programme in any European museum or science centre. [Its] mission is to be the most innovative place in the world for uniting people who are passionate about STEM. All this makes it the perfect venue for Maker Faire UK.’ [MFU]

There was a noticeable effort to explain for the local audience what Making is about. This seems to be of even more importance in countries where languages other than English are spoken, as becomes visible in MFV's introduction: ’Maker Faire has its roots in the USA. Americans call it The Greatest Show (& Tell) on Earth, which means that Maker Faire is an inventor's exhibition, a fun fair and at the same time something completely new.’ [MFV] What might seem more obvious for those with English as their native tongue seems to do here better with a little ’translation’ for a German-speaking audience and helps to establish the event as ’Austria's biggest Do-It-Yourself-Festival’ [MFV].

Using the branded franchise gives an international vibe to the local instantiations of Maker Faire: ’Hundreds of Makers from around the world will be heading to [MFU] later this month for its annual festival of crafting, coding and inventing that is fun for all of the family.’ [MFU] – ’At the beginning of May, Vienna will again become the maker capital of Europe.’ [MFV] At the same time, it seems to be equally important to the organisers to highlight what is unique about the local maker scene and to promote how well local makers fit into their event. For example, organisers announced some official highlights including ’Viennese start-ups present their innovative products at [MFV] 2018’ [MFV] or ’local craft groups, beautifully textured and scented soaps created by Durham-based The Blind Soap Maker, a glass blower, a chainsaw carver and a local Dalek builder.’ [MFU]

4.1.3 Fostering General Positive Connotations. Despite the efforts to translate Maker Faire into an event format relevant to the local context, there is a general absence of a clear definition what Making exactly is. According to the organisers’ marketing Maker Faire seems to be somewhat about science and tech - yet not only: ’It's really great fun for adults and kids alike, with all sorts of different things to try your hand at from traditional crafts to hi-tech hacks.’ [MFU] – ’The festival is dealing with current topics at the interface of new technologies, science, crafting, art & design’ [MFV]. Instead of coming up with a clear definition, both Maker Faires tended to emphasise general positive connotations they associate with the Maker Movement such as being fun, creative, humorous, innovative, exciting, educational and useful: ’The atmosphere is one of collaboration, sharing and experimenting, where you'll try new things, deepen existing knowledge and have loads of fun! Be prepared to be inspired.’ [MFU] – ’It's a family-friendly festival for innovation, creativity and technology. Makers are resourceful DIY-enthusiasts, creative minds, unconventional thinkers or technology experts who enjoy tinkering and can be found in all age groups.’ [MFV]

Concentrating on celebrating the general positive aspects about Making (whatever these imply) seems to be a strategy both for staying inclusive for the many different types of exhibitors (no matter how ’mainstream’ or niche they like to be) and for ensuring that there will be something interesting in the program for every visitor (no matter how special-interest they are). As such, it constitutes a practice that connects the social mission of widening participation with marketing and the commercial interests of organising a profitable large-scale event.

4.2 Visitors

Visitors filled out question cards while taking part in our hands-on activity. We received a total of 140 answers and even though many answers were brief or in note form, they provided rich insights on the participants’ experiences of Maker Faire as well as their aspirations on how their attendance could in some way forward their personal maker activities (as the vast majority of the participants did self-identify as makers in some way).

4.2.1 Who visits Maker Faire? Both MFU and MFV were large-scale events with thousands of visitors over the course of a weekend. We do not have any exact demographic information on the people who attended but through our own participation hosting a stall we had the chance to interact with a fraction of them. Our impressions of the audience (as captured in the reflection part of the data) included ’interested and curious folk of all ages’ [MFU] and ’kids and people who would buy 3D printers’ [MFU] and ’a mature and diverse audience’ [MFV]. In the course of the two Maker Faire weekends we interacted with individuals, couples and families (kids, parents, and grandparents). In our conversations many of them stated being creative hobbyists or technology professionals, while others outed themselves as researchers, event organisers, teachers/educators, political activists, charity service workers or just someone who finally wanted to see 3D printers in real life. They seemed to have many different reasons for being there, some for work and some for pleasure. Yet in aggregation, three over-arching themes emerged from their answer cards:

4.2.2 Seeking Inspiration. Many visitors stated that they attend Maker Faire looking for some kind of inspiration. Aligning with the organisers’ practice of keeping the definition of Making deliberately open, the visitors came generally open-minded hoping to learn about ’a lot of different places for making’ [MFU] and that they would have the chance to ’learn new things’ [MFU]. For them it was important that Maker Faire would provide them with opportunities to experience many different facets of Making in terms of skills, knowledges, materials and projects. A visitor stated that they had ’found this [hands-on activity] in electronics making really helpful + inspiring :)’ [MFV] which was most likely connected to the experience of learning that ’[y]ou can make all sorts of things using many different materials, even ones you would never expect’ [MFU]. Such answers might be related to the way we designed the hands-on activity using a variety of crafts materials, yet the importance of inspiration through unexpected forms of making was highlighted in relation to other exhibitors, too. A visitor refers to a stall about ’baking cuneiform clay’ as a personal highlight because it was ’showing how writing has been done and potentially making people interested in learning new languages and scripts’ [MFU]. Apart from general curiosity, many participants seemed to look for specific inspiration for new projects. This could be ’to find an idea for the BT-printer’ [MFV], ’inspiration for my job’ [MFU], or ’ideas & inspirations for DIY projects’ [MFV]. In this respect, a lack of inspiration could eventually turn into a real problem: ’I'd like to build electronic devices, so for me the challenge is inspiration’ [MFU]

4.2.3 Barriers to Engaging in Making. Visitors seemed to find it difficult to reflect on what they found challenging about making. We received the least answers in relation to the support people needed in their making (23 answers, representing 16.4%), yet these were often very honest and even more notable given the general celebratory atmosphere of Maker Faire which could inhibit critical thoughts. Apart from lacking inspiration, many answers referred to the fact that Making is a resource-intensive endeavour requiring tools, materials, space, skills, time and effort: ’I would like to learn programming, but I lack the time’ [MFV] - ’Private space [would make a major difference]. Materials and consumables available NOW. Nothing is more off-putting than next-day delivery’ [MFU] - ’Time Space Mentors [are challenges for me to becoming a maker]’ [MFU]. The important role of guidance in the form of mentors or facilitators was particularly emphasised, stating that they would need ’experts sharing their knowledge, experience and feedback’ [MFU], ’people who are there for me and support my ideas’ [MFV] or a ’welcoming makerspace’ [MFU]. At Maker Faire this wish for mentorship had obvious implications for the design of hands-on activities and workshops taking place during the event. Facilitators should be patient and not assume any previous knowledge. Despite the general dogma that ’everyone is a maker’, not everyone ’felt like one’ [MFU]. Interestingly however, these people seemed to blame ’[their own] LACK OF SKILLS & UNDERSTANDING’ [MFV] rather than external factors. One visitor even seemed to have given up on engaging in Making oneself and said to attend Maker Faire because of ’[meeting] people who do things that I am interested in but lack the expertise/knowledge/confidence to take part in’ [MFU].

4.2.4 Constructing Personal Maker Biographies. If visitors identified however as makers, it seemed to be easy for them to reflect on their own maker practice. In many cases the visitors would frame their personal maker identity around their activities. This could involve relating to a specific kind of making (eg. ’I really enjoy electronics and woodwork’ [MFU]), or just listing different things they had made for themselves or for others, such as ’school bags out of card, fabric, creative foam | Sewing | electronic switch | school courses | presents in school’ [MFV]. Occasionally they mentioned a specific task to give more nuance to their interests: ’I am interested in physical programming and making new things out of old things.’ [MFU] - ’I like making things I can play with. I like designing things in my own way.’ [MFU] Many different reasons were mentioned for their engagement in making. No matter if these were the joy of ’making it yourself with your own hands’ [MFV], seeking ’new challenges’ [MFU], aspiring to ’become an Architect’ [MFU], or enjoying the ’independence’ [MFV] because ’[i]t allowed me to stop being a sheep inside a box of rules someone else made’ [MFU], having a specific reason seemed to constitute a significant if not defining factor in their practice. Moreover, it was emphasized that making was not only for themselves but a social activity by referring to the roles friends and family were playing, for example making ’blankets & scarves for friends & family’ [MFU] or imagining a perfect day of making to ’start with a friend who is practical and skilled at fabrication’ [MFU]. Yet occasionally, the allegedly only reason for attending Maker Faire was ’because Mum wanted me to’ [MFV].

4.3 Exhibiting Makers

While the MFU website stated its aim was to bring ’together over 300 hackers, crafters, coders, DIYers and garden shed inventors from across the globe’ [MFU], MFV announced ’900 makers show[ing] their innovative ideas’ [MFV]. We counted 121 entries in the MFU program and 218 in the MFV program. The exhibitor voices in our data included the description texts of these 339 program entries (which the exhibitors had written themselves) and our own reflections as exhibitors.

4.3.1 Who exhibits at Maker Faire? Both Maker Faire program booklets featured many different types of makers. The exhibitors labelled themselves as ’felt artists’ [MFU], ’textile designers’ [MFV], ’freelance sculptors’ [MFU], ’hardware designers & manufacturers’ [MFV] or ’an eclectic group of makers, creatives, programmers, scientists and engineers’ [MFU]. While most makers seemed to be specialised on a subset of skills, altogether they brought a huge diversity of maker skills into the event. Some makers exhibited their projects individually, whereas others represented larger groups (eg. makerspaces), institutions (eg. universities), or companies (eg. retailers of maker equipment). We noticed that both Maker Faires featured the full bandwidth of amateurs, non-profit organisations and large-scale companies. While organisers tended to describe makers as ’resourceful DIY-enthusiasts’ [MFV] or ’garden shed inventors’ [MFU] in their promotion and thereby implied rather passionate hobbyists than professionals, one of our exhibitors mentioned in her reflection that she ’was surprised about the amount of things exhibited to sell products’ [MFU]. This observation resonates with the literature, where Making has often been portrayed as an endeavour of hobbyists [ 4 , 23 , 33 ], while the Maker Movement has also been described forming a market, been celebrated as a think-tank for potential start-ups and sometimes criticised for the infiltration of commercial interests [ 5 , 15 ].

4.3.2 Commercial and Non-Commercial Makers. At Maker Faire commercial and non-commercial interests often merged and during the analysis of the description texts it was not always easy to categorise the exhibitors in terms of their financial interests. For example, an artist ran a free hands-on activity but also sold his own work: ’[E]xplore ancient writing techniques and relief printmaking in a free, hands-on arts workshop. [...] Works inspired by the subject matter, will be on display and sale alongside this workshop’ [MFU]. Sometimes it was not explicitly stated if the works would be for sale: ’VikiBuNNy is a manga artist. Her motives are colorful and cute. She will provide acrylic charms, prints and original artwork’ [MFV]. Several companies were also present for promoting products such as ’monthly design engineering kits for kids’ [MFU], 3D-printers of the ’new model with more features’ [MFV] or even specialised software for ’managing a community workshop’ [MFV]. At Maker Faire these commercial entities were often offering ’free training courses’ [MFV] or ’running free drop-in coding and building workshops, where you can learn to build and program their robots, with no previous experience needed’ [MFU]. Simultaneously, there were also hobbyist makers sharing their skills in leathercraft [MFU] or glass making [MFU] workshops but taking a small additional charge for these which can be assumed to cover the costs of the provided materials.

4.3.3 Hierarchies of Expertise. While there was a huge variety of maker types with diverse financial interests, there was a strong sense in the description texts that exhibiting makers are highly skilled experts: ’Experts from Axminster Tools & Machinery will be giving practical demonstrations of some of their latest kit’ [MFU]. While expressed in a friendly and approachable way, highlighting expertise implies that makers distinguish themselves from the general audience. For example, announcing to ’[talk] visitors through their creations, and how they go from hand sketches, to the finished product’ [MFU] or to ask them to ’let our team show you the latest trends in 3D printing and robotics’ [MFV] indicates the assumption that visitors are passive non-experts. This might be true in some cases, yet not always given the diversity among visitors. While hierarchies based on skills, expertise and mastery are to some extent traditional to craftsmanship, reinforcing a distinction between ’real’ makers and newcomers might be counter-productive to the dogma of ’everyone is a maker’ – especially if people already lack confidence in their own skills as stated by some visitors.

5 FINDINGS: CO-CREATING MAKER FAIRE

Our findings pointed to some differences in the stakeholders’ motivations and needs. We observed diverse interests, such as those in successful event management (organisers), showcasing skills, projects or products to a wide audience (exhibitors) and gaining useful information or inspiration for developing an own maker biography (visitors). To understand the complex dynamics between them, we also need to reflect on how these stakeholders relate to each other.

5.1 Interrelations between Stakeholders

Maker Faire is a symbiosis of organisers, exhibitors and visitors. As stakeholders relate to each other through their respective interests, some of these interrelations are straight-forward: Visitors pay for tickets and thereby contribute to the success of the event (measured by the organisers in visitor numbers and ticket sales). Organisers create a varied and interesting event program for the visitors and rely on a large number of makers volunteering to provide suitable content to fill this program. If accepted by the organisers, makers are provided a platform to present their projects, products or initiatives to a large audience. While visitors can browse through the event content getting inspired by their interactions with exhibitors and potentially even active as makers themselves, these interactions are also beneficial to the exhibitors who – depending on their respective agenda – can use these for example for gaining recognition, selling products or recruiting members for their initiatives.

Altogether, these interrelations illustrate how the event is co-created by organisers, visitors and makers at the nexus of different technological, social and economic interests while leaving space for diverse practices. Without the participation of all stakeholder groups, the event would not be possible, but together they foster a temporary celebratory culture and create a sociotechnical space around an open notion of Making. This co-creation comes in a specific form: Operating in informal ways, it involves momentary co-existence rather than long-term collaboration (neither within nor across stakeholder groups). People have many different reasons and ways to participate in Maker Faire and all these interests aggregate to a structure that affords participation in goal-oriented but non-committal ways: Visitors can learn about different makerspaces without becoming members. Makers can test different target groups for their product ideas without setting up a start-up. The stakeholders do not need to engage with each other, there is only the general opportunity to find like-minded individuals or groups and use the platform for one's own purposes.

Beyond the straight-forward interrelations there are several nuances shaping the co-created sociotechnical space. In the following we describe how they constitute mechanisms that hinder or support certain interactions between stakeholders or the development of individual maker biographies.

5.2 Synthesis of a Shared (Branded) Label

Maker Faire is about presenting a huge variety of knowledge and skill ’ at the interface of new technologies, science, crafting, art & design’ [MFV organisers] and highlighting that ’there [are] a lot of different places for making’ [MFU visitor]. While keeping the definition of Making deliberately open, the event still serves the purpose of collectively synthesising a shared label for a diverse range of creative activities. On the scale that Maker Faire operates, it brings together a bit of everything: high-tech, specialist crafts and lo-fi DIY/hacking; hobbyists and professionals; extreme exemplars of euphoric idealism and commodification as well as projects operating somewhere in between. Through coming together and having a browse through what other people are making, the event attendees are part of a process that gives a specific feel for this open notion of Making. This synthesis is of particular relevance in three ways:

(i) It constitutes a tangible opportunity for visitors who are newcomers to connect with the Maker Movement. Especially for people who otherwise would not know about Making or the existence of makerspaces, Maker Faire can be the key means of raising awareness and providing them access.

(ii) Collectively synthesizing a label for DIY activities across skills, groups and regions creates opportunities for ’community-building’ among makers. By temporarily uniting under the label, they can connect with like-minded, network and generate ideas for collaborations beyond Maker Faire.

(iii) Maker Faire is a brand whether participating actors subscribe to the international Maker Media franchise or not, the whole process of label synthesis takes place within circumstances that are shaped by the brand. Most of all, it affects the event organisers as it forces them to professionalise their endeavour and to make it profitable. Beyond that, the branded nature of the event might also impact who considers participating or not. For instance, it is unlikely to meet people at Maker Faire who boycott the brand.

5.3 Agency within Aggregated Structures

Maker Faire aggregates different interests of the participating stakeholders and shapes structures around them. As interests can align or diverge, the aggregated structures are dynamic and under constant negotiation. Some (more or less) tacit tensions can arise: hi-tech versus crafts, entrepreneurs versus hobbyists, technological innovation versus the passion of making something with the hands, expert makers versus non-experts who just have a general interest in Making. Given that stakeholders participate in goal-oriented and non-committal ways, Maker Faire can resemble a marketplace where expert-makers show what they have to offer to a rather passive non-expert audience. This contradicts the values celebrated around the Maker Movement which would suggest framing the event more as something that is creating a democratic space for everyone to get active, share and ultimately create positive change for society and the environment. Another tension arises around the dogma of ’Everyone is a Maker’ which glosses over the fact that Making is a resource-intensive activity where people rely on the availability of tools, materials, skills and time. This material dependency creates certain barriers that depending on their different life-situations are easier or more desirable for some people to overcome than for others.

There is however also space for individual agency within these aggregated structures both for makers and those wanting to become makers. The construction of a personal maker biography is an active and social process. A maker identity is framed not only through the own practice of making but also by choosing reasons for engaging in it and by experiences of challenges (for which ’meeting like-minded + inspirational people + projects’ [MFU] can be a real game-changer!). With the definition of Making being kept open, people can choose (or literally buy into) one that is provided (eg. by Make Media, a local makerspace or companies producing tools for makers), create their own one or also keep it deliberately open. No matter which way they choose, this insight places emphasis on an active practice of these makers in constructing their personal Maker Biographies. Even if they just ’buy into’ a given definition of Making they first need to engage and decide to identify with it. As Making can imply many different skillsets and agendas, there is more space for personal interpretation and shaping an individual maker biography.

5.4 Diversity and Diversity Issues

There is much diversity in respect to the different kinds of people, their ideas and reasons for attending Maker Faire – yet there are still diversity issues in terms of who is expected to be a maker and who not. With two of our own exhibitors identifying as being disabled, we made some interesting observations in the context of disability. Apart from them only one other exhibiting maker stated having a disability in the program: ’Meet The Blind Soapmaker and see their hand-crafted range of luxury solid and liquid soaps, and bath bombs too’ [MFU maker]. A newspaper article took the opportunity to promote Maker Faire by telling the ’uplifting story’ behind her participation. Compared to the maker's self-description which focuses on the quality of her products rather than on her disability, the article picked her health history as a sensationalised central theme. It is interesting however how the maker is quoted to identify a particular potential in making for people with disabilities and that she was actually invited by the organisers to participate: ’I was pleased to be approached by [MFU] to take part as it's a great showcase for my products at a time when my business is starting to grow. Only one in 10 blind people in the UK are employed in the workplace, so self-employment is a great practical solution for me to build a career.’ [MFU media]

Apart from this one case, however, we noticed only instances where exhibiting makers dealt with issues related to disability in ways that reflected an internalised spirit of technosolutionism. Rather than featuring people with disabilities as makers themselves, the related projects at Maker Faire involved able-bodied makers ’trying to build solutions based on biofeedback on brain activities as a tool for helping disabled people’ [MFV maker], a charity ’actively looking for volunteer makers who could help [disabled people] with their next creations’ [MFU maker], and able-bodied makerspace members using open-source hardware and software to build a power wheelchair (at Maker Faire they showed how quick it could go by driving it around themselves). Our intention is not to criticise these projects and we sincerely appreciate the efforts of the exhibitors to make a difference, yet we want to draw attention to the problematic fact that by showcasing only such approaches at Maker Faire people with disabilities are still othered and treated as those who need to be helped. The dogma of ’everyone is a maker’ does not seem to equally apply for them.

6 DISCUSSION

Given the limited space of this paper we could only present few exemplary themes emerging from our findings, yet we want to stress that the identified nuances can be used to tease out the complex dynamic inherent to maker cultures that integrate a multitude of co-existing technological, social and economic interests.

The findings pointed our attention to how Maker Faire had two important sides to it: On the one hand, it was something that was actively designed, curated, shaped and promoted. On the other hand, it was highly dependent on who was actually participating and for which reasons. This highlighted a complex dynamic between a top-down structure and its bottom-up use and how both were results of the same aggregation of organisers, exhibitors, and visitors. Having outlined this aggregated structure, we now discuss its implications for HCI research and how such a wider understanding can potentially be productive.

6.1 Opportunities for Constructions and Curations

The co-creation of Maker Faire holds specific opportunities for makers and those who want to become makers. As people actively construct their maker biographies within a dynamically evolving maker scene, it raises the importance of their knowledge of different opportunities relevant and available to them. Maker Faire caters specifically for developing such knowledge and thereby promotes the active construction of personal maker biographies. This holds particular empowering potential for newcomers by strengthening their confidence in that they do not need to conform with any externally provided definitions of Making. Depending on their resources, they could be encouraged to just start out with their projects on their own terms or to even start new maker communities (which could again create new chances to connect with like-minded people).

Depending on who decides to exhibit, Maker Faire's representation of the maker scene can be flawed and incomplete. While indeed showing a diverse set of skills, there are prevalent issues with other kinds of diversity and inclusion. To some extent, this is related to the curation of the event and one possible approach to improving the situation would involve the organisers being more mindful of these diversity issues and acting more carefully in their selection of exhibitors. This is not to say that ’white wealthy men’ should be excluded. They should have their space just as they do now. An inclusive curation, however, could involve reaching out more actively in the local area and invite less-typical underrepresented groups to exhibit. Organisers also choose program highlights and thereby give special status to certain themes. MFV for example placed explicit emphasis on sustainability topics. So why not choose a maker with disability or a female DIY-expert of colour for the keynote talk? Actions like these do not change anything for those who already make best use of the platform, yet it could make it more welcoming for outsiders and create a more diverse program beyond just ’typical makers’.

6.2 Maker Faire as a Site for HCI Research

Our study probed Maker Faire as an interesting yet challenging context for conducting HCI research. In our experience, it is relevant for research that tries to engage with a wide audience consisting of different kinds of people who do not share much else other than a general interest in the Making. The huge variety of people can however cause problems for an effective study design - especially if the approach is based on assumptions of a certain target group. While Maker Faire is an event explicitly about Making, our findings have shown how it involves not only makers but also other stakeholders (e.g. interested not-yet-makers, educators, tool vendors). The vast scale of the event makes it difficult to reach specific kinds of people within the audience. Having said this, Maker Faire as a site for HCI research holds much potential for wider explorations and examinations of stakeholder interrelations. By capturing different voices part of the same events, we aimed to demonstrate how the rich pluralism of different experiences could be embraced for analysing the complex dynamics of maker cultures. To further develop such holistic approaches, we suggest to conduct similar studies at other Maker Faire events and other venues of the Maker Movement (e.g. makerspaces, online communities, umbrella organisations), and to compare results across these accounts.

6.3 Limitations and Future Work

Like any qualitative study this work can only provide a snapshot of the full picture – again a fragment. We do not claim to have captured an exhaustive set of data or to have teased out every possible aspect through our analysis. The large numbers of people participating in such events and the lack of any systematically gathered demographic data on them pose many challenges for our analysis (and highlights a significant potential for future research). We are also aware that the design of the exhibition and hands-on activity at our stall that allowed us to be participant-observants framed our own experiences as much as it affected the participation of different kinds of visitors - just as much as our chosen sample from two European cities is likely to be different from maker cultures in other parts of the world. We argue however that taken together the data we gathered was sufficiently rich to allow us to sketch a nuanced portrayal of the two Maker Faires we took part in and illustrate some complex dynamics within (and around) it. Our study approach allowed us to look at the Maker Movement from a different angle and therefore develop a more inclusive perspective on different types and levels of makers, from non-makers to experts. Again, we are aware that this inclusiveness is limited (i.e. in regards to makers who could/did not attend Maker Faire), yet looking at the voices of different participating stakeholders provided access to a plurality of diverse complementary experiences and the many different agendas in and around maker cultures and their socio-economic interrelations.

In future work, we intend to explore the data in even more detail and investigate the fit of conceptual framings that deal with macro-micro-dynamics on a more theoretical level. Deliberating on the themes we can for example see strong similarities with Lave and Wenger's concepts of Communities of Practice [ 25 ] and Landscapes of Practice [ 59 ]. We would not be the first ones to use the communities of practice concept in context of the Maker Movement, as this has already been done in relation to makerspaces [ 53 ]. However, to our knowledge nobody has yet discussed the Maker Movement in relation to the wider concept of landscapes of practice. Based on the outcomes of this study, we see a good opportunity to do so – for example, through a specific exploration of how interested not-yet-makers enter and leave different maker communities.

7 CONCLUSION

In this paper we presented a qualitative study that aimed to give a nuanced description of Maker Faire at the nexus of different technological, social and economic interests. We identified a lack of such an account in previous work and given that Maker Faire is understudied in HCI research, we argue that contributing such a focused description adds to HCI's understanding of the Maker Movement and its different venues. In our study, we made sure to collect data representing the different voices of visitors, event organisers and exhibiting makers and to elaborate how their respective interests related to each other while collectively shaping Maker Faire to what it is. Finally, we used our findings to discuss the particular nature of the complex dynamics we observed at Maker Faire and how this knowledge could be productive for both HCI research and practice within this co-created sociotechnical space.
