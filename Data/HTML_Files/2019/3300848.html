This paper presents the first investigation into using the goal-crossing paradigm for object selection with virtual reality (VR) head-mounted displays. Two experiments were carried out to evaluate ray-casting crossing tasks with target discs in 3D space and goal lines on 2D plane respectively in comparison to ray-casting pointing tasks. Five factors, i.e. task difficulty, the direction of movement constraint (collinear vs. orthogonal), the nature of the task (discrete vs. continuous), field of view of VR devices and target depth, were considered in both experiments. Our findings are: (1) crossing generally had shorter or no longer time, and higher or similar accuracy than pointing, indicating crossing can complement or substitute pointing; (2) crossing tasks can be well modelled with Fitts’ Law; (3) crossing performance depended on target depth; (4) crossing target discs in 3D space differed from crossing goal lines on 2D plane in many aspects such as time and error performance, the effects of target depth and the parameters of Fitts’ models. Based on these findings, we formulate a number of design recommendations for crossing-based interaction in VR.

1 INTRODUCTION

Target acquisition is a fundamental task in current user interfaces and can be accomplished by many human-computer interaction paradigms such as pointing [ 31 ] or crossing [ 2 ]. Crossing-based selection means object acquisition by crossing its boundary rather than pointing inside its perimeter. Such paradigm has three main interactive advantages. First, crossing is as efficient as pointing for object selection with pen or finger input and can be a viable substitute to pointing and clicking [ 2 , 4 , 29 ]. Second, crossing is not only more expressive than the equivalent point-and-click interfaces [ 2 , 3 , 29 ] but also enables a fluid composition of commands for the development of more continuous style of interactions [ 3 , 15 ]. Third, crossing can be combined with other interaction paradigms like pointing [ 30 ], dragging [ 17 ] and gesturing [ 3 ] to provide new interaction possibilities. Therefore, the crossing paradigm has been theoretically investigated and practically applied to interface design for mouse and trackball input [ 17 , 51 ], stylus input [ 2 , 3 , 19 ], direct finger touch [ 29 , 30 ] and remote display interaction [ 36 ].

Motivated by these benefits, this study investigates the use of crossing in target selection with VR head-mounted displays, which has not been explored in previous studies. We examine the potential of ray-casting crossing as an alternative to ray-casting pointing, one of the most commonly used techniques in commercial VR systems such as Oculus Rift and HTC Vive. Ray-casting pointing relies on a ray (ray cursor) emitted from the user's hand or the controller to intersect and select targets (e.g. [ 6 , 12 , 22 , 42 ]). It thus supports users to select objects beyond their area of reach and requires relatively less physical movements than virtual hand techniques (which acquire an object by touching it with the controller or bare hand [ 39 , 55 ]). The efficacy of ray-casting pointing has been validated by previous studies [ 10 , 22 ]. However, ray-casting pointing has some drawbacks as well. Selecting small or distant objects (e.g. the target disc or goal line in Figure 1 ) through ray-casting pointing is a difficult task [ 20 , 23 , 24 ], due to the fact that the interaction takes place in free space without physical support for the hand. In addition, users have to keep the controller orientation and position steady until the selection is confirmed by, for example, pressing a button of the controller. Such action is likely to induce a change in the controller's orientation or position, known as Heisenberg effect [ 9 ], potentially leading to a wrong selection. For such cases, goal-crossing may be more adequate than pointing-and-clicking. It is thus worthy of exploring ray-casting crossing as a substitution or complement to circumvent the drawbacks or promote the performance of ray-casting pointing.

Crossing with VR head-mounted displays can not be simply justified by referring to empirical evidence for crossing with the stylus or finger due to their different interaction characteristics in both motor and visual spaces. Unlike stylus or touch based crossing where movement distance of the input tool is nearly equal to that of the pen or finger cursor, ray-casting crossing requires the user to translate the physical movement distance of the controller to the virtual movement distance of the ray cursor; a small controller movement produces a large cursor displacement. Such translation may influence performance and attentional requirements [ 49 ]. Users’ perception of visual representations in the virtual environment also differs from that in the real world for two main aspects. One is Field of View (FOV), the extent of the observable environment. FOV is an important contributing factor to immersion and the effect of presence in VR [ 26 , 28 , 52 ]. Current head-mounted VR devices, such as the Oculus Rift CV1, are limited to around 80° horizontally and 90° vertically [ 27 ], which are significantly smaller than binocular visual field of humans (130-135° vertically and 200-220° horizontally [ 8 ]). Thus, FOV may have a more pronounced impact on crossing interaction for VR than for pen and finger interfaces. The other is object depth, the distance between a virtual object and the user. Virtual objects can be positioned at different places in virtual environments, leading to varying object depths. Object depth has been studied as an important factor for 3D object pointing in VR (e.g [ 40 , 46 ]). However, stylus or finger based crossing interactions commonly do not take this factor into account. Overall, it is unclear whether these intrinsic motor and visual characteristics with VR head-mounted displays affect user performance of ray-casting crossing.

Therefore, we conducted two experiments based on the experiment design of indirect stylus crossing [ 2 ]. Pointing selection in VR can act upon objects either in 3D space or on 2D plane. The former type is undoubtedly quite common (e.g. [ 6 , 22 , 55 ]). The latter type can be found in applications such as the embodiment of 2D menus in virtual environments [ 11 , 13 , 14 , 25 , 33 ]. We thus considered crossing tasks with two object types: 2D target discs in 3D space and 1D goal lines on 2D plane (Figure 1 ), corresponding to pointing at spheres in 3D space and circular targets on 2D plane respectively. In addition, we considered task difficulty, the direction of movement constraint (collinear vs. orthogonal), the nature of the task (discrete vs. continuous), FOV of VR devices and target depth dimensions when designing the experiments. We aim to answer the following questions: 1) Would ray-casting crossing outperform ray-casting pointing? 2) Can ray-casting crossing be modelled by Fitts’ Law [ 18 ]? 3) What would crossing objects in different levels of depth be? 4) How would crossing 2D targets in 3D space vary from crossing 1D goals on 2D plane? We discuss these questions according to data analysis results, and conclude our study with design implications.

2 RELATED WORK

Crossing-Based Selection

An early introduction of the crossing paradigm dates back to Accot and Zhai's study of steering tasks [ 1 ], in which the goal-crossing experiment formed a stepping stone towards devising the steering law. In their follow-up crossing study, they systematically investigated four types of crossing tasks in comparison to two pointing tasks with indirect stylus input [ 2 ]. Generally, crossing task time was faster or equivalent to pointing. Since Accot and Zhai's crossing study, researchers have applied the crossing paradigm to user interface design with stylus input, direct finger touch, as well as mouse and trackball  input.

The pen is a proper tool to perform crossing tasks, mainly because it is naturally suited to draw strokes. Forlines and Balakrishnan [ 19 ] investigated direct and indirect stylus input with tactile feedback using the six tasks in Accot and Zhai's study [ 2 ]. They found direct input significantly outperformed indirect input for crossing selection (16% faster). Apitz and Guimbretière [ 3 ] proposed CrossY, a pen-based drawing application that employs crossing rather than point-and-click as its fundamental target acquisition scheme. Their work demonstrated that crossing-based interfaces are not only as expressive as standard point-and-clicks interfaces, but also offer the option of composing several commands in a single stroke. A follow-up study by Dixon et al. [ 15 ] further examined crossing target density and orientation for parameter selection dialog boxes. It was found that crossing-based dialog boxes with direct pen input can be faster than their point-and-click counterpart while having spatially efficient.

Direct finger touch input has also been used to execute crossing tasks, although it suffers from finger-occlusion problem and relatively high contact friction on the display in comparison to pen input. Luo and Vogel [ 29 ] conducted a fundamental experiment to evaluate crossing-based selection tasks with direct touch input based on a close adaptation of indirect stylus crossing experiment design by Accot and Zhai [ 2 ]. Their study revealed similar trends for direct touch input and some differences (e.g. not all crossing tasks with finger input can be modelled accurately with standard Fitts’ law [ 18 ]). There are also many crossing-based applications and interaction techniques proposed for touch input. For example, Attribute Gates was designed to set a sequence of scale orientation and access multi attributes by crossing them on horizontal interactive surface [ 44 ]. HandyWidgets invoked widgets by crossing implicit goals defined by hand position on a multi-touch tabletop [ 54 ]. Pin-and-Cross was a one-handed command input technique which combines one or more static touches (“pins”) with another touch to cross a radial target [ 30 ].

Some studies are also focused on crossing with the mouse. Wobbrock and Gajos [ 51 ] compared pointing and crossing with mouse and trackball input for able-bodied and motor-impaired participants respectively. Data from able-bodied participants indicate that crossing was suboptimal for having lower Fitts’ throughput [ 31 ] than pointing. Some techniques combined crossing with point and drag operations. For example, Dragicevic [ 17 ] proposed fold-and-drop, a method for seamlessly dragging and dropping objects between overlapping windows based on crossing techniques and paper-metaphor windows.

Our review emphasizes the need to test and validate crossing performance across input modalities given that crossing results varied among them. In addition, there is no study of crossing-based selection with VR head-mounted displays. The present study aims to shed light on this topic.

Object Acquisition in VR

Object selection is a basic task in VR. We review related work from the perspective of interaction space and selection techniques. A thorough review can be found in [ 6 ].

The majority of object selection tasks take place in 3D virtual space with 3D objects (e.g. [ 6 , 22 , 55 ]). However, selecting 2D objects on 2D planes which are embedded in 3D space can be found in many application scenarios as well, for example, selecting 2D menus and toolboxes [ 11 , 13 , 14 , 25 , 33 ]. Previous studies also leveraged the benefits of 2D interaction in 3D space by enabling target selection on 2D planes rather than in 3D space, such as Aperture [ 20 ], Image-plane method [ 38 ], EZCursorVR [ 40 ], and One-Eyed Cursor [ 48 ]. Our study thus considers crossing interaction on 2D plane and in 3D space respectively.

Object acquisition in virtual environments mainly relies on two types of selection techniques, either virtual-hand or ray-based ones [ 6 ]. Virtual-hand techniques allow users to acquire targets directly with a rare hand or controller, leading to a closely coupled visual and motor spaces [ 39 , 47 , 55 ]. Ray-based techniques cast a virtual ray from a tracked hand, a hand-held controller or even an eye and the object intersecting the ray is selected when a trigger event is issued (e.g. [ 6 , 12 , 22 , 42 ]). Compared to virtual hand techniques, ray-based pointing techniques allow for longer-distance object selection while requiring relatively less physical hand movements, thus have become a major selection method in current commercial VR devices (e.g. Oculus Rift and HTC Vive) and have been widely studied in VR interface design. Many studies have validated the effectiveness of ray-based selection. For example, Bowman et al. [ 10 ] showed ray-casting outperformed the Go-Go technique [ 39 ] which is a virtual-hand technique. Grossman and Balakrishnan [ 22 ] confirmed that ray-casting was faster than virtual hand for volumetric displays. Some studies also improved ray-based selection to address the difficulty of acquiring small objects by increasing the size of the ray cursor (e.g. [ 20 , 23 , 38 ]). In this study, we will evaluate the performance of ray-casting crossing for object selection in virtual environments by using ray-casting pointing as a baseline.

Models for Pointing and Crossing Tasks

Pointing and crossing in VR are two task types examined in our study. We review models associated with the two tasks and use related ones in our analysis. Fitts’ law is one of the most successful quantitative models in HCI [ 18 ]. It states that the amount of time required to move a pointer (e.g. mouse cursor) to a target area is a function of the distance to the target divided by the size of the target. Although multiple variants of Fitts’ law formulas exist, a common form for movement time ( MT ) prediction is MT = a + blog 2 ( A / W + 1), where A means the distance between the centers of the start target and the end target, W represents the width of the end target in the direction of travel, and a and b are constants reflecting the efficiency of the pointing system. The logarithmic term log 2 ( A / W + 1) denotes a pointing task's “index of difficulty” (ID), measured in bits.

Initially, Fitts’ law was applied to one-dimensional target acquisition tasks [ 18 ]. However, pointing tasks in most standard interfaces are typically two-dimensional (e.g. [ 32 ]) or three-dimensional (e.g. [ 21 ]) with the constraints of both target dimensions and movement (approach) angles. Many extensions of one-dimensional Fitts’ law formulas thus have been proposed to account for effects caused by these constraints. It is beyond the scope of our study to review all Fitts’ law studies and we refer the reader to [ 31 ] for a detailed review. Because the focus of our study is not on investigating the impact of target dimensions and movement angles, we used circular targets in the 2D pointing experiment and spherical targets in the 3D pointing experiment to simplify our experiments, so that target size is represented by a single parameter, width W. Such method has also been used in previous studies (e.g. [ 16 , 45 , 46 ]).

Accot and Zhai [ 1 , 2 ] showed that Fitts’ law holds for multiple types of indirect stylus crossing, that is, the time required to cross a goal depends on the distance to it yet correlates inversely to its width. The relationship among movement time, movement distance and the constraint of the goal width has the same form as in Fitts’ law ( MT = a + blog 2 ( A / W + 1), where W represents goal width). Follow-up studies confirmed that Fitts’ law can be applied to crossing tasks using direct stylus input with visual and tactile feedback [ 19 ], mouse and trackball input [ 51 ]. For direct touch crossing, most but not all tasks can be modelled accurately with standard Fitts’ law [ 29 ]. Instead, “FFitts” law [ 7 ] shows better fits for discrete touch crossing with a directionally constrained target. In our study, we will explore if ray-casting crossing in VR can be described by Fitts’ Law.

3 EXPERIMENT ONE: CROSSING 2D TARGETS IN 3D SPACE

In this experiment, we investigated the performance of crossing 2D discs in comparison to pointing at 3D spheres in 3D space. We used disc targets as they are a typical object type in VR and the natural 2D equivalent of spherical targets in terms of object selection interaction [ 43 , 45 ], which can enable comparisons between 2D crossing and 3D pointing.

Apparatus

The experiment was conducted on a VR-ready computer with an Intel core i7-8700 quad core processor, a Nvidia Geforce 1060 GPU, and 8GB of RAM, running Microsoft Windows 10. We used an Oculus Rift CV1 head-mounted display connected to the computer via HDMI. The CV1 featured a resolution of 1080 × 1200 per eye, a 90Hz refresh rate and a 110° field of view [ 37 ] (around 80° horizontally [ 27 ]). The experiment program was developed in Unity with external libraries for the Oculus Rift hardware. An Oculus Touch controller was used to perform ray-casting pointing or crossing.

Participants

Fifteen right-handed participants (4 female) with mean (± SD) age of 22.7 ± 2.4 yrs took part in the experiment. None had experience in using VR devices.

Experiment Design

Table 1: Values of experiment factors. Note short and long target distances refer to small and large distance values for each target depth.

The experiment was a within-subject repeated-measures design. The within-subject factors were target depth, target distance, target width and task type.

Target depth was the distance between the view point (virtual camera position in VR) and the target in the virtual environment. The Oculus Rift CV1 worked by tracking head movement to maintain camera poses for rendering in the virtual world. This caused a problem of setting target depth as it always changed along with head movement. To address this problem, we fixed virtual camera position so that target depth was independent of head movement. Participants could view virtual objects as normal except that they could not move their view close to or away from objects. This was a reasonable approach to improving internal validity with limited threats to external validity in experiments. According to [ 53 ], the most comfortable range of depths for a user to look at in the Oculus Rift was between 0.75 and 3.5 meters. We therefore selected 0.768, 2.304 and 3.456 meters as the shallow, middle, and deep depths respectively. For each depth, all targets were at the same visual depth from the participant (e.g. target 1&4, Figure 2 ). As recommended in [ 43 ], we used a virtual room environment to enhance depth perception, so that participants did not have to rely on stereo alone (Figure 3 ). This setting was in line with many existing studies (e.g. [ 35 , 40 , 41 ]).

Target distance (D) was the length between two targets’ centers (a target pair). Each target pair corresponded to a target angle (either 20.4° or 90.2°, Figure 2 (a)) that was made up by the positions of the two targets and the virtual camera with the camera's position as the vertex. Target width (W) was the diameter of spheres or discs. For each target depth, D had two levels and W had five. D and W were selected based on Accot and Zhai's stylus crossing study [ 2 ] and scaled by a ratio to fit the setting of our experiment. The values of D and W are illustrated in Table 1 . Across all D-W combinations, the ID range is 1.58 to 7.01. Task IDs were unaffected by target depth as both W and D were scaled by the same factor due to depth. For example, the ratio of 768 mm depth to 2304 mm depth is equal to the ratio of their smallest target sizes. So for a target and its counterparts in other depths (e.g. the smallest target for each depth), their visual size and distance should be the same for participants. This was to enable a fair comparison of selection performance for the three target depths. Targets were located on two sides of the default orientation (z-axis) of the virtual camera with their centers on the x-z plane 1 (Figure 2 (a)). For crossing tasks, all discs were parallel to the camera's default orientation (z-axis) (Figure 3 (b-e)), so that participants could move the ray cursor to “pass” through a disc from its one side to the other. Given VR objects are positioned either within or outside the FOV of the apparatus, we deliberately designed target locations - for each target depth, targets with short distance (target angle: 20.4°, Figure 2 (a)) were within the apparatus’ FOV (horizontal FOV: around 80° [ 27 ]) while targets with long distance were not (target angle: 90.2°, Figure 2 (a)), indicating participants had to first locate a target using head motion before making a selection.

Task type was an adaptation of Accot and Zhai's indirect stylus crossing experiment [ 2 , 4 ] with a major revision that there was only one pointing task type. The four crossing tasks differed both in movement precision constraint (directional/amplitude) and in the nature of the action (discrete/continuous). We follow the revised terminology introduced in [ 4 ]. The details of each type are described as follows.

A/DP: Amplitude and Direction-constrained Pointing (Figure 3 (a)). Previous crossing studies with stylus and finger touch [ 2 , 29 ] treated amplitude-constrained and direction-constrained pointing separately. We did not adopt such setting as (1) pointing tasks in most standard interfaces are either 2D [ 31 ] or 3D [ 21 ] with the constraints of both target dimensions and approach angles; (2) the focus of this experiment was not on investigating amplitude and direction constrained pointing per se, but on looking into crossing vs. pointing in virtual environments. Instead, we used a pointing task with spheres which required both the movement direction and amplitude to be equally precise. This is a typical 3D target selection task [ 5 , 40 , 45 , 50 ] and intended as a baseline for comparing pointing and crossing performance in this experiment.

Following the traditional Fitts’ pointing experiment design [ 18 ], we asked participants to click alternately two horizontal spheres with diameter (width) W. The two target centers were separated by distance D. Participants pointed at objects with a virtual ray emitted from the controller and pressed button A of the controller (Figure 2 (b)) to confirm the selection.

D/AC: Discrete, Amplitude-constrained Crossing (Figure 3 (b)). The task was to cross two horizontal discs of diameter (width) W with center separated by distance D. Participants were asked to cross each disc with a downward stroke for consistency in a reciprocal pattern. Unlike in pen and touch interfaces, the ray cursor can not fly in, cross, and fly out, so the stroke was made by holding down a button (button A in our experiment, Figure 2 (b)) of the controller only when crossing a goal. A wall-like barrier between targets was drawn to remind participants to cross the goals with discrete strokes; otherwise stroking through the barrier would cause a beep. We used 2D disc targets to ensure there was only a single W (size) parameter, so the variables of crossing tasks can match those of pointing tasks for comparison.

D/DC: Discrete, Direction-constrained Crossing (Figure 3 (c)). Two vertical discs with diameter (width) W and distance D were each crossed from left to right for consistency in a reciprocal pattern. As in the previous task type, a wall-like barrier was drawn between the two goals to remind participants not to press down button A of the controller when moving the ray cursor between them.

C/AC: Continuous, Amplitude-constrained Crossing (Figure 3 (d)). Participants alternately moved the ray cursor through two horizontal discs of diameter W over distance D while holding down button A of the controller. The system would beep when the button was released throughout the reciprocal task until it was pressed down gain.

C/DC: Continuous, Direction-constrained Crossing (Figure 3 (e)). Participants moved the ray cursor reciprocally through two vertical discs of diameter W over distance D with holding down button A of the controller throughout the reciprocal task. Same as in task C/AC, the system would beep when the button was released.

For each of the five tasks, there were 30 sets of target depth-width-distance combinations. Each set of combinations required 5 reciprocal selections of targets. Selection time was defined as the duration between two serial target selections. A pointing was successful if the selection ray intersected the sphere when pressing button A. A crossing was successful if the selection ray emitted from the controller “cut” through the disc from one side to the other. When successful, the disc changed color from white to purple. An error tone sounded to remind the participant to improve accuracy when the ray passed through the plane which was coincident with the disc but not within the area of the disc. In case of the error, participants continued the trial until they hit the target. Error rate was calculated as the ratio of the number of errors to the number of trials. We removed error trials in the time analysis as previous studies [ 2 , 29 ].

Task and Procedure

Participants were first given instructions on how to do the experiment task. They were asked to sit in a chair with the Oculus Rift on their head and hold the controller to perform the task with their dominant hand (Figure 2 (b)). For each of the five tasks, participants performed 3 blocks of 30 sets of target depth-width-distance combinations. The order for the 30 sets of trials was randomized. For each set of combinations, participants were required to perform 5 reciprocal selections of spheres or discs, so as to create 4 measured data trials. The order of executing the five task types was counterbalanced across participants using a Latin Square. Participants were instructed to perform tasks as quickly and accurately as possible. Auditory feedback was provided through headphones of the Oculus Rift. Participants took a 1-minute break between blocks, and 2-minute break between different task types. Participants on average took 80 minutes (including breaks) to complete the experiment. In summary, the experiment consisted of 15 participants × 5 task types × 3 target depths × 5 target widths × 2 target distances × 4 measured data trials × 3 blocks = 27000 trials.

Results and Analysis

We analyzed the results using repeated measures ANOVA and post hoc comparisons with Bonferroni adjustment.

Learning Effect. We first checked the learning effect on selection time over the three blocks of trials to see if the data collected had reached a level of stability. There was a significant main effect for block on selection time ( F 2, 28 = 4.08, p < 0.05). Block one significantly differed from block two and three (both p < 0.05), but no significant difference was found between the latter two blocks ( p = 0.58). We thus used block two and three in the rest of data analysis.

We performed a (task type × target depth × ID) within-subject ANOVA on average selection time. Task type had a significant main effect on selection time (Table 2 ). Post hoc tests revealed A/DP was significantly faster than D/DC ( p < 0.01) but slower than C/AC, C/DC and D/AC (all p < 0.05) (mean values and SD are illustrated in Table 3 ). No significant differences were found between C/AC, C/DC and D/AC (all p = 1.00). Depth had a significant main effect on selection time. The shallow depth led to significantly longer time than the middle and deep depths (both p < 0.05), and the latter two depths had similar time ( p = 1.00). ID had a significant main effect on selection time. Generally, higher ID resulted in longer time, but such difference was not significant between ID of 1.58 bits and 2.32 bits ( p = 0.51).

There was no interaction effect between task type and target depth (Figure 4 (a)). Generally, time decreased as target depth increased for all tasks. Target depth had a significant simple main effect on time for each task type (all p < 0.01). For A/DP, C/AC, C/DC and D/AC, a significant difference was only found between the shallow and deep depths ( p < 0.05). For D/DC, the shallow depth significantly differed from the middle and deep depths (both p < 0.05), but the latter two depths were not significantly different ( p = 1.00).

There was a significant (task type × ID) interaction (Figure 4 (b)). A/DP resulted in longer time than C/AC and D/AC for IDs higher than 4.09 bits. In general, D/DC led to longer time than the other three crossing tasks across the seven IDs. Compared to A/DP, D/DC had longer time when ID was lower than 5.04 bits, but shorter time when ID was higher than 6.02 bits.

Discussion

Results indicate C/AC, C/DC and D/AC were generally faster than A/DP while having comparable selection accuracy (crossing success rate). D/DC had significantly longer selection time than other task types. A main reason may be that the crossing action for D/DC has been strongly affected by the wall-like “obstacle” placed between the two targets; crossing movements had to slow down in order not to pass through the obstacle. However, such results are not true for the D/AC task because the ray cursor always landed above the horizontal discs.

All task types can be well modelled by Fitts’ law. Note the D/DC task had a relatively lower r 2 than other task types, probably due to the “obstacle” effects described above. Also note A/DP tended to be slower and less accurate than crossing tasks for high IDs, which confirms the difficulty of pointing to small and distant targets.

Target depth affected task performance and these effects varied by task types. For A/DP, target depth had an effect on selection time but not on error rates. For C/AC, C/DC and D/AC, shallow depth led to significantly longer time and more errors than deep depth. The effects of target depth on D/DC are not consistent with other crossing tasks; shallow depth tended to have longer time and more errors than both middle and deep depths. Overall, the results above suggest crossing tasks should not take place too close to the user.

4 EXPERIMENT TWO: CROSSING 1D GOALS ON 2D PLANE

As summarized in the Related Work section, there are many cases with the introduction of 2D desktop interface elements into VR environments, for example 2D menus and toolboxes [ 11 , 13 , 14 , 25 , 33 , 34 ]. They are usually restricted to being flat 2D objects on 2D planes which are embedded in 3D space. Following previous pen and finger crossing studies [ 2 , 29 ], we compared the performance of crossing 1D goals and pointing at 2D targets on 2D plane.

The design of this experiment was similar to Experiment One. It was also a 5 × 3 × 2 × 5 full-factorial design, with four within-subject factors (task type, target/goal depth, distance between targets/goals (D), and target/goal width (W)). The values of the latter three factors were identical as Experiment One (Table 1 ). The target/goal widths and distances represent Fitts’ Index of Difficulty (ID) values from 1.58 to 7.01 bits.

The main difference between Experiment One and Two lies in task type. For pointing tasks (A/DP: Amplitude and Direction-constrained Pointing, Figure 7 (a)), we adopted a standard 2D pointing design with circular targets [ 16 ] given pointing tasks in most 2D interfaces are typically two-dimensional ([ 32 ]). Target positions were the same as in Experiment One except that they were placed on constrained planes which were parallel to the default viewport of the virtual camera (perpendicular to z-axis, Figure 2 ). This was to provide a view of the targets in accordance with users’ familiar interactions with 2D desktop interfaces. The planes were positioned in a virtual room to offer a contextual view of ranges of target depths. According to the traditional Fitts’ pointing experiment design [ 18 ], we required participants to click alternately two horizontal circular targets with diameter (width) W and distance D. Participants pointed at the target with a ray from the controller and confirmed the selection by pressing Button A of the controller.

For all crossing tasks (D/AC: Discrete, Amplitude-constrained Crossing; D/DC: Discrete, Direction-constrained Crossing; C/AC: Continuous, Amplitude-constrained Crossing; C/DC: Continuous, Direction-constrained Crossing, Figure 7 (b-e)), task design was the same as their counterpart in Experiment One, except that we used goal lines which were displayed on constrained planes.

The experiment procedure was identical to Experiment One. On average the experiment took 80 minutes for each participant. The entire experiment had 15 participants × 5 task types × 3 target depths × 5 target widths × 2 target distances × 4 measured data trials × 3 blocks = 27000 trials.

Results and Analysis

Repeated measures ANOVA and post hoc comparisons with the Bonferroni adjustment were used in the data analysis.

Learning Effect. We first checked the learning effect on selection time over the three blocks of trials. Block had a main effect on selection time ( F 2, 28 = 20.78, p < 0.01). Block one significantly differed from block two and three (both p < 0.01), but no significant difference was found between block two and three ( p = 0.18). We thus used block two and three in the rest of data analysis.

Selection Time. We performed a (task type × target depth × ID) within-subject ANOVA on average selection time. Task type had a significant main effect on selection time (Table 4 ). Post hoc comparisons revealed that A/DP led to significantly longer time than C/AC, C/DC and D/AC (all p < 0.01), but similar time than D/DC ( p = 0.56). Table 5 shows the mean values and SD. There were no significant differences between the four crossing task types except between C/DC and D/DC ( p < 0.05). Target depth had a significant main effect on selection time. Shallow depth did not differ from middle depth ( p = 0.84). And both had significantly shorter time than deep depth (both p < 0.05). Task ID had a significant main effect on selection time. Generally, time increased as ID increased. Significant differences were found between all IDs ( p < 0.01) except between IDs of 1.58 bits and 2.32 bits ( p = 1.00).

These main effects were qualified by a complex interaction pattern. We observed a significant (task type × target depth) interaction (Figure 8 (a)). Thus we further calculated the simple main effect of target depth for each task type. Interestingly, target depth had a significant main effect for C/DC and D/DC (both p < 0.01), but not for A/DP ( p = 0.06), C/AC ( p = 0.56) and D/AC ( p = 0.54). Target depth affected direction-constrained crossing, but not amplitude-constrained crossing. For C/DC, shallow and middle depths had similar time performance, and both resulted in significantly shorter time than deep depth ( p < 0.05). For D/DC, shallow depth led to significantly shorter time than both middle and deep depths ( p < 0.05), while the latter two depths had similar time.

There was a significant (task type × ID) interaction, as illustrated in Figure 8 (b). A/DP tended to be slower than all crossing tasks when ID was greater than 4.09 bits, indicating ray-casting pointing was not good at selecting small and far targets. A/DP, C/AC and D/AC had similar performance when ID was less than 4.09 bits. C/DC was faster than D/DC, probably because there was no obstacle in the C/DC task, it was possible to cross the two goals in one stroke when they were very close to each other.

Fitts’ Law Modeling. Five tasks were modelled well with Fitts’ Law with high fitness values (see below). As illustrated in Figure 5 (b), C/DC was uniformly faster than A/DP. The regression line of A/DP intersects those of C/AC and D/AC at very low IDs, indicating the two crossing tasks was faster for most conditions. D/DC tended to be faster than A/DP when ID was greater than 4 bits, and the reverse was true when ID was less than 4 bits.

Error Rates.

We performed a (task type × target depth × ID) within-subject ANOVA on error rates. There was a significant main effect for task type (Table 4 ). Post-hoc tests indicated that A/DP produced significantly more errors than C/DC, D/AC and D/DC (all p < 0.05) except C/AC ( p = 0.32). Table 5 lists the mean values and SD. In addition, the four crossing tasks were quite alike in selection accuracy (all p > 0.74). Depth had a main effect on error rates. The shallow depth had significantly more errors than the middle and deep depths (both p < 0.01), while the latter two depths did not differ in accuracy ( p = 1.00). A significant main effect was found for task IDs. Significant differences were found between all IDs ( p < 0.05), except between IDs of 1.58 bits, 2.32 bits and 3.17 bits (all p = 1.00), as well as between IDs of 4.09 bits and 6.02 bits ( p = 0.44).

Target depth significantly interacted with task type (Figure 9 (a)). As depth increased, error rates increased for A/DP but decreased for the four crossing tasks. We further calculated the simple main effect of target depth for each task type. Target depth had a significant main effect for C/AC, C/DC, D/AC and D/DC (all p < 0.01), but this did not hold for A/DP ( p = 0.15). For all crossing tasks, errors decreased as depth increased, although there was not significant difference between the middle and deep depths (all p > 0.18).

There was a significant interaction effect between task type and ID (Figure 9 (b)). A/DP led to more errors than crossing tasks when ID was greater than 4.09 bits. Note when ID was equal to 5.04 bits, error rate was higher than the that for ID of 6.02 bits. One reason may be because the ID of 5.04 bits is partly attributed to the smallest target width and selecting such small targets was error-prone.

Discussion

Results from time and error analysis show the four crossing types generally outperformed pointing, confirming the effectiveness of ray-casting crossing for object selection.

According to the analysis of Fitts’ Law Modeling, there was a lawful regularity between movement time and ID in each of the five tasks ( r 2 > 0.88 in all tasks). Note as Experiment One, the D/DC task also had a relatively lower r 2 than the other task types in this experiment.

While target depth did not affect ray-casting pointing performance, it did significantly impact on ray-casting crossing performance. For time performance, target depth had an influence on direction-constrained crossing (C/DC and D/DC), but not on amplitude-constrained crossing (C/AC and D/AC). Further, the effects of target depth varied between C/DC and D/DC, suggesting a need to carefully consider the relation between crossing tasks and target depth. For selection accuracy, target crossing on near virtual plane tended to commit more errors, indicating the plane for crossing interaction should not be too close to the user.

5 GENERAL DISCUSSION

Crossing 2D Targets in 3D Space and 1D Goals on 2D Plane

We conducted two experiments to investigate crossing-based selection for 2D targets in 3D space and 1D goals on 2D plane. Recall we proposed four questions in the Introduction section. We discuss these questions based on the data analysis results to gain insights into crossing interaction in virtual environments.

Would ray-casting crossing outperform ray-casting pointing? Crossing tasks generally outperformed the pointing task in selection time and accuracy. Especially, the advantages of the four crossing types became more evident for tasks with high IDs. This confirms the poor performance of ray-casting pointing for small and far objects as revealed by previous studies [ 20 , 23 , 24 ], and also proves that ray-casting crossing can substitute pointing as a promising method for target selection.

Note C/AC, C/DC and D/AC were quite alike in terms of selection time and accuracy. Despite the different constraints and varying action patterns across the three crossing tasks, the laws of these tasks fall into a narrow band in both experiments (Figure 5 ), which further verifies their similar performances in target selection. These results suggest the three tasks can be equally applied to crossing-based interface design from the perspective of selection efficacy.

Can ray-casting crossing be modelled by Fitts’ Law? This study first reveals that for crossing interaction in 3D space and on 2D plane, participants’ temporal performance in each of the four tasks was dependent on the index of difficulty formulated in the same way as in Fitts’ law (although the parameters differed by tasks). Note D/DC had a relatively lower r 2 than other task types. The scatter plot of D/DC (Figure 5 (a&b)) shows an upward curvature of time away for the regression line for low values of ID. Such results can be partially explained by the “obstacle” positioned between the two targets. As the distance between the two targets reduces, the constraint of taking off the ray cursor between the obstacle and the left target, as well as landing the ray cursor between the obstacle and the right target increases, hence slowing up the crossing process.

What would crossing objects in different levels of depth be? Crossing performance depends on target depth. In general, for crossing 1D goals on 2D plane, selection time increased and error rates decreased as target depth increased; for crossing 2D targets in 3D space, both time and error rates decreased as target depth increased. Further, for crossing 2D targets in 3D space, target depth had significant effects on the four crossing tasks in time and error rates. For crossing 1D goals on 2D plane, target depth only influenced direction-constrained crossing (C/DC and D/DC), but not amplitude-constrained crossing (C/AC and D/AC) in time. The effects of target depth on crossing tasks differed between Experiment One and Two. Such contrasts may be caused by the introduction of 2D plane. It is very likely to diminish depth cue information, leading to a reduced depth perception for participants, which in turn affects crossing performance.

How would crossing 2D targets in 3D space vary from crossing 1D goals on 2D plane? In comparison to crossing interaction in 3D space (C/AC: 1362 ms; C/DC: 1419 ms; D/AC: 1356 ms; D/DC: 1751 ms), crossing interaction on 2D plane had shorter average time (C/AC: 1290 ms; C/DC: 1197 ms; D/AC: 1345 ms; D/DC: 1482 ms) (Table 3 and 5 ). Especially, direction-constrained crossing (C/DC and D/DC) tended to have bigger difference than amplitude-constrained crossing (C/AC and D/AC). These trends can also be found in the error rate analysis. Error rates decreased from crossing input in 3D space to on 2D space (C/AC: 0.043 vs. 0.042; C/DC: 0.054 vs. 0.030; D/AC: 0.043 vs. 0.033; D/DC: 0.048 vs. 0.032). Again, such decrease was more obvious for direction-constrained crossings (C/DC and D/DC) than for their counterpart of amplitude-constrained crossings (C/AC and D/AC). Overall, participants can perform crossing-based selection faster and more accurately for 1D goal lines on 2D plane than for 2D target discs in 3D space. This is partly due to the constraints of ray cursor movements. For crossing on 2D plane, ray cursor movements are restricted on planes, while for crossing in 3D space, the ray cursor can be freely moved and may be more difficult to control. Interestingly, pointing tasks did not follow the same trend of crossing tasks in both time and error rates. By contrast, the values of the two measures increased from pointing at spheres to circles (time: 1560 ms vs. 1608 ms; error rate: 0.059 vs. 0.074). Selecting spheres with ray-casting pointing seems to be easier than selecting circles.

The task D/DC had different performances for 2D target selection and 1D goal selection. For 2D target selection, D/DC was the slowest among all the task types examined. However, for 1D goal selection, D/DC was slightly faster than A/DP but slower than C/DC, falling in the middle range of performance. It seems that the “obstacle” positioned between the two targets/goals had more pronounced effects on D/DC with 2D targets than with 1D goals. For both 1D goal and 2D target crossing, participants needed to carefully control ray-cursor velocity to pass through the goal/target while avoid crossing the “obstacle”. The difficulty of executing such actions increases when crossing disc targets as their visual profile depends on the viewing angle, which requires participants to adjust the movement velocity accordingly.

Ray-casting Crossing in VR vs. Pen and Finger Crossing on Touch Screens

As noted in the Introduction section, ray-casting crossing in VR differs from pen and finger crossing on touch screens in both motor and visual spaces, we therefore make direct comparisons with crossing performance for indirect stylus [ 2 ], direct stylus [ 19 ] and finger touch [ 29 ], so as to obtain deeper understanding of crossing interaction in VR. This is possible because our experiments, the direct stylus and finger crossing studies are close replications of Accot and Zhai's original study [ 2 ]. Our comparison uses quantitative results determined by analyzing figures in papers [ 2 , 19 ], so quantities are approximate.

Generally speaking, ray-casting crossing led to longer time than stylus and finger crossing. Such difference may be attributed to two reasons. One is related to motor control aspect. Ray-casting input tends to have higher control-display ratio (the ratio of control displacement divided by cursor displacement) than stylus and finger input, making it more difficult to execute selection tasks that require high precision. This increases task time. The other is about visual space. For ray-casting crossing, participants needed to rotate head to search targets that are out of view, which incurs additional time cost.

Error rates in our study (3.0% - 5.9%) are close to those of indirect stylus crossing [ 2 ]. Participants properly maintained an approximate 4% error rate, which is usually required for Fitts’ law studies [ 31 ]. This partially indicates target sizes selected are appropriate for our crossing experiments. Error rates in our study are lower than those of direct stylus and finger touch crossing, due to extremely small targets tested in direct stylus crossing [ 19 ] and “fat finger” problem in finger crossing [ 29 ].

6 IMPLICATIONS FOR DESIGN

Our findings not only enrich the crossing paradigm in the context of VR interaction, but also provide recommendations for crossing interface design in VR. We present four general guidelines and four practical suggestions below. It is worth emphasizing that user interface design should be grounded on the relative pros and cons of pointing or crossing tasks. In some cases (e.g. disc vertical to the user's sight, sphere) pointing would be preferred; in other cases (e.g. disc horizontal to the user's sight), crossing (discrete or continuous) would be preferred.

General Design Guidelines

(1) For object selection in both 3D space and on 2D plane, ray-casting crossing is as efficient as, if not more efficient than ray-casting pointing. Especially, ray-casting crossing offers greater advantages when selecting small and distant targets. Thus, the crossing paradigm can be a viable alternative to pointing for target selection in VR.

(2) For continuous crossing, targets can be positioned either orthogonal (C/DC) or collinear (C/AC) to the direction of movement. For discrete crossing, targets should be positioned collinear (D/AC) to the direction of movement when possible.

(3) Like previous pen and finger crossing studies [ 2 , 19 , 29 ], Fitts’ law can be a theoretical tool for the quantitative evaluation of crossing-based user interface design in VR. The Fitts’ model can account for the effects caused by FOV constraints of current VR head-mounted displays and also the effects by varying target depths in virtual environments.

(4) Crossing-based interface design in VR should be aware of the differences between 2D target crossing in 3D space and 1D goal crossing on 2D plane. Such differences lie in many aspects such as the time and accuracy performance of ray-casting crossing tasks, the parameters of Fitts’ model for each crossing task, and the effects of target depth on crossing performance. Our study has generated detailed results about these differences, which can provide necessary support for future crossing interaction design in VR.

Practical Design Suggestions

(1) In VR systems, it is difficult (even unlikely) to select objects like discs or bars shown in Fig. 1 with pointing. Instead, designers should suggest users select them with crossing by rendering visual or audio guides to indicate how to perform.

(2) When designing small and remote objects in VR, designers should consider crossing-friendly objects like 2D surfaces or 1D bars when possible. Crossing-based techniques thus can be employed along to aid users in target acquisition.

(3) For successive selecting tasks (e.g. acquiring a row of books on a bookshelf in VR), designers should choose crossing as the primary option due to its advantages of continuous selection.

(4) Designers can refer to recommendations in previous crossing studies, e.g., applying designs of CrossY [ 3 ] to crossing-based drawing applications in VR, selecting multiple menu items by a continuous goal-crossing action [ 2 , 15 ] and crossing a goal to invoke a command instead of clicking a button [ 2 , 3 ].

7 FUTURE WORK

One of our future work is to examine crossing performance with virtual-hand techniques, another commonly used target selection method in virtual environments. Unlike ray-casting crossing that relies on ray cursors, virtual-hand crossing uses virtual fingers to perform object passing tasks. Due to such motor control difference, the conclusions of our study may not be directly generalized to virtual-hand crossing without further investigation. Future empirical work also includes evaluating successive crossing tasks (e.g. Dixon et al.’s [ 15 ] crossing dialog experiments) with ray-casting input in VR, as well as the expressiveness of ray-casting crossing in comparison to standard point-and-click interfaces in VR like stylus crossing [ 3 ]. Future work will examine crossing 2D surface objects with different shapes like squares while our conclusions would hold as their visual profiles are alike to discs’ with our experiment setting. It is also of interest to study crossing objects that might be occluded from the user's viewpoint in cluttered environments.

8 CONCLUSION

In this work, we first studied human performance of ray-casting crossing for object selection in VR environments, with systematic variations in terms of task difficulty, the direction of movement constraint (collinear vs. orthogonal), the nature of the task (discrete vs. continuous), FOV and ranges of target depth. We conducted two experiments to evaluate crossing performance with 2D disc targets in 3D space and with 1D goal lines on 2D plane respectively in comparison to target pointing. In general, crossing time was shorter or equivalent to pointing time, and crossing accuracy was higher or similar to pointing accuracy. Fitts’ models for each crossing task resulted in good fits to the data. The relative performance of crossing was dependant on target depths and types (2D target discs and 1D goal lines). Our results provide the foundation for designing effective crossing-based user interfaces with VR head-mounted displays.
