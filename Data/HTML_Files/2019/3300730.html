CHI '19: Proceedings of CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Glasgow, Scotland UK, May 2019

Recent research suggests that a robot's motors make sounds that can influence users’ perception of the robot's characteristics. To more deeply understand users’ associations with specific sonic characteristics, we adapted methods from sensory science including Check All That Apply (CATA) questions and Polarized Sensory Positioning (PSP) to tease out small differences in motor sounds in an online survey. These methods are straightforward for untrained people to do in an online setting, mathematically rigorous, and can explore a variety of subtle auditory and perceptual stimuli. We describe how to use these methods, interpret the results with several intuitive visual representations, and show that the results align with a previous study of the same dataset. We close by discussing benefits and limitations of applying these methods to study subtle phenomena in the HCI community.

1 INTRODUCTION

Traditionally, user experience designers of technology products have focused on the graphical, formal, or behavioral aspects of interaction; the sonic experience is often an afterthought. However, as more everyday objects become automated, and particularly as autonomous systems such as robots and drones enter our daily lives, noises they make will become more ubiquitous and increasingly important for designers to understand and control as part of the overall user experience.

When designing sound for a new technology or product—sometimes called sonic interaction design [ 13 , 43 , 44 ]—designers must contend with what Langeveld et al. [ 23 ] define as two types of sound: intentional sound such as phone notifications or beeps, and consequential sound produced as a result of a product's operation or motion such as from motors, gears, or fans.

While many have studied intentional sound (e.g. [ 42 , 47 , 56 ]), consequential sound is less explored, and arguably more common than intentional sound in some products, such as cars and robots. Recent work in HRI has explored how motor sounds are interpreted, and could be designed in interactions [ 11 , 33 , 51 ], following the tradition of product sound design [ 29 , 30 ].

A useful addition to this vein of exploration in the HCI community is the field of sensory science [ 26 , 53 ], which builds on psychometrics, and has spurred several fast and useful analysis techniques for capturing the finer details and nuances in consumer perception. In contrast with traditional approaches, the novel techniques are targeted towards quantifying the experiences of consumers (not expert panels), using straightforward questioning and associated statistical analysis. Although developed for sensory (taste) experiments, they have recently also been used for audio purposes by Bang and Olufsen [ 12 , 38 ]. The outputs from these methods are typically figures and graphs that can be interpreted and applied by designers or designer teams, as in Figure 1 .

In this study, we applied methods from sensory science to understand how users responded to recordings of servomotor sounds in an online survey. This work contributes to two main areas: (1) introduce new research and statistical methods from sensory science to the HCI community, and (2) continue exploratory research of the impact of motor sounds on interactions with technology. We explain these methods and potential results in the context of consequential sound to aid the HCI and design communities in the study of subtle phenomena.

2 RELATED WORK

Consequential sound, such as motor sound, is a very subtle phenomenon, and can be difficult to characterize [ 5 ]. However, work in HCI and HRI has begun to understand how sound plays a role in interactions with robots and other technology, and is an increasingly important factor in product development [ 45 ]. Sensory science offers some potentially new ways to study sound and design it into interactions with technology [ 26 , 53 ].

Sound as a Channel of Communication

As technology matures, sound can become an unexpected mode of communication. For instance, the hum of automatic doors preparing to open subtly initiates interactions with passersby [ 17 ]. And recently, the United States passed a regulation requiring electric vehicles to make artificial noise at low speeds so that pedestrians can hear them. Engine noise helps pedestrians to be aware of cars in places such as parking lots [ 36 ].

Such unintended consequences also offer a unique design space; what was previously a noisy byproduct (and in the case of a car engine, a byproduct already tuned by some car manufacturers), now becomes a wide open design space to communicate other behavior, such as inviting a pedestrian pass in front of a car by modifying its sonic profile.

Studying Consequential Sound

Sounds naturally moderate many of our interactions with modern technology. For instance, consider the many different messages you easily associate with a given notification sound, such as a new text message, a sent email, or a completed purchase. Komatsu et al. explored the effect of beep sounds [ 19 ] and vibrations [ 18 ] (which also make consequential sound), on communicating a system's confidence level. Song et al. [ 47 ] explored how sound, combined with vibration and color, could express the emotions of an appearance-constrained social robot.

Inspired by earlier work studying timbre of musical instruments including Wessel [ 54 ] and McAdams et al. [ 32 ], recent work has begin to explore how consequential sounds such as motor sounds influence interactions with technology. Moore et al. [ 33 ] explored how users evaluated and anthropomorphized different characteristics of motor sounds, and Tennent et al. [ 51 ] demonstrated that changing a robot arm's motor sounds subtly shape how users evaluate a robot. Frid et al. [ 11 ] also designed different types of motor sounds on a Nao robot, finding that consequential sounds can communicate emotional properties. Cha et al. [ 6 ] showed how a robot's consequential sounds were an important element of auditory localization when it was collaborating with humans.

Designing Consequential Sound

The field of product sound design [ 30 ] seeks to understand how people interpret sounds from consumer products such as vacuum cleaners [ 30 ], kettles [ 10 ], beverage cans [ 48 ], cars [ 20 , 35 ], and lighter clicks [ 22 ]. In particular, car and motorcycle manufacturers recognize the importance of designing consequential sound, perhaps most famously the American Harley Davidson motorcycle, whose manufacturer tried to patent its signature roaring sound [ 34 ]. A car's door closing, engine, and muffler all communicate something about the quality and characteristics of the car, and therefore are elements of the product to be designed [ 20 ].

Tünnermann et al. [ 52 ] describe the concept of blended sonification , whereby sounds can be manipulated in an environment to subtly convey communication. For instance, one could manipulate a door knock to sound differently if a person is in a room versus when a person is not in the room.

Many factors influence the acceptability of a sound to any given person. But this acceptability is inherently subject to individual context and biases (e.g., a friendly neighbor playing music vs. an unfriendly neighbor speeding in a quiet neighborhood). Sound processing is colored by many things including “non-auditory senses, prior knowledge, and association to actions and emotions” [ 5 , p. 750].

Because of the many associations and interactions between sound and the world around us, it can be difficult to directly gauge users’ impressions of sound independent of other factors. For instance, adjusting a sound's timbre (analogous to adjusting the equalizer on an audio system or phone) can make potato chips seem fresher [ 49 ], and hands feel drier when touched [ 16 ]. People also correlate brightness of sound and brightness of color [ 31 ], noisiness [ 10 ], and visual metaphors such as “heavy” [ 8 , 28 ].

The greatest challenge in designing consequential sound is that users do not necessarily know what they want to hear. And if they don't know what they want to hear, they cannot articulate to designers what they would prefer. To tackle this challenge in the context of consequential sound, we must explore new methods that tease out users’ perceptions and desired traits.

Sensory Science

Sensory characterization provides a complete description of the sensory characteristics of products, being one of the most powerful and sophisticated tools in sensory science. The food science arena has developed descriptive techniques with the use of consumers, and they are widely used in both industrial and academic settings for the development and marketing of new products [ 53 ]. These new descriptive methods are slowly migrating to areas as cosmetics [ 37 ], and product design [ 40 ].

There are three general approaches to consumer-based sensory characterization: (1) methodologies based on specific sensory attributes (e.g. “Check-All-That-Apply”, or CATA), (2) methodologies based on the comparison with references (e.g. “Polarized Sensory Positioning”, or PSP), and (3) holistic methodologies, based on the evaluation of global similarities and differences among products (e.g. projective mapping). These methods are very simple and quick for consumers who are not experts in the product or experience being evaluated.

CATA is particularly well suited for self-paced, online questionnaires. CATA questions consist of a list of words or phrases from which respondents select the word(s) they associate with the stimuli under study. It allows multiple options to be selected, instead of limiting respondents or forcing them to focus on the rating of specific attributes. Based on the frequency of selection of the different attributes, one can get a bi-dimensional representation of the perceptual space (sensory map of samples and attributes) via Correspondence Analysis (CA) [ 50 , 53 ]. CA has been used previously in HCI as a method of grouping user personas [ 24 ].

In PSP, consumers evaluate the degree of similarity of the samples to a set of reference stimuli. The method was originally developed for assessing very small flavor differences in mineral waters [ 50 ]. It is often easier for consumers who do not have a vocabulary to describe fine details, such as difference in mineral waters, to describe products as similar or different to these reference products. PSP is a fairly new technique, little researched even in the food area. However, in principle PSP is very applicable to the study of sounds, as it has been reported to be good at discriminating fairly subtle stimuli [ 2 ].

In addition to exploring them in an HCI context, we include CATA and PSP in this study to test how well they work online, as these are traditionally used with time consuming, in-person studies. We evaluate how well these methods adapt new contexts by explaining the methods, interpreting the first layer of results, and suggesting avenues of future analysis.

3 METHOD

We created an online survey that asked participants to evaluate these sounds that were coming from the robot in Figure 2 through appropriateness, CATA and PSP. We then statistically analyzed the results using multivariate methods.

Study Design

We adapted a dataset previously generated and analyzed by Moore et al. [ 33 ] to better compare the results of the sensory science methods with existing approaches. Moore et al. recorded the sounds of 20 unloaded servomotors making simple and quick 180 degree sweeps. These servos represented a variety of types and prices, however to keep the length of the survey reasonable, we downselected from the original 20 samples to 6. To do so, we ran a principal component analysis 1 (PCA) on both the objective acoustic measures calculated and subjective survey data collected in the original study to identify six sounds that best represented the set of possible sound qualities (See Figure 3 ). The resulting subset, in Table 1 and available to listen to in the attached video figure, includes representative sounds with a broad range of acoustic properties. These sound clips are very short, between 0.5 and 2.0 seconds long, and were normalized for loudness.

With the set of stimuli in hand, we created an online survey to have a wide variety of users evaluate the sounds. For each sound, we asked participants to rate how well it fit a particular robot added for context (Figure 2 ), and to select descriptive words they associated with each sound for the CATA analysis. These words were adapted from the subjective metrics in [ 33 ], and expanded to ensure that each descriptor also had an antonym in the list, as bipolar pairs make trends easier to identify later in the analysis.

PSP was conducted with three sounds as references , known as R1, R2 and R3, selected among the six sounds. In principle, the reference sounds do not need to be a part of the study sounds, however they were for this study.

Common practice suggests choosing three poles defining a “plane” to represent the perceptual space; two poles could be enough in some cases, however, the solutions may be more unstable with only two poles [ 1 ]. These reference sounds were selected from this set of six sounds 2 , as representatives of the perceptual space. In total, participants compared each sound once to every other sound, for a total of 18 comparisons.

Survey Procedure

Participants first read study instructions and for context watched a simple video of the robot arm in Figure 2 moving. On this page, participants were asked to type in a password heard on a recording (a voice saying “2018”) into a text field to confirm they had working sound. Instructions asked participants to use headphones to listen to the audio.

Next, there were six similar pairs of pages that asked participants to listen to and evaluate one sound file at a time. We gave each sound file a randomly generated name, shown in Table 1 (e.g. “Sound 530” represents the sound from the HXT500 Micro servo), and asked participants to imagine that sound was coming from a robot shown in Figure 2 . For each sample, we asked participants the following questions:

  • Appropriateness: How well do you think Sound 530 fits the robot? (on a scale from 1 to 9)
  • CATA: Check all the words that you associate with Sound 530: (options: annoying, pleasant, expensive, inexpensive, weak, strong, imprecise, precise, smooth, rough, untrustworthy, trustworthy)
  • PSP: Compare Sound 530 to References R1, R2, and R3, respectively. Move the slider to indicate how similar or different the sounds are on the scale from “Exactly the same” to “Completely different” (See Figure 4 )
  • Free Associations: Do you have any other thoughts on Sound 530? (open text box)

This set of questions was repeated for each of the additional sounds. Each participant heard all six sounds, and compared each sound to the three reference sounds. Presentation order of the sounds and words was randomized.

The survey closed with demographic questions such as a participant's age, gender, how they listened to the sounds (i.e. headphones, speakers), hearing ability, and familiarity with electronics and robots.

Analysis Approach

All data were analyzed using the open source statistical software R [ 41 ], and we describe the specific methodologies below. Analysis scripts and data are included in the ACM digital library.

Appropriateness. The appropriateness data (“how well does the sound fit the robot”) were analyzed by a linear mixed model ( lmer ) using a model with sound, gender, age group (above/below 30 years old), their interaction and participant gender as factors. As this was a within subjects study, participant ID was a random effect, and all other factors were fixed effects. This model provides estimates of the differences between genders and age groups. Post-hoc tests were conducted using the Tukey-HSD method. The model was fitted using the lme4 [ 3 ] and lmerTest [ 21 ] R packages.

Check All That Apply (CATA). CATA data were analyzed using the Cochran's Q test [ 7 ] (accompanied by the Sidak procedure [ 46 ] for multiple comparisons) for identifying words that significantly distinguish sounds. Correspondence analysis (CA [ 14 ]) was applied to obtain a graphical view of the main tendencies in the data set. The output of the CA can be presented graphically as a two-dimensional map with the sounds and the words in the same plot showing which sounds are perceived as more or less related to the different words. Visually, the closer words are to the sound numbers, the more often they showed up as relevant to that sound. CA can be performed in R using CA (package FactoMineR [ 27 ]) or epCA (package ExPosition [ 4 ]). Cochran's Q testing was done using qchisq in the stats [ 41 ] package. Additional testing procedures for the CA can be obtained using the PTCA4CATA package 3 .

Polarized Sensory Positioning (PSP). Different methods can analyze data from PSP. In the original publication of PSP [ 50 ], the data were analyzed using both STATIS and multi-dimensional scaling (MDS) giving approximately the same solution. However, Multiple Factor Analysis (MFA [ 9 ]) has been applied for analyzing the data in later publications, and is the method chosen in this work.

MFA [ 9 ] is basically a PCA run on a data set where all the individual data sets are normalized and then put beside each other. MFA was performed using FactoMineR [ 27 ]. The normalization of the data sets (for each individual consumer) in MFA is done by dividing each set by its first singular value. In this way, all consumers get the same weight in the final analysis. The results from MFA can, in the same way as for CA, be displayed graphically in two-dimensional maps. MDS can also be used to process this data, as has been done to evaluate differences in musical instrument qualities [ 54 ].

Free Comments. The additional free comments given by the participants can be projected into the a similar space to the CATA words from the CA in order to interpret the components. However, as the participants tend to use different words and phrases to describe the same idea, the text needs to undergo several steps in order to obtain a simplification of the comments given, and this analysis is not included.

Comparing Data Collection Methods

MFA was also applied to compare the different types of data: the original subjective and objective measurements from [ 33 ], the CATA contingency table, and the scores (consensus) of the PSP data. We briefly introduce the objective and subjective metrics from the previous study below.

Subjective Measures. In [ 33 ], Moore et al. asked participants to evaluate pairs of sounds along several dimensions. For example, given Sound A and Sound B, which sound is more “precise”? (options: Definitely A, Probably A, No Preference/Can't Tell, Probably B, Definitely B. This was repeated along several dimensions for every possible pair of sounds, and summed to produce a subjective score for each sound along each dimension of interest.

Objective Measures. In [ 33 ], Moore et al. also analyzed the sound samples with a number of acoustic measures using MATLAB. For example, we highlight two of the objective measures that are also listed in Table 1 :

  • Spectral centroid : the mean frequency of a frequency spectrum, strongly correlated with perceptual brightness of sound (analogous to a physical object's center of mass).
  • Spectral spread : the spread of a frequency spectrum around its centroid, or center (analogous to standard deviation around the mean).

For a more thorough description of these and other objective metrics used in this analysis, see [ 25 , 33 , 39 ].

Participants and Data Filtering

Participants ( N = 300), were recruited from Prolific 4 , a crowdsourcing platform similar to Amazon Mechanical Turk focused on research, and paid $ 1.25 for an estimated 10 minute survey (actual time M = 9.8 minutes, SD = 4.2 minutes).

We then filtered participants who (1) did not use headphones as the instructions requested, and (2) did not accurately indicate identical sounds as the same on the PSP portion of the survey.

More precisely, we computed a quality score for each participant by summing the number of acceptable answers for all sound/reference comparisons. Participants’ input on the graphic slider in Figure 4 converts to a raw score from 0 (Exactly the same) to 100 (Completely different). When the sound and references were the same, an acceptable score was below 25. When the sound and references were different, an acceptable score was anything above 25. The threshold of 25 was chosen after inspection of boxplots of the raw data. Only participants with high quality scores (acceptable scores on all 18 comparisons) and who claimed to use headphones were included in the final analysis.

This heavy filtering left us with N = 101 responses. These participants represented a broad age range ( M = 31 years, SD = 10 years), and were almost balanced in gender (56% male, 44% female). 82% of participants listed English as their first language (the rest listed English as a second language), and participants were from a variety of countries: the United Kingdom (46%), the United States (29%), and the rest from Australia and other countries in Europe and North America.

A more detailed view, Figure 10 shows the loadings of each variable in the different studies. 5 DISCUSSION

These data give us a better understanding of the kinds of words and characteristics that users associate with the subtle phenomenon of motor sounds, and by looking at clusters of those associations, we can begin to interpolate characteristics that would be most appropriate to hear from a robot's motors. However not limited to robots, these methods can be used to test sonic profiles of any kind of system that will generate consequential sound, or even more broadly as a way to explore intentional sound such as beeps or voice agents.

Interpreting Results

While this sample size is not large enough to broadly generalize across all motor sounds, we offer a few observations from these data as sample interpretations from our methods.

Appropriateness. The appropriateness data indicate if a sound is suitable for the desired application, and can also suggest how much participants like a sound. This is a first level of analysis that can distinguish what sounds warrant further analysis using the CATA and PSP data.

Most of the sounds only fit the robot moderately well (scores between 5 and 7 out of 9), which makes sense considering that all sounds came from small servomotors, not a large-scale robot. The sound that fit the least, Sound 530, was also the least expensive and lowest quality servo in the set. Users effectively discerned the servo's quality from the sound alone. Consider if this quality of sound was part of a fuller, more intricate experience, it would very likely have negative impacts on a user's perception of the system.

CATA and CA. “Precise” was often selected for all sounds except Sound 530. As a result, precise also did not explain a lot of variance in the CA, and it so its circle is very small in Figure 7 . Given that so many sounds were perceived as precise, the lack of precision could be used as a quick way to rule out truly inappropriate sounds. A lack of precision is associated with other negative connotations. Around Sound 530 on Figure 7 , we see clusters of words that indicate similarities of meaning to participants, including the words “untrustworthy”, “weak”, “imprecise”, and “inexpensive”. It's clear from this cluster that participants did not like the quality of Sound 530. Likely not coincidentally, Sound 530 was also rated as the sound that fit the robot the least, and so both of these are indications that users do not desire that particular sound in this context.

PSP and Consistency with Previous Methods. The MFA plot of the PSP data generally resembles the CATA data, and when combined with MFA from the objective and subjective evaluation from the previous study (Figure 9 ), we can see substantial similarities suggesting that there are underlying characteristics that users react to in a sound. Partial replication of the previous study allows us to increase certainty that these new methods are valid in exploring this subtle phenomenon. Moreover, the methods seem to be robust to noise in the data. The analyses on the complete data set ( N = 300) and the filtered data set ( N = 101), were only marginally different.

Benefits of Methodology

Our methods rely on intuitive associations (e.g. “what words do you associate with this sound?”) whereas the previous study [ 33 ] asked participants to make more difficult decisions, such as comparing which sound was more “precise” or “trustworthy”. While we did not conduct an explicit ease of use comparison, we strongly believe that CATA and PSP methods are easier for users to use than the methods in the original study for several reasons.

With CATA, it is easier to have more descriptive words than other methods (in this case 12 vs. 6 before), though too many can cause survey fatigue. It is also easy to pull out words that best articulate differences between samples and explain the most variance. To distill these trends, we represented the CATA data using correspondence analysis (Figure 7 ). In addition, when all participants see all samples, it is easier to get profiles of individual participants that can be used in future analysis.

With PSP, comparing sounds to a set of references is a straightforward task for users, holistically evaluates the samples free from associations with other descriptors as in CATA, and identifies sounds that can span the sound space (Figure 8 ). In this case, the sounds were quite different from each other, except for Sounds 765 and 923, which were perceived as similar by most people in the PSP—however they were perceived as more different in the CATA (Figure 7 ).

Using references can also be advantageous if researchers would like to use existing, familiar samples as a baseline, such as market leaders. However this can sometimes not be ideal if participants know too much about the references than the other samples, as this can bias their judgment.

PSP is ideal for separating very similar samples, such as the case of motor sounds. This also has the advantage of not requiring a universal vocabulary to describe the samples, if such a vocabulary is not yet known. As such, PSP might be particularly effective when conducting cross-cultural research where language barriers may exist.

CATA and PSP gave consistent results to the previous data set, suggesting that researchers interested in the design of consequential sound can use whichever method is most convenient. Not all participants listed English as their first language, but there was still strong internal consistency between PSP and CATA (Figure 9 ), suggesting that both methods can achieve the same goal. Participants wrote largely positive comments at the end of the survey, describing it as enjoyable and unique.

The graphical outputs from the sensory analysis are likely to be useful beyond the scope of product evaluation. These figures, and possibly their interactive and 3-dimensional extensions, are also a good starting point for group discussions in a design or product development cycle. A typical problem in a design sprint or other design process is that it is very difficult to say what matters to the end user before the product has been built, especially if iterations are needed to get the correct design. Using rapid methods, such as the ones described in this paper, it is possible to quickly prototype and quantify user perception at high speed and at reasonable cost, and thereby bring the user's quantified opinion into the design process.

Limitations of Methodology

Though every new method has its benefits, there are also limitations to keep in mind.

The PSP method is not necessarily the most efficient experiment design, and it does not work well with large numbers of samples. We reduced the set of sound samples from 20 down to 6 to allow for a reasonable length of online survey. If we had kept with the same 20 sounds, there would have been 60 comparisons, rather than 18, which due to survey fatigue likely would have reduced data quality on a crowdsourced platform. Appropriateness of sound can also help to downselect from a large set of samples.

PSP also generally requires additional data to interpret effectively, which is why CATA complements it so well. However, the usefulness of CATA is highly dependent on the choice of words given to participants. Ideally, the words given have already been validated in prior study, but in addition CATA can be used to identify effective words that explain variance in a dataset. However, the opinions gathered are not necessarily the intuitive opinions of participants, as their association with the word may be clouded by other factors, or familiarity with the English language.

In online surveys, there is always potential for variance in data, so while this is a quick way to gather data from many people, its important to keep in mind that they may not be paying close attention to the survey. For this reason, we filtered the data heavily based on audio listening method and accuracy of comparisons with references. However this may have been more filtering than necessarily, as the results did not significantly change.

Future Work

Often, the design of sound is driven by generating many candidate sounds, as there are few sonic heuristics to guide the design process. This requires generating many sounds and evaluating each individually. To aid this process, these samples could serve as a starting point for the creation of artificial consequential sound, where the design space is wide open. For example, if a designer sought to create artificial engine sounds for electric vehicles at low speeds, these samples could serve as a starting point for a sound that conveys characteristics such as “strong” or “pleasant”, and once candidate sounds are created, these same methods could be used to fine tune the final sound and interaction. Several avenues of future work lay ahead.

First, the free comments written with the each sample can be broken down and processed with CA, which can help to fully interpret the PSP MFA beyond its consistency and similarity to the CATA results.

Second, to look at a greater number of sounds that could more easily generalize across this perceptual space, different sets of comparisons can be distributed across many different participants. While this can increase the number of samples analyzed at once, this incomplete design would lose some ability to analyze individual differences between participants.

Third, though we ensured users had working sound using an audio password, we did not use more sophisticated methods to verify they were using quality headphones, such as those proposed in [ 55 ]. This may help to get higher quality data from online platforms.

Fourth, new methods can be explored to collect and analyze data. For instance to analyze the PSP, we could compare MFA against INDSCAL (a three way variant of MDS). Other data collection methods exist that can be used as well, such as projective mapping.

Going forward, these methods can also be applied to the design of other interactions besides sound. For example, these approaches could be applied to the design of semantic-free utterances [ 56 ], voice-agent speech, visual design, and webpages.

6 CONCLUSION

We conducted an online study to evaluate how users interpreted servomotor sounds using novel methods from sensory science. CATA and PSP easily enabled untrained participants to evaluate these sounds in an online survey. Numerous visual representations of these data helped to pull out descriptors and variables that can potentially draw out distinctions in this design space. Though traditionally conducted in time-consuming, in-person studies, bringing these methods online allowed for quick data collection and exploration.

We hope these methods are useful to the HCI community as they are easy to use, mathematically rigorous, and highly sensitive to small perceptual differences. This work demonstrates not only how new analytical techniques that can be used to help understand robot motor sounds, but also more generally to make distinctions and models in areas where consumers cannot easily articulate their preferences.
