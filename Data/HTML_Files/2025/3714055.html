             Abstract
            

            

             This work introduces the concept of Embodied Measurement (EM), designed to improve the validity and inclusivity of cognitive load assessments by incorporating physical interactions that mirror mental effort. We implemented a haptic force-feedback turning knob as an alternative to traditional Likert-scale ratings and compared it with visual (mouse-based) and combined (haptic and visual) modalities. Participants completed a cognitive load task with varying difficulty levels using each modality, while biosignals such as heart rate variability, skin conductance, and pupil size were recorded to objectively assess cognitive load. In addition, qualitative feedback was gathered to explore participants’ experiences with each input method. Our findings highlight the potential of EM to offer more tangible and intuitive ways of measuring cognitive load, with the combined modality providing the most comprehensive feedback. This study contributes to human-computer interaction (HCI) research by proposing new approaches for measuring cognitive and emotional effort through physical interaction.
            

           

          

         

         

          

           

            

             1 Introduction
            

            

             On a scale from 1 to 10, how well would you rate your ability to rate your holistic experience with one single number? This question illustrates an issue that befalls the very core of user research: The reliance on self-report measures, which prompts participants to quantize their experience into a single digit. From psychometrics and test construction we know of terms like the validity or reliability of questionnaires [
             
              19
             
             ], but at the very core of rating items on a scale lies a bias or confounding factor: the ability to abstract and reflect your experience on a cognitive level and put a number on it [
             
              58
             
             ]. This abstraction process introduces biases, such as a tendency to rely on heuristics like choosing extreme categories [
             
              68
             
             ], which can distort ratings by oversimplifying complex experiences and avoiding the "hard" task of reflecting, abstracting, and giving more valid ratings.
            

            

             To combat this bias and to potentially make the process of rating one’s experience more inclusive and valid, we propose the concept of
             
              Embodied Measurement (EM)
             
             . This approach aims to make the rating of experience more tangible, by designing input devices that mirror physical sensations related to the measured construct. Alternative methods, such as tangible interfaces [
             
              1
             
             ,
             
              61
             
             ] or resistance sliders [
             
              66
             
             ], have sought to address limitations of traditional self-report measures, but have not been studied in the context of mental effort and task-related demands. In this work, we focus on the measurement of cognitive load using a haptic force-feedback turning knob, employing the metaphor of physical effort representing the mental effort. This focus reflects an attempt to reduce the cognitive demands of abstraction by providing a more direct and embodied way of expressing mental effort. The concept of EM though goes beyond that, where we envision multisensory input devices, that dynamically portray different levels of sensations associated with a given phenomenon.
            

            

             To assess the value of the EM concept for experience measurement, in particular for cognitive load, we designed and implemented a haptic force-feedback interaction and tested it against the conventional Likert-scale approach of rating items. Our primary goal was to investigate whether embodied interactions can mitigate abstraction bias and improve the validity of self-reports, particularly in the domain of cognitive load measurement. To compare the different rating modalities, we included the measurement of different biomarkers for cognitive load from different biosensors (ECG, EDA, and Eye-Tracking), and investigated which rating modality showed the best correspondence to the validated biomarkers.
            

            

             This work therefore addresses two research questions:
            

            

             

              

               •
              

              

               

                
                 RQ1:
                
                How do different input modalities (visual, haptic, and combined) affect the accuracy and ease of assessing cognitive load during mental tasks?
               

              

             

             

              

               •
              

              

               

                
                 RQ2:
                
                How do participants experience and perceive haptic feedback alone or in combination with visual feedback when rating and reflecting on tasks with varying levels of cognitive load?
               

              

             

            

            

             To answer our research questions, we employed a mixed-method approach integrating both quantitative and qualitative data. We used a cognitive load task, varying its difficulty to manipulate cognitive demand (RQ1). Participants completed the task using three input modalities: visual (mouse-based), haptic (force feedback knob), and combined (haptic and visual). We recorded biosignals (e.g., heart rate variability, skin conductance, pupil size) to objectively assess cognitive load and compare how well each modality reflected changes in these physiological markers. This multi-method approach allowed us to explore how different input modalities affect both the subjective experience of rating and the alignment of self-reports with objective measures of cognitive effort.
            

            

             Qualitative feedback was gathered through open-ended questions to explore participants’ experiences and perceptions of the usability and feedback of each modality (RQ2), especially in relation to cognitive effort in the more difficult tasks.
            

            

             This work makes several key contributions to the HCI community. First, we introduce the concept of Embodied Measurement (EM), where physical interactions like haptic force feedback mirror cognitive and emotional effort. By targeting abstraction bias, this approach offers a more tangible, inclusive alternative to traditional self-report methods, potentially enhancing both their validity and usability. Additionally, this study is the first to use force feedback for cognitive load measurement, correlating it with biosignals such as ECG, EDA, and pupillometry. This opens new possibilities for understanding how physical interactions can reflect mental effort in HCI but also broadens the design space for embodied UX measurement by incorporating multisensory feedback systems that more holistically capture user experiences.
            

           

           

            

             2 Related Work
            

            

             

              2.1 Tangible Interfaces and Multimodal Feedback
             

             

              Haptic force-feedback is ubiquitous and has contributed to enhancing user experience and immersion [
              
               33
              
              ,
              
               45
              
              ]. It is a well-established mechanism to provide users with tactile sensations in many fields, including surgery [
              
               11
              
              ,
              
               50
              
              ,
              
               63
              
              ], rehabilitation [
              
               12
              
              ,
              
               23
              
              ], robotic teleoperation [
              
               32
              
              ], gaming [
              
               17
              
              ,
              
               44
              
              ] and painting [
              
               6
              
              ].
             

             

              Multimodal feedback [
              
               52
              
              ], which integrates multiple sensory outputs such as visual, auditory, and haptic signals, is also crucial in improving accessibility, particularly for users with visual impairments [
              
               3
              
              ,
              
               67
              
              ]. In our work, we explore various feedback modalities—visual-only, haptic-only, and their combination—in supporting user interactions.
             

             

              Tangible interfaces, which allow users to manipulate physical objects to control digital information, have been widely studied [
              
               7
              
              ,
              
               22
              
              ,
              
               37
              
              ]. Depending on the context, they may serve as alternatives to graphical user interfaces [
              
               73
              
              ] or complement them [
              
               54
              
              ,
              
               71
              
              ]. For instance, SmartPuck [
              
               40
              
              ] is a multi-modal device that allows users to interact with digital information on a tabletop display. While both, SmartPuck and our proposed embodied measurement approach (see
              
               3.1
              
              ), use an actuated wheel, SmartPuck generates a clicking sensation and limits rotational movement. In contrast, we evaluated a haptic pattern with increasing torque from minimum to maximum when collecting cognitive load ratings.
             

             

              Tangible embodied devices have been previously developed for subjective, quantitative self-reporting [
              
               1
              
              ,
              
               55
              
              ,
              
               57
              
              ]. These devices provide an alternative to traditional surveys by enabling users to physically interact with an object to give ratings. Stevens et al. [
              
               62
              
              ] used a hand-held device and a stylus to continuously collect engagement ratings. VoxBox [
              
               31
              
              ] for adults and SmallTalk [
              
               30
              
              ] for kids were introduced to gather opinions in a playful, engaging way.
             

             

              More closely related is the work by Stamm et al. [
              
               61
              
              ] who evaluated two continuous self-report devices—a mechanical slider and a stress ball—by examining their correlation with physiological arousal. While the stress ball provides tactile sensation when tension is applied or released, their work did not specifically explore haptic force feedback or other modalities, such as visual output. Berkel et al. [
              
               66
              
              ] conducted a comprehensive evaluation of six self-report devices, including a resistance slider that uses elastic bands, although they did not investigate correlations with biosignals.
             

             

              Despite these advances, previous work focused on certain dimensions and, in particular, did not extensively explore the tangible measurement of cognitive load as a target metric. Our work builds on these efforts by introducing the
              
               EM
              
              concept, which uses a haptic force-feedback mechanism to capture cognitive effort. In our study, we examined a tangible embodied device with multimodal feedback mechanisms and correlated these subjective self-reports with biosignal data.
             

            

            

             

              2.2 Biosignals & Cognitive Load
             

             

              Biosignals have become essential in cognitive load research, especially within HCI [
              
               25
              
              ]. Various physiological signals, such as electrocardiogram (ECG), electrodermal activity (EDA), and pupillometry, provide valuable insights into users’ mental states during tasks. This section provides an overview of how these biosignals are linked to cognitive load and highlights their strengths, limitations, and applications in real-time user experience studies.
             

             

              Among the most commonly used biosignals in cognitive load research are ECG, EDA, and pupillometry. These physiological markers are considered reliable indicators of cognitive and emotional states, particularly in response to task complexity [
              
               59
              
              ]. Heart rate variability (HRV) and heart rate (HR) is often used to assess changes in autonomic nervous system activity, reflecting the balance between sympathetic (fight or flight) and parasympathetic (rest and digest) responses [
              
               42
              
              ]. Metrics such as RMSSD, SDNN, and LF/HF ratios have shown strong correlations with mental workload and stress, e.g. in real-life application like occupational training [
              
               64
              
              ,
              
               65
              
              ,
              
               72
              
              ]. EDA measures skin conductance changes related to sweat gland activity. It is commonly used to assess arousal and stress [
              
               13
              
              ]. Pupil dilation is a direct and sensitive marker of cognitive load [
              
               26
              
              ].
             

             

              There is substantial evidence connecting cognitive load with physiological markers, making biosignals an integral part of cognitive load research. HRV decreases with increased cognitive load, as the sympathetic nervous system activates in response to task demands. A lower HRV indicates heightened mental effort, particularly during complex or multi-tasking scenarios. EDA has also been shown to rise with increased task difficulty. The frequency and amplitude of skin conductance responses (SCR) reflect arousal levels, which often increase alongside cognitive load. Pupillometry provides a dynamic, real-time indicator of cognitive load. Rather than focusing solely on absolute pupil dilation, the Low-High Index of Pupillary Activity (LHIPA) emphasizes fluctuations in pupil size, capturing the balance between high- and low-frequency components of pupil changes [
              
               24
              
              ,
              
               25
              
              ]. This metric has proven effective in tracking cognitive effort, with lower LHIPA values signaling greater mental strain during challenging tasks. By analyzing these rapid shifts in pupil dynamics, LHIPA offers a more nuanced and sensitive reflection of cognitive load compared to traditional pupil dilation measures.
             

             

              While biosignals provide valuable insights into cognitive load, they are not without limitations. HRV is reliable for long-term monitoring but can be influenced by factors such as emotional states or physical activity, complicating short-term analysis. EDA is sensitive to immediate changes in arousal, but environmental factors such as room temperature and humidity can introduce noise into the data. Pupillometry provides real-time feedback on cognitive load but can be affected by external lighting conditions [
              
               53
              
              ], making data normalization crucial in real-world settings.
             

             

              While biosignals offer valuable insights into cognitive load, they have been critiqued by researchers with qualitative or ethnographic perspectives for focusing too narrowly on measurable, objective data. These perspectives argue that physiological markers alone don’t capture the full subjective experience of cognitive effort. This is where embodied measurement may add value, providing participants with a tangible way to express their cognitive load, thereby offering a more holistic view.
             

             

              Electroencephalography (EEG) is widely used in cognitive load research due to its high temporal resolution and ability to capture neural activity, offering insights into mental workload and task complexity [
              
               2
              
              ,
              
               20
              
              ]. However, we excluded EEG from this study for several reasons. While conducted in a controlled environment, the study aims to evaluate Embodied Measurement’s (EM) potential for real-world usability applications. EEG setups often involve lengthy preparation, such as electrode cap fitting and signal acquisition, which can disrupt iterative usability contexts. Additionally, EEG is sensitive to movement artifacts and noise, limiting its adaptability to less controlled settings [
              
               43
              
              ], and requires complex preprocessing [
              
               41
              
              ]. In contrast, the biosignals chosen for this study—ECG, EDA, and pupillometry—offer a balance of reliability, ease of use, and applicability to usability studies. These methods are faster to set up, less invasive, and more practical for exploring EM’s feasibility in future usability evaluations. While EEG could provide additional granularity, this study prioritized biosignals that align with its practical objectives.
             

            

            

             

              2.3 Limits of Questionnaires
             

             

              Self-report questionnaires, such as Likert scales, have long been a cornerstone of subjective measurement in psychology and human-computer interaction (HCI). Their benefits include ease of administration, standardization, and the ability to collect large amounts of data efficiently. Additionally, validated scales offer the advantage of being grounded in established psychometric frameworks, providing reliable and interpretable measures of multidimensional constructs [
              
               19
              
              ]. Tools like the NASA TLX for cognitive load measurement [
              
               34
              
              ] or the System Usability Scale (SUS) for usability evaluations [
              
               14
              
              ] have demonstrated their utility across diverse applications, offering comparability and consistency in subjective reporting.
             

             

              However, these methods are prone to a number of limitations. Common biases, such as social desirability, extreme responding, and central tendency bias, can distort responses and lead to inaccurate representations of participants’ true experiences [
              
               8
              
              ]. For instance, respondents may choose extreme answers when unsure or may avoid them altogether, sticking to more neutral options [
              
               27
              
              ].
             

             

              Another significant issue lies in the challenge of accurately communicating subjective feelings. Participants often struggle to express complex, nuanced experiences using predefined rating scales, which may not always capture the full depth of subjective experience. While validated questionnaires use multiple items and factor analysis to measure multidimensional constructs, requiring participants to reflect on and categorize their experiences across different dimensions, the process of introspection and abstraction still places cognitive demands on respondents [
              
               4
              
              ]. Validated multi-item constructs, such as NASA-TLX, attempt to mitigate some introspection biases by distributing ratings across multiple dimensions and reducing reliance on singular retrospective judgments. However, participants may still face challenges in quantifying subjective states, as numerical and categorical formats do not always align with internal perception. Introspection itself can be biased by memory reconstruction and heuristic shortcuts, further complicating self-reports [
              
               36
              
              ].
             

             

              In HCI, the problem of abstraction is further highlighted by the concept of qualia, which refers to rich, subjective experiences that are difficult to fully capture through standardized, numerical categories [
              
               35
              
              ]. For instance, the qualitative nature of cognitive effort or frustration during a task may be reduced when translated into a single number on a scale. While multidimensional constructs can be captured by validated questionnaires using multiple items, the challenge remains in abstracting complex experiences into simplified responses. Cognitive biases, such as those outlined by Kahneman [
              
               39
              
              ], can also influence responses, as participants may rely on heuristics such as anchoring or availability, which may affect the accuracy of their self-reports. Furthermore, the cognitive demands of abstracting complex experiences can lead to errors, particularly when participants rely on mental shortcuts during rapid decision-making.
             

             

              While traditional questionnaires are susceptible to various biases, such as social desirability and extreme responding, this work specifically focuses on addressing the challenge of abstraction. Social desirability, recall bias, and other response biases remain relevant issues in self-report methods, but they are outside the scope of this study. Instead, our concept of Embodied Measurement aims to tackle the cognitive demands of abstraction by providing participants with a more intuitive, tangible mechanism for expressing their experiences. Research in embodied cognition suggests that interacting with physical objects can offload cognitive processing, reducing the need for purely mental introspection and numerical reasoning [
              
               46
              
              ,
              
               69
              
              ]. Similarly, cognitive psychology has shown that introspection is often unreliable, as people have limited access to their own internal states and frequently construct retrospective explanations based on assumptions rather than direct experience [
              
               49
              
              ,
              
               70
              
              ]. By grounding subjective ratings in physical interactions, such as haptic feedback or tangible interfaces, Embodied Measurement leverages sensorimotor processing to reduce cognitive strain, making subjective reporting more intuitive and potentially more accurate [
              
               38
              
              ,
              
               46
              
              ,
              
               69
              
              ]. Prior work on tangible self-report interfaces has demonstrated that physical engagement—such as squeezing, pressing, or turning a dial—can provide a more natural and accessible way to externalize subjective states compared to introspective questionnaires, such as pain or emotional intensity [
              
               1
              
              ,
              
               29
              
              ]. However, for more conceptual constructs, validated multi-item questionnaires may still be necessary for capturing distinct cognitive and contextual dimensions. By reducing reliance on post-hoc recall and numerical abstraction, Embodied Measurement complements traditional self-report methods, offering an alternative way to capture subjective experiences while maintaining the benefits of validated multi-item scales for structured assessment.
             

            

            

             

              2.4 Research Gap
             

             

              Despite extensive research on multimodal feedback, biosignal-based cognitive load measurement, and self-report questionnaires, key gaps persist. Tangible interactions like haptic feedback are rarely explored as direct reflections of cognitive effort. While biosignals are established indicators of cognitive load, few studies have compared these objective measures to novel input methods. Additionally, traditional questionnaires suffer from abstraction biases. With the
              
               EM
              
              concept, we introduce the use of a haptic force-feedback mechanism to capture cognitive effort, evaluated in conjunction with physiological measurements. The novelty lies in the combination of haptic feedback and the use of established biomarkers as a benchmark to evaluate how well haptic and tangible input methods reflect cognitive load compared to traditional visual ratings, aiming for a more reliable and intuitive measurement.

             3 Concept & Design
            

            

             While validated multi-item self-report scales attempt to mitigate introspection biases by structuring responses across multiple dimensions, they still require participants to reflect, categorize, and quantify their experiences, introducing cognitive effort and potential inconsistencies [
             
              36
             
             ]. Abstraction bias, in particular, remains a fundamental challenge in self-report, as individuals must map their internal states onto predefined scales, often leading to distortions in how subjective experiences are captured [
             
              49
             
             ,
             
              70
             
             ]. This issue is compounded when assessing dynamic or nuanced experiences, such as cognitive load, where self-report relies on post-task recall and interpretation rather than direct expression [
             
              38
             
             ].
            

            

             To address this challenge, we introduce Embodied Measurement (EM), a tangible approach that translates self-report ratings into physical interactions rather than abstract numerical estimations. Research in embodied cognition suggests that sensorimotor engagement can reduce cognitive effort by enabling individuals to express subjective states through intuitive physical actions rather than introspection alone [
             
              46
             
             ,
             
              69
             
             ]. Studies on tangible self-reporting further support this, demonstrating that haptic and physical controls can improve reporting accuracy and ease cognitive burden by leveraging natural sensorimotor mappings [
             
              1
             
             ,
             
              29
             
             ]. For example, squeeze-based interfaces for pain self-reporting have been shown to enhance response accuracy and reduce abstraction bias by enabling users to express intensity through direct physical manipulation [
             
              1
             
             ].
            

            

             Building on these principles, we chose cognitive load as the first construct to be translated into an embodied form of rating. Prior research by Ouwehand et al. [
             
              51
             
             ] explored symbolic weight representations for cognitive load measurement, suggesting that physical metaphors can enhance the intuitive expression of effort. Inspired by this, we employ haptic force feedback as a direct, interactive representation of cognitive effort, allowing participants to engage with the rating process in a more sensorimotor-based rather than purely introspective manner.
            

            

             Haptic force feedback has been widely adopted in fields such as gaming (e.g., force-feedback steering wheels [
             
              48
             
             ]) and virtual reality training [
             
              18
             
             ], demonstrating its effectiveness in providing intuitive, embodied interaction. For our study, we selected a programmable force-feedback turning knob, the ’KeWheel’
             
              
               1
              
             
             , which enables precise, scalable control of haptic resistance patterns. This design allows us to test whether tangible, force-based input can enhance the accuracy and usability of cognitive load reporting compared to traditional numerical scales. Further details on the implementation and bias mitigation strategies of the haptic force-feedback design are provided in Section
             
              3.3
             
             .
            

            

             

              3.1 Force-Feedback Turning Knob: The KeWheel
             

             

              The KeWheel, developed by KEBA, served as the core device for prototyping our embodied measurement approach (see Figure
              
               2
              
              b). The KeWheel was selected for its flexibility and ability to provide precise haptic feedback. Unlike traditional input devices, the KeWheel allows for customizable torque, resistance, and tactile sensations, making it ideal for prototyping various force-feedback patterns. It is a fully adaptive turning and push button knob designed to combine multiple control elements, such as handwheels, operating mode selectors, and user interface inputs, into a single device. At the heart of the KeWheel is the HAPTICORE library, which enables the dynamic generation of various sensory effects and torque stages. These can be customized in real-time, allowing the device to produce different feedback patterns, such as ripples, blockages, or torque adjustments. For example, the turning knob can simulate barriers at specific points, enforce stops at critical positions, or provide resistance that scales with the current angle. In our implementation, the KeWheel was dynamically adjusted through an interface to the Unity game engine, allowing for real-time changes in torque, the introduction of "ticks" for fine-grained control, and the application of barriers at key points on the scale.
             

            

            

             

              3.2 Prototyping & Pilot Study
             

             

              To determine a suitable force-feedback pattern, we conducted an internal prototyping workshop with N = 6 HCI experts. During this workshop, various multi-modal representations of cognitive load were brainstormed, e.g. a tone getting more and more high-pitched representing increasing cognitive load, increasing heat, but also increasing force-feedback including more and more chaotic vibrations. With this, we wanted to open the design space for embodied measurement for potential future implementations of the concept.
             

             

              In the second part of the workshop, we introduced the KeWheel to the participants. Using the HAPTICORE demo application, participants could interactively program force feedback modes, using combinations of torque, barriers, and ticks in different variations, to get an understanding of the capabilities of the device.
             

             

              Once participants felt they had explored all functionalities of the KeWheel, we asked them to fill out schematic templates of the turning knob and sketch force-feedback patterns based on their experiences. The template and the drawings of one participant are shown in Figure
              
               1
              
              ). A final group discussion then led to a preference voting of all provided patterns, which resulted in the one we used in the subsequent study to test our hypotheses (Figure
              
               1
              
              c). The barriers were set at 180°, with the torque steadily increasing from left to right, making it increasingly harder to fully turn. On top of that, 20 ticks (i.e., small haptic ripples) with the same intensity were added (corresponding to 21 levels of the NASA TLX scales [
              
               34
              
              ]), to give a feeling of how far one has turned.
             

            

            

             

              3.3 Addressing Potential Bias
             

             

              One concern with the haptic force-feedback rating method is that the increasing torque required to rank higher numbers could bias participants toward lower ratings due to the additional physical effort involved. To mitigate this, torque levels were calibrated to remain moderate, avoiding excessive effort that could cause discomfort or fatigue. This calibration was informed by participant feedback during the prototyping workshop, where users adjusted settings to ensure natural and manageable interaction.
             

             

              Participants were also given a familiarization phase before the study, allowing them to practice with the haptic knob and become comfortable with its operation. These design choices balanced physical engagement with usability, minimizing the likelihood of systematic bias. Although higher ratings required more physical effort, this effort was kept moderate to preserve the validity of the measurement process.
             

             

              Beyond potential rating biases, haptic force feedback was specifically introduced as a mechanism to support reflection on cognitive effort. Research in cognitive psychology suggests that awareness of one’s effort level plays a key role in metacognition, as recognizing increased difficulty can prompt self-regulation and adaptive strategies [
              
               28
              
              ,
              
               38
              
              ]. Haptic force feedback provides a tangible proxy for mental effort, making an otherwise invisible cognitive process more perceptible through physical resistance [
              
               5
              
              ,
              
               69
              
              ]. Prior studies on tangible self-reporting have demonstrated that force-based interactions can enhance awareness and reporting accuracy, particularly in domains where introspection alone is prone to bias [
              
               1
              
              ,
              
               29
              
              ]. Participants were informed that the resistance in the knob corresponded to cognitive effort levels, ensuring they understood the mapping between force feedback and mental workload.
             

             

              Another potential issue relates to the rotation mapping of the KeWheel to the NASA TLX scale. The knob’s default starting position was set to 0, corresponding to "low" on the scale. This design reflects the common heuristic of reading scales from left to right, aligning with participants’ expectations. While this setup could potentially prime participants toward lower ratings, no participants reported such issues during post-study interviews. Future work could explore alternative starting positions, such as a neutral midpoint, to examine their effects on rating behavior.
             

             

              Lastly, this study focused on evaluating Embodied Measurement (EM) as a novel approach to experience rating by assessing its potential to align subjective ratings with biosignals. To maintain focus, a single haptic feedback pattern was tested, enabling a controlled assessment of its interaction with biosignals. Future research should expand on this by exploring diverse feedback patterns to deepen our understanding of the design space for haptic rating systems. Nonetheless, the current study provides a foundation for evaluating EM’s feasibility in integrating subjective and objective measures of cognitive load.
             

            

           

           

            

             4 Study
            

            

             The study aimed to explore the relationship between subjective cognitive load ratings and physiological markers across three interaction modalities (Visual, Haptic, Combined). Specifically, we examined how electrocardiogram (ECG), electrodermal activity (EDA), and pupil size data align with self-reported cognitive load during tasks in the different modalities.

               4.1.2 Task.
              

              

               The cognitive load task in this experiment was based on a mental arithmetic task adapted from Siegenthaler et al. [
               
                60
               
               ]. The task was designed to manipulate cognitive load through varying levels of difficulty in mental counting. For the easy condition, participants were instructed to mentally count upwards in steps of two, starting from a randomly generated three-digit number. For the difficult condition, participants had to mentally count downwards in steps of 17, starting from a randomly generated four-digit number. This manipulation of task difficulty allowed us to explore the effects of increased cognitive load on participants’ performance and engagement with the input modalities.
              

             

            

            

             

              4.2 Methods
             

             

              The study followed a 2 (task difficulty: easy, hard) × 3 (modality: combined, haptic-only, visual-only) within-subjects design. Each participant completed three blocks, one per modality, with the order of modalities and task difficulty within each block randomized and counterbalanced to control for order effects. Each block began with a short rest period, followed by two tasks (one easy and one hard). See Figure
              
               4
              
              a and Section
              
               4.2.3
              
              for details on the timings and procedure. Physiological data were continuously recorded throughout task performance, while subjective cognitive load ratings were collected at the end of each block using the NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX). This design allowed for direct comparisons between physiological responses and subjective ratings across the different modalities and task difficulties.
             

             

              

               4.2.1 Participants.
              

              

               A total of 45 participants of varied ages (M = 44.9, SD = 14.7), balanced gender distribution (22 female, 22 male, 1 non-binary), and diverse educational backgrounds (9 with lower secondary education or below, 6 with upper secondary or post-secondary non-tertiary education, 11 with a bachelor’s, 14 with a master’s, and 5 with a doctoral or equivalent degree) were recruited through an internal participant pool used for various research studies. They received a remuneration for participation and signed an informed consent before participating. Regarding eye-tracking, for inclusivity, we did not exclude participants wearing glasses but asked them to wear contact lenses if possible. This resulted in poor data quality for N = 4 participants, leading to the exclusion of their eye-tracking data from the analysis. A total of 4 participants were left-handed. To accommodate them, we switched the KeWheel’s location to the left side, ensuring usability for their dominant hand. However, the turning direction of the knob was not adapted for left-handed use. None of the left-handed participants reported difficulties during the familiarization phase or the study, but this aspect may have introduced subtle variability in their interaction.
              

             

             

              

               4.2.2 Measurements.
              

              

               All biosignals, including ECG, EDA, and pupillometry, were collected and synchronized using the iMotions research platform
               
                
                 2
                
               
               . The platform facilitated the real-time acquisition and integration of physiological data from multiple devices, ensuring the precise synchronization of biosignals with task events and subjective assessments.
              

              

               
                ECG
               
               . Electrocardiogram (ECG) data were recorded using the Polar H10 sensor
               
                
                 3
                
               
               , which captures heart activity at a sampling rate of 130 Hz. This allows for analysis of heart rate variability (HRV) metrics, which have been shown to closely correlate with cognitive load in previous research. Heart rate (HR) typically increases with increased cognitive load due to the activation of the sympathetic nervous system in response to stress or mental effort. In addition to HR, we extracted various HRV metrics, including time-domain measures such as SDNN (Standard Deviation of NN intervals) and RMSSD (Root Mean Square of Successive Differences). These metrics are commonly used to assess the balance between sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous system activity, providing insight into cognitive and emotional demands. The Polar H10 was connected to the recording PC through LabStreamingLayer to synchronize the ECG data with other physiological and task-related data streams.
              

              

               
                EDA
               
               . EDA data were recorded using the Shimmer3 GSR+ unit
               
                
                 4
                
               
               at a sampling rate of 128 Hz. EDA, which reflects the activity of the sweat glands controlled by the sympathetic nervous system, is commonly used to assess cognitive load. The primary EDA metrics include Skin Conductance Level (SCL), representing the baseline skin conductance, which tends to increase with higher cognitive load, and Skin Conductance Response (SCR), referring to transient spikes in conductance following a stimulus. Higher SCR frequency and amplitude typically indicate greater cognitive demands. Additionally, the Number of Skin Conductance Responses (NSCR), representing the frequency of these responses, is a key metric, where an elevated count suggests increased cognitive load. The Shimmer3 GSR+ was connected via Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) to the recording PC and synchronized with the iMotions software for data collection and analysis.
              

              

               
                Pupillometry
               
               . Pupillometry was measured using the Tobii Glasses 3
               
                
                 5
                
               
               , which record eye gaze and pupillometry data at a sampling rate of 50 Hz. Each participant completed a calibration before the experiment. The extracted pupil size data was of interest due to prior work by Duchowski et al. [
               
                24
               
               ,
               
                25
               
               ], who developed an index of pupillary activity, which serves as an indicator for cognitive load. The data was recorded by attaching the glasses recording unit to the PC via LAN, enabling synchronization with the other signals via the iMotions experimental software.
              

              

               
                NASA TLX Ratings
               
               . Subjective cognitive load ratings were collected using the NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) and saved as a CSV file through the Unity platform, with each entry matched to the corresponding participant via a unique user ID. The recorded data included the six subscale ratings (mental demand, physical demand, temporal demand, performance, effort, and frustration), the time spent completing the ratings, task descriptions, task difficulty, and block number. This structured data allowed for precise matching with the physiological biosignals and experimental conditions, enabling detailed comparisons of subjective workload ratings across the three modalities (Combined, Haptic, and Visual).

               4.2.3 Procedure.
              

              

               Participants arrived at the university office, where they signed an informed consent form received via email before the session. After a brief introduction to the study’s goals, they completed a demographic questionnaire and were equipped with physiological sensors for eye-tracking, electrodermal activity (EDA), and electrocardiogram (ECG) recording. The sensors were synchronized via the iMotions research platform to ensure continuous data collection.
              

              

               Before starting the experimental tasks, participants were familiarized with the haptic feedback knob, which they used in the Haptic and Combined modalities. They were encouraged to try the interaction patterns to ensure comfort and understanding of the system.
              

              

               The experiment consisted of three blocks, each corresponding to a different modality: Combined (visual + haptic), Haptic-only, and Visual-only. The order of the blocks and the task difficulty (easy or hard) within each block were randomized and counterbalanced across participants. Each block consisted of a sequence of tasks, as shown in the schematic overview (see Figure
               
                4
               
               ).
              

              

               Each block began with a 30-second fixation point explicitly designed as the
               
                control condition
               
               . This period provided a baseline for physiological stabilization, particularly for pupillometry data, to ensure consistent reference points for measuring cognitive load changes during task performance. Metrics such as pupil dilation and physiological arousal during the tasks were compared against this control baseline.
              

              

               Participants then received a task description (easy or hard), such as mental counting, which they completed over the course of 30 seconds. The task was followed by an answer screen, where participants entered their responses. After completing the task, participants rated their cognitive load using the NASA TLX on a modality-specific variant of the rating screen, enabling the collection of subjective workload data specific to each modality relative to the baseline. This process was repeated for two tasks within each block.
              

              

               In the Results section, changes in physiological and subjective metrics were assessed relative to this control condition, allowing for a consistent point of comparison across blocks and modalities.
              

              

               At the conclusion of the experiment, participants were debriefed, and the sensors were removed. A brief interview was conducted to gather additional qualitative feedback on the tasks and modalities.
              

             

            

            

             

              4.3 Data Analysis
             

             

              The biosensor recordings were annotated within the iMotions software, marking the start and end times for the task, control, and rating windows. The synchronized data export from iMotions was processed and analyzed using Python libraries such as Pandas, Seaborn, Neurokit2, and SciPy. The control phases, consisting of 30-second baseline periods at the start of each block, were specifically marked to provide reference physiological measures for comparison with task phases.
             

             

              EDA and ECG data were cleaned using Neurokit2. Following bandpass (ECG) and low-pass (EDA) filtering, ECG and EDA metrics were extracted for each task and control phase using Neurokit2 functions. The
              
               ecg_analyze()
              
              function computes relevant features and indices from ECG data, such as heart rate variability measures, while the
              
               eda_analyze()
              
              function extracts skin conductance responses and tonic levels. These functions facilitate the extraction of meaningful physiological metrics from complex signal data.
             

             

              For pupil data, the Low-High Index of Pupillary Activity (LHIPA), as described by Duchowski et al. [
              
               24
              
              ], was employed to capture cognitive load through pupil dynamics. Blink artifacts were removed. After cleaning, wavelet decomposition was applied to the pupil diameter signal, splitting it into low- and high-frequency components. After normalization, LHIPA was then calculated as the frequency of significant transitions per second, offering a direct measure of cognitive load based on pupil activity.
             

             

              The processing steps resulted in a final data frame containing ECG, EDA, and LHIPA metrics per participant for each phase of the experiment. These phases included three control phases (30-second baseline periods at the start of each block) and three blocks of two tasks each, categorized by task difficulty (easy or hard). The control phases provided baseline physiological measures, which were used as a ground truth to assess changes in cognitive load during the task phases. Specifically, the mean values from the control periods served as a reference point for evaluating task-related biomarkers, enabling a comparison between baseline and task conditions in the results analysis (see Section
              
               5.1.3
              
              ). Descriptive statistics between modalities and difficulty levels were calculated, along with correlations between NASA TLX cognitive load ratings and the extracted biomarkers, to evaluate how well subjective ratings from the three input modalities aligned with physiological markers.
             

             

              To ensure the validity of all ANOVA analyses performed in this study, we conducted assumption checks for normality and homogeneity of variances of all NASA ratings across the three modalities and for the extracted biomarkers. Shapiro-Wilk tests for normality were conducted for each combination of modality and difficulty. While some groups deviated from normality, visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots suggested that these deviations were not extreme, with data distributions appearing approximately symmetrical. Levene’s test for homogeneity of variance across modalities confirmed that the assumption of equal variances were met. Furthermore, as ANOVA is generally robust to minor deviations from normality [
              
               10
              
              ], the parametric approach was retained for subsequent analyses.

               5.1.1 NASA TLX Ratings.
              

              

               The NASA TLX ratings revealed interesting patterns across modalities and task difficulty levels. On a descriptive level, participants rated their cognitive load slightly higher using the visual modality compared to the haptic and combined modalities, particularly in the hard task condition. For example, in the hard condition, participants gave higher ratings in the visual modality (M = 12.65, SD = 3.92) compared to the combined modality (M = 11.59, SD = 4.65) and the haptic modality (M = 11.52, SD = 4.72).
              

              

               Results from the two-way ANOVA indicated no statistically significant differences in cognitive load ratings across the three modalities (F(2, 90) = 0.97, p = 0.384). This finding supports the notion that the increased physical effort required in the haptic condition did not systematically bias participants toward lower ratings. However, the main effect of task difficulty was highly significant (F(1, 45) = 116.40, p < 0.0001), showing that participants rated the hard tasks as significantly more demanding than the easy tasks. The interaction between modality and difficulty was not significant (F(2, 90) = 1.88, p = 0.159), suggesting that the effect of task difficulty on cognitive load ratings did not vary significantly between the modalities.
              

              

               Thus, while descriptive trends suggest participants rated tasks higher in the visual modality, these differences were not statistically significant.

Significant effects of task difficulty were observed for two key measures: the number of skin conductance responses (SCR Peaks N) and the Low-High Index of Pupillary Activity (LHIPA).

However, no significant difference was found between the Easy and Hard conditions. This highlights the sensitivity of LHIPA in detecting differences between baseline and task-related cognitive load but points to limitations in resolving finer differences between difficulty levels.

              5.3 Lessons Learned from Qualitative Feedback
             

             

              For the analysis of the interview transcripts, we employed an inductive categorization approach based on Mayring’s principles of qualitative content analysis [
              
               47
              
              ]. This approach allowed for a systematic exploration of participants’ responses to the open-ended questions, enabling the identification of emerging themes directly from the data, free from the limitations of predefined categories.
             

             

              A prominent theme is the
              
               reflection of cognitive load through haptic feedback
              
              . Participants who favored haptic feedback found that it effectively mirrored their cognitive effort, with the increasing resistance of the haptic device providing a physical manifestation of the difficulty of the task. One participant mentioned, “The haptic feedback and resistance captured my mental load very well” (ID 133), while another remarked, “The physical feedback from the turning knob reflected how hard the task felt” (ID 125). This sentiment was echoed by five participants, indicating that haptic feedback, especially when combined with visual cues, could serve as an effective tool for reflecting the mental demands placed on users.
             

             

              In the qualitative data, a clear
              
               preference for combined haptic and visual feedback
              
              was found. Participants indicated that the integration of both types of feedback provided a more intuitive and reassuring experience. This combination allowed them to better perceive and adjust their input through both tactile and visual cues, increasing their confidence in the accuracy of their ratings. One participant stated, "The combination of haptic and visual feedback is fascinating. The haptic element captures the physical effort much better than a traditional scale, while the visual feedback remains crucial for orientation. The resistance and physical effort of the haptic feedback strongly mirrors the mental strain." (ID 129), while another appreciated the synergy of the two modalities, remarking, “Haptic and visual together helped me accurately reflect my feelings” (ID 121). In total, 62% of participants favored this dual-modality approach, indicating that combining haptic and visual feedback could provide a more reliable and user-friendly method for input during cognitively demanding tasks.

               ease of interaction using the mouse

A total of eight participants provided such feedback, emphasizing areas where the input devices could be refined to better suit user needs and improve overall functionality.
             

            

           

           

            

             6 Discussion
            

            

             This study investigated the potential of Embodied Measurement (EM) in cognitive load assessment by comparing visual, haptic, and combined rating modalities. The use of biosignals such as electrocardiogram (ECG), electrodermal activity (EDA), and pupillometry provided an objective measure of cognitive load, allowing us to correlate these physiological markers with subjective self-reports. Our findings offer several key insights, with implications for both human-computer interaction (HCI) research and practical applications in cognitive load measurement.
            

            

             One of the main contributions of this work is the introduction of haptic force-feedback as a means to reflect mental effort. The combined haptic and visual modality appeared to be the most engaging and reflective of participants’ experiences, based on qualitative feedback and modest trends in correspondence with physiological markers. As participants interacted with the haptic turning knob, the increasing resistance mirrored the difficulty of the mental task, providing a physical manifestation of cognitive strain. While no statistically significant differences were found in self-reported cognitive load between modalities, participant feedback indicated that the combined modality offered the clearest representation of their mental effort. This is consistent with prior research suggesting that embodied interactions can enhance user engagement and reflect mental states more accurately than abstract rating scales alone [
             
              1
             
             ,
             
              56
             
             ]. When looking at the NASA TLX ratings, no significant differences were found between modalities, suggesting that the KeWheel does not introduce systematic biases. However, slight offsets toward lower ratings were observed in the haptic condition, which could reflect individual variability or the physical effort required to interact with the knob. These offsets were minor and not statistically significant, but they warrant further investigation in future work.
            

            

             This notion was supported by the qualitative feedback we gathered, with participants reporting increased reflection on their feelings during rating, and also by the fact that 71% chose one form of the haptic rating modality (Combined or Haptic only). Haptic feedback appeared to mitigate abstraction bias by grounding participants’ ratings in a physical interaction, reducing the cognitive effort required to reflect and quantify their mental state numerically. This abstraction process can be cognitively demanding and prone to biases such as extreme responding or social desirability bias [
             
              15
             
             ,
             
              68
             
             ]. In fact, several participants reported that the increasing resistance of the knob provided a tangible representation of the mental effort they were experiencing. This aligns with theories of embodied cognition, which posit that physical actions and sensory experiences can enhance cognitive processes by grounding abstract concepts in tangible interactions.
            

            

             By embodying the mental load in a physical form, haptic feedback offered users a more intuitive way to express their effort, reducing the need for cognitive abstraction often required by traditional Likert scales. Instead of converting their experience into numerical ratings, participants could "feel" the difficulty of the task as they rated it, leading to what some described as a more reflective and accurate representation of their mental state. While this mitigates abstraction bias, it introduces its own potential trade-offs, such as physical effort bias, which could subtly influence ratings. This trade-off highlights the importance of balancing abstraction reduction with usability considerations in embodied measurement systems. This supports the idea that embodied measurement, through tangible interactions, could reduce some of the biases typically associated with self-reporting, such as abstraction bias or cognitive overload during rating.
            

            

             At the same time, the 23% who preferred the conventional rating method mostly mentioned habit as their main reason. As seen in the rating times, a clear learning effect was visible after 2 blocks of the experiment, indicating that habituation to this novel form of rating could be achieved rather quickly. This finding has important implications for the real-world application of force-feedback devices like the KeWheel in UX assessments. While initial interactions may require more time as users familiarize themselves with the tactile experience, the learning curve appears to be relatively shallow. Once users adapt to the physical interaction, they can perform tasks with comparable efficiency to traditional input methods. In practical settings, this suggests that haptic feedback could be seamlessly integrated into UX measurement tools with minimal training. However, usability considerations—such as offering clear instructions or a guided onboarding phase—should be emphasized at the beginning to minimize initial interaction difficulties.
            

            

             These findings also suggest several promising avenues for adapting embodied measurement to real-world scenarios. For example, in UX evaluations, the use of haptic feedback could complement traditional usability testing methods by providing a more intuitive way for participants to articulate cognitive load during complex workflows or product interactions. Similarly, accessibility tools could leverage haptic feedback to assist individuals with visual impairments, offering an inclusive mechanism for self-reporting mental effort. By bridging the gap between subjective experiences and tangible input mechanisms, embodied measurement has the potential to enhance evaluation processes across diverse domains.
            

            

             Our results further highlight the sensitivity of certain biosignals to cognitive load. In particular, both the number of skin conductance responses (SCR Peaks N) and the Low-High Index of Pupillary Activity (LHIPA) showed significant differences between control and task conditions. This aligns with existing research, which has established that EDA and pupillometry are reliable markers of cognitive load, with higher task demands typically resulting in increased skin conductance and pupil dilation. Interestingly, LHIPA could not differentiate between the easy and the hard task. Though as expected in direction (LHIPA
             
              Easy
             
             = 1.92, LHIPA
             
              Hard
             
             = 1.86), no significant difference could be found, which is in line with the results from Duchovski at al.[
             
              24
             
             ]. Other biosignals such as HRV RMSSD did not show significant differences between difficulty levels, despite following the expected trends. This may be due to the short duration of the tasks or individual variability in autonomic nervous system responses. While HRV metrics such as RMSSD are typically associated with cognitive load, their sensitivity can be affected by other factors such as emotional states. This limitation has been noted in previous studies, where HRV is more reliable for long-term monitoring than for detecting short-term changes in cognitive load [
             
              9
             
             ].
            

            

             One key finding from our study was the difficulty some participants experienced with the haptic-only modality, particularly in the absence of visual confirmation. While haptic feedback provided a tactile representation of cognitive load, several users reported uncertainty regarding the precision of their input. This suggests that haptic feedback alone may not provide enough clarity for users who are accustomed to visual cues. The lack of visual feedback made it harder for participants to gauge the range and endpoints of the turning knob, leading to confusion and discomfort. This challenge highlights a potential limitation in broader usability: haptic feedback, while valuable for reflecting mental effort, may need to be supplemented with other modalities (e.g., visual feedback)—especially in tasks that require a high degree of precision in rating.
            

            

             Concluding, our answers to our two RQs can be summarized as follows:
            

            

             
              
               RQ1: How do different input modalities (visual, haptic, and combined) affect the accuracy and ease of assessing cognitive load during mental tasks?
              
             
             The combined modality (haptic and visual) provided a more engaging and intuitive experience compared to the traditional visual-only modality, with trends in physiological marker correspondence and participant feedback supporting its advantages. Participants showed a clear learning effect over time, adapting to the haptic modality. Although there were no statistically significant differences in self-reported cognitive load between modalities, the combined feedback self-report correlated more closely with physiological markers of cognitive load, with participants reporting increased reflection and matching with their experience in the combined modality.
            

            

             
              
               RQ2: How do participants experience and perceive haptic feedback alone or in combination with visual feedback when rating and reflecting on tasks with varying levels of cognitive load?
              
             
             Participants generally preferred the combined modality, as it provided both physical and visual cues that mirrored the difficulty of tasks, enhancing their ability to reflect on cognitive effort. Haptic-only feedback was less intuitive and introduced uncertainty, especially without visual confirmation. The physical resistance of the haptic knob aligned well with users’ mental effort, helping participants feel more connected to their ratings and improving the reflection of their cognitive state.
            

            

             

              6.1 Limitations and Future Work
             

             

              The presented study shows promising results and helps to gain a deeper understanding of the design space for embodied UX measurement. Nevertheless, certain limitations need to be addressed in future work.
             

             

              In this study, we evaluated one haptic feedback pattern. The KeWheel offers a wide set of possibilities, thus a multitude of other haptic feedback design patterns are possible. We based the haptic feedback design on an expert workshop, striving for an optimal design of the haptic pattern. Nevertheless, in future work further haptic feedback design patterns shall be explored. Further, one participant noted they found it too hard to turn the knob to the extreme. This should be addressed in future iterations, for example by adding a calibration process beforehand, thereby adapting the force-feedback to the individuals’ strength.
             

             

              While our study aimed to investigate how embodied measurement can reduce abstraction bias in self-reporting, it is important to acknowledge that this approach does not eliminate all biases. For example, slight offsets toward lower ratings were observed in the haptic modality, which may reflect a physical effort bias introduced by the increasing torque. Although these offsets were not statistically significant, future studies should further investigate and address potential biases inherent in embodied interactions.
             

             

              Also, the concept of EM could be explored both in regard to other phenomena to be measured (e.g., hedonistic dimension of user experience [
              
               21
              
              ]) and in regard to using different feedback modalities for EM. Temperature, ambient lighting, ambient noises, smell, etc. could be used in various qualities and intensities to reflect these different constructs. In this work, we constrained our approach to haptic and visual feedback, with a primary focus on the haptic modality, to enable a controlled study of its effects. We encourage the CHI community to build up on this work and explore the large potential other feedback mechanisms could provide to embodied measurement.
             

             

              A further direction for future work is the investigation of benefits for certain user groups. We envision that the proposed embodied measurement approach may be highly suitable for a wide range of user groups. For example, children who cannot read or have less of a mental model of rating scales might benefit from this approach [
              
               16
              
              ]. Also, visually impaired and blind people may benefit from the haptic feedback, as visual scales are difficult to perceive for this group. In the presented study, we aimed for a homogeneous sample of participants; therefore, no children or visually impaired and blind people were involved in the study. Future research with a more diverse range of user groups is needed to gain further insights into the potential benefits this approach could bring to these groups.
             

             

              Another limitation of the study is related to left-handed participants. While the KeWheel’s location was adjusted to accommodate left-handed participants, the turning direction of the knob was not adapted, which may have introduced subtle variability in their interaction. Future iterations of the study could include an ambidextrous design or adapt the turning direction to further ensure usability for all participants.
             

             

              Future work should also explore how embodied measurement can align with industry-standard tools and frameworks in HCI to facilitate broader adoption. For example, integrating haptic feedback mechanisms into widely used UX research platforms, such as ThinkAloud or SUS (System Usability Scale), could help normalize embodied interactions as part of standard usability evaluation workflows. Similarly, aligning the design of embodied measurement tools with accessibility standards like WCAG 2.1 could broaden their applicability to diverse user groups, including individuals with disabilities. By establishing interoperability with existing methodologies and standards, embodied measurement could provide a clear roadmap for adoption and scalability, making it a practical addition to the HCI toolkit.
             

             

              Finally, this study was conducted as a highly controlled setup with a standardized, yet artificial, task (mental arithmetic task [
              
               60
              
              ]). To gather more insights into the real-world applicability of the proposed embodied measurement, future studies are needed that pilot this method in real-world usability studies and UX evaluations. Moreover, the reliance on trends in physiological markers and qualitative feedback to evaluate the combined modality’s effectiveness highlights the need for more robust statistical evidence. Future work could benefit from larger sample sizes and alternative constructs to strengthen the generalizability of the findings.
             

            

           

           

            

             7 Conclusion
            

            

             This study introduces the concept of Embodied Measurement (EM) through the use of a dynamically adaptable force-feedback device (KeWheel) for assessing cognitive load after mental tasks. Our findings suggest that tangible feedback mechanisms offer a promising approach to enhancing the accuracy and intuitiveness of cognitive load assessments. The results demonstrate that the combined modality (haptic + visual feedback) provide comprehensive reflection during the rating process, supporting the notion that multimodal feedback can offer richer and more meaningful user experiences. This points to the potential for EM to serve as a more holistic and inclusive method for assessing user experience, reducing reliance on traditional Likert-scale questionnaires, which are often subject to abstraction biases.
            

            

             While the KeWheel is a commercially available and replicable device, our study opens the door for further exploration of embodied UX measurement. Other force-feedback mechanisms, haptic devices, and multisensory designs could be leveraged to expand the design space of EM. There is significant potential for future studies within the HCI community to explore how different forms of physical interaction can serve as reliable indicators of mental and emotional states.
            

            

             Looking ahead, we believe that embodied, multimodal feedback mechanisms have the potential to reshape how user experience is measured in HCI, creating more intuitive, accessible, and valid tools for UX research. By continuing to investigate the intersection of physical interaction and cognitive assessment, we can move towards more inclusive and tangible ways of capturing complex user states.
