CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Being a little more careful about the sound that people produce is difficult in shared houses because individuals can generate several unintended living noises and sounds. We designed Tunee to help each housemate better understand the others’ context and desired noise-level. It is an interactive speaker that allows people to share noise-level preferences through the position change of nodes. Our three-week in-field study with four groups of participants revealed that expressing noise-level preference through nodes reduced the burden of verbally delivering issues about the trivial noises of everyday life, and the intentions of the lowered preference were referred to and deemed significant. We also identified how participants figured out what behavior was acceptable for others according to each noise-level. Our findings imply considerations in designing interfaces to support coordinating behaviors and awareness of social contexts in shared spaces.

1 INTRODUCTION

In a shared house, many social interactions with housemates occur [ 3 ], and they generally try to avoid potential conflicts with each other. In the process, each individual interprets the others’ social cues, opinions, behaviors, and intentions to understand the social context and infer what behavior is acceptable to others [ 45 ]. Additionally, most of their daily behaviors require attention so as not to affect the others negatively. In particular, unconscious living noise and sounds individuals generate intentionally (e.g., music from a speaker, TV sound) can affect spaces beyond walls. Moreover, when housemates are in their room, it is difficult for them to understand each other's context and guess how quiet they should be. Although it is possible to ask other to be careful about noise through talk or messages, considering the social relationship with housemates, it is not always easy to ask others to be careful about making a sound and explain the situation frequently [ 24 ]. In addition, noise sensitivity differs between people [ 53 ], leaving room for misinterpretation of the level of attention others request. Therefore, to pay attention to noise properly based on housemates’ contexts, additional help could be needed to make aware of others’ contexts and desired noise level in a shared house.

In this regard, researchers in the field of human-computer interaction (HCI) suggest the possibility of supporting awareness of social contexts to consider one's actions in space that affect others. According to Erickson and Kellogg [ 21 ], ‘awareness’ in the process of social interaction is defined as knowing information about others that can be used during social interactions or interpersonal communication to behave in a socially appropriate manner. Also, it has been noted that if awareness is hard to achieve naturally, it is important that the system support awareness to enable the user to act while considering others’ situation. Specifically, awareness of social context is needed in a place where one's action can affect others, and a system providing a user information about social context at the right moment can help people coordinate activities among each other [ 26 ]. Furthermore, to support awareness of social context effectively according to the system's purpose, it is necessary to consider what information should be provided and how to represent it [ 46 ].

In response to the above issues, researchers have suggested ways to increase the visibility of information about social contexts by sharing individuals’ information in a shared space [ 35 , 64 ]. In particular, the information (e.g., availability, preference, disturbance) users manually input was considered important because it contains the interpretation and intention of an individual's context, which is difficult to estimate with automatically collected information. In Avbox [ 55 ], which allows office members to share information by manually inputting their availability, concentration, time pressure, and disturbance in a shared office, the intentional information the user enters helps the others infer the reason for and context of the information. According to interface studies on shared individuals’ preferences for the lighting environment in a shared office [ 47 ] and home [ 8 ] and the indoor climate in a home [ 59 ], an interface that visualizes individual preferences makes it easier for people to understand others’ needs and supports coordinating the setting of the shared environment through discussion. At the same time, visualizing meaningful information is important so users can find it easily and present the information at the right time to use it [ 1 ]. On the other hand, the ways systems can share and visualize individual information about noise-related contexts in a shared house have rarely been studied. Building upon the lessons from these previous studies, we found a new design space for supporting awareness of the noise-related social context in a shared house by delivering each member's manually input information about noises to others who co-occupy their living spaces.

To investigate the above research opportunity, we employed a research through design approach [ 63 ]. In this research, we designed and developed Tunee (Figure 1 ), an interactive speaker sharing each housemate's noise-level preference through the position of nodes. Tunee further functions as a probe, thus allowing us to prompt people to reflect more broadly on the context-information-sharing system. To investigate how Tunee can support awareness of the noise-related social context in a shared house, we conducted a three-week field study in four participants groups’ shared houses. This paper makes two contributions. First, it introduces Tunee and its design process, both of which provide implications for designing information-sharing systems to express their noise-related context. Second, it describes the results of a three-week field study of Tunee, providing insights into how sharing noise-level preference affects awareness of each other's noise-related context and coordination of behavior regarding noise in a shared house.

2 RELATED WORK

To understand designing interfaces considering awareness of the noise-related social context in a shared house, we investigated related literature that concerns interactive systems that help increase awareness in a shared space through two perspectives. First, we looked into a social perspective to understand the effects of sharing information and the characteristics of living spaces. Second, we reviewed ways of choosing and presenting information to increase the visibility of social context-related information.

2.1 Sharing information for awareness of the social context in shared space

2.1.1 Understanding others’ context by sharing information. As a shared space is a space where different individuals are together, the needs for environmental settings differ depending on personal taste [ 44 ] and comfort [ 54 ]. In a shared space, it is important to act in a socially appropriate manner that considers others because one's actions can affect others. For example, to control the lighting in a shared office, learning information about others’ needs (e.g., mood and lighting preferences, level of acceptance to change, and duration of time spent at a desk) could help people consider each other's needs properly [ 58 ]. As such, awareness of surrounding contexts, or ‘an understanding of what is going on’ [ 20 ], plays a significant role in enabling people to act fittingly based on the social context [ 26 , 62 ]. According to a social-translucence framework [ 21 ], understanding social context and considering one's actions are driven by three factors: visibility of information obtained from one's surroundings, awareness of that information, and accountability for one's actions. Also, it has been noted that the system should sufficiently provide the above three factors to support coordinating actions among people and to encourage them to consider others’ needs. In particular, researchers have stressed the importance of sharing information to allow people to understand others’ contexts in everyday situations [ 15 , 19 ].

To convey information about one's context to others, people use various social cues in shared spaces [ 60 ] (e.g., holding a door open as an indicator of availability). However, this is open to misunderstanding; understanding a group's sign requires a person to be immersed into the culture. In this regard, studies on information sharing through an interface to provide context awareness have continuously progressed for years in the HCI field. In particular, Magielse et al. [ 37 ] emphasized that interfaces can mediate different social mechanisms through information sharing. Specifically, a computer program [ 30 ] and a shape-changing doorknob [ 18 ] that share each other's availability and interruptions in shared spaces have shown that it is possible to reduce differences in understanding of social cues. Several studies have noted that the expressivity of an interface can help form a shared vocabulary for lighting settings [ 10 ] and visibility for personal screens [ 34 ], reducing the gap in understanding. Furthermore, it has been indicated that sharing information through an interface encourages communication to coordinate actions [ 51 ]. Ensuring that the control interface is visible to users, it could help people initiate communication about their actions [ 57 ]. With the above examples, we saw the possibility that sharing information through an interface can help people understand social context in a shared space. For better context awareness, it is necessary to consider what information people need and how the interface could present it in different situations [ 46 ]. In particular, it is crucial to explore how the design of an interface can share meaningful information for people to take socially appropriate actions in a shared space.

2.1.2 Awareness of social context for different lifestyles in a living space. Most social-awareness studies have been conducted for shared offices or workspaces. However, living spaces such as homes and shared houses differ from workspaces in terms of diversity and members’ motives, and these differences should be considered [ 39 ]. Unlike the workspace, the living space is where personal activities mainly occur. According to studies measuring individual availability at home [ 40 , 41 , 56 ], an individual's availability varies according to his/her home activity and location. Even for the same activity, availability can vary from individual to individual. More specifically, Niemantsverdriet et al. [ 48 ] stated that conflicts occurring among the members may result from a difference in preference, activity, or attitude; and that the system needs to represent context differences to help them recognize and resolve conflict. In addition, Neustaedter et al. [ 42 ] stressed the importance of sharing information about other inhabitants (family, roommates, and significant others) because people often plan their home activities based on others’ location and activities and act according to others’ conditions when at home. Moreover, to obtain information about others’ context, they make an effort to find visual cues in the house and to interact with others [ 43 ].

In this regard, researchers have conducted studies in which systems share individuals’ information to support awareness of context differences in living space. Systems that shared a family's conversational availability [ 29 ] and location [ 9 ] showed the potential to support adjusting individuals’ activity plans based on others’ information shared by interfaces placed in the home environment. There have also been studies of interfaces that share preferences for home environment. According to systems for sharing preferred lighting in the home [ 8 , 48 ] and sharing preferred indoor environment in a shared house [ 59 ], systems that visually show individual preferences could help members understand each other's needs and negotiate settings in their environment. These studies showed that increasing awareness of different lifestyles through information sharing can help people coordinate behavior in a living space. At the same time, these studies additionally mentioned the need to explore the experiences with new interfaces and communication methods that are suitable for living spaces.

2.2 Increasing visibility of social information through the system

2.2.1 Social information containing context in shared spaces. Research to find out what kind of social information can support social context awareness in shared spaces was deeply investigated in HCI. Initially, previous studies focused on sharing context using qualitative information, such as video or audio-based systems [ 17 , 27 ], but concerns arose about privacy invasion [ 7 , 14 ]. In addition, research using contextual information measured by system sensors rather than qualitative information has increased. These cases include location information of people in the laboratory tracked by beacons [ 32 ] and availability predicted based on life rhythm [ 4 ], current activity [ 22 ], and sound around the user [ 23 ]. These approaches offered advantages in that the user can obtain others’ information without seeing them and less effort is required for users to enter information into the system.

Furthermore, researchers have studied not only the content but also the character of information, such as the purpose or intention [ 16 ]. In particular, in shared spaces, users’ interpretation of their contexts and their social relationships should be considered important. In this regard, there have been studies that use data users entered manually or allowed users to modify individuals’ information, which the system predicted [ 5 , 28 ]. For such manually inputted information, the subjective contents and timing entered by the user could indicate an additional meaning. Especially, in Avbox ’s case [ 55 ], the information the user entered could provide others accurate information containing the user's intent. Based on previous studies, we considered the possibility of using manually inputted information with the user's intention. Since more detailed information does not mean better awareness due to privacy invasion or clutter [ 23 ], it is necessary to consider what kind of information the system can provide to understand the social context in shared spaces better.

2.2.2 Representation of social information in spaces. Studies have been conducted not only on what information should be shown but also ways to present the information. For the information the system provides to play a sufficient role, it is needed to be available without any effort to find the information [ 1 ]. For a system to provide users with easy-to-find information, it is crucial for system designers to consider the presence, placement, and persistence of the information [ 46 ]. In this regard, there have been cases where information was physically expressed to increase presence and placement. Studies on visualizing personal information in a shared office with lighting ( Flowlight , which visualizes interruptibility [ 64 ], and Element , which visualizes emotional engagement [ 11 ]) have suggested a way to obtain social information naturally in space. In addition, these studies showed that visualizing information in a space could make information peripherally visible [ 25 ]. In particular, movement [ 50 ] and sound [ 26 ] that are generated when using the tangible interface can signal information more explicitly. If the result of the interaction remains after interacting with the interface, it could also help with persistence. For example, the tangible lighting interface Canvas allows users to visualize individual preferences and to understand the needs of others and reflect them through the position of blocks and drawn boundaries for lighting settings in a shared office [ 47 ] without seeing the moment of interaction.

3 TUNEE DESIGN

In sections 2.1 and 2.2, we discussed the possibility of using information containing users’ intent and increasing the visibility of information in a tangible interface that supports awareness of social context. Based on lessons from previous studies, we designed a technological design probe, Tunee, that can help people share their noise-level preferences with their housemates through the tangible interface using physical movements. In what follows, we describe the design rationales of Tunee and its details.

3.1 Sharing individual noise-level preferences with housemates

Tunee's design aims to allow users to express their noise-related context and understand others’ noise-related contexts in a shared house. According to previous studies, sharing information about individuals could support understanding of others’ social contexts [ 21 , 26 ]. But what information needs to be shared to be effective highly depends on the target context [ 46 ]. Therefore, we investigated what kind of information individuals need to be shared to express their noise-related context in a shared house.

We first considered the characteristics of noise in a shared house. A variety of activities are held in the residential environment. Depending on the activity, activities might occur in which noise is inevitable (e.g., vacuuming, cooking) or noise-free conditions are required (e.g., online interview, sleep). Additionally, noise sensitivity differs between people [ 53 ]. An acceptable noise level in the same context can also be different (e.g., when sleeping, some people wake up with a slight noise, and some do not even wake up with loud noise). Thus, we found that users might need to present the objective noise-level preference according to his/her context. To share objective noise levels, we have chosen a range from 30-40dB (e.g., whispering, quiet library), which is the minimum indoor noise, to 90-100dB (e.g., drilling, rock concert, loud singing), which is almost the maximum indoor noise. We allowed users to choose their preferred noise level between them. We divided noise levels into 10 steps (0 to 9), allowing participants to determine their noise-level preference with fewer options intuitively. Moreover, if users need to discuss someone's noise-level preference, we wanted to allow them to know whose noise-level preference it was. Also, to prevent disrespectful use (e.g., always setting the lowest noise-level preference regardless of one's context), we decided to provide whose information it is along with the noise-level preference. Doing so makes users responsible for the information they present [ 33 ] and allows for discussion of the noise level.

Afterward, to allow users to take into account only the noise preferences of users currently in the shared house, we considered how to show whether the user is in the shared house. Initially, we designed a device to be used with Tunee to provide users others’ presence information (Figure 2-c ). However, providing others one's location information could lead to privacy invasion [ 6 ]. Moreover, because a shared house is a residential space, not a workspace, one's location could be more sensitive information. Therefore, we decided to provide users information that the system interprets once rather than providing presence information directly. For this, we integrated the device designed to provide presence into Tunee. Tunee automatically presents the absent user's noise-level preference as the maximum noise level (Figure 3-b ) so other users will not know whether the absent user is in the shared house or whether he has set the maximum noise-level preference; as a result, the absent user's noise-level preference is not considered by other users because it is the maximum. As a way to sense whether a user is in a shared house, we used a beacon to collect only information about whether the user is near a Tunee (not tracking their location with GPS). The beacon was made as an accessory that a user can attach to their mobile phone (Figure 2-d ) to take it with them when they leave.

3.2 Position-change interaction of physical nodes representing noise-level preferences

We designed an interface that allows users to enter their information and view information from the others in the shared house. First, we considered the presence of information to make it more accessible. Among the types of interfaces that can present information, such as graphical interfaces with display and physical interfaces, we decided on a physical interface that is suitable for increasing the presence of information. According to information-sharing-system studies, physical interfaces are physically located in space, and this spatiality of information makes it easy to glance up and perceive information [ 2 , 62 ]. In addition, Luria et al. [ 36 ] revealed that physical interfaces are most useful in helping users perceive information compared to graphic and voice interfaces due to their physical characteristics. In this, the placement of information that can be easily found should also be considered to increase the presence of information [ 46 ]. Based on the above lessons, we devised a physical device that only has a noise-related information sharing function and placed it in a space to always show information, rather than allowing users to find and view information on their multi-functional devices (e.g., a tablet or a smartphone). To design a physical interface that will be placed in a space, we explored in which space the physical interface could provide information most appropriately. We considered the living room, entrance foyer, and individual's room (Figure 2-a ). Of those options, we chose each individual's room, where their main personal activities take place.

After that, even if users do not capture the moment when others’ information changes, we investigated ways to persist the information for the user to check later. In addition, since this device is to be placed in an individual's room, we investigated ways to present information without interfering with the user's daily life. Therefore, rather than designing an instant alarm that uses sound or vibration, we devised ways to physically emphasize changes while maintaining changes in information, such as angle, position, and volume. Based on previous studies on tangible interaction, we applied the position-change interaction of physical nodes to represent noise-level preference. The moment of position change can provide explicit signaling of information [ 49 ], and changed positions can provide intuitive recognition of the current state as a type of expression [ 65 ]. From the perspective of the interface that controls information, position change interactions help quickly check and change the state that the users have set by providing clear and unambiguous feedback about the current state [ 34 ]. For intuitiveness of use, we borrowed the horizontal linear slider, often used to control speaker volume, and allowed the nodes to move along the slider according to noise-level preference (Figure 2-b ). The user manually moves his/her node to input a noise-level preference, and others’ nodes automatically move according to the noise-level preferences others have input (Figure 3-a ). By placing nodes side by side, we want to let users easily see and compare each other's noise-level preferences at a glance. Also, each node is a different color so that users of each node are recognizable (Figure 2-e ). To look deeply into an individual's experience, we made three nodes for a group of three people, the smallest group size. We also considered designs in which nodes could be added as the number of group members increases (Figure 2-d ). Finally, we added a speaker function so users could hear each noise level, and we placed the speaker dial on the same axis as the sliders of the nodes (Figure 2-e ). The final Tunee design follows in Figure 2-e .

3.3 Interaction of Tunee

3.3.1 Moving the topmost node to convey my noise-level preference. The red Tunee's user moves the topmost, red node to input his/her noise-level preference. The red node in the yellow and blue Tunee automatically moves to the same position as the red Tunee, and the yellow and blue Tunee users check the red Tunee user's noise-level preference through the node's position (Figure 3-b ). The yellow and blue Tunees are likewise used.

3.3.2 Checking the noise level with the speaker. If the user wants to hear the loudness of the other's noise-level preference, he/she would move the colored part of the speaker dial to the same position as the other's node and then connect a personal device to the speaker via Bluetooth and play music (Figure 3-c ).

3.3.3 When the user is not at home. If the red Tunee does not detect the red Tunee user's beacon nearby, it interprets that red Tunee user is not at home. The red Tunee informs the other Tunees that the user is not at home. Then, the yellow and blue Tunees move the red node to the maximum noise-level preference (Figure 3-d ).

3.4 Implementation

The three Tunees share each user's noise-level preference and beacon presence by uploading them to Firebase. The Arduino (Figure 4-a ) inside the Tunee sends the current noise-level preference detected through the rotary sensor connected to the top node (Figure 4-a ) to Raspberry Pi (Figure 4-a ) as a value between 0 and 9. Raspberry Pi uploads to Firebase the data received from Arduino and the presence of a beacon sensed by its Bluetooth module as a value of 0/1. When one Tunee's data changes in Firebase, the other two Tunees’ Raspberry Pi download the data and send it to their Arduino. Based on the received data, the Arduino controls the stepper motor (Figure 4-a ) to move the node based on each Tunee's noise-level preference. We used a Bluetooth amplifier module (Figure 4-b ) and a speaker unit (Figure 4-b ) to allow the user to hear the loudness of each noise level. Those speaker modules are used for general speakers and provide similar sound quality as commercial portable speakers. The user can connect his/her device to the Bluetooth amplifier module and listen to music at either noise-level (30-40 dB as the minimum and 90-100 dB as the maximum). The beacon consists of a Bluetooth 4.0 module (Figure 4-c ) and a coin-battery module (Figure 4-c ). The battery life is about ten days, and the battery is replaced once every seven days.

4 USER STUDY

We conducted a field study [ 52 ] to look deeply into how Tunee affects housemates’ awareness of each other's noise-related context and coordination of behavior regarding noise in a shared house. In particular, we wanted to identify 1) how Tunee expresses one's context and helps people understand each other's context through noise-level preference and 2) how Tunee affects daily noise behavior in a shared house. The study was conducted for three weeks with four groups of participants who lived in shared houses. For the first week, participants accurately understood the device's functions, and we explored their new experiences with sharing each other's noise-related context. Over the following two weeks, we explored participants’ adapted use of Tunee and the changes in noise-related behavior. To inquire participants about their experiences with a new design intervention, Tunee, we gave them a diary for a week before the field study and conducted weekly interviews. Through those, we could see and hear how the participants usually get the context of other members and their lifestyles regarding noise.

4.1 Participants

We recruited four groups (G1-G4) of three participants (Red, Yellow, Blue) living together in a shared house. Our recruitment criteria were as follows: 1) people who are not related to each other living together, 2) people using their private rooms, 3) people who have been concerned about noise or have experienced conflicts with noise, 4) people who are in their 20s and 30s, the main age group living in shared houses for reasons such as school or work [ 38 ]. Using these criteria, we selected groups who were aware of the need to consider others’ contexts and coordinate their noise-related behavior in a shared house. Furthermore, we selected groups who had lived together for at least one month to compare their previous co-living experiences about noise with a new experience. Also, all groups were of Asian culture. They fully understood the culture of the region where they currently live, so there was no cultural difference according to the region. The groups’ detailed information follows:

  • G1 Red-node device: Female, 24 | Yellow-node device: Female, 24 | Blue-node device: Female, 24 | Close friends from the same university; four years of friendship; two months of living together
  • G2 Red-node device: Male, 30 | Yellow-node device: Male, 27 | Blue-node device: Male, 28 | G2-Blue gathered the other two; G2-Blue and G2-Red's are co-workers for three years; G2-Blue and G2-Yellow were roommates for one year two years ago; three months of living together
  • G3 Red-node device: Female, 23 | Yellow-node device: Female, 23 | Blue-node device: Female, 23 | Friends who met for the first time as housemates; Students from the same university; they have known each other for four years; 19 months of living together (lived together all the time except for about two years, when they did not go to university due to COVID-19)
  • G4 Red-node device: Male, 29 | Yellow-node device: Male, 30 | Blue-node device: Male, 27 | Work colleagues; they met at work and lived together; they have known each other for over two years; 13 months of living together.

To find out about the participants’ noise-related lifestyles, we asked them to write in a diary once a day for a week. The diary included questions on each page about being careful about sound as well as daily noises the participant cares about. On the last day, each participant explained the diary's contents in detail in a 30-minute personal interview. We asked the participants several questions related to indoor noise sensitivity (e.g., “I am easily awakened by noise,” “I'm good at concentrating no matter what is going on around me,” and “I get mad at people who make noise that keeps me from falling asleep or getting work done” [ 61 ]), and had them subjectively rate their noise sensitivity on a five-point scale (i.e., not at all, slightly, moderately, very, extremely). 4.2 Deployment and interview

Through the interview, we were able to learn about the specific physical qualities of the participants’ shared houses (Figure 5 ). In all rooms, low volume conversations or speaker sounds (about 50-60dB) in other rooms could not be heard when the door was closed; however, the sound of vacuum cleaners, hair dryers, and loud conversations (approximately 80-90dB) could be heard even with the doors were closed. In addition, most of the sounds coming from common spaces (e.g., living room, shower room) and corridors were relatively audible even with closed doors. Specifically, participants with rooms located near the front door said that they could hear the sound of opening and closing even when their room's door was closed. G1, 3, and 4 tended to live with their doors open due to stuffiness and ventilation, whereas G2 tended to live with their door closed for privacy reasons.

After the diary interview, we offered instructions regarding the use of Tunee. Participants tried out Tunee with the experimenter so that they could fully understand how to use it and how their noise-level preference is expressed on others’ Tunee. Participants freely installed Tunee in a comfortable place in their room in a shared house (Figure 5 ). Also, each participant was provided with a beacon in the form of a mobile phone accessory and attached to the mobile phone case. We asked participants to consent to information being collected about whether they were at home during the experiment and to carry a beacon at all times. We required minimal use (at least one or more noise-level preference changes per day) to give participants awareness of the field study without affecting each Tunee usage pattern.

So that we could listen to detailed experiences expressed by the participants, we provided them with a diary with four questions related to their experience of using Tunee. The participants filled out a diary every day. We conducted weekly individual interviews over three weeks. Participants answered the interview by referring to their diaries and recalling their experiences. Each interview was semi-structured and lasted about 40 minutes. 4.3 Data collection and analysis

We collected two types of qualitative data (interviews with and diaries of participants) and two types of quantitative data (log data of when the noise-level preference was changed and whether participants were at home – Figure 6 ). We fully transcribed 1,248 minutes of interview recordings and generated open codes right after each interview. Using these codes, we went through an iterative affinity-diagramming process and checked statements regarding the noise-level preference usage along with the log data. We gathered the common statements of the participants and supplemented them with related statements from the diaries. In addition, we carefully looked at whether any coinciding or conflicting statements existed among participants and the same group members. Through several iterative groupings by our research team, major themes were divided into three topics: 1) influence of Tunee on expressing noise-related contexts to others; 2) how Tunee affected the awareness of the others’ noise-related contexts; and 3) influence of Tunee on noise-related behaviors. Afterward, we extracted subthemes and related statements by reviewing open codes in each cluster, creating six subgroups. By applying a triangulation of descriptive quantitative analysis of the usage logs and the interviews, we detailed how Tunee supported participants’ experience with awareness of each other's noise-related context in a shared house.

5 FINDINGS

We were able to see how participants shared and reflected their noise-level preference through Tunee. According to the diaries, most of the participants changed their noise-level preference after returning home (n=5), before going to bed (n=6), after waking up (n=6), and according to personal activities (e.g., study (n=5), ready to go out (n=6), rest (n=7), meeting (n=2)). Some participants (n=5) noted that their sensitivity to noise varied according to their condition, which affected how they set their preferred noise level. The range of noise levels used by each participant was different. Four (G1-all, G3-yellow) participants used all ranges of noise levels. In addition, eight participants (G2-all, G3-red, blue, G4-all) did not use the low-noise level (0-3) (Figure 6-A ), and remarked that it was because they did not want others to act too uncomfortably in order to make less noise. G4-red mentioned, “I am a light sleeper, so to get a good night's sleep, it is good to lower it (noise-level preference) to 2 or 3, but I set it to 5. Because I go to bed and wake up later than others, it is a little difficult for housemates to behave according to too-low noise level (2-3) in the morning.”

In addition, most participants mentioned from the interviews and wrote in their diaries that they checked Tunee after returning home (all), after waking up (n=8), before making a sound (all), while making a sound (all), and when others’ nodes moved (all). G1-yellow stated, “I think looking at Tunee has become a habit. When I was wondering whether to turn on the speaker or not, I looked at Tunee instead of just guessing. ” All participants noted that checking the noise-level preferences of others with a Tunee allowed them to know when to be careful about noise and when they did not need it. G4-yellow stated, “I think I found the time when I could be a little freer. I figured out when people usually set their noise-level preference to the maximum. I think I have more time than before when I do not have to worry about noise.” In particular, participants (G1-red, G2-all, G4-yellow, blue) who tend to always be careful about noise when someone else is at home (Table 1 ) mentioned that they were able to do more activity that requires noise even when others were at home (e.g., using speakers instead of earphones, exercising in a room, watching media at a high volume). At the same time, when the noise-level preference of others was lower than usual, nine participants felt they were not free because they paid more attention to the use of sound than before this user study.

All participants noted that they reflected to their everyday behavior in terms of making noises based on their housemates’ current lowest noise-level preferences. Because, they all had some kind of social relationship with their housemates, and they tried to accommodate each other's needs as much as possible to maintain that relationship. In the following subsections, we explain how Tunee affected the understanding and reflections of each other's needs.

5.1 Casual sharing of the desired noise-level based on their daily activities and conditions

With Tunee's interactions through nodes that did not require conversations with others, nine participants remarked that they felt less of a burden in conveying their desired noise levels. G4-yellow stated, “To ask him to be quiet, I had to go to his room and see what he was doing. Now I can end communication by just setting my noise-level preferences in my room, regardless of the other person's situation. I think that was convenient.” Participants noted that they could convey their wanted noise-level through Tunee to housemates according to their current activities (n=10) or conditions (e.g., tiredness, ill health) (n=6), which was challenging to say in words repeatedly, as G3-red mentioned, “Because it is hard to convey in words how sensitive I am every day. When everyone is busy with something, and the doors are closed, I cannot say, ‘Hey, I am tired.’ It [Tunee] is a way to express my condition without words. ” G1-yellow stated, “I think it's used as a status line in SNS about what I'm doing, like ‘I'm studying’ and ‘I'm watching a drama.’ I don't say it, but I tried to express it with the node itself so that other people can think, ‘She's doing an activity that needs quiet.’ It feels like I am expressing things that can't be said from time to time. ”

At the same time, two participants (G1-blue and G3-blue) mentioned that there were a few cases where they wanted to deliver the reason for their noise-level preference because their housemates may feel bad without explanations. For this, they used their messaging apps, and specifically, G3-blue asked her housemates for permission to take a late-night shower via messenger. She mentioned, “When my housemate is going to sleep, I think it is not polite to my housemate to move the node and just go to the shower. I think it would be more polite to say, ‘I am going to be a little late today. Do you mind if I take a shower?’ It is because words can explain the situation, but nodes cannot explain the situation.”

According to previous studies [ 47 ], for the system to maintain accurate and up-to-date information, it is necessary to reduce the burden of users entering information into the system. We confirmed that mediating noise-related communications among housemates through nodes of Tunee helped reduce the burden of talking with others face-to-face to express their desired noise levels to others. This allowed participants to share their noise-level preference according to their daily noise-related activities and conditions. However, as the node could not explain the reason for their noise-level preference, asking polite permission for participants’ future occurrence of noises through the node was difficult. In this regard, we found a need for additional methods to deliver more detailed explanations for changes to prevent further subtle discomfort or miscommunication.

5.2 Guessing and reflecting the intentions and contexts of others

All participants mentioned that others’ noise-level preferences allowed them to guess the others’ contexts because they had been living together for more than two months and knew to some extent about their housemates’ approximate lifestyle patterns. First, nine participants inferred the intention of others to lower their noise-level preference based on the time of day. G3-Red noted, “Depending on the time, I was able to guess the intention. If she lowers it in the evening, it means she will go to bed early. If she lowers it during the day, it means that she works, and sometimes she has meetings at home, which means ‘Now I have a meeting.’ Or she can take a nap.” Four participants guessed the others’ condition based on their noise-level preference. G2-red stated, “On days when he is tired, he sets the node a little lower to say, ‘I want to sleep deeply,’ and ‘I want you to be quiet.’ ‘Could this be what it means? For example, today, G2-yellow slept with the node set lower than usual. I saw his node and thought, ‘He is a little tired.’”

In particular, eight participants mentioned that even if the nodes of housemates who usually set a high noise-level preference were lowered a little, the intention of a slight change was considered significant. When G3-blue, who usually set the noise-level preference between 7 and 5, lowered her node to 3 for an important online meeting at home (Figure 6-C ), G3-yellow noted that she was especially careful. She continued, “I think there must be a reason why the G3-Blue did not set her node to high noise levels as usual, but to a medium level, so I was trying to be careful about noise.” Similarly, two groups (G2 and G4), who mostly set high noise-level preferences, were particularly careful about noise even when the noise-level preference of others was near 5 (Figure 6-E ). G4-mentioned, “I thought the housemates were very tolerant of noise. So when their preferred loudness was lowered, I guessed, ‘They need a quiet environment.’ So I paid more attention to the noise.”

On the other hand, five participants pointed out that it was difficult to guess how much others’ noise-level preferences were important, so they mostly tried to behave by reflecting all others’ preferences. G4-red stated, “I was trying to be careful whenever others’ noise-level preferences were lowered because I could not determine each preference's importance.” He continued, “I think it would be better if there were something that could indicate ‘I am in a good mood so I can tolerate a little over the noise level’ or ‘Because I am not feeling very well today, if you make a sound over this noise level, I will fight you.’”

In line with previous studies’ findings [ 55 ], we were able to see that sharing intentional information provided by others can help users guess others’ activity or condition. Participants behaved by reflecting the noise-level preferences of others based on their guessed intentions and others’ contexts with the help of previously known life patterns. This caused the participants to identify others’ intentions about their small changes in noise-level preferences. In addition, we also found that there is a need for users to understand the strength of importance when others’ noise-level preference was moved; in order for users to respond with their behaviors in a flexible way.

5.3 Forming a shared understanding of the group for each noise level

Noise levels are subjective and are influenced by personal physiology, fatigue, and cognitive load. Each participant's room conditions differed depending on the thickness of the walls between rooms and doors and the room's location. Although participants knew and could directly hear the set volume for each level (0∼9) of noise preference through the Tunee's inside speaker module, they had their own standard of accepting the noise levels. Thus, they needed time and process to settle their standard in understanding and behaving based on positions of node expressed in Tunee. G3-red stated, “It took me about a week to get what behavior was acceptable for each noise level. Over the next week, I got used to thinking about how much to lower my noise-level preference, and I knew how my housemates behave at each noise level.” After the participants confirmed each other's acceptable noise-related behaviors, they used Tunee based on a shared understanding of each noise level formed in each group. Below we describe how the participants understood each other's acceptable noise-related behavior at each noise level.

Referring to others’ sounds and changing one's noise-level preference. Six participants initially set their noise-level preference based on the loudness provided by Tunee. After that, they listened to the other person's sound at each noise level and changed the range of the noise-level preference they represented. G2-yellow noted, “When I first used it, I listened to the loudness [through Tunee's speaker] and set the noise level that would interfere with my life if the sound was higher than this, but my housemates did not make a sound than I expected. So, I raised the noise-level preference a bit” (Figure 6-D ). In particular, G3-yellow set the noise-level preference to 3 for sleeping at the beginning of the experiment. After others made a louder sound than expected, she set the noise-level preference to 0 for sleeping (Figure 6-B ). She mentioned, “ In the beginning, I did not use the minimum noise level, but the other person's sound seemed higher than [noise level] 3, which I expected, so I used the minimum noise level a lot. I set it extremely so that others would reflect the noise level I expect.”

Based on others’ reactions. Most participants (n=10) did not ask others whether the sound they generated was noisy. This is because they did not want to disturb others’ activities (n=8) or hear direct complaints about the noise (n=3). They considered their behavior to be acceptable if there was no negative feedback (e.g., verbal feedback or the sudden closing of others’ room doors) from others on their behavior. G1-red mentioned, “I am using earphones less in the room than last week. If there had been feedback that the speaker was noisy when I turned it on last week, I would have turned down the speaker volume, but since there is no feedback, I think it is okay to use a speaker.” On the other hand, two participants confirmed whether the loudness they make is appropriate by asking others in face-to-face. G4-red mentioned that he took a shower in the shared shower booth, and the next day he asked G4-blue (who had a low noise level at that time) if his sound was okay. He stated, “I asked him, ‘I came in late yesterday and took a shower, but did you wake up because of that?’ And he said, ‘no.’ Then, I thought the shower was acceptable.”

Checking the change in position of others’ nodes. All participants said they paid attention to the changes in others’ nodes when making noises in the house. G4-yellow stated, “It is hard to precisely match my sound to others’ [noise-level preferences]. So I paid attention to the movement of the nodes. When the node moved from high to low, I thought, ‘Am I a little noisy?’, so I turned down the speaker's volume to reflect the change.” Also, eight participants mentioned that they identified that the sound they were making was suitable if there was no change in the position of others’ nodes. G4-red noted, “The position of the node I checked when I got home and checked before going to bed did not change, so I kept this sound level.”

Despite the fact that Tunee provided the participants with objective loudness for each noise level, it was not a sufficient guideline for coordinating the noise-related behaviors due to environmental and individual differences. This required participants to identify acceptable noise-related behaviors at each noise level. In this regard, we confirmed that participants set their noise-level preference by referring to the others’ sounds, and adjusted their noise-related behaviors based on feedback from others’ reactions and node changes.

6 DISCUSSION AND DESIGN IMPLICATIONS

6.1 Designing interfaces for awareness of social context in shared houses

Unlike a workspace such as a shared office, a shared house is a living space. Each housemate has a different lifestyle and performs different activities [ 39 ]. In particular, most shared house members gather for economic and practical reasons, so it is important to balance each other's community and privacy [ 12 ]. This characteristic of shared houses may require interfaces to support awareness of social contexts, and we propose three design considerations.

First, time is an important factor for understanding others’ information in shared houses. Most of what happens at home is routine and personal. As shown in section 5.2, participants shared preference levels depending on their life patterns and inferred others’ context based on the time of day others’ preference levels changed. They were able to understand others’ context approximately based on preference level and time without directly telling each other about their activities. Abstracting information helps people deal with the invasion of privacy due to social information sharing [ 31 ]. In this, time could be a hint to guess and predict context through abstract information. At the same time, for some events that were difficult for others to predict (e.g., showering in a shared bathroom after 12pm), participants notified others of the expected time in advance using their existing communication mediums (e.g., text messaging). Information shared in advance allows users to be aware of others’ future context and to adjust their plans or prepare the environment accordingly (e.g., doing work that requires a quiet environment before someone else showers, or close the door and go to sleep) [ 21 ]. Although Tunee only shared information on the current context, sharing information on users’ future context in advance could help others understand the context over time of day in a shared house. This can be one way for users to plan their personal life based on time while considering others’ contexts.

Second, the information shared and its effect may differ depending on the location of the interface because the characteristics of spaces in a shared house (bedrooms, and social spaces, such as living rooms and kitchens [ 3 ]), the context or needs of individuals using each space can be different, and the accessibility of the interface is affected by the location [ 57 ]. In this study, Tunee was installed in each personal room, which made it possible to share information related to personal activities that would be difficult to obtain non-intrusively because the participants use separate spaces. In addition, participants were made aware of and able to reflect on changes in others’ contexts during their personal activities due to the visibility of others’ information in their room. At the same time, an interface placed in a social space for everyone to use together may have provided experiences related to enhancing group conversation and facilitated discussion [ 8 , 48 ]. This may be advantageous for sharing information related to communal living, such as activities using shared facilities (e.g., cooking facilities, washbasins) or environment settings of social spaces (e.g., lighting and heating in the living room). In this regard, according to the different needs of the members in a group, for example, individuals’ privacy, the type/way of information they want to share, active communication, and availability of common spaces/facilities; the location and design of the interface that expresses need to reflect those considerations. Specifically, privacy of individual activities and transparency of communal use of facilities can be important in some groups in a shared house, the system may provide different type of message delivery using the level of its strength and timings.

Lastly, indicating when it is unnecessary to consider the social context is needed to be considered. A shared house is a shared space but, simultaneously, a space where private life takes place. Thus, a balance between being considerate of others and feeling privacy is important [ 13 ]. If users are always paying extra attention to others, they may have problems with the freedom they should have in their living space. In addition, sharing social information could generates natural social constraints [ 21 ]. In our study, the interaction with Tunee helped participants to identify the time when they needed to pay attention and when they were free to act. This consequence provides opportunities for designing interfaces to support better user awareness of others’ contexts by visually emphasizing whether users need to care about housemates, further showing potential for designing both communal and private experiences in a shared house.

6.2 Coordinating behavior through social systems in shared spaces

Shared social information can provide awareness of social contexts for coordinating activities [ 21 ]. During the analysis of findings, we confirmed that sharing noise-related preferences through Tunee increased awareness of others’ context and helped coordinate noise-related behavior. In this respect, we were able to identify two considerations that may help when designing social systems to coordinate behavior in shared spaces, not only shared houses but also shared offices and open co-working spaces. Particularly, these considerations might be applied to system designs for many stakeholders who have conflicts but have to live together due to other reasons (e.g., budget, lack of choices).

The first consideration is to provide the intensity of intent with which the information is presented. In shared spaces, sharing information with users' intentions can help users understand each other's context [ 55 ]. Tunee provided intentional information by sharing the preferences manually entered by the user, but it did not provide a way to convey the intensity of the intention to share the information, which can vary depending on the situation (e.g., must be considered, open to negotiation). In the case of our participants, all information was considered important to maintain good social relationships. In addition, some participants were hesitant to enter their preferences because they were concerned that all of their preferences would be considered too important by others. For the system to receive accurate and up-to-date information, the user should not be burdened with inputting information [ 47 ]. At this point, for a system to reduce the burden and encourage users to present information with light intentions, it may need to allow users to enter and share the intensity of intent with the information in the system. Furthermore, in other conditions, the intentions of others may be taken lightly and ignored or users' different preferences may collide, causing confusion and conflict in coordinating each other's actions [ 48 ]. Sharing the intensity of intent along with the information could help users prioritize their needs or preferences for negotiation or reflection. In particular, it can be helpful to control systems that can affect each other in places where many people use it or where the number of members changes depending on the situation, such as shared offices or co-working spaces. For example, this could be applied to lighting or sound systems (e.g., SONOS, everyone in a household can control audio from every speaker) in shared offices that require agreed-upon controls based on members’ preferences or needs. An interface that allows users to share the intensity of their wants or needs, along with their preferred lighting settings or speaker volume levels, would help people consider and set priorities for each other's opinions.

The second consideration is to design the system to provide a physical representation of feedback on the user's current behavior. Our participants were able to express and receive complaints or understand the meaning of okay about current noise through changes in preference level. These expressions, which were conveyed as feedback, enabled users to check and reflect on whether their actions were socially appropriate. If these feedback and reflections are continuously made, users can form a loop to act socially appropriate in consideration of others’ context. In the case of Tunee, the node (a physical expression) became a means of non-verbal communication and helped to give and receive frequent feedback between users. Moving a node reduced the burden of dealing with others face-to-face or considering nuances in verbal expressions, which allowed users to provide more frequent feedback on others’ behavior. Also, frequent feedback can facilitate communication by reducing users' time waiting for others’ reactions to their behavior [ 57 ]. In addition, our participants viewed others’ nodes not moving as positive feedback about their current behavior. Such positive feedback could help users gain confidence that their current behavior is socially appropriate. For this to be the case, the system needs to help the user find other people's information not only when the information has changed, but also when there is no change. The persistence of physical expressions, such as a node changing position, can increase the visibility of information even if it is not seen the moment the information changes [ 47 ]. Feedback through physical representation could be applied to a system to coordinate behaviors that need to be sensitively adjusted according to changes in time or situation (e.g., noise-related behavior, room temperature control, lighting setting). For example, a shared office or shared house could benefit from an interface that can indicate desired modifications to other people's lighting brightness settings through the height of a physical bar. If other people's lighting settings aren't appropriate for a user's context (e.g., short breaks in dim light or paperwork in bright light), they could easily give the other person feedback by adjusting the height of the bar. In addition, the person who set the lighting can maintain the lighting setting if there is no change in the bar or can immediately redo the lighting settings based on the change in the height of the bar.

7 LIMITATION AND FUTURE WORK

This paper focused on how participants understand other people's noise-related context and coordinate their actions using Tunee in a shared house. Similar to how people express their noise-related needs might differ across cultures, how they use Tunee might also differ. For example, in cultures where people actively express their needs, Tunee could be a means to help them express their needs more objectively and conveniently. In cultures where people passively express their needs, Tunee could encourage them to express their opinion and wants about the noise-levels. In this study, we did not focus on how Tunee helps noise-related communication based on different cultural backgrounds but explored its possibilities for sharing noise-level needs with people concerned about noise or who have experienced conflicts with noise. We expect investigating the effects of sharing noise-level needs upon different cultural groups would be an interesting area to understand the future roles that coordinate sensitive information affecting members in a shared house.

In our future work, we are also considering adding nodes to make our prototype available to a larger group of users and installing it in their shared houses. It would be interesting to look deeply into contexts where it is more challenging to be aware of each other's contexts. In addition, recruiting participants with more diverse relationship types (e.g., people who do not know each other at all, co-workers with a hierarchy) will make it possible to further explore the factors that affect Tunee use. In addition, by improving the technology to detect whether the user is in the room, it will be possible to use the battery in a mobile phone rather than the coin battery that can affect the environment.

8 CONCLUSION

We introduced Tunee to explore how sharing noise-level preference affects the awareness of each other's noise-related context and coordination of behavior about noise in a shared house. The results from a three-week study with four groups showed that mediating noise-related communications among housemates through nodes of Tunee helped reduce the burden of talking with others face-to-face to express their desired noise levels to others. This allowed participants to say about the trivial noises of everyday life. We also found that the intentions of the lowered preference were referred to, and changes in preferences were deemed significant. Moreover, Tunee required participants to identify acceptable noise-related behaviors at each noise level. In this regard, others’ sounds, reactions, and changes in the nodes affected adjustments to their behaviors. Based on the findings, we discussed new considerations for the system that utilize the noise-level preference for coordinating noise-related behavior in shared houses. Specifically, we suggest providing the the priority of the presented noise-level preference and supporting users to clearly understand the individuals’ differences in perceiving noise-level preferences. In addition, our findings imply the need to consider features of shared houses in designing interfaces for awareness of the social context.
