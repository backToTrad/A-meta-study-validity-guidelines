We introduce Thermotion , a novel method using thermofluidic composites to design and display thermochromic animation effects on object surfaces. With fluidic channels embedded under the object surfaces, the composites utilize thermofluidic flows to dynamically control the surface temperature as an actuator for thermochromic paints, which enables researchers and designers for the first time to create animations not only on two and three-dimensional surfaces but also on the surface made of a few flexible everyday materials. We report the design space with six animation primitives and two modification effects, and we demonstrate the design and fabrication workflow with a customized software platform for design and simulation. A range of applications is shown leveraging the objects’ dynamic displays both visually and thermally, including dynamic artifacts, teaching aids, and ambient displays. We envision an opportunity to extend thermofluidic composites to other heat-related practices for further dynamic and programmable interactions with temperature.

1 INTRODUCTION

Color changing on object surfaces is widely researched in HCI fields. Designers and researchers have explored new interfaces via dynamic and programmable changes in the appearance of physical objects to convey information and aesthetic expression[ 40 , 63 , 65 ]. Recently, an emerging trend aims to achieve dynamic displays in arbitrary shapes (e.g., curved surfaces[ 7 , 21 , 25 ] or flexible surfaces[ 30 , 40 , 45 ]) to expand the functionality and expressiveness of the interfaces, as well as embed display capability in everyday materials (e.g., fabric[ 12 , 15 ] or paper[ 24 , 34 , 64 ]) to conserve the familiarity and aesthetics of the interfaces for human.

For this purpose, there are various studies on color-changing mechanisms and techniques. A majority of works used thermochromic materials to create color-changing effects due to their applicability to different substrate materials[ 15 , 28 , 64 , 69 ]. Researchers have used electronic components including resistive heaters[ 62 ] and Peltier elements[ 49 ] to activate the thermochromic paints. However, these methods could only support a uniform color-changing effect in one area driven by one electronic component, which greatly increases the volume and complexity of the circuitry to display dynamic and delicate information such as animation effects. Researchers also used photochromic materials to re-program the multi-color appearance of a 3D object[ 25 , 71 ]. However, this method requires configuring the object in precise optical equipment for exposure for at least one minute, which makes it challenging to utilize the color-changing process as a dynamic display. Other color-changing interfaces rely on fluidic materials[ 9 , 23 , 33 , 40 , 41 ] or luminous materials[ 7 , 21 , 45 , 63 ] to create 3D dynamic displays, while the requirement of transparent or luminous surface limits the surface apart from everyday materials. Overall, few previous works demonstrate the design and fabrication workflow and the necessary technical implementation of a color-changing interface with the capability of dynamic display on complex shapes and compatibility with everyday materials.

To address the above limitations, we introduce Thermotion , a novel method using thermofluidic composites with thermochromic paints to create color-changing interfaces, which enables dynamic and programmable animation effects to display on two and three-dimensional object surfaces compatible with flexible everyday materials, e.g., paper and fabric. Here we used thermofluids to refer to the fluidic material used for heat transfer with controllable temperature and flow behavior, which originates as a mechanical engineering study[ 1 ] and now widely involved in disciplines spanning from energy system analysis[ 51 ], biological research[ 10 ] to HCI fields as well. Drawing on the flexibility and the thermal controllability of thermofluids, HCI researchers have developed flexible and dynamic thermal feedback devices, e.g., thermal rings[ 20 ] or thermal sleeves[ 18 , 35 ] for research on thermal feedback. These methods inspire us to utilize thermofluids as an actuator for thermochromic materials to explore a color-changing interface with a higher dynamic expressiveness on complex shapes and wide compatibility with flexible everyday materials.

Specifically, Thermotion allows us to use thermofluidic flows in the channels embedded under the object surfaces to dynamically control the temperature and the color-changing effects of the thermochromic paints on the object surfaces. We presented the mechanism and structure of the composites and conducted a quantitative analysis of the thermochromic performance. We then addressed the design space of the animation effects derived from the structure, including a series of animation effect primitives and two modification effects. We proposed the design workflow of Thermotion with a customized design tool that aids users in fluidic structure design and real-time animation simulation. We also demonstrated the fabrication pipelines of Thermotion, which are compatible with flexible substrates (e.g., silicone) and everyday sheet-like materials for the surface (e.g., paper and fabric). Based on these, we evaluated the simulation performance, repeatability, durability, and size of Thermotion composites. Visually and thermally dynamic objects, including dynamic artifacts, teaching aids, and ambient displays, are used in a series of application scenarios. As an exploration, we expected to use Thermotion to envision not only research issues on multi-modal information display with both dynamic visual and thermal feedback, but also an opportunity to extend thermofluidic composites to other heat-related practices in HCI (e.g., thermal haptic interfaces or thermoreactive shape-changing interfaces) to create further dynamic and programmable interactions with temperature.

Our contribution includes:

  • Design and fabrication workflow of Thermotion, a thermofluidic color-changing composites that enables animation effects to display on two and three-dimensional object surfaces compatible with flexible everyday materials, e.g., paper and fabric.
  • The basic structure of Thermotion composites and the derived design space of the animation effects using Thermotion.
  • A customized design tool to aid users in designing and simulating Thermotion based on finite volume method (FVM) and a fabrication pipeline of Thermotion compatible with a few flexible everyday materials.
  • Application scenarios that demonstrate the benefits of Thermotion in HCI.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Colour-Changing Interfaces

Color changing is a critical output channel in the tangible user interface. In HCI fields, multiple mechanisms are explored to design and fabricate two and three-dimensional color-changing interfaces.

Many researchers used thermochromic materials to create color-changing interfaces (e.g., dynamic crafts, on-body decorations, and textiles), with different temperature-changing devices including Peltier elements[ 34 , 49 , 65 ], printed conductive ink[ 62 , 64 ], conductive thread or other resistive heating elements[ 12 , 15 , 27 , 28 , 65 , 69 ]. However, the above techniques could only support a uniform temperature-changing process of one area driven by one electronic component, resulting in a uniform color-changing effect in that area. To create an animation effect, joint use of multiple components is necessary, which significantly increases the volume and complexity of the circuitry. Besides, rigid Peltier elements can hardly support complex 3D curved surfaces, and resistive heaters cannot actively cool down to reverse the color-changing effects.

Jin and Wessely et al.[ 25 , 71 ] used photochromic materials to change the appearance of a 3D object. By exposing the photochromic materials to the light of different wavelengths, different color channels can be saturated or desaturated to display different colors on the object. However, this method requires the object to be placed inside precise optical equipment and a few minutes (≥ 1 mins ) of exposure to achieve the color change. Therefore, it is challenging to utilize and design the color change process as an animation display.

Other studies used fluidic interfaces to create 3D color-changing interfaces, such as programmatically controlling the position of the colored fluid in transparent flexible tubes[ 23 , 33 ] or transparent milli-/microfluidic devices[ 9 , 16 , 40 , 41 ]. Some researchers also used luminous materials to change colors on 3D surfaces, including applying the electroluminescent material on the object surfaces[ 21 , 45 ] or using optical materials to refract the internal LED's light to the object surfaces[ 7 , 63 ]. All of these methods can create dynamic displays, while the requirement of an external transparent or luminous surface limits the compatibility of the object surface to everyday materials, and few of them demonstrated the necessary technical implementation to create animations.

Our work adds to these studies and contributes a new color-changing mechanism that can create dynamic and programmable animation effects on two and three-dimensional surfaces using thermofluidic composites. The surface material of the composites is also applicable with a few everyday materials, e.g., paper or fabric.

2.2 Thermofluidic Composites in HCI

Heat, as a kind of immaterial, has been widely researched in HCI. And various techniques of manipulating heat have been practiced. In studying human thermal feedback, many researchers have used resistive heaters and thermoelectric heating elements such as Peltier elements[ 4 , 48 , 50 , 72 ] for temperature control. However, emerging techniques such as thermofluidic composites have shown significant advantages as an alternative, since the utilization of fluid as the heat transfer medium makes the composites not only flexible to fit complex curved surfaces but also capable of dynamically controlling the temperature in both temporal and spatial dimension by the fluidic flow and temperature control. For instance, HydroRing[ 20 ] used a finger-worn device with liquids traveling through a latex tube to provide haptic sensations of dynamic temperature, pressure, and vibration, on the fingertip in mixed-reality scenarios. Therminator[ 18 ] proposed a thermal display, using liquids with different temperatures flowing in heat conducting tubes to provide warm and cold on-body feedback coupled with VR visual feedback. ThermoCaress[ 35 ] presented an inflatable haptic device, using water and air for presenting temperature and pressure haptic feedback to create a stroking sensation on the forearm.

In the study of thermoreactive materials, although resistive heaters and thermoelectric heating elements have also been widely used to activate the changes of the materials’ physical properties such as color[ 12 , 34 , 62 ], shape[ 19 , 22 , 44 , 59 , 66 ] or phase[ 8 , 17 ], few studies have used thermofluidic composites as the actuator. As for the comparative studies, research on 4D printing uses hot water/air bath to actuate the throughout pre-programmed shape-changing effect of the objects made by shape memory thermoplastic[ 5 , 57 , 67 , 68 ] or phase transition materials[ 26 , 54 ]. Mueller[ 43 ] and Ko[ 31 ] use hot air guns to provide a hot air flow and selectively activate the phase change of local structures of the plastic objects for a local shape modification. Even though these works used fluid as the heat source, they ignored the use of the fluidic structure as the container to control the fluidic flow and temperature in order to activate the thermoreactive effect of the materials in a more accurate, dynamic, and controllable way.

In this work, we focused on using thermofluidic composites to activate thermochromic materials as an exploration, expecting to take advantage of the thermofluids’ capability of dynamic temperature control in the design practice of thermochromic materials.

2.3 Fabrication of Interactive Objects with Fluidic Channels

In HCI, various studies have designed interactive objects with embedded fluidic channels to provide diverse functions with many explorations of different fabrication methods.

3D printing is a frequently-used method. Previous work directly printed fluidic channels inside 3D objects with 3D printers to achieve different input and output functions. For example, Tejada[ 60 ] printed air channels inside the object to perform pneumatic touch sensing. Yan[ 73 ] printed hydraulic chambers to act as a shape-changing actuator. Savage[ 53 ] printed inner channels with electronic sensing or actuation components routed through to add interactivity to 3D prints.

The multi-layer construction is another feasible method. Researchers used printed molds to cast or engrave the substrate with open fluidic channels and then sealed the substrate with a top layer to construct inner fluidic channels[ 11 , 36 , 58 , 61 , 74 ]. Some researchers also used laser cutting to hollow or engrave the middle layers as the channel layer and then stuck multiple layers together to construct a 3D object with fluidic channels[ 16 , 40 , 41 ]. The multi-layer method applies to both solid and flexible materials.

Besides, researchers also used heat sealing to seal multi-layer plastic films with designed patterns to fabricate fluidic channels or bags[ 9 , 35 , 37 , 47 ], which is capable of fabricating fluidic structures in sheet-like form factor.

In this work, we used consumer-grade 3D printers to fabricate thermofluidic composites with fluidic channels embedded under both planar and curved surfaces, and we used silicone casting to make flexible composites. Besides, we also implemented a design tool to assist users in designing and generating fluidic channel structures with high complexity in 3D scenes.

3 THERMOTION COMPOSITES

3.1 Mechanism and Structure

The fundamental working mechanism of Thermotion is as follows: Thermofluids of different temperatures flow through the channels embedded under the object's surface. As a result of heat conduction, the temperature of the object surface is dynamically changed and controlled. This activates the color-changing effects of the thermochromic paintings on the object's surface, thereby creating animation effects. To systematically utilize the above mechanism, we divided the Thermotion thermofluidic composites into four layers, shown in Figure 2 , including channel layer , fluid layer , heat diffusion layer , and painting layer . Each layer has multiple properties that can be configured or constrained to achieve different functions.

The channel layer is the container of the thermofluids with fluidic channels embedded under the object surface. The layer is used to transform the flow of the thermofluids into a temperature-based "flowing" display on the outer surface via heat conduction. To obtain an ideal result, on the one hand, we set a small thickness (0.5 mm ) of fluidic channels’ outer wall to reduce the vertical thermal resistance. On the other hand, we chose a low thermal conductivity for the channel layer materials to weaken the undesirable horizontal heat transfer of the channel fins. In our work, we used common 3D printing materials (e.g., photosensitive resin and polycarbonate) and silicone as the channel layer materials. We set a minimal wall thickness of 0.5 mm to ensure good processing quality. The cross-section of the fluidic channel can vary from a semicircle to a rectangle or other polygons to fit the shape of the outer surface. In terms of the size of the cross-section, a smaller diameter will give better controllability of the fluid movement against the gravity effect, nevertheless increasing the resistance of the flow and the risk of blockages of the channels during 3D printing procedures[ 60 ]. Considering the above, we set the diameter of the narrowest point of the cross-section to 2 ∼ 3 mm in our work. The geometry pattern of the fluidic channels can be designed either linear or non-linear to obtain different thermofluids distribution patterns, thus opening the design space for temperature-changing and color-changing patterns. Each connected channel has two exit channels connected to two nozzles, one as an inlet and another as an outlet.

The fluid layer refers to the thermofluids with different temperatures inside the channel layer. The layer works as the cold and heat sources of the thermofluidic composites. The temperature, rate, and direction of the thermofluidic flow can be dynamically changed to compose various temperature-changing and color-changing processes. In our work, we used water as the thermofluids because of its wide availability and relatively large specific heat capacity (4.2 × 10 3 J /( kg · K ) at 20 ℃), which can reduce the decay of the temperature difference between the thermofluids and the environment during the flow.

The heat diffusion layer is an optional layer attached to the outer surface, which is used to intensify the temperature diffusion on the outer surface. A larger thermal conductivity of the diffusion layer will give a smoother temperature distribution of the layer's covered area, resulting in a smoother color-changing effect. In our work, we used isotropic copper tapes with a thickness of 0.05 mm as the heat diffusion layer material, while we still anticipate a larger design space using materials with different thermal conductivities or anisotropy.

The painting layer is the outermost layer of the composites, which contains thermochromic paints with customized contents. The paints can be painted either directly on the object surface or on a sheet-like material (e.g., paper or fabric) attached to the object surface. Benefiting from the controllability of the thermofluids’ temperature, we can control the color of the paints by using the thermofluids to display specific temperatures according to the paints’ color-temperature mapping property.

3.2 Thermochromic Performance

We conducted a quantitative analysis of the thermochromic performance of the composites. We designed a test device and recorded the temperature and color change with time.

The fluidic structure and dimensions of the test device are shown in Figure 3 a, which was designed to evaluate the thermochromic performance of two kinds of heat (and cold) sources: the linear heat source by a single-lined fluidic channel (lower), and the surface heat source by a group of compact-lined fluidic channels (upper). These two models can abstract most heat sources by fluidic channels. We 3D-printed the channel layer with water-washable resin by a stereolithography 3D printer. We then fabricated the devices both with a copper tape heat diffusion layer (blur-on device) and without a copper tape (blur-off device). We tested both kinds of the devices. The painting layer was fabricated with 45℃ discoloration red paints, which displays red under 45℃ and white above 45℃[ 32 ]. We first painted the paints on a printing paper and then attached the paper to the surface of the devices.

To evaluate the thermochromic performance, we first waited for the test device to have an even temperature at the room temperature of 26℃ and then perfused with 60℃ water at a rate of about 260 mL / min . We used a camera and thermal imager to record the photography and thermography videos. Temperatures of two checkpoints P 1 and P 2 (marked in Figure 3 a) were recorded by the thermal imager. We repeated the above procedure three times.

Figure 3 b shows the temperature-changing curves (0-30s) of P 1 and P 2 on both blur-on and blur-off devices, where four curves were calibrated by eliminating the delay of the flow's movement. Figure 3 c-f shows the photography and thermography images of the color-changing process. We observed that the color changed from red to white, as expected, when both linear and planar heat sources raised the temperature on devices both with and without a heat diffusion layer. We also found that it all took a few seconds for the color to change from red to white, which was attributed to the duration of heat conduction (see the temperature rising curve in Figure 3 b) and the temperature range for color changing[ 32 ] (see the region between two horizontal dashed lines in Figure 3 b). To describe the above phenomenon, We defined local color-changing duration τ c as a measure of the duration of one point to switch color. An example of τ c (≈ 3.7 s ) of P 1 without a heat diffusion layer is shown in Figure 3 b. Implied by the mechanism of Thermotion, we can infer that τ c is a result of many factors, including the material's thermal conductivity, the thermofluidic temperature, the color-temperature mapping of the paints, etc., and it may influence the key factors of animation effect design, e.g., duration and visual resolution. These factors will be discussed in the following section.

4 ANIMATION EFFECT DESIGN

An overview of the animation effect design space of Thermotion is illustrated in Figure 4 . In the following section, we first introduced the animation effect primitives in six different types and two variations on two properties. We then introduced two modification effects, including blur effect and multi-color effect . At last, we analyzed two key factors of animation effect design, which are duration and visual resolution.

4.1 Animation Effect Primitives

Animation effect primitives describe the basic manipulation of imagery to create dynamic visual effects. Based on the framework of animation design in the relevant fields of visual communication, e.g., film editing[ 13 ] and GUI design[ 29 ], we focused on a set of primitives that may function by Thermotion, tested and found the available primitives, and framed them by the design properties of the thermofluidic flow as follows.

By varying the geometry pattern of the fluidic channels in the channel layer, Thermotion can create various animation effects of one activating area to change the color from one to another. Each row of Figure 5 demonstrates an example of an animation effect primitive on a planar or curved surface. All except Figure 5 d3 change the color from white to red, using 45℃ discoloration red paints, which are pre-heated by 60℃ water to display white and then perfused with 20℃ water to activate the turning-red animation effect. Figure 5 d3 uses liquid crystal, which changes multiple colors in sequence from 28-34℃, as the paint to dynamically display a writing symbol of a treble clef. The device is perfused with 20℃ and 60℃ water in turn. Details, including the size, duration, and 3D model of each example, are given in Figure 5 . Photography and thermography images by the camera and thermal imager are also recorded in Figure 5 . We also provided the 3D models for these examples to help replicating 1 .

The primitives are clustered into the following types:

  1. Wipe uses channels with a compact-lined (serpentine or helical line) pattern to change the temperature line by line along the arrangement direction, which creates a wiping animation effect.
  2. Spread uses channels with a spiral pattern to change the temperature radially from the center to the border of an area or inversely, which creates a spreading animation effect.
  3. Texture uses channels with a non-linear mesh-like geometry pattern to display a temperature distribution with a similar pattern, which leads to a textured animation effect.
  4. Path uses serpentine channels (Figure 5 d1,2) arranged along a given path, which changes the temperature as well as the color along the path. When it meets an intersection point, a bridge channel is designed to connect the channels in one direction across the channel to another direction. Path primitives can also be realized with a drawing curve (Figure 5 d3). However, due to the sparse distribution of the thermofluids in this case, the gradient of the temperature distribution is likely to be small. So we recommend using highly sensitive thermochromic material (e.g., liquid crystal) for the painting layer in this case.
  5. Customization . Thermotion can also support channels with customized patterns to enlarge the design space of the animation effect primitives. For example, Figure 5 e1 uses a Hilbert line to create a pixel-style animation effect. Figure 5 e2 uses a growing curve[ 52 ] to create an irregular-shaped animation effect.
  6. Fade does not rely on any particular channel pattern as long as the channels are compactly arranged. It instead depends on a gradual change of the fluid temperature and a blur effect, which are further introduced in the following.

Based on different types of primitives, Thermotion can also create variations of the primitives on two properties, which are continuity and direction , by varying the temperature and direction of the thermofluidic flow.

Continuity refers to whether the animation effect is spatially continuous or spaced, which is determined by the sequence of the thermofluidic flow in different temperatures. Figure 5 a-e show the continuous version for each primitive, where each primitive adopts a single temperature change in one animation duration. By contrast, if we increase and decrease the temperature with a certain period shorter than the full animation duration, we can create the spaced version of the primitives. Figure 6 a2 shows the spaced version for primitives of horizontal wiping, redial spreading, and latitudinal wiping, respectively, where we can observe broad stripe patterns in both photography and thermography images. It is worth mentioning that if we instead change the temperature in a sufficiently slow and gradual way, it is likely to achieve an approximately uniform temperature change of an area, which leads to the fade primitive in Figure 5 f.

Direction refers to the spatially moving direction of an animation effect. This property is available only for the primitives with a definite thermofluidic flow direction (e.g., * marked primitives in Figure 5 ), which determines the moving direction of the animation. By coordinating the changes of the thermofluidic direction and temperature, two looping modes can be created (Figure 6 b). Specifically, after an animation effect is finished, if we continue to supply the thermofluids while only reversing the temperature changes without reversing the flow direction, the color will change back to the initial state, maintaining the same animation direction, which creates a one-way loop . By contrast, if we reverse the temperature changes and the flow direction simultaneously, the animation will change back to the initial state as a backward playing, which creates a reversed loop .

4.2 Blur Effect

Thermotion can also design the blur effect of the animation to expand the visual expressiveness. The blur effect can be realized by adding a highly thermally conductive material (e.g., copper tape in this work) as the heat diffusion layer to smooth the temperature distribution as well as the color distribution. This effect can be used to alleviate the uneven traces appearing in the color-changing region of an areal primitive. (The traces are caused by the uneven heat conduction between the channels and their fins. Typical examples can be observed in Figure 5 a,b) Figure 7 gives two examples of horizontal wiping and growing curve adopting the blur effect. Compared with the corresponding examples without the blur effect, we can see smoother patterns in both photography and thermography images, which create a softer visual effect. Besides, this effect is also a preferable choice for the fade primitive to achieve an approximately uniform temperature distribution of one area.

4.3 Multi-color Effect

Color is an essential factor of animation design. Thermotion can support multi-color effect based on multi-stage thermochromic paints and the controllability of the thermofluids temperature. Figure 8 gives two examples using the horizontal wiping primitive, where we mixed three kinds of discoloration paints (22℃ discoloration blue, 35℃ discoloration yellow, and 45℃ discoloration red) to make multi-stage thermochromic paints. Figure 8 a,b shows the same color-changing bar displaying four different colors, respectively, which are black, orange, red, and white, perfused by the thermofluids with four temperatures. Figure 8 c,d shows the same bar displaying a chromatographic effect. The bar was perfused with 10℃ and 60℃ water successively from right to left to create a temperature gradient to activate this chromatographic effect.

4.4 Duration

Duration is an important factor in animation effect design. According to the mechanism of Thermotion, we can infer that two main factors affect the duration of the animation effects by Thermotion, which are the motion of the thermofluids and the heat conduction process. Taking the above factors into consideration, we proposed two theoretical formulas to estimate the animation effect duration t animation .

For most animation effects caused by a moving thermofluidic flow with a sudden change of temperature (e.g., wipe , spread , and path primitives):

\begin{equation} t_{animation}\approx \tau _c+V/Q \end{equation}
(1)
where τ c is the local color-changing duration, defined in Section 3.2 , to describe the duration of one point to switch color caused by heat conduction. V represents the volume of the fluidic channel, and Q represents the rate of the thermofluidic flow.

For animation effects caused by a gradual change of the thermofluidic temperature (e.g., fade primitive):

\begin{equation} t_{animation}\approx \tau _c+t_{\Delta T} \end{equation}
(2)
where t ΔT represents the temperature-changing duration of the thermofluidic flow to switch the thermochromic color.

The above formulas can be used as a primary method to conduct the design of the animation duration, i.e., if τ c is estimated, users can approximately determine Q or t ΔT of the thermofluidic flow to achieve an expected design of the animation effect duration. However, the duration reached by this method may bring a large error, and the duration caused by the heat conduction through the heat conduction layer (e.g., texture primitive) is hard to estimate. This prompts us to provide a simulation tool to assist in designing and verifying the animation duration with a higher accuracy, which is further introduced in Section 5.2 .

It is worth mentioning that, implied by Equation 1 and 2 , a large τ c may limit the sensitivity of the animation effect by limiting the minimal animation duration. To keep a small τ c , one potential approach is to accelerate the heat conduction effect, such as increasing the thermal conductivity or the temperature difference, or decreasing the thickness of the outer wall of the channel layer, according to Fourier's law of heat conduction. Another potential approach is to apply more sensitive thermochromic materials such as liquid crystal, which narrows the temperature range for color changing, leading to a shorter color-changing duration.

4.5 Visual Resolution

The visual resolution of the animation effects by Thermotion can be analyzed in two aspects. One aspect is the resolution of the content in the painting, which is determined by the fabrication method of the painting layer. Benefiting from the compatibility with a hand-drawn or a printed painting layer, our method has an opportunity to reach a multi-color display with a high level of resolution of the content, compared to other works using fluidic or luminous material as the display mechanism[ 21 , 33 , 63 ].

The other aspect is the resolution of the animation. As we observed, the animation effects caused by a moving thermofluidic flow all perform a blurry transition zone at the moving boundary of the color-changing area (e.g., In Figure 5 a1, the transition zone of the horizontal wiping primitive displays a gradient fill from white to red with a width of about 3.5 cm ). This phenomenon determines the spatial resolution of the animation effects. We attribute this phenomenon to the effect of τ c spreading along the movement of the thermofluidic flow, which indicates us to estimate the transition zone width w transition by:

\begin{equation} w_{transition}\approx v\times \tau _c \end{equation}
(3)
where v represents the velocity of the thermofluidic flow. Potential approaches to decrease w transition include reducing the flow velocity v or the local color-changing duration τ c .

5 DESIGN TOOL OF THERMOTION

To help researchers and designers with the Thermotion animation effect design, we introduce a customized design tool to assist in the composites’ structure design and animation simulation.

The tool is oriented to accomplish the complex structure design of the composites with only a few parameters, according to a particular animation effect. It is also implemented with a simulation function to provide a real-time simulation of the compiled effects of the layered composites with photography and thermography views that aim to help users virtually evaluate and modify the composites by trial and error before the fabrication process. The design tool can export necessary digital files and data to support the subsequent fabrication process after the configuration of the composites. A comprehensive walk-through of the design tool is shown in Figure 9 , where only the relevant section of the GUI is shown for each step.

Different from existing computational design tools and simulation platforms such as COMSOL, our design tool, on the one hand, integrates a series of geometric calculations to provide a simple and intuitive structure design procedure for Thermotion composites. On the other hand, it simplifies the physical model specifically for the Thermotion mechanism to support a real-time and interactive simulation function. The design tool is developed based on Rhino 7 Grasshopper platform with the simulation algorithm developed as an extension, written in c++, and launched on Ubuntu(20.04) on windows subsystem for Linux (wsl). We provided the release version and the open source codes for the design tool 2 .

We next introduce the detailed walk-through and implementation of the design tool through the following two stages: structure design and animation simulation.

5.1 Stage 1: Structure Design

During the structure design stage, the user designs the fluidic structure of the composites with a few parameters according to the expected animation effect. This stage is conducted with the following steps:

  1. Import the 3D model for animation effect creation.
  2. Generate the channel lines (Figure 9 a). The user first chooses a primitive of channel lines according to the expected animation effect (e.g., choosing the spiral line for a spread effect in Figure 9 a) and then configures a few basic parameters and geometry of the primitive (e.g., drawing the boundary line and inputting the spacing of the spiral). The system automatically fills the rest of the channel lines in real-time for debugging during the process. Customized channel lines are also allowed in this step.
  3. Generate the channel structures (Figure 9 b) based on the channel lines from step (2). The user first selects a generation method according to the geometric situation and inputs a few dimension parameters. Then, the system computes the 3D channel structures for a few seconds.
  4. Complete the fluidic structures (Figure 9 c). The user needs to add inlet and outlet piping to the fluid structures in this step. They first input a series of control points of the flow paths and necessary dimension parameters, and then the system provides a real-time preview of the generated pipes during the process. After that, users can export the 3D files used in the following fabrication pipeline.

To support fluidic structure in various two and three-dimensional shapes, we provided a few primitives of the channel lines and two methods to generate the fluidic channel structures. Figure 10 a shows an example of a Stanford bunny with different fluidic structures using this design tool. The example includes primitives of linear lines and spiral line , which are generated based on an iterative "growing" process along the curved surfaces for a uniform distribution, and helical line , which works based on interpolation of a series of cross-section outlines. A growing curve [ 52 ] is also shown in Figure 10 a to demonstrate the extensibility of other customized channel lines.

We provided two methods to calculate the channel structures along the channel lines to meet different geometric situations. Figure 10 b shows the difference method , which calculates the Boolean difference of an areal fluidic structure by a generated thin channel wall along the channel lines (in red) as the fluidic channels (in blue). Figure 10 c shows the intersection method , which calculates the Boolean intersection of lofted 3D carving traces along the channel lines (in red) and the substrate as the final channels (in blue). The intersection method can also dynamically vary the cross-section size to pursue an even spacing between the channels. In all of our tests, the difference method usually performs a fine consistency of the spacing between the channels, while the intersection method is more likely to support complex channel curves or complex curved object surfaces. In the current version of the design tool, we open the above choices for users to achieve the best performance in customized assignments.

5.2 Stage 2: Animation Simulation

In the simulation stage, the user obtains a real-time simulation of both photography and thermography animation effects of the composites with the following steps:

  1. Initialize the simulation configuration , including the geometry for different layers, the color-temperature mapping of the thermochromic paints, and other material or empirical parameters.
  2. Start the simulation (Figure 9 d). Users can play with the thermofluidic flow i.e., the flow temperature, rate, and direction, in real-time and switch between 3D thermography and photography views to watch the real-time simulation results. In this step, users can virtually arrange the thermofluidic flow to rehearse the animation effects.
  3. Record and playback . When the simulation is done, thermography and photography videos and operation logs of the thermofluidic flow with time stamps are provided for deeper analysis.

Our simulation implementation consists of three computation units: fluidic simulation, thermal simulation, and color-temperature mapping.

The fluidic simulation unit computes the dynamic distribution of the thermofluids in the fluid layer. To simplify the simulation process, we assumed that the water flows are laminar, neglected the fluidic dynamic effects of the flows, e.g., density, gravity, and viscosity, and only calculated the flows’ movement with kinematic models: For the linear water flows, we simplified the water flows as a series of moving segmented heat sources and neglected the exchange of matter and heat between the cross sections of the flows. For mesh-like water, we neglected the irregular perfusion process of the meshed structure and simplified the water as a meshed heat source with an even temperature. We allowed different dimensions of the fluidic channels, which satisfied the above assumption of the kinematic models.

The thermal simulation unit computes the dynamic temperature distribution on the composites’ surface caused by heat conduction. Due to the low thermal conductivity of the substrate material of the channel layer, we neglected the heat conduction and residue between channels and only considered them on the composites’ surface. Our simulation tool used the finite volume method (FVM)[ 38 , 39 ] to simulate heat distribution on the outer surface of the channel layer and the optional heat diffusion layer. Since layers are thin, we simplified the heat conduction along the layers as a 2D problem and assumed that the heat conduction between the layers is proportional to the temperature difference based on Newton's Cooling Law. Therefore, the heat conduction between layers can be expressed with a unified formula:

\begin{equation} \frac{\partial Q}{\partial t}=hA(T_2-T_1) \end{equation}

The temperature-color mapping unit computes the dynamic color distribution on the painting layer. We simplified the relationships between temperature and color are approximate one-to-one mappings. We also assumed that the temperature distribution on the painting layer is consistent with the inner neighbor layer. Therefore, when provided with a customized color-temperature mapping, our simulation model results in color distribution according to the temperature distribution.

Among all the parameters that need preset, intrinsic parameters of materials such as heat capacity can be determined theoretically, while coefficients of heat conduction between layers (Table 1 ) should be determined empirically. In this version, we used the parameters of resin as an approximation for all the other materials used for the channel layer since the physical properties of these materials are similar. We applied an exemplar method to estimate those empirical coefficients, which is detailed in Section 7.1 .

6 FABRICATION PIPELINE OF THERMOTION

After the design procedure, the Thermotion composites can be fabricated with the following pipeline in four steps. Figure 11 shows the overall fabrication pipeline.

6.1 Step1: Fabricate Channel Layer

We propose two methods, 3D printing and silicone casting, to fabricate rigid and flexible channel layers respectively.

We used the 3D printing method to print rigid channel layers directly. We tested stereolithography (SLA) (Figure 12 a-c) and fused deposition modeling (FDM) (Figure 12 d-f) techniques, using 3D printers of ELEGOO Mars 2 Pro, FormLabs Form 2, and Ultimaker S5. Both techniques performed successful prints of 3D objects with internal channel structures as the 3D model files exported by the design tool. To make these objects work effectively as the channel layer, both techniques need additional post-processing steps: For SLA prints, we used syringes to push cleaning solvent (e.g., isopropyl alcohol or water) through the channels several times before the post-cure process to drain the inner residual resin and prevent blockages (Figure 12 b). For FDM prints, we applied UV glue to cover the curved outer surface of the fluidic channels to avoid leaking (Figure 12 e). Comparing these two techniques, we found SLA performed a shorter printing time and better printing quality for complex curved structures, while FDM had good support for planar structures, especially for those larger than the printing size of a consumer-grade SLA printer.

The silicone casting method can be used to produce a flexible channel layer. In this method, we first used 3D-printed molds exported by the design tool to cast the channel layer open towards the outer surface (Figure 12 g) and then sealed the open surface by casting a 0.5 mm -thickness silicone sheet (Figure 12 h). Although we used planar molds for the casting procedure, this method draws on the silicone material's flexibility to fabricate the flexible channel layer to fit rigid curved surfaces or flexible surfaces.

6.2 Step2: Fabricate Heat Diffusion Layer

In this project, the heat diffusion layer was generally fabricated by cutting copper tapes into designed shapes and pasting them at the corresponding position on the outer surface of the channel layer. While for a curved surface, we first split the curved shapes into small pieces, cut small planar pieces, and then pasted them one by one to construct fitted curved shapes (Figure 12 i). The micron-scale thickness and high flexibility of copper tapes ensured a good fit of the curved shapes. Although the above procedures are conducted manually or assisted with printed outline templates, there is an apparent opportunity to optimize the process with a digital laser cutter or a vinyl cutter[ 14 , 34 ], discussed in Section 9.2 .

6.3 Step3: Fabricate Painting Layer

The easiest way we tested to fabricate the painting layer is to paint the thermochromic paints directly on the object's outer surface (Figure 12 j). The object surface can also be coated with background paint in advance when necessary (e.g., using white paint to cover the copper tapes or black paint as the background of liquid crystal). To ensure good adhesion of the thermochromic paint, we made the thermochromic paints by mixing the thermochromic pigments with a painting medium (e.g., gesso or glue solution). Besides, we also tested by first painting the thermochromic paints on a piece of everyday sheet-like material (e.g., paper or fabric) or using printed thermochromic papers and then attaching them to the object surfaces as the painting layer (Figure 12 k,l). This method maintained a specific tactile texture of the surface material, which makes it possible to conserve the familiarity and aesthetics of the interfaces. Furthermore, tests of the silicone channel layer combined with the paper-/fabric-based painting layer maintained the flexibility of the paper/fabric. Above all, the thermochromic materials used in this work were provided by Shenzhen Dongfang Color Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China.

6.4 Step4: Generate Fluid Layer

To generate the fluid layer, we implemented a low-cost hydraulic circuit, which could programmably control the flow rate, temperature, and direction and then activate the programmed animation effects.

Figure 13 a gives an overview of the prototype. Two flows of water are drawn by two peristaltic pumps (Kamoer KHS-SW3N40) from the hot and the cold tanks, respectively (with flow rates of Q 1 and Q 2 ), and then merged to generate the expected thermofluidic flow. Thus, the rate of the thermofluidic flow is adjusted by Q 1 + Q 2 , and the temperature of the thermofluids is adjusted by Q 1 / Q 2 [ 20 ]. We used a flowmeter to measure the flow rate and used a thermal imager to measure the temperature. In this prototype, we can reach a flow rate from about 36 to 260 mL/min and adjust the temperature between the water temperatures of the hot and the cold tanks. Note that users may alter the pumps’ type and the water temperatures to meet customized situations. The flow direction is switched by an "H-bridge" module, which works similarly to the H-bridge circuit but uses four solenoid valves (ZhiRongHuaGuan Fa2021A) as the switches. Figure 13 b shows one of the working states when only half of the valves are open. The flow direction can be reversed when the valves of the other half of the module are open.

We used Arduino Nano and L298N driver boards to control the pump flow rates by PWM signals, as well as the on-off states of the four solenoid valves in the "H-bridge" module by bit signals. Arduino can output the above control signals by scheduled timers in the animation programs to display the programmed animation effects. We also provided manual regulators for the control circuit (i.e., two knobs for flow rate and temperature, and one button for flow direction), as well as the simulation tool, as methods to arrange the thermofluids in order to rehearse the animation program in the design workflow.

In addition, we also tested two non-electrical methods, including manual injection and natural force to generate the fluid layer. Specifically, we used syringes to manually inject fluid into the composites to conduct some rapid tests or as an approach for interactive application scenarios. We also tested using natural force to activate the thermofluidic flow as a passive method, e.g., using gravity to actuate the flow in structures like communicating vessels. This method can compose some self-contained systems that can create animation effects without electric power.

7 EVALUATION

7.1 Simulation Performance

To evaluate the performance of our simulation model, we first used the test device in Figure 3 a to estimate all four empirical parameters in Table 1 . The thermofluidic configuration (i.e., flow rate and temperature) in the virtual simulation platform was set the same as the experimental test. We then manually adjusted the four parameters, conducted the simulation repeatedly, and compared the simulated temperature-changing curves with the results from the experimental tests. Figure 14 a,b shows the results of the simulated temperature-changing curves using the estimated empirical parameters. The root mean square (RMS) between each simulated and experimental curve is no larger than 1.2 ℃; this is the best performance we have achieved. We also compared the simulated photography images with the results from the experimental test, both with and without the heat diffusion layer, shown in Figure 14 c-f, where the simulated and the experimental images show high consistency. Results of the estimation of the four determined empirical are shown in Table 1 . Note that the four empirical parameters are mainly relevant to the material physical properties and layer thicknesses. According to the simulation model in Section 5.2 , we may allow these variables by re-estimation of the empirical parameters through the above procedure.

We then adopted the above four estimated parameters for the simulation model and evaluated the performance of other Thermotion devices in different shapes. Figure 14 g-l show the results of three examples, including blurry growing curve, spaced latitudinal wiping and multi-color horizontal wiping. In the simulation, the thermofluidic configuration was set according to the experimental test. The color-temperature mapping of the thermochromic paints was estimated according to the experimental result in Figure 3 b and previous research[ 32 ]. Each example is simulated for a complete cycle of the animation effect. Comparing the photography images from both experimental and simulated results, we found that the simulation not only performed a high similarity with the photography images at the same moment, but also showed a high consistency of the animation effect during the complete cycle. These results demonstrate that our simulation tool can provide a highly reliable reference for the animation effects.

The durability of the composites varies from the materials. The devices with an FDM-plastic or silicone substrate performed good durability, with none of them damaged after four months at most. However, part of devices with resin substrates had deformation problems during or after the tests, e.g., warping or swelling. 18 of these devices, particularly made of water-washable resin, finally cracked. Besides, we observed a corrosion problem in part of the copper tapes used for the heat diffusion layer when covered in water-based paints for a few days.

Appropriate material choices may improve the durability of the composites. The deformation problem of the composites with a resin substrate may be attributed to the resin's shrinkage after printing, which is a common phenomenon of resin-based 3D printing[ 2 ]. When the resin shrinks, the different densities of the solid and the fluidic channel regions may cause different macro shrinkage rates, leading to the composites warping or swelling. As a result, the resin with lower shrinkage rates, along with higher stiffness and higher strength, may be an ideal choice to reduce the deformation and the risk of cracking under a certain deformation[ 3 ]. (As an example, the composites used clear resin 3 in our fabrication records performed 0% crack problem with a higher tensile strength (>25%) than water-washable resin 4 which performed 38% crack problems.) Besides, the thermal properties of the substrate material are also important factors. We should choose the resin and the plastic with a heat deflection temperature higher than the upper limit of the thermofluidic temperature to avoid deformation when heated. As for the corrosion problem of the copper tapes, potential solutions include using corrosion-resistant aluminum tape or graphite sheet for heat diffusion layer as alternatives, or using a paper-/fabric-based painting layer to separate the copper tapes from the water-based paints.

7.3 Size and Thickness

Among all the successful tests, the smallest device contains the fluidic structure within the size of 4 × 3 × 2 cm , which is used as a facial part of a model (Figure 17 d). The size of the largest fluidic structure is within 26 × 16 × 1 cm used for a planar rectangle thermochromic painting. Multiple devices can be connected in series to reach a larger animation region while the resistance of the flows increases simultaneously. As for the thickness of the composites, we observed that a thinner thickness is more likely to cause a warping problem for composites with a resin substrate. In our tests, the thinnest thickness reached 5 mm , where the diameter of the fluidic channels was set to 3 mm . The 5 mm thickness was successfully applied to devices with all resin, FDM-plastic, and silicone substrates for at least one successful test.

8 APPLICATION SCENARIOS

In this section, we detail three application scenarios that are implied by the various dynamic animation effects of Thermotion. The 3D printing models used in the following examples are provided for replicating 5 .

8.1 Color-changing Artifacts

Various methods of dynamic artifacts have been explored to convey information or aesthetic expression through changes in objects’ appearance[ 15 , 21 , 33 ], which inspired us to propose Thermotion as an alternative method to create dynamic color-changing effects on 2D and 3D objects with a high expressiveness. Here we give three examples.

Figure 16 a&g show a dynamic calligraphy work for the Chinese character "movement". We installed a 3D-printed flow channel on the back of the paper written with thermochromic paints. By applying the path primitive, the static Chinese characters on the paper can dynamically show the writing process, making the works more aesthetically expressive. Figure 16 b&h show a dynamic water cup with a path primitive. The inside of the water cup is embedded with a fluidic channel that communicates with the inside of the cup. When the user pours hot water into the cup, driven by gravity, the hot water automatically flows into the flow channel, activating the surface of the cup wall to appear with a writing animation. Such interactive and interesting cups could be applied to unique scenes such as souvenir designing or tea ceremonies. The third example is a series of dynamic spherical tabletop sculptures in Figure 16 c&i, d&j, e&k, f&l. Through the combination of different primitives and blur effects, four different animation effects are finally produced on four spheres of the same size, including a blurry longitudinal wiping effect, a scattered-dot texture effect, a blurry irregular rendering effect, and a latitudinal ascending spiral-line effect from top to bottom. The expressiveness of these four dynamic spherical sculptures implies the opportunity for them to express metaphors for the weather, such as sunny, rainy, foggy, and windy.

Except for the dynamic water cup, all artifacts used the hydraulic circuit to generate the fluid layer to activate the programmed animation effects. The above dynamic expressiveness of Thermotion may expand the design space of dynamic sculpture creation.

8.2 Interactive Teaching Aids

Different from previous work[ 4 , 72 ], which focuses on combining visual and thermal feedback to enhance emotional perception, the higher dynamic display allows Thermotion to transmit more complex information through the combination of these two feedback channels. This property inspired us to explore the applications of multi-modal interactive teaching aids.

In geography education, scientific materials in the form of dynamic data visualization[ 6 , 55 ] are used to demonstrate the polar environment changes through the seasons. Using Thermotion, we can imitate the visual animation on a tangible book while augmenting the information expression by adding a thermal haptic channel to it. Figure 17 a-c show a dynamic book example, which uses a continuous, reversed-looped spread primitive to show the spread animation effects of sea ice increase and land vegetation decrease in winter. The user can see the white "ice" spreading from the pole, see the green "vegetation" disappearing radially, and feel the cold temperature spreading out simultaneously. The demonstration of summer reverses both visually and thermally. Figure 17 d-f shows another example of a dynamic physiology teaching aid, which uses a blurry, continuous, reversed-looped spread primitive to show the fever symptoms. Specifically, when we see redness on the model's forehead and cheeks, we feel warm touching these areas. When we take appropriate treatments, such as applying a cold compress, the redness will fade, and the model's temperature will return to normal. This interaction agrees with everyday human habits of handling a fever.

The above two examples both used the hydraulic circuit to generate the fluid layer to demonstrate the interactive experience. In the future, we can add more interactive fluidic structures to expand the interactivity of these teaching aids. These explorations also inspire us to explore whether the mixture of visual and thermal feedback would enhance the sense of information reception in specific scenarios.

8.3 Dynamic Skins for Ambient Display

Previous work has explored dynamic ambient display in many forms, including color changing[ 15 ], shape changing[ 22 ], or texture changing[ 47 ] to build dynamic wearables, notifications, or skins. Thermotion can provide non-intrusive visual and haptic feedback by changing the color and temperature at the same time on an object's surface. Its painting layer is also well-compatible with different everyday sheet-like materials such as paper, fabric, etc. These features allow us to see the potential of making Thermotion as dynamic skins for ambient display.

For example, we can apply Thermotion to the armrest of the seat. Figure 18 shows flexible composites made with silicone and attached to a fabric of an armrest. After people sit for a long time, the armrest will present a visual spaced-wiping effect (Figure 18 b), and at the same time, it will be accompanied by an uneven moving temperature distribution presenting a dynamic alternating warm and cold thermal feedback (Figure 18 f). Both thermal and visual feedback will remind the user to get up and rest in a non-intrusive way . When the user gets up, the temperature of the armrest is designed to remain high enough (Figure 18 g) so that the letters on the armrest will not disappear (Figure 18 c), prompting the user not to return to the seat. When the rest time is over, the user will see the text disappear (Figure 18 d), and the armrest will return to normal temperature (Figure 18 h), reminding the user to return to the seat.

Note that during the test, we ensured a safe contact temperature for the surface by limiting the upper temperature of the thermofluids (The experiment's highest temperature of the skin to touch was 44℃ according to the thermal imager). This example used the hydraulic circuit to generate the fluid layer to perform the functionality. In the future, we may integrate Thermotion with other fluidic systems, such as microfluidics, fluid logic system, etc.[ 40 , 42 ] to add input and calculation functions to the fluidic system and expand the design space.

9 DISCUSSION, LIMITATION, AND FUTURE WORK

9.1 Thermofluidic System

The thermofluidic system is the essential functional module of the composites, including the fluidic channels and the fluidic temperature and flow control systems. In this project, the electrical hydraulic circuit we prototyped is an open-loop control system with limited control accuracy. While referring to existing work[ 20 ], we believe the system could be optimized to be close-loop with integrated sensors and reach higher accuracy. The water we used currently is also heated or cooled by separate devices and recycled manually, which limits the hardware system's size and portability. Further technical work could build more self-contained systems in smaller size[ 41 , 46 ] to integrate the circuit to the dynamic objects in application scenarios.

Besides, the 3D printing techniques we used for fluidic channel fabrication limit the minimal scale of the channels. With an emerging trend of studies on microfluidic systems[ 40 , 42 ] and fabrication techniques[ 58 , 61 ], we envision that combining these techniques may downscale the composites to micro-scale and extend the functionality of the composites, such as performing more subtle and high-resolution animation effects in micro-scale, creating animations on thinner and more flexible materials and integrating computing functions to the composites. Related applications may include dynamic wearables or smarter ambient displays on more everyday objects. Meanwhile, the thermal performance of the thermofluidic system on a micro-scale needs to be further studied.

9.2 Design Tool and Simulation

The Thermotion design tool allows users to design the fluidic structures and simulate the functions of the composites with a GUI interface, while there are still limitations. Currently, the design tool only provides three integrated primitives of the channel lines, and geometric errors may occur when generating fluidic channels under some sharply curved surfaces. The algorithm could be optimized to be competent for fluidic structures with a wider variety and complexity. For the heat diffusion layer design, future work such as exporting the digital outline files or The design and fabrication process of the heat diffusion layer could be optimized with automatically unfolding and exporting the planar outlines of curved shapes[ 5 , 70 ] and cutting with digital machines[ 14 , 34 ]. Other tools such as adding pipes automatically[ 53 ] or choreography GUI[ 33 ] could also be integrated to accelerate the design process.

As for the simulation function, we only evaluated the performance with limited materials (e.g., resin) and a few constrained structural dimensions (e.g., layer thickness). Although different materials and layer thicknesses may be adapted by re-estimation of the empirical parameters, there is still further work to validate, evaluate, and expand the simulation applicability. Besides, more effects ignored in the current version (e.g., gravity, heat residue, and the decay of the thermofluidic temperature) could also be studied and included to improve the simulation accuracy.

9.3 Extension of Thermofluidic Composites

Thermotion uses thermofluidic composites to control the temperature of object surfaces dynamically. Combination with other thermoreactive materials or techniques may extend the value of thermofluidic composites.

High-resolution full-color inkjet 3D printing is an emerging technique to produce high-quality textures on 3D object surfaces[ 56 ]. Introducing this technique to Thermotion may provide an opportunity to fabricate dynamic color-changing objects with a single 3D printer and create high-quality and high-complexity animation effects on object surfaces. This may become a powerful approach for complex dynamic artifact creation or research issues on information reception with both visual and thermal multi-modal feedback. In addition to thermochromic materials, another promising direction is to combine thermofluidic composites with other thermoreactive materials, such as materials that change shape[ 5 , 44 ], stiffness[ 17 ], phase[ 37 , 54 ], etc. according to temperature. We may use thermofluidic composites to provide programmable temperature changes to control the above properties (e.g., activating a shape-changing effect in sequence). This may open the design space of thermofluidic composites to robotics or other programmable material interfaces. Besides, haptic and wearable devices[ 35 ] may also benefit from thermofluidic composites to provide a dynamic thermal display for humans, which could be utilized in virtual-reality or mixed-reality applications and research. In general, we believe that introducing thermofluidic composites to other heat-related practices in HCI may have an opportunity to create further dynamic and programmable human-temperature interaction.

10 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we presented Thermotion , a novel method using thermofluidic composites with thermochromic paints to create color-changing interfaces, which enables dynamic and programmable animation effects to display on two and three-dimensional object surfaces compatible with flexible everyday materials, e.g., paper and fabric. We presented the mechanism and structure of the composites and conducted a quantitative analysis of the thermochromic performance. We then addressed the design space of the animation effects, including a series of primitives and two modification effects, proposed the design workflow with a customized design tool that aids users in designing and simulating, and demonstrated the fabrication pipelines compatible with a few flexible everyday materials. Based on these, we evaluated the simulation performance, repeatability, durability, and size of the composites. A series of application scenarios were shown of both visually and thermally dynamic objects, including dynamic artifacts, teaching aids, and ambient displays. As an exploration, we expected to use Thermotion to envision not only research issues on multi-modal information display with both dynamic visual and thermal feedback, but also an opportunity to extend thermofluidic composites to other heat-related practices in HCI (e.g., thermal haptic interfaces or thermoreactive shape-changing interfaces) to create further dynamic and programmable interactions with temperature.
