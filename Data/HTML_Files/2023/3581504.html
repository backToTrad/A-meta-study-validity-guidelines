An ongoing mystery of HCI is how do well-intentioned designers consistently enable products with unintentionally evil consequences. Using “questionable values” as a lens, we retell and analyze four design scenarios for smart homes that were created by participants with an IoT toolkit we designed. The selected design scenarios reveal practices that violate principles of responsible smart home design. Through our analysis we show (1) how participants explore sensor-driven objectification of the home then leverage data for surveillance, nudging, and control over others; (2) how the dominant technosolutionist narratives of efficiency and productivity ground such questionable values; (3) and how the materiality of mass-produced sensors pre-mediates questionable design scenarios. We discuss how to attend to and utilize questionable values in design: Making space for questionable values will empower design researchers to better “look around corners”, anticipating tomorrow's concerns and forestalling the worst of their harms.

1 INTRODUCTION

With Internet of Things (IoT) enabled smart objects and services, surveillance of low wage workers [ 29 ] or optimization of industrial workers’ performance [ 28 ] are increasingly common practices. Likewise, the domestic sphere is steadily becoming more populated by smart objects and services that allow various others ‘to listen in’: Records from smart voice assistants are analyzed for quality control [ 60 ], imagery from camera-equipped smart doorbells has been handed over by smart home providers to law enforcement [ 17 , 37 ], and smart toys listen in to children's rooms [ 54 ]; collectively embedding workplace values such as efficiency and productivity into people's most private spaces. Simultaneously, there are calls within academia for a more nuanced understanding of the situated and heterogenous values a “good” home entails [ 1 , 8 , 21 , 58 , 59 , 81 ], spelled out in manifestos arguing for responsible smart home IoT design [ 10 , 30 , 45 ].

These discussions around smart homes and IoT speak to classic debates within HCI: How can we design technology together with those who will be using it in the future, to better align with people's needs and moral values [ 65 , 72 ]? But, what are preferable values? While HCI appreciates and often elevates the perspectives of users, what happens when designing with users results in designs that do not align with the social values that we as researchers feel committed to [ 20 , 30 , 64 , 78 ]? With this paper, we revisit four design scenarios for the smart home, that exhibit “questionable” values.

We begin this inquiry by contrasting the values exhibited by smart home designs found in the Western marketplace today with the values articulated by people who embrace living situations “alternative” to the mainstream [ 1 , 8 , 21 , 58 , 59 , 81 ]. Employing this contrast and responsible IoT manifestos [ 30 ] we define “questionable values” to describe design scenarios that irritate principles of trustworthiness, transparency, openness and/or responsibility and as a result surface problematic wants, needs, and values [ 30 ].

With questionable values as a lens we reflect on design scenarios for smart homes that were created and used by people in their own homes by utilizing a smart home IoT toolkit we designed. Revisiting our original transcripts and initial reports of three original studies, we select design scenarios that reveal “questionable” values. Through our analysis, we show 1) how participants explore sensor-driven objectification of the home, then accidentally or intentionally leverage data for surveillance, nudging, and control over others; 2) that the dominant narratives of efficiency and productivity already ground such questionable values; 3) how mass-produced sensors shape the possibilities of designing the smart home.

Our goal in this inquiry is to retrospectively reflect on the ethical issues in our own work and to shed light on the age-old phenomenon of “good” designers creating things that turn out to be “bad.” Cultural narratives (here: smart homes), available technologies (here: mass-produced sensors), and the materiality of numerical data itself collide to reproduce workplace values of surveillance, nudging, and control over others. How can we break this cycle? We call on design researchers not to ignore questionable outcomes in favor of “good” outcomes but instead, to make space for them early in the design process, creating a safe environment to explore these values before they impact people's lives. We offer specific recommendations to productively engage and embrace questionable values within design practice.

This paper contributes: 1) Questionable values as a lens to discuss and reflect ethical issues in co-design; 2) An empirical account of how to use questionable values to methodically look back and reflect on the ethical issues of one's own co-design work; and 3) Practice-oriented design proposals for a) employing questionable values when developing design activities; b) creating IoT materiality that enables, subverts, and amplifies values; c) engaging in public discourse around what sensors can do and should be allowed to do in order to better anticipate “accidentally evil” values.

2 RELATED WORK

Related work falls into three strands of research: 1) dominant values in smart home IoT design and resulting conflicts; 2) ways to bring forth alternative values in the IoT design space; 3) design toolkits and methods for the IoT design space that support people in imagining of and reflecting on smart home design. We take these as the foundation for our working definition of “questionable values” that we will use as a conceptual lens for our subsequent analysis of smart home designs created and utilized by our co-designers.

2.1 Values in Smart Home Design

The goals of people differ and so does the personal and social perception of what is right or wrong in terms of belief, ethics, politics, power, bias, or justice. As such: “Values is an expansive, inclusive term for people studying, or designing for, moral and practical goals alike” [ 70 ].

The home is our most private space and increasingly populated with smart agents that bring with them powerful sensing and computing power [ 47 ]. The smart home that we see in the Western consumer marketplace today builds on the availability of sensors and actuators for temperature, humidity, movement, light, motion, cameras and microphones; and the computing power to algorithmically interpret these. As Bill Gaver predicted 20 years ago: “There is a danger that as technology moves from the office into our homes it will bring along with it workplace values such as efficiency and productivity at the expense of other possibilities.” [ 32 ], A plethora of smart home devices today focus on a close set of predominant values. The design of these devices conceptualizes “the home” as homogenous sites to optimize for values closely tied to efficiency and productivity. Smart homes aim to: 1) increase energy efficiency [ 27 ]; 2) enforce compliant behavior [ 9 , 53 ]; 3) automate repetitive tasks such as hoovering or shopping consumables [ 71 ]; 4) increase security with smart locks or smart cameras [ 36 , 61 ]; 5) deliver convenience through automation [ 54 , 74 ].

Using sensor reading to increase efficiency is relatively easy, as sensor readings can be numerically operationalized: For example, designing a system where low energy consumption is good and high energy consumption is bad. However, personal and social goals, beliefs, ethics, and values do not follow a numerical dichotomy. They need to be negotiated on multiple layers of interests, conflicts, and contradictions and between various stakeholders. Sensor equipped smart home devices have been shown to disrupt values such as privacy, autonomy, transparency; and to trigger serious personal and social effects [ 19 , 46 , 69 ]. For example, smart homes have been repurposed by their main user to prevent unwanted use of domestic spaces by other people in their homes, to police access to commodities [ 26 ], or to explicitly monitor fellow dwellers [ 15 ]. Smart locks are one well-researched example for how such repurposing takes place and leads to value conflicts between home dwellers. Delegating home security to a smart lock can be simultaneously based on the common value “security” while undermining and supporting other values: Both parents and their teen children see smart locks as beneficial for home security, however, their interest in auditing home entries is in conflict with each other. Here, parents’ expectations of peace of mind collide with their teen-children's expectations of privacy [ 69 , 77 ].

If values are not negotiated between all dwellers, there is just a small step from using to misusing smart home devices. To date, the imagination and implementation of smart home designs follows a largely technical understanding of ubiquitous computing [ 12 , 42 , 80 ] and the economic imperative to scale up technology to decrease costs. While the last 20 years of smart home design centered on efficiency and productivity, there is plenty of proof of the values that are disrupted in doing so. Still, with commercial smart homes, these conflicting values only become apparent after their deployment [ 60 ] and there is relatively little attention to engage with conflicting values in the smart home sphere before these devices are produced and installed.

2.2 Alternative Values in Smart Home Design

Design research is well equipped to engage with conflicting values [ 68 ] through critique [ 24 , 78 ], reflection [ 68 ], and speculation [ 25 ]. These strategies help to understand and reflect on values and goals in designing. They also help to explicate underlying assumptions that designers and participants are not automatically aware of, such as techno-solutionism [ 11 ] or economic forces of scale that are embedded in the material with which we design. These strategies make visible the tensions and conflicts of certain values.

The awareness and unease about value conflicts in the smart home lead design research to engage with diverging, conflicting, or colliding values and actively question the dominant values of efficiency and productivity—and extended through the examples outlined above: compliancy, automation, security, and convenience. This research approaches ”the home” as a place that is not homogenous, but filled with individual meanings that go beyond simple dichotomies of good and bad. It aims at departing from visions of the home that are simplified and constrained, and that, instead, recognize conflicting values to use them productively in designing future smart home IoT. These inquiries provide nuanced accounts on how future smart objects may look like for non-stereotypical ways of domestic living, e.g. people living on houseboats or tiny apartments [ 21 , 58 , 59 ], shared apartments [ 19 ], co-housing [ 43 ], or mobile homes [ 82 ]. Design researchers thereby actively articulate seemingly peripheral and heterogenous values, contrasting the dominant, homogenous, one-size-fits-all, presumed global ideal of smart home design following workplace values. This body of work recognizes that tensions may not call for being designed against. To the contrary: If diverging needs and values are acknowledged and designed, rather than designed against, seemingly conflicting values can be used productively to understand and negotiate their ramifications for home dwellers [ 38 ]. Likewise, deliberate speculation on questionable, unintended, or wicked use of smart home devices can empower users and inform future design. For example, speculative sensors help people understand the benefits and risks of sensors in the home [ 16 ]. Kurze et al. [ 46 ] used printouts of anonymized smart home sensor readings for participants to speculate on the domestic activity behind them. Also, speculating on malicious repurposing can help to anticipate and subsequently counter threats from smart home devices for domestic abuse [ 50 , 74 ].

A methodological commonality of these inquiries is that they directly involve people into designing smart home design scenarios. This builds on values from participatory design [ 65 , 72 ], namely that involving potential users in the design of future things can lead to more humane designs. Indeed, many design researchers follow ethical intentions, striving to differentiate themselves from the mainstream smart home market and defining the “good” smart home in contrast to the commercialized vision of the smart home. As such, co-design of and co-speculation on smart home design can productively negotiate between conflicting values [ 77 ].

However, we argue that heterogenous and situated values cannot be assumed to simply negate homogeneous and global values. Rather, we hypothesize that investigating how the latter emerge despite actively being “worked against” following participatory design research principles can provide design researchers with a more nuanced understanding of designers’ and participants’ values.

2.3 Smart Home IoT Toolkits

Next, we review design toolkits and methods aimed at involving lay-people as participants in order to actively design and pro-actively reflect on conflicting values. Supportive tools and methods have a long tradition in design, e.g. PlexCards [ 51 ]. In particular, for the Internet of Things, a variety of analog/card-based [ 3 , 18 , 22 , 57 ], digital/electronic toolkits and methods [ 1 , 4 , 39 , 48 ] and hybrid [ 8 , 49 , 56 ] exist to involve participants in designing smart home scenarios. They offer possibilities to explore and understand sensors and actuators to make and try out various design scenarios. Some of these toolkits and methods enable people to design scenarios directly within their actual domestic lifeworlds, allowing them to put ideas into practice and to experience the personal and social impacts of doing so. While supporting different stages of the design process, these toolkits and methods mainly focus on supporting people in understanding what various sensors and actuators actually do. Only some of these methods enable people to reflect on the implications of the created design scenarios [ 1 , 6 ]. While some of the analogue/card-based methods support reflecting ethical, legal or social aspects and implications of the envisioned design scenarios [ 22 , 57 ], none of the functional toolkits [ 6 , 39 , 48 , 49 ] do. However, a suitable methodical embedding of the toolkits, e.g. through group discussion allows for reflection of such usages [ 1 , 19 , 46 ].

Going beyond what IoT design tools and methods can offer, more generic methods exist for considering ethical aspects in design. They help to address ethical aspects (1) on different levels: from ethical values to best practices; (2) in different ways: as guidelines for reflection or prompts for asking value minded questions; (3) with different purposes: raising awareness of potential implications and support for avoiding ethically questionable decisions. The Design Mindset Cards [ 41 ], Tarot Cards of Tech [ 2 ] and Moral Agent (part of Ethics for Designers Toolkit [ 34 ]) are examples in broader perspectives, while the Privacy Ideation Cards [ 52 ] serve as inspiration to think about the complexities of designing for privacy with data. While these generic methods offer more opportunities for reflection on values, they are disjunct from actual implementations and exploration of material capabilities such as sensing or actuating data in situ.

Most IoT design tools and methods focus on exploring functionality and interactivity of concrete sensors and actuators in the home. Links between the two remain weak and no toolkit or method exists to productively engage with conflicting values in a “good home” and how to design with it. The dominant conception of design scenarios for smart homes, assumes homogeneous and presumed global values. It has been challenged with prior work, exploring heterogenous and situated values. However, prior work has paid little attention to how functional toolkits and methods may also afford values that researchers would deem contradictory and what that means for design research and for researcher responsibility in co-design.

Next, we develop a working definition of “questionable values” in order to subsequently revisit and analyze four smart home design scenarios that were designed and used by people in their homes through using a smart home IoT toolkit we designed.

3 ACCIDENTALLY EVIL: QUESTIONABLE VALUES

Through our positionality as design researchers in the smart home design space, we echo arguments that involving people into designing and reflecting on future smart home is needed and that focusing on heterogeneous values can be productive. However, a strong commitment to such values can lead researchers to strive for safeguarding participants against design scenarios that violate such values. By highlighting design scenarios that are in line with alternative or “good” values, design researchers in the past may have made ethical decisions for and on behalf of participants. A pivot towards questionable values seems overdue.

“Questionable concepts” have been used in HCI design research to inspire participants to articulate opposing standpoints, goals, or values. In the context of banking for older adults, Vines et al. created questionable concepts to provoke a “spirit of critique” for participants to produce harsh critique to scenarios that were not “in line with their values” [ 78 ] . While being useful for enabling conversations about diverging values, the original approach is limited, as the concepts were both hypothetical and also intentionally designed to be questionable by the designers.

Taking “questionable concepts” as a point of departure, we propose “questionable values” as an analytic lens, enabling focus on the complex interplay of values, assumptions, and goals in co-design. In this way it acts as dialogue builder between all stakeholders and their respective value stances towards a design scenario.

The concept of “questionable values” differs from approaches such as critical and speculative design. In critical and speculative design, designers intentionally create scenarios that violate the social values they have commitments to, as a form of “thought experiment” to understand what types of things or scenarios people may find undesirable. Usually, researchers and co-designers broadly have a shared understanding of which values are important to protect and promote before engaging in design activities.

“Questionable values,” in contrast, is an analytical lens that rises from a disagreement in perspectives between researchers, co-designers, and other involved stakeholders. Questionable values highlight emergent situations where co-designers create design concepts that they deem as desirable, but violate the social values that researchers, or other stakeholders have commitments to. For example, a co-designer may desire a highly invasive surveillance system, which if implemented, would violate a researcher's commitment to not contribute to designing new harmful surveillance technologies, or the social values of the co-designers’ housemate. Questionable values raise questions about how researchers should shape activities with co-designers (e.g., should we constrain design activities for questionable values to not emerge?), as well as how researchers should navigate interactions with co-designers when questionable values emerge.

Next, we propose how to operationalize “questionable” for the IoT design space. Defining “good” values in IoT design are an area of great concern for IoT design and research. A recent analysis of 28 IoT manifestos [ 30 ] carved out how the HCI & IoT design research community voiced, in unison, a number of common concerns about values in future smart home design. With sensors disappearing from sight, and ethical issues appearing accordingly they argue for supporting people in understanding how sensing technology works, what it senses, how and where data is processed, and to what end data is used and by whom. The manifestos ultimately urge respect for the human experience [ 30 ] by collectively calling for trustworthy, transparent, open, and responsible [ 10 , 30 ] IoT, that:

  • does not manipulate people’ behavior
  • does not engage in invisible and ubiquitous surveillance
  • makes it clear how sensors are used and data is processed
  • observes good and responsible data practices
  • distributes power over as many people as possible
  • makes it easily understandable for lay-people

When the values of trustworthiness, transparency, openness, and responsibility, and their subsequent obligations are violated, we use the descriptor questionable as a conceptual shorthand. It describes when taken the values that researchers view as “good” (in the case of this paper's authors, the values listed above) are irritated, infringed, or violated through the use of a smart home device, service, or toolkit. We acknowledge that other groups of researchers may find a different set of values to be “good”, but the concept of “questionable values” would still apply when participants’ values do not align with those of the researchers.

We employ the term “questionable” to open inquiry into values in design scenarios as it begs reflective clarification on such issues as “for whom is x questionable” or “by what means does x appear questionable”. It thereby unfolds the values between at least two parties, e.g. between the dominant smart home user and their fellow dwellers or between design researchers and their participants. A questionable scenario can be perceived as useful but unfolds two conflicting sides of one or more values. In doing so, it enables a nuanced understanding of the multiple layers of interests, conflicts, and contradictions at play between the involved stakeholders.

We define “questionable values” to describe smart home design scenarios that irritate principles of trustworthiness, transparency, openness and/or responsibility and as a result surface problematic wants, needs, and values.

4 MOTIVATION & METHODOLOGICAL APPROACH

With questionable values as an analytical lens, we unfold the values built into four smart home design scenarios that were designed and used by people in their homes through using a smart home IoT toolkit we designed. In what follows, we will detail the design rationale of this toolkit as well as the ethical and methodological considerations for our analysis at hand.

4.1 The Smart Home IoT Toolkit

In the context of a five year design research project at the intersection of participatory design and smart home design, Berger and colleagues aimed to empower people to create situated and idiosyncratic ideas for the smart home [ 6 , 8 ], while also supporting data literacy and fluency in participants [ 46 ]. As a methodological and technological grounding we built a generic smart home IoT toolkit that gives participants complete control over sensor placement, data flow and interpretation.

The Sensing Home Kit is a smart home IoT toolkit with all necessary components to quickly setup the collection of sensor data and to let its users view the collected data in real time (Figure 1 ). The Sensing Home Kit uses a number of TI SensorTags [ 75 ] for collecting simple environmental sensor data in the home. The SensorTags offer battery operation, wireless communication (BLE) and a large number of different simple sensors (luxmeter, thermometer, hygrometer, barometer, accelerometer, gyrometer and magnetometer). The toolkit includes up to eight SensorTags in optimized 3D-printed housings that make them easily distinguishable (color) and attachable on furniture or objects in the home. In contrast to most commercial Smart Home products, the Sensing Home Kit allows for complete control over sensor placement, data flow and interpretation. In designing it, we drew inspiration from intersecting cultural probes and toolkits in co-design [ 66 ]. The Sensing Home Kit includes a preconfigured tablet computer to give its users full access to their own live and historic sensor data. It undertakes very little pre-processing, presenting close to ‘raw’ data, to prevent interpretation bias. It uses simple time series line graphs for visualizing data for temperature, light level, humidity, barometric pressure and movement (accelerometer values). The open and ready to use aspect from deployment and data collection up to data visualization is very important for this type of toolkit. While it reduces aspects of usability problems in installation and configuration it allows focusing on aspects of sensor exploration, data usages, data interpretation and sensemaking, e.g. to investigate the social situatedness of IoT data in the home

4.2 Study Design, Ethical Considerations, Data Collection and Analysis

The smart home IoT toolkit was used in various deployment studies with participants in different homes. Some of these have been reported elsewhere [ 7 , 8 , 19 , 46 , 63 , 76 ]. The commonality of all these deployments is that they all have been based on the same technical platform, with the same sensors, and the same capabilities for processing the data. Our research approach for all deployments was explorative with a participatory mindset and a focus on domestic applications. During all studies participants had control over sensor placement and access to a dashboard with past and live sensor data. Participants were free to use these sensors and their readings for design scenarios, they found interesting. They were neither prompted nor encouraged to design questionable design scenarios. The deployments differ in their research question, hardware enclosure of the toolkit, participant demographics, prompt for participant engagement, length of deployment and subsequent interviews.

For our subsequent analysis we focus on three previously published deployments of the Sensing Home Kit: “Guess the Data” [ 46 ], “Sensorstation” [ 19 ], and “Strange Sensors” [ 76 ]. All three deployments were aimed beyond functional exploration and fostered reflections about it. We did not include other deployments (such as pre-studies, teaching projects, hackathons) as their foci differed and the collected data did not allow an analysis of embedded values. We selected a diversity of deployments to underline the fact that questionable design scenarios occur in a wide range of deployments. With all deployments we included diverse groups of participants in various domestic settings. Participants were recruited from two middle sized German cities, through word of mouth, social media, or leaflets in supermarkets.

Ethics committee approvals were not required at our university when our studies were conducted. To counterbalance, we reflected in an interdisciplinary team of social scientists, designers, and computer scientists on the effects of our deployments, ran a series of trial deployments with researchers from our department and discussed our approach at two conference workshops. Before deploying the smart home IoT toolkit, we informed participants how data would be anonymized and provided a summary of relevant information in a written informed consent form. For all deployments, one or two researchers explained how to use the smart home IoT toolkit in participants’ homes, offered continued on-site and online support with any issues and revisited participants as needed.

All scenarios were created and used by participants. We did not encourage participants to experiment around possible questionable values. We explicitly asked participants to use our smart home IoT toolkit only with informed consent of others being subject to its sensing capabilities. After all deployments the authors of the corresponding publications conducted debriefing interviews or group discussions that were audio recorded and transcribed for further analysis. Subsequent data analysis followed a grounded theory approach with the respective authors involved in this analysis.

It only became clear in the subsequent analysis of these deployments, that participants understood that the sensor data could be used for a variety of questionable smart home scenarios. This insight became the basis for the paper at hand.

4.3 Rationale for Selection of Scenarios

The extensive range of scenarios stemming from the three selected deployments is exemplary for the everyday creativity of our participants in understanding and making use of the smart home IoT toolkit. Many scenarios aim at increasing efficiency in the home. People monitored the temperature in their homes to find optimal thermostat settings to reduce heating costs. They also used sensors to find leaky windows, monitored the humidity in their homes to prevent damping, used movement sensors to be reminded of open windows when leaving their home, or used the light sensors to not overstress their eyesight when reading in the evening.

Taking the breadth of available scenarios from the field data of these three deployments and the lens of “questionable values” as a starting point, the authors of this paper collaboratively revisited our original transcripts and initial reports from these deployments and selected four pertinent design scenarios that do not align with “good” values. To do so, we revisited the transcripts from the studies, re-analyzed the data and found 17 design scenarios where participants used the sensors and their data in a questionable way are described in Appendix 1. Out of these we selected four pertinent examples where participants used the smart home IoT toolkit to surveil, nudge and control others. We retell these scenarios in the next section. To that end, the argument brought forth in this paper is a value-based commentary, based on prior empirical work.

5.1 Deployment Guess The Data

Guess the Data [ 46 , 63 ] used the Sensing Home Kit embedded in a data driven participatory research method involving participants that were living in different households and social constellations. The method helps to understand how people not only individually make sense of simple sensor data from their homes but also how they collectively contextualize simple sensor data from other homes. The first part of the field study was a data collection in the homes for 10 to 14 days. We focused primarily on predetermined positions of the sensors that typically show good traces of everyday activities (Figure 2 ). During the data collection, participants had full access to the data visualizations and were encouraged to perform “data work” [ 27 ] by browsing, selecting, and annotating interesting graphs on a daily routine. We neither set a specific task focus for participants nor primed them in a specific way. In the second part of the study we used anonymized large format prints of data graphs from the homes as stimuli for group discussions with the participants from multiple households. These group discussions typically provided findings on how people collectively make sense of sensor data, revealed a lot of uses of the sensors not reported before, and often led to self-driven reflections on the implications of simple sensors that the participants were not aware of before.

5.1.1 Design Scenario #1 “Yes, but you were not in the garden”. One participant used the data visualization of a sensor mounted to an apartment door to surveil and shame their partner:

So that was funny, because he was away and said: “Yes, I will go straight to the garden when I get home”. I don't know where I was, but four hours later I came back and he said “I've been in the garden all the time”. And I laughed and said “That can't be true, because the apartment door only opened at 17:30.” And he said “Really?” I said: “What did you do?” “I think, I lay on the sofa for an hour and slept.” I said: “Yes, but you were not in the garden.” And he said: “Did you monitor me?” “Yes.” It was funny. I didn't explain to him how it worked. [ 46 ]

In this use case it became clear that the only goal was to surveil the partner, to check whether her spouse was telling the truth about leaving the home and doing garden work, as proof that the partner was lying and finally to shame him into revealing his lie. Here we can identify the embedded (questionable) values in the direction of ‘objectivity’ of the sensor data, and the ordering of behavior that such quantified objectivity promotes. The surplus of information replaces the need to trust the participant's spouse by controlling whether it is true or not based on the objective data.

The usage of data in the reported way clearly upends sought-after values of privacy, participation and self-conception. In terms of privacy : The sensor system allowed insights for the wife otherwise not possible if not at home at the times in question. In terms of participation : The sensor system was used asymmetrically. Explanation of the technology and access to it were actively denied. In terms of self-conception: Once installed, demonstrated and acknowledged by the participant to work as means of surveillance, the technology will influence the conditions for the surveilled partner, to either behave as expected or expose him to the risk of being shamed for lying.

5.1.2 Design Scenario #2 “Light in the corridor, again”. One participant corrected their partner's careless behavior regarding the light in the corridor which he often would forget to turn off. She confronted him with the light sensor data visualization which indicated when the light was switched on by him and also, that it was on for hours while he was in another room. The participant reported that their partner “was a little bit shocked” and that he promised to change his behavior. Further, the participant reported their partner was ‘shocked’ not because of the fact that he felt surveilled but due to the fact that he was confronted with the amount of energy he wasted. However, it also became clear in the report that it was a behavior already problematized before. The sensor data was welcome in the context of the social dynamic to be used not only to problematize unwanted/wasteful behavior but even as evidence to prove such behavior to each other and, to quantify wasteful behavior, and subsequently shaming the wasteful participant. This quantification was not directly possible before using the sensors. The goals of this use case were reported as saving money and reducing waste of energy, embedding primarily positive and shared values such as frugality and environment/climate awareness and responsibility (resource fairness). The participants used the sensor data to educate and promote more compliant behavior in this sense. We consider this design scenario a borderline case, not yet completely questionable in value by outsets and intentions, but already affecting the value of privacy and therefore being ultimately questionable. It is easy to think this further: If deployed in another home, where the power relationship between partners was even more asymmetrical, this scenario would be even more questionable.

5.2 Deployment Sensorstation

Sensorstation is a research product [ 19 ] aimed at empowering people to design simple sensor applications within their domestic environment, enabling insights into how and why people use sensor technology and how this technology may affect communal living. Sensorstation extends the Sensing Home Kit with two tablet computers in a cork encasing as a central station for easy interaction with the data (Figure 3 ). The input interface guides a person through the process of configuring a sensor, setting a condition when to get notified and a message to be sent. These messages resulted in a steady live stream of sent messages visible to the participants at any time on the output display as well as a notification on participants mobile phones. Sensorstation was deployed in a shared apartment and placed in the kitchen, the residents’ common space, for 19 days while participants were able to freely explore sensors in their home. At the end of the study participants discussed their deployments and speculated on possible future smart home applications. While the sensors were used for entertainment and to create positive interactions between the residents, they also used them to develop questionable scenarios for nudging and behavior control.

5.2.1 Design Scenario #3 “The Organic Waste Bin Expects Your Compliance”. In the beginning of the Sensorstation study participants used the sensors primarily for creating positive connections between flatmates. They created smart home design scenarios that aimed at making each other happy. Later on, they also proposed design scenarios to monitor their own behaviors for reasons of self-optimization (e.g. ensuring optimal light conditions for reading, temperature and airing), they also developed and speculated about a variety of sensor uses to control others. These design scenarios were aimed at monitoring each other, rewarding desired behavior and punishing non-compliant behavior. Most of these scenarios were fictional, however, one was realized. This scenario was aimed mainly at compliant behavior in the shared spaces of the flat (kitchen, hallway, bathroom) and to avoid possible conflicts with other flatmates by proving that dwellers behave in a certain way. For this purpose, one participant attached a sensor to the organic waste bin flap to promote correct waste separation. (Note: Waste separation is a very important and heavily debated topic in Germany, as consumers are expected to sort their garbage themselves).

Every time the motion sensor registered the flap opening, a message was automatically sent to all other flatmates: “Attention: Only place compostable waste in the organic waste bin!” The dweller who installed the sensor explicitly mentioned that he wanted to instruct and nudge the other flatmates so that they behave the way he prefers them to. In the discussion, it became clear that there was consensus in the group that proper waste separation was a goal worth supporting and that sensor-based monitoring of others might be allowed under certain circumstances, such as being voluntary and being done in a transparent manner. Only one flatmate voiced concerns about surveillance in shared domestic spaces. This participant was very hesitant to report this, because they were the newest member of the residential community.

The design scenario affects privacy and autonomy by opting for efficiency. The aspect of automation as means of maximizing efficiency is noteworthy here. Social trust among the dwellers to behave on their own according to an agreed upon social norm was substituted by automatic and active reminders to nudge them.

5.3 Deployment Strange Sensors

Strange Sensors [ 76 ] is a Critical Design use of the Sensing Home Kit that is employed to challenge the mindsets of people using ubiquitous smart home systems. The study aimed at an individual level and shows how people are reacting to unusual messages from smart systems. The system was co-created over several workshops where different scenarios and possible outputs from the system were imagined. Strange Sensors was installed over one week in a single household to register domestic situations and produce notifications to the resident. One sensor was placed at the fridge door (Figure 4 ) and two in the living room on a cupboard and on a window. The collected data was automatically interpreted and given to the participants via messenger notifications about situations in their homes. This includes how often the fridge was opened or how long the light was on at night. Over time the notifications became stranger as they included comparisons with random units of measurement, were less comprehensive and sometimes included errors. This change in the notifications was intended to cause suspicion in the users and urge them to question the way in which smart home systems actually work and are interconnected.

5.3.1 Design Scenario #4 “We are on a diet and you opened the fridge yet again”. Two out of three participants in Strange Sensors changed their behaviors on account of the system notifications. As intended, they were irritated by the output of the sensors, trying to reconcile their own behavior with the feedback of Strange Sensors . The third participant revealed to us that the notifications seemed to them more like white noise after a few days. It was soon clear why there was a discrepancy in reactions: Rather than living alone as they previously stated, their partner stayed with them during parts of the study period. Although the participant was given a complete overview over the sensor network, they deliberately kept some of this information from their partner.

“Well, on the first day I didn't tell my boyfriend that this was being measured. Then I wrote to him, “You've opened the fridge again.” [laughs] Well... he didn't know that. He didn't know that the thing there senses ‘open and closed’, and ‘open and closed’. - Yeah, and he hasn't eaten anything from my fridge since he found out!” [ 76 ]

During the deployment of Strange Sensors, the participant asked for some additional details about the sensor at the fridge door to the ones they already received. Afterwards they revealed that they asked because they and their partner are dieting but that the participant suspects him to take some of their food without disclosing it. In another part of the interview, the participant reveals that their partner checks at least all his technical appliances for security risks. While they did not disclose more details about the reaction of their partner, they mentioned a conversation with another friend.

“then my other friend texted whether she could book my apartment for one night for 50€? And she wrote 'You've got your sensors everywhere, I won't eat anything from you!’ … I thought to myself, ‘All right!’” [ 76 ]

While the participant did not seem to have issues with monitoring the behavior of their partner and shaming him after his misconduct, the confrontation with their friend made them feel uncomfortable. In the interview itself it was not discernible if it was because the friend wanted to use their flat or because their friend thinks that the participant will also control them due to lack of trust.

6 ON SURFACING QUESTIONABLE VALUES

Our co-designers and participants have imagined and used questionable— sometimes accidentally evil—design scenarios. In this section, we show how these scenarios were premediated by the material of the smart home IoT toolkit, normalized by dominant narratives of efficiency and productivity and afforded by our own assumptions and fallacies stemming from assumptions on materiality, narratives and design practice. Next, we argue that design research needs to acknowledge and engage with, rather than downplay, questionable values. This is crucial because it helps to foresee potential misuse and to reduce the potential for ill-intent of technology design at large.

6.1 … Afforded by our own Assumptions and Fallacies

We acknowledge that parts of the reported design scenarios may stem from our participants' curiosity with what the sensors can do and subsequently falling victim to zemblanity , i.e. to accidentally discover what they'd rather not want to know. The fully functional toolkit enabled such scenarios by design, the scenarios were actively used, and actively communicated back to us researchers. In that, we see a slippery slope from accidentally discovering a functionality to intentionally abusing it. In the #4 “We Are On A Diet And You Opened The Fridge Yet Again” example, we see participants crossing a line from exploring a functionality to exploiting it. The participant knew that their partner was unaware of what the sensor at the fridge is sensing and used this to their advantage, resulting in a text message accusing their partner for infringing on their mutual diet plan.

In Vines et al. [ 78 ] questionable concepts were defined beforehand by designers to spark dissent and to inspire discussion among designers and participants. In our scenarios, the cases became questionable, because the underlying values were manifested in implementing functional design scenarios for specific goals such as surveilling home dwellers towards goals of control over others, and nudging for behavior change. These goals collide with our assumptions of what a ‘good’ smart home is. We built the toolkit based on open and transparent values, to account for the power imbalances between researchers and participants, but we did not sufficiently account for the power imbalances down the line. In that sense, the toolkit was open to any design scenario, which supports imagination and exploration—but is thereby also open to less “noble” goals. Articulated through the openness of the toolkit, we expected trust, but simultaneously empowered questionable values. In lieu of checks and balances, the toolkit became a threat for the privacy of our participants’ fellow occupants. Supporting open and transparent values in creating the toolkit and having participants attend an ethics briefing does not just automatically rub off on the actual design cases.

It is even more apparent in #1 “Yes, but you were not in the garden” that the participants used the toolkit asymmetrically and with both intent and awareness to monitor their partner. Even if the design scenario was discovered by accident, data was eventually used in questionable ways. Data literacy of the participants, their ability to interpret and to use the data improved by freely exploring the toolkit unbeknownst to their partner, who had neither data access nor interpretation ability and against whom that knowledge was used. It becomes clear that the only reason to look for certain data patterns, in this case door movements, was to check the plausibility of their partner's story and to prove they were lying. The freshly procured smart home data literacy led to a significant power imbalance within the family that was immediately abused with intent.

Openness of the toolkit led to covert data collection and operation. This in turn enables asymmetrical access to the data, unfolding a power imbalance. What responsibilities do the open toolkit and the designers bear? We find these small scale surveillance scenarios to be worrisome. Do they stem from people being desensitized to such scenarios by the dominant narrative in public media?

6.2 … Normalized by Technosolutionist Narratives

#2 “Light In The Corridor, Again” and #3 “The Organic Waste Bin Expects Your Compliance” examples begin with the intent to be environmentally friendly, by using sensor data to save energy or to increase the recycling rate of compostables respectively. However, both examples clearly show the potential of misusing that sensor data to imagine scenarios that are both technologically unfeasible but normalized by technosolutionist narratives.

Our goal was to engage people in exploring IoT technology and to improve their data literacy. We also hypothesized our participants would propose creative, idiosyncratic design scenarios showcasing heterogeneous and alternative values. Indeed, participants developed very creative applications, illustrating how fast and profound their data literacy improved. However, these scenarios aligned well with predominant workplace values of efficiency, optimization, and control over others. Participants picked up some of the design scenarios commercial providers are criticized for in the media everyday and situated them in their homes. In doing so, questionable scenarios surface some of the tensions in how society picks up smart IoT technology.

Using smart home sensor data to enforce behavior change represents a power imbalance between those who collect sensor data and exercise the exclusive right for interpretation and those who are objectified by these practices. Those who collect and interpret data, may then substitute trust for control at will and they may enforce behavior change by using sensor data as evidence. This is an imminent threat for privacy and subsequent negative side effects, such as upsetting others or disturbing trust in a shared home. There is also a danger that smart home users gloss over such negative externalities, because they feel that they pursue a noble goal, such as saving energy. Being exposed to surveillance capitalism [ 83 ] through everyday use of the internet and smartphone, accustomed users to its practices, that they further perpetuate onto other users with less data power.

More importantly, participants used seemingly objective data to define a truth (e.g., #4 “We Are On A Diet And You Opened The Fridge Yet Again” ) that is immediately enforced (e.g., registering and disclosing fridge openings). An interpretation of data of temperature, light, or movement sensors is not necessarily a true interpretation of the world. Likewise, in #3 “The Organic Waste Bin Expects Your Compliance” , the ”lid lifted” reading from a movement sensor, was designated to “somebody is throwing something away”, and subsequently used to improve the recycling rate of compostables. This scenario follows values of efficiency and compliance, while at the same time being technologically unfeasible . The used movement sensor has no ability to recognize whether trash is biodegradable or not. While being both unjustified and technologically unfeasible, this design scenario exemplifies how smart home data and its ”objective” interpretation are normalized to technosolutionist narratives of enforcing complaint behavior that are sneaking into the smart home. These designs tangibly surface problematic cultural discourses around surveillance and behavior economics. At first glance, nudging does not seem as questionable as the surveillance scenario of the “garden” example. Yet, it is a well-known dark pattern and shows how “persuasion design” normalizes disproportionate use of technology to grasp human attention in exchange for very little benefit. While commercial home IoT applications on the market may embody and promote these values, we view them as questionable due to their detrimental effects on transparency, openness, and responsibility.

More sophisticated sensor data might be able to correctly classify compostables and issue helpful recommendations. Until then, a loose relation of movement data is seemingly enough to nag their fellow occupants ”to do the right thing” regardless of their intentions. Using such fuzzy data unrightfully objectifies people and tasks but also normalizes technological control through unfeasible means. Our participants nudged their fellow occupants because they themselves are conditioned by smart phone notifications to optimize their lives. Additionally, discourses around smart technology are overpromising (cf. artificial intelligence) while pretending a miraculous future has already arrived. Taken together, this prevalence in public discourse and daily experiences further normalizes questionable values.

6.3 … Premediated by Smart IoT Material

The ways in which sensor data for movement, temperature, or humidity objectify their environments and human behavior afford an endless variety of congruent scenarios in design. The materiality of functional smart home IoT toolkits such as ours pre-structures ideation, imbuing it with the dominant narratives of efficiency outlined above. The cheap and ubiquitous material available for IoT design originates from the same techno-capitalist logic, from material resources to stockroom logistics to online retailers, that drive the promise of an efficient smart home. In theory, all sensors and actuators could be creatively connected and reconfigured to enable just about everything in the smart home. However, this is more difficult in practice: Sensors gather data from their environment and translate them to numbers that can be mathematically calculated against each other. In the case of smart home design, numeric values premediate humane value judgments. In the case of the #2 “Light in the corridor, again” example, the materiality of numerical sensor calculations is read as an open door and subsequently interpreted as a partner who wastes energy.

Put differently: sensors, like any technology, operate against specific interpretive backgrounds (e.g. mathematics and techno-capitalist efficiency) while their materiality premediates these backgrounds to begin with. Sensors materially embody a capacity for objectifying the world from minute events to the behaviors of individuals. Being digital technologies, these capacities predominantly manifest as highly selective readings of particular sensings (e.g., a door is opened or not, an organic waste bin is used or not). Such readings indicate that there are certain pre-selected “thingly” interpretations of the world embedded in sensors [ 40 ]. On the one hand, sensors can only objectify (i.e., make legible) their surroundings based on their material make-up. But on the other hand, home dwellers can only make sense of such objectifications in light of a specific literacy : the legibility of an open door is read-as an errant spouse, the legibility of an organic waste bin is read-as a wasteful flatmate.

In light of this interpretation, we may have overestimated our participants’ creative capability within their situated domestic practices. Simultaneously, we may have underestimated the material makeup of sensors and computing in smart home materiality and its affordance.

With techno-capitalist logic, there are incentives for inventing the ”next big thing” and thus an inflated interest in “creativity” and marketable “innovation”. As such, there are certainly incentives for reporting highly creative design scenarios, such as inflatable cats [ 8 ]. But the positive design scenarios that design strives for may conceal that the same materiality of available sensors breeds surveillance scenarios. Additionally, with an interest in situated and heterogeneous values, design researchers may tend to report favorable design scenarios over questionable ones. Regardless, whether the latter are downplayed to safeguard participants or for being dull and uninspired, they exist nevertheless. And they exist because of their materiality, and not despite of it. When looking at the repeating scheme of questionable values and dominant narratives of efficiency, it remains a crucial question: How does IoT material afford these types of ideas? Materiality in smart home design is usually not questioned. It is available, considered as “raw” and capable of enabling highly creative smart home design scenarios. However, given both our problematization as well as utilization of questionable scenarios, we argue that design researchers should pay more attention to the complicity of IoT material in how the latter come about.

7 EMBRACING QUESTIONABLENESS: OPPORTUNITIES FOR DESIGN RESEARCH

We have analyzed four design scenarios to reflect on the dominant narratives of efficiency and productivity against which they were produced, how the IoT toolkit materiality affords questionable values, and how our assumptions as designers mediate these values in design. In what follows, we will argue that such design scenarios have most likely occurred in other design settings.

Next, we propose how design research can learn from these intuitively unwelcomed propositions and offer actionable insights and opportunities for design to productively embrace them.

7.1 Questionable Values in Design Research and Practice

Designing together with future users has the potential to decrease power imbalances between designers and those we strive to design for. Likewise, it has the potential to enable participants in exploring and creating future design scenarios and by doing so, it gives designers a glimpse into the situated domestic practices of their participants. It is certainly a good thing for designers to make particular values, such as openness, actionable in design practice. Still, design research needs to be aware that design scenarios happen through smart IoT material and against a background of workplace values. We see the occurrence of questionable scenarios as an opportunity, rather than an obstacle, for unfolding the breadth of values in smart home design.

In our own practice we witness design research on smart homes concealing questionable values emerging in the design process. Design scenarios conflicting with humane values seem to be less often reported on in favor of positive design narratives or innovative solutions with a quirky market potential. Questionable outcomes of collaborative design may be seen both as exposing participants and as an admission of failure in design practice. Generally, they may raise critical questions. We argue that in order to be fully value-minded design researchers we need to acknowledge that participants may propose questionable values and that our design practice affords these types of design scenarios in the first place. At the same time, researchers do not have to report on these questionable values in a “neutral” way, but instead can critically reflect on the types of power imbalances that these questionable uses reflect. Shedding light on power imbalances in the (smart) home through questionable values and use can help inspire us to more intentionally design smart homes differently.

Opportunity #1: We ask design researchers to create space for questionable values to arise in participatory research. There is value in paying attention and to address problematic use: When creating co-design activities, designers may use questionable values as a lens to foresee questionable use and to create participatory activities that address questionable use.

Opportunity #2: When organizing design workshops with participants, designers may designate activities that trigger participants to create end reflect questionable scenarios. In so doing, participants can openly explore questionable scenarios, collaboratively understand their ramifications, and critically reflect on the values adhered (or sacrificed).

Since the inception of cultural probes [ 31 ], ambiguity, messiness, inefficiency, and even disorder [ 33 , 35 ] are crucial elements of design-based inquiry. Shifting control over the design process from designers to participants has the potential for rich insights to emerge that were not intended by the designers [ 35 ]. This, however, poses a problem: Even in its most innocent form, design researchers can hardly control how participants utilize the material provided, e.g. the camera from a cultural probe. This is even more troublesome with toolkits containing ambient sensors for open exploration. In our experience, these possibilities may not be designed out of our toolkits, because questionable design scenarios already lurk within IoT materiality and the wider discourse around the smart home. Questionable scenarios are part and parcel of toolkits and methods researchers utilize.

Opportunity #3: When analyzing data from smart home design deployments, designers should not downplay questionable values, but rather report on these scenarios and seek for reflection with participants. There is further potential for more reflective design practice. For example, taking our study as a case exemplar, designers may revisit past deployments and report on less favorable design scenarios that have been created, but not reported.

Opportunity #4: We ask designers to more closely collaborate with researchers from law, philosophy, and ethics, as to seek guidance in navigating accidental and sometimes impossible to foresee encounters with thorny values and scenarios arising from participatory design practice with open and exploratory technology. Creating periodic touchpoints throughout the design process with scholars from law, philosophy, and ethics, allows for a deeper ethical reflection in design practice.

7.2 Questionable Values in Materiality

In discussing “designer fallacy,” Ihde cautions designers against presuming that their intentions will be mirrored by end users, as technologies always prove “multistable”—i.e., the way they make the world legible is always context-relative [ 40 ]. However, in the case of sensors, aspects of this multistability are always-already withdrawn—both researchers and participants, are always late to the scene, as output categories for sensings pre-select how we experience them. This suggests that, if we seek to introduce a questionable dimension into sensors themselves to more deeply probe the relationship of materiality, values and design scenarios, we ought to spend more time reflecting as a community on how this may be done practically in developing smart sensor materiality.

Opportunity #5: There is an opportunity to re-design the materiality of sensors themselves—how they measure, observe, and quantify the world. Taking the original questionable concepts as a starting point, there is an opportunity to design functional sensing devices that question dominant logics of surveillance and control. Boucher et al's VisionCam captures surveillance footage, but protects privacy by retaining only contour animations [13,14]. We ask design research to also look the other way, and create probe tools, that actively probe into logics of surveillance, nudging, and control over others, making questionable use apparent and inevitable.

Crafting non-functional, speculative technology [ 23 ], or involving sensor-trigger feedback and machine learning technologies, offers further, more speculative opportunities. Wakkary et al.’s Morse Things [ 79 ], for instance, rely on reinforcement learning to infer time slots for communication between individual research products, doing so without explaining their decisions and communications to the participants they have been deployed with. The embedded probabilistic readings thereby make the home and goings-on within it questionable, prompting participants to question what they expect of technologies in the home. This would leverage computational sensing itself to question efficiency-oriented objectification: Creating speculative sensors that indulge in a “thingly uncertainty” [ 5 ] would unfold a productive continuum of probabilistic objectification.

Opportunity #6: This could translate into an opportunity, where sensors would produce data that is fuzzy or that makes certain activities impossible to measure or sensors that would invert or subvert objectification. Other speculative sensors may only measure once per day, or sensors could be split into several discrete sensors, so that one can only measure activity, while the other can only measure stillness.

Opportunity #7: Building on intrinsic fuzziness or modes of counter functionality [ 62 ], sensors could invite participants to actively probe the mode of their operation. A prototypical example for this would be a thermometer where data is not visualized as a continuum, but in discrete temperature ranges. Or, a movement sensor that, in order to function, has to be labeled by participants and their housemates in discrete steps, like hectic and quiet.

Building material restrictions, sensitivities or exaggerations directly into sensors, has the potential to spark creativity in co-speculation in the smart home context, to question objectification and to question homogenous workplace-inspired values. Such speculative sensors would make sophisticated socio-technical rules more actionable and they could exaggerate interpretation, making data analytics, such as machine learning, actionable for collaborative design.

7.3 Questionable Values in Discourse

We have interpreted the questionable values of our participants' design scenarios, to stem from technosolutionist narratives that permeate everyday life. It is trivial to state that the cultural backdrop against which participants construct questionable scenarios, e.g. Western techno-capitalism taking over the home, cannot be overcome solely by (re)designing smart home IoT toolkits. Differently put, the heterogeneous and alternative values promoted by design researchers cannot “undo” the values that they set themselves up against by contrast. We suggest that creating change at the level of discourse and narrative may help in efforts to promote moral values.

Embracing questionable values on a discourse level, has the potential to mitigate risk in explicitly designing discussion spaces for evil design scenarios. Following the notion of a speculative harm analysis [ 73 ], design research should explore IoT toolkits that actively foster questionable behavior to foster critical thinking in smart home design with future users. Activities like Fiesler's Black Mirror writers’ room provide starting points for this type of interaction with participants [ 44 ]. All design scenarios presented in our reflective study share the fact that data literacy and fluency of our participants improved quickly and led to remarkable outcomes. Debriefings with participants showed that they were aware of the questionable nature of some of their design scenarios.

Opportunity #8: Leveraging the everyday creativity of participants, there is an opportunity for sensor toolkit deployments, where people can use simple sensors in imaginative ways to speculate on potential harm of interpreting sensor readings in objectifiable ways. For example, a functional IoT toolkit could present guided and provocative tasks for participants to i) create questionable scenarios; ii) reflect on them in action; iii) debrief and to discuss acceptance or rejection of such a scenario in the context of their home.

Future users have concepts of surveillance, nudging, and control over others at their creative disposal, and we argue for design research to raise awareness during and after questionable issues surface. Functional toolkits would greatly benefit from explicit and immanent reflection on expected benefits and possible harms. In the Guess the Data deployment [ 46 ] there was a reflective group discussion, and likewise Ambe et al. [ 1 ] embedded co-design practice into a value-minded setting, where design researchers and participants discussed the values embedded in sensor usage design scenarios while co-creating them together and in situ at the kitchen table. This could mean to raise awareness for the values future users embrace, when co-creating against the background of their own domestic experiences. Foregrounding ethical decisions in the design process could mean to combine smart home IoT toolkits with ethical reflection tools like Moral Agent [ 34 ].

Opportunity #9: Functional IoT toolkits could include tutorials, motivating participants to explore design scenarios through the lens of a certain value. One example could be to ask participants to use sensors to save energy in a privacy friendly way and in a way that would be more intrusive, in order to reflect on both. There is an opportunity to include value discourses directly into toolkits, encouraging participants to consider checks and balances in their own smart home. Taking inspiration from the candle devices [ 67 ], future users may design their own smart home in a value sensible way, e.g. preventing raw data access, making data accessible only in a 4-eyes principle, or to include fake data or to mute data.

Lastly, questionable scenarios are useful by themselves as they allow stakeholders to explore and highlight the inevitable, and often banal, nuances of making use of sensor data. There is an opportunity for design research to engage in public discourse around what sensors can do and what people think they do. “Sensing is believing” [ 55 ] and thus, we ultimately argue for design research to take a step forward and advocate in public discourses for smart home sensor technology that ultimately respects the humane condition.

8 CONCLUSION

Why do good people make bad things? The dynamic of well-intentioned designers and engineers who build things that are supposed to make life better but instead, to their horror, make life worse, is an ongoing theme throughout the history of HCI. From “The Facebook” connecting the world's people to Cambridge Analytica manipulating the worlds’ democracies; to IoT for public restrooms that supports replenishing hygiene products to using it for the surveillance of low-wage workers [ 29 ] , “good” technologies seem to keep ending up “accidentally evil.” How can we break this cycle? This paper sheds a small but pointed light at this phenomenon, and provides observations and opportunities as to how we might break it.

We offer “questionable values” as a conceptual shorthand to describe when taken for granted, “good” values for a smart home are violated. We argue that questionable values are 1) afforded by designer's assumptions and fallacies; 2) premediated by existing smart IoT material; 3) normalized by the predominance of techno-solutionist narratives, around dominant workplace values, embedding logics of power and dominance into design and engineering. Our proposal is this: We urge designers and engineers to make space for questionable values, to embrace and thus anticipate the spectrum along which heterogenous and homogenous values are contended. Thus empowering designers and engineers to better “look around corners,” anticipating tomorrow's concerns and forestalling the worst of their harms.
