Sound zone technology enables multiple people to have personal and shared listening experiences without disturbing each other. Methods for constructing sound zones have now matured enough to allow installations outside of experimental laboratories, making it essential for further development to conduct empirical studies about how people adopt, use, and interact with sound zones in, e.g. , domestic settings. To that end, we conducted a four-week field study with a sound zone system in five households. Through an inductive reflexive thematic analysis, we identify three themes relating to 1) experiencing sound zones in everyday life, 2) sound zone usage patterns in households, and 3) interacting with sound zones. Based on these themes, we discuss how sound zones can be used to manage sound in homes in new ways to allow for better social coexistence and listening experiences. We present four directions for future HCI research and interaction design to comply with user needs and considerations when using this novel technology.

1 INTRODUCTION

At home, people experience both audio and visual media in a plethora of ways. The shared domestic space can be filled with sounds from shared devices ( e.g. , smart speakers and televisions) as well as personal devices ( e.g. , smartphones and tablets), resulting in a sometimes chaotic environment [ 26 ]. To counteract, people have several techniques to curate their own sound environment by, e.g. , distancing themselves from others or wearing headphones [ 18 ]. However, these techniques lead to inhibited social interaction [ 19 , 35 ], and furthermore reduced awareness as well as a sense of presence and safety [ 36 ].

Through advanced signal processing techniques and new loudspeaker technology, people can now control wanted and unwanted sound within limited spatial regions also referred to as ‘sound zones’ [ 21 , 40 , 41 ]. Within the domestic context, sound zone systems provide users with new opportunities to reconfigure shared physical environments by restricting sound to specific areas of a room without causing disturbances and, most importantly, without impairing the ability to communicate with family members. As an example, in a living room, one person can sit at a dinner table listening to music, while a second person sits on the couch watching television. In a home with conventional sound systems, this can create a chaotic and noisy environment with overlapping sounds. Sound zones allow people to have personal listening experiences [ 4 , 13 ] without disturbing others physically close to them, while still maintaining the opportunity for social interaction. Additionally, sound zones have two unique, spatial characteristics that can be dynamically controlled, i.e. , position and width. The position of individual sound zones can be moved, and the width ( i.e. , the size of the area covered by the zone) can be increased or decreased. This opens up different listening configurations, where one sound zone can be set up for one person to listen, while a wide sound zone can cover an entire area so multiple people can listen to the same sound content.

Within HCI, one strand of research considers how people could interact with spatial sound, tackling the intangibility of sound, e.g. , by interacting with sound through mid-air gestures [ 38 ], creating music through a ball metaphor [ 11 ], or interacting with music on a tangible desktop interface [ 29 ]. Previous research on sound zone interaction has explored potential designs [ 27 ] and characterised challenges for interaction [ 26 ]. More recently, visualisations for sound zone technology in lab settings have been investigated ( e.g. [ 23 , 25 ]). However, sound zone technology has lacked maturity and currently only exists as proof-of-concept setups in acoustically optimised laboratories. Thus, while prior research on sound zone interaction provides insights into how people can interact with sound zone systems, there is still no empirical understanding of how people might experience this new way of listening to sound in their daily lives. This is the primary focus of the research presented in this paper. Taking sound zone systems out of the lab space necessitates several considerations to be taken into account. This includes ensuring that a sound zone system supports both shared and personal everyday media use, while simultaneously allowing users to explore new uses and situations that may emerge when using sound zone technology as their primary media system.

In this paper, we present a sound zone system for domestic use, which allows for two co-existing sound zones, with a set of interaction techniques for modifying the spatial properties of and content within the sound zones. Based on data from our field study in five different households over the course of four weeks, we present three themes describing how the participants experienced the sound zones, used them in everyday life and how they interacted with the technology. Our paper contributes to the growing literature on managing sound by discussing the potentials of sound zone technology in homes. Specifically, we show that sound zone technology can be instrumental in controlling sound exposure to both listeners and non-listeners based on the variety of needs and preferences in a household. Our findings indicate an improved domestic environment which allows for better social coexistence. Furthermore, we point to four directions for future HCI research and interaction design that can be explored.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Sound in Homes

Sound research within the HCI community has taken diverse forms. Early research included investigating the use of sound as interface feedback [ 7 , 17 ], but research has since expanded to broader topics, such as music production and mixing [ 11 , 38 , 39 ], and a range of domains, such as a chemical factory [ 1 ] and a university campus [ 51 ], where the aim is not to investigate sound as a supporting feature but rather as an experience itself.

In this paper, we report on a study within the domestic domain. As we cover in this subsection, previous research in this area is concerned with uncovering people's management of and interactions with sounds in their homes as well as particular sound interventions in the domestic sphere. Oleksik et al. conducted an investigation into the “domestic soundscape” in response to prior research mainly focusing on urban or office contexts [ 42 ]. They conducted “sound tours” in seven households and uncovered different ways sound is managed by and holds meaning to participants. Inspired by the findings, they conducted a design workshop to address challenges and opportunities for sound and HCI in homes. They propose that an untapped potential exists for this topic. With a narrower view, other research focuses particularly on music listening practices.

Leong and Wright studied the social aspects of listening to music in homes through interviews and diary entries from five households [ 33 ]. They found that interactions between people around music were free from place- and time-bound technologies. It is no longer necessary for people to be co-located to share music. Furthermore, they found that technologies inside the homes were continuously reconfigured to support different social experiences with, e.g. , the television functioning as a radio. Both Oleksik et al., as well as Leong and Wright, point out the complexities of investigating sound and music practices in homes, given the fluid definition of what constitutes a home, and where to draw its boundaries, physically and socially. Given this challenge, qualitative depth is emphasized as opposed to quantitative generalisability.

Similar to the focus of this paper, Lundgaard and Nielsen present a study of sound zone systems in homes. They conducted a design ethnography in seven households that investigated potential use situations for sound zone systems [ 26 ]. This research offers a point of reference for how people imagine sound zone systems to fit into their daily lives, but the way such systems are actually used is still not investigated.

2.2 Sound Zones

Sound zones are limited, physical areas within which the volume of a sound is maximised, while simultaneously minimising everywhere else [ 13 ]. This makes it possible to have areas within the same room where people listen to different sounds without using headphones and without disturbing each other [ 4 ]. Potential uses for this are varied and include car cabins [ 9 ], homes [ 26 ], and outdoor concerts [ 20 ]–in short, any situation where multiple people share a space but have different desires for what to listen to or different preferences for the sound in regards to, e.g. , volume levels.

The Illinois Institute of Technology initially hosted an early concept demonstration in 1967, but most progress has occurred within the past two decades. One example includes Microsoft's “Personal Audio Space” [ 37 ] that involves a speaker array directing a sound towards a specified user and lowering the volume for other bystanders at the same time. That example, however, only allows for one sound zone. For multiple sound zones within the same physical space, a common method is acoustic contrast control. Utilising this method, a distinction is made between “bright” zones and “dark” zones, where the bright zones are the sound zones and dark zones are areas where the volume is minimised [ 8 , 10 ]. Ramo et al. present guidelines for how well this contrast must be in order for people in one sound zone not to be disturbed by another sound zone [ 46 ].

With current construction methods for sound zones improving, it is now essential to investigate how such systems are adopted in real situations. This will guide both the technological development but also drive research from an HCI perspective, e.g. , considering how the interaction can be designed. Due to the intangible and invisible nature of sound, the concept can be difficult to grasp for novice users [ 25 , 34 ]. Early explorations of interaction design concepts exist [ 22 , 34 ], but evaluations have mainly concerned visualisation of sound zones in a lab-based environment [ 23 , 24 , 25 ].

Sound zone technology falls under the broader category of “personal listening technology” which entails people curating individual listening experiences [ 19 , 28 ]. The difference is that sound zones do not require users to wear headphones. Recently, Haas et al. presented a study on “Auditory Mediated Reality” which is a type of technologically mediated listening experience, enabled by hearables [ 18 ]. Hearables are headphones equipped with microphones that record sounds around the user. These sounds are played inside the headphones, allowing the user to both hear sounds from the surrounding environment and digital sounds, e.g. , from a music player. While these prior papers present studies of personal sound, there have yet to be studies on how sound zones alter the practice of listening to music and other media. Furthermore, while Lundgaard et al. [ 28 ] investigate social aspects of different personal listening technology to inform sound zone research, their study lacks real-world situations.

3 A DYNAMIC SOUND ZONE SYSTEM

In this section, we present a dynamic sound zone system for domestic use. The system comprises a soundbar speaker array, tracked remote controllers, visualisation devices to support interaction, and a complete sound streaming setup. Our main motivations behind the deployment of the system are to 1) learn how people experience media and use media devices in homes with different family constellations and 2) understand potentially novel usage situations sound zones may prompt, compared to conventional sound systems. With our system, users can have two sound zones simultaneously in the living room with sound content selected by themselves. They can alter the spatial properties of the sound zones, allowing them to modify both their personal and shared sound environment. The deployment prototype is designed in such a way as to provide a complete replacement of existing media devices within the living room of the study participants.

We provide each household with a complete media system consisting of a TV, a soundbar ( 3.2 ), streaming components ( 3.3 ), control devices for sound zone interaction ( 3.1.1 ), position tracking system ( 3.1.2 ), an ultra-wide display for sound zone visualization ( 3.1.3 ), and a laptop PC connecting the setup components. Everything is mounted on a stand and technical equipment is hidden inside a cabinet. Figure 1 shows the complete setup (A), an overview example of the spatial setup of the sound zone system with components in a livingroom (B), a close-up of the control device (C) and the system in one of the participating households, with potential zone positions in a couch area, single chair at the wall, and dining room table closest to the camera (D). Below we describe the system components in detail.

3.1 Sound Zone Interaction

In this section, we present how users can interact with our sound zone system. The interaction design concepts are designed with the goal of providing the users with control and visualisation of the spatial properties of sound zones ( i.e. volume, width and position). To that end, we designed a custom interface consisting of two tracked remote controllers, and a custom visualisation device to communicate the state and behaviour of active sound zones.

3.1.1 Controlling sound zones. Compared to conventional sound systems, dynamic sound zones allow users to design their sound environment in new ways. Similar to other systems, sound zones naturally have a volume, but also allow users to change the size and position of the sound zones. Users can control the sound zones by interacting with two BeoRemote Halo controllers (see figure 2 , left). We decided to use the Halo controllers for three reasons primarily. First, the Halo is a commercial product - making it robust, safe and suitable for long-term use, second, it allows for interaction through buttons on a display as well as continuous adjustments through an outer ring, and third, an open API allows us to develop custom controls specifically for sound zone control. The controller has a 3.4-inch touch display (800x340 pixels) as the primary mode of interaction and a rotating outer ring for adjusting different properties. We implemented a custom interface for sound zone interaction (see Figure 2 , middle) with two different views. The first screen consists of three buttons. The first two buttons let the user adjust the volume and width using the wheel, e.g. , increasing the size of the zone, from covering one listener to covering multiple. The last button locks the current position of the sound zone (elaborated in 3.1.2 ). On screen two, users can select the source of the sound content in the sound zone modified through that controller. Here, we provide three options, one for television and two for wireless audio streaming (elaborated in 3.3 ).

3.1.2 Repositioning Sound Zones. To accommodate changing listening positions and situations of use, users can modify the position of the sound zones by moving the two controllers. As part of the system, we integrated a complete Ultra-Wide Band (UWB) position tracking system (from Sewio) with four tracking anchors and two tracking tags. The two tracking tags are seamlessly integrated onto the back of the controllers under a 3d-printed cap (see Figure 2 , right). However, in some situations, it is not feasible having the controller at the preferred listening position. Therefore, users can lock the current position on the touch display and place the controller at another location than where the sound zone is. When they unlock, the sound zones automatically position themselves to the position of the controller again.

3.1.3 Sound Zone Visualisation. One of the major challenges for sound zone interaction is how users can obtain information about the current state of the system as well as how sound zones behave when the system is modified [ 27 ]. Prior research has found that sound zone visualisations can improve accuracy and efficiency [ 23 ] and convey information on sound zone behaviour to listeners [ 25 ]. We designed and built an ultra-wide display for visualising volume, position, and width of the two active sound zones (see Figure 1 ). Inspired by Johansen et al. [ 25 ], the shape of the visualisation is an upwards-facing arc, that changes in width and height to depict the spatial properties and behaviour of sound zones (see Figure 3 ). The height of the arc reflects volume, i.e. , and an increase in volume is indicated by raising the top point and vice versa. The width of the arc indicates a change in the size of the sound zone. Finally, the position of the sound zones is visualised by moving the shape across the ultra-wide display, hence mapping the length of the display to the width of the room (as also done by [ 23 ]). The display frame consists of 12 rows of 193 RGB LEDs (2316 pixels in total), that are individually controlled by a Teensy 4.0 [ 45 ] at a rate of 60 frames per second.

3.2 Soundbar

The soundbar is a state-of-the-art speaker array featuring 29 channels; 9 for the middle range (400-2500Hz) and 13 (+7) for the treble range (>2500Hz). Relying on the principle of constructive and destructive wave interference, beamforming is utilized as the main method to generate multiple sound fields simultaneously. The system is capable of delivering two sound zones, each one featuring its own content, level, shape and position. Depending on the frequency band as well as the acoustic settings, the soundbar achieves a separation of up to 18 dB between two sound zones.

3.3 Adding Sound Content

Our goal throughout was that the system would be able to substitute all primary media requirements that a household of today's world might have in a shared living room. This includes watching television, streaming music or other audio-only listening, and using the system as an extended speaker for, e.g. , activities on a laptop computer. To serve our goal, we implemented three independent inputs for adding content to the two zones. First, we built two streaming devices into the setup (Bang & Olufsen's BeoSound Core). The devices give the users the possibility to stream sound content (including, but not limited to music, podcasts, games, and videos) from their devices through standardised commercially available protocols such as Apple AirPlay, Google ChromeCast, and Bluetooth. Second, we integrated an Apple TV into the setup to function as a hub for all audiovisual content. Audio from the Apple TV was extracted and sent to the soundbar whereas the visual signal was connected to a 55” television screen included in the setup. Together, these devices provided three separate source inputs for each sound zone. On the Halo controller, users can select which source to listen to ( i.e. , Apple TV, Core A, or Core B) in that particular sound zone. We thereby provide users with great flexibility in terms of input and output routing to support different scenarios ( e.g. , different or same content within sound zones). For instance, having the same television audio in two sound zones allows users to adjust the volume in each zone independently, thereby supporting individual preferences or hearing abilities.

4 METHOD

To approach an empirical understanding of real-world use of and interaction with dynamic sound zones, we conducted a four-week field study with five households. The systems were installed in living rooms and family rooms, and each family used our sound zone setup as their primary media system throughout the period. Below, we describe the characteristics of the participating households and the process of recruitment followed by a presentation of the three distinct methods used for data collection before, during, and after field deployments.

4.1 Participants

Data for the study was gathered from 5 participating households in Denmark, over a period of 4 weeks. Participants were recruited through snowball sampling on social media and through word of mouth. To be eligible for the study families needed to live up to 3 criteria, namely: (1) the family should have a need to listen to different media sources at the same time; (2) a diverse selection of media consumption across family members; (3) a sizable room for installing the setup with the possibility of multiple listening positions and scenarios. A complete listing of the participants and their characteristics are available in table 1 .

The living rooms within the participating households ranged from 27 m2 to 43 m2. All living rooms and family rooms had a television placed at a central location in the room. In three households, the living room was equipped with a high-end speaker system for high-quality television audio and music reproduction. One of the living rooms was characterized by improved acoustics through sound-absorbing ceiling tiles.

Three types of family compositions were included in the study ( i.e. , families with young children, adolescent children, and without children), each with different everyday needs, habits, and routines. All families were frequent and proficient users of audiovisual and audio-only content streaming ( e.g. , television, music, audio books, etc.) through major streaming platforms (Netflix, HBO, Spotify, etc.) for both shared and personal activities. Families with children were frequent users of headphones as a means to manage disturbances resulting from multiple personal devices ( e.g. , smartphones and tablets). Nevertheless, family members prioritized being together while attending to each of their own activities within the same room.

4.2 Procedure

We deployed our dynamic sound zone system in five different homes. After the setup was installed we introduced each family to the sound zone system. With all adults in a household participating, and the adolescent members participating if possible. First, we introduced the participants to a single zone. Here we showed how the participants could change the spatial properties and add content to a sound zone. The participants tried the system themselves and walked around the room to feel the effect. Second, we activated a second sound zone, and the participants again explored the room, now with two zones simultaneously. Finally, we instructed the participants to use the sound zone system as their everyday media system in any way they saw fit.

4.3 Data Collection

For our study, we used multiple methods including interviews in three stages of deployments ( 4.3.1 ), contextual technology tours  [ 3 ] combined with sound walks [ 52 ] ( 4.3.2 ), and experience sampling [ 50 ] for in-context data collection ( 4.3.3 ). Throughout the deployments, we logged all interactions with the system, including volume, width, position changes and choice of media source on both controllers.

4.3.1 Interviews. After signing up for the study, two researchers visited each household to conduct a pre-deployment interview. Following a semi-structured procedure, each interview was conducted to gain insight into the physical and social aspects of the acoustic environment. Householders were asked to describe daily life situations in living areas to uncover how they experience, organize, and manage sounds within the home. The pre-deployment interviews were recorded on a hand-held audio recording device for later transcription. After two weeks of deployment, a mid-way interview was conducted online with each household. The purpose of these interviews was to allow householders to elaborate on preliminary use and experience as well as to discuss and motivate alternative use scenarios. The mid-way interviews were recorded and automatically transcribed through Microsoft Teams for later processing. Finally, we conducted a post-deployment interview with each household to invite participants to describe their overall experiences with a focus on what was different in the final two weeks compared to the first two interviews. These meetings were conducted remotely using the same procedure as the mid-way interviews. In all households, we strove to include as many of the adult participants as possible for each interview, but because of limited time and difficulties of arranging shared schedules, two interviews were conducted with only one adult (see Table 1 ).

4.3.2 Contextual Technology Tour and Sound Walk. During the first home visit we conducted a contextual technology tour which allowed us to invoke informal conversation about the families’ current use of media systems, their habits around them, and what specific ecosystems, if any, they have set up in their homes ( e.g. , smart speakers, multi-room systems etc.) [ 3 ]. The technology tours served the purpose to contextualise existing technology use within the five homes. The tours were structured around four key issues: present technologies in the living room, where it was placed, who used it, and what activities it supported. During the tour, we asked participants to describe any conflicts of ownership and problematic situations they had experienced with the technology. Following this, we conducted a sound walk to spur reflections on the domestic sound environment and daily life situations within the home [ 52 ]. The sound walk method was used to gain insights into family member experiences by asking participants to describe memories of sound within the home. Prior to each sound walk household members were asked to draw a floor plan of their home in order to attach sound source labels which carried meaningful associations for household members. Lastly, a semi-structured interview concluded the pre-deployment visit as described in Section 4.3.1 .

4.3.3 Experience Sampling. Aimed at measuring in-context behaviour, thoughts, and feelings of individuals throughout day-to-day activities, data was collected through self-reports provided by householders. Throughout the four-week deployment period, householders were proactively notified to log their responses on an iPad. Two contingency configurations (i.e. event-based and weekly) were applied to trigger self-report reminders linking to a Qualtrics survey. The event-based reminders were triggered shortly after system usage and a maximum of once a day. By employing principles related to The Experience Sampling Method, the self-reports were collected in situ to reduce reliance on participants’ long-term memory [ 50 ]. The self-reports provided information on sound zone content ( e.g. , music, television, audiobooks) and which of the family members were present in and outside sound zones within the same room. Furthermore, the participants reported the social character of the activity to identify household-specific usage patterns and individual needs related to personal and shared listening experiences. Following the generalised characterisation of domestic situations presented in [ 27 ], participants provided a social vs. private and connected vs. separate characterisation of the activity by moving two bipolar sliders. Prior to the mid-way and post-deployment interviews, participant self-reports were used to elicit sound zone experiences with the purpose of guiding and structuring interview questions.

4.4 Data Analysis

We analysed our data by following the approach of Braun and Clarke [ 5 , 6 ], performing a qualitative thematic analysis. All interviews (before, during, and after) were audio recorded (a total of 11 hours and 12 minutes) with consent from each household. Our analysis can be split up into five parts. 1) We started by transcribing the interviews and through which the first round of familiarising ourselves with the data occurred. Following, two authors read through all transcriptions to get a full picture of the generated interview data. All the quotes were gathered in a spreadsheet with a timestamp, which household it belonged to, and information on which family member said it, as well as from which interview ( i.e. before, during or after) the quote originated. 2) Two coders individually started an initial code generation. Each quote was associated with a code, which was further developed throughout the analysis. As part of this process, we generated initial codes based on the presence in the data, from which recurring themes were identified. 3) After finalising the coding of the data, the two coders met and through an iterative process discussed themes, aligned inconsistencies as well as disagreements and ensured the generated themes were soundly rooted and present in the data. 4) Before completing the analysis, an author without previous access to the data was included in the discussion. Each theme and subtheme was discussed, including quotes exemplifying particular parts of the analysis. 5) After the completed analysis, each theme and subtheme was titled and given a description. The result of the analysis is the three primary themes presented in section 5 .

5 FINDINGS

In this section we present the three primary themes that we identified from our thematic analysis: 1) Experiencing sound zones, 2) Using sound zone technology, and finally, 3) Interacting with sound zone technology. Experiencing sound zones revolves around how the participants experienced having sound zones in their everyday lives, with a focus on being in a zone and outside of a zone as well as simultaneous sound zone experiences. The theme, using sound zone technology, describes how participants adopted sound zones in their daily practices, the wish to control the sound exposure, and how they could listen in on sound zones. Finally, we present how the participants interacted with the system. In this section, we refer to each household by its number and with the signifier of ’a’ or ’b’ for the two adults.

Before presenting our themes, we report on how much the participants used the sound zone system and what they used it for (see Table 2 ). Each system was active between 14 and 29 days in the entirety of the deployment, where the days not being active were because: “ Having three children, a lot of my time is simply logistics, and watching television is only if there is time ” (4B), and “ We are often away for the weekends, and have primarily used the system as part of our everyday news update ”(5B). Over the course of the deployment, the participants listened to a total of 180 hours spanning from 17 hours and 59 minutes (family 4) to 75 hours and 24 minutes (family 3) between each family averaging to between 57 and 160 minutes per day. Families had an average of 54 listening sessions, averaging 40 minutes, with family 5 having longer average sessions of 64 minutes compared to family 1 with an average of 25 minutes. For calculating the listening time we took two safety measures. First, we calculated the listening time by logging all sound input into the system and then filtered out whether the audio was routed into any of the sound zones ( i.e. , the audio source was selected on a controller). Second, we filtered out the listening time where the sound volume in the respective zones was turned all the way down. We thereby ensure that the audio playing was indeed being played out into the room and avoided including “empty” zones. We distinguish listening time into single zone and multi zone. Single zones were the primary use of the system accounting for 87% of all listening, with 13% for all multi zone listening. Four out of the five households used two simultaneous zones with the same content for watching television (with family 1 using it for more than seven hours), but not by family four at all. Only one of the families had the same audio in two zones at once, listening for 24 minutes. For two zones with different content, four of the five households used the system to listen to both television and audio at the same time, with only household 5 using the system to play two different audio sources simultaneously. The households used the system primarily in the evenings after 6 pm (65%), next in the afternoon between 12 pm and 6 pm (28%) and least in the mornings before 12pm (7%). Family 5 was the family using the system mostly in the afternoon. To investigate whether there was any change in daily listening over the period of the deployment, we conducted a Pettitt's test [ 44 ] to inspect if a shift occurred in listening tendency ( i.e. change-point detection). We found no significant shift across any of the five households (all p > 0.2).

Additionally, we logged how participants interacted with their system, and modified the spatial properties of the zones, namely: volume, width and position 3 . To ensure that we captured an entire interaction and not every value change on the rotary wheel, we aggregated interactions into what we refer to as adjustments. We count one adjustment as the process of setting a specific property to the desired value, i.e , adjusting the value up and down until the goal value is reached. This is done by monitoring every time 10 seconds have passed since the last change in the particular property. Furthermore, for position adjustments, we only counted an adjustment when the position changed in a radius of more than 25 centimetres from the initial starting point. This is to limit the number of false positives, e.g. , as people may change the position of the controller slightly by readjusting their seat on the couch, but without actually intending to move the sound zone to a new listening position. We logged a total of 1702 volume adjustments (around 18 per day per household), 567 width adjustments (around 6 per day per household), and 1768 position adjustments (around 18 per day per household). Interaction based on time of day followed the same patterns as listening, with evenings consisting of 54% of all adjustments, next 35% in the afternoon and 11% in the morning. We attribute the small difference between listening and interaction on time of day to longer listening sessions in the evenings, and thereby less need to adjust the spatial characteristics ( e.g. when watching a movie). Similar to listening tendency, we conducted a Pettitt's test [ 44 ] to inspect if a shift occurred in how much peopler interacted with the sound zone system during the deployment. We found no significant shift across any of the five households (all p > 0.1).

5.1 Experiencing Sound Zones

Across the five families, we identified interesting ways of articulating the intangible nature of sound zones within households. On multiple occasions, participants expressed their conceptions of sound zones as closed entities with minimal or zero sound leakage. These conceptions confirm that sound zones offer possibilities of sound exposure control that extend previous understandings of spatial and temporal sound behaviour. As an example, a household member stated: “We have introduced the word ’sound bubble’ at home” (3a). This view resonates with the notion that having sound zones in a household is something new compared to conventional speaker systems, challenging current understandings of how sound is spatially distributed. While not explicitly expressed, participants actively experimented with different configurations of sound zones to achieve optimal listening experiences for themselves while also being aware of limiting their own sound exposure to others. For instance, next to the living room in direct line of sight to the speaker unit, participant 5b brought the sound zone into his office to listen to music: “I am sitting in the office while listening to music, and ’A’ just said that she can't hear it anywhere else” , (5b). Previously, this particular situation would entail putting on a pair of headphones to facilitate a private listening environment as well as shielding others close by. In this case, however, sound zones replaced the drawbacks of headphone usage ( i.e. , increased isolation and missed information) by enabling a personal listening experience and at the same time retaining a connection with the shared acoustic environment.

5.1.1 Being in the Zone. While reducing sound exposure, subjective sound quality within a sound zone should live up to the current standards of conventional speaker systems. However, several challenges emerge in real-life settings which require compromises in terms of adjusting the right balance between the level of sound spillover and sound quality within sound zones. Limiting the frequency bandwidth offers higher degrees of sound reduction outside sound zones and vice versa. Carefully designed audio filters enabled a well-balanced sound quality while retaining a satisfying level of separation between sound zones. However, as one participant noted, limitations of the sound zones became apparent in situations where he reached the maximum sound level. “I think you should be able to amplify the bubble [i.e., the sound zone] a little bit. It lacks some power” , (3b). Despite this, experiences of being in the zone generally yielded positive responses towards sound quality. “It is also some great [speaker] units. The sound is great” (2a). Furthermore, the experience of being immersed in sound led another participant to actively explore the sound zone's capabilities of reproducing content with high accuracy. “I think that we might have been too keen on finding movies with great sound quality” (2b). What characterizes this situation is that it involves multiple people in a shared activity through a wider sound zone. This suggests that sound zones not only facilitate personal listening but also shared listening scenarios similar to conventional speakers.

What became an interesting side-effect of being in a sound zone was the heightened awareness of reducing sound exposure for others. Participant 2a explained that she often watches television on the couch while her husband is working in the same room. “I have enjoyed sitting on the sofa watching something while ’B’ is working by the dining table. It worked really well. I could turn it up even higher and get more sound from the movie while being less disturbing for others” (2a). A tendency seen across families was that the participants’ ability to shield others from sound appeared to increase awareness of not disturbing others while in a sound zone.

5.1.2 Being on the Outside. Multiple participants described situations where sound zones prevented individual family members from withdrawing to another room or from wearing headphones to shield themselves as well as others. However, as one participant explained regarding his experience of being outside a zone, the sound was noticeable, but not to an extent that resulted in any disturbances. “Yes, I could hear it, but I wasn't bothered by it” (2a). Situations like these, where household members would typically wear headphones, enabled participants to reflect on future scenarios where they would benefit from having sound zones: “I guess we'll also have grandchildren at some point who will have to watch some horrible children's show. It would be perfect if we didn't have to listen to that” (2a). While reducing sound exposure within the same room, obviously, these benefits also limited disturbances in rooms connected to the living room as stated by one participant. “Yes, I experienced that there is less sound [in the adjacent room]. It is barely noticeable in our kitchen” (4b). Although the sound is not completely isolated within sound zones, the reduced exposure to unwanted sound for people outside sound zones appeared to neutralize disturbances that would otherwise result in family members retracting from the living room. Naturally, the character of the situation as well as the time of the day might influence how little is perceived as too much sound leakage. While overall sound exposure was reduced, participants noted that they still wanted to be aware of what was happening in sound zones of which they were not a part. This suggests that complete isolation of content within sound zones might not necessarily support one of the most essential needs of families; the feeling of being together.

5.1.3 Multiple Concurrent Sound Zone Experiences. While the participants reported several positive experiences related to the reduced sound exposure, engaging with multiple concurrent sound zones with different content yielded less positive responses. “If you expect two people to be able to listen to different sound sources at the same time, then the effect must be stronger” (3b). Two participants noted that the acoustic settings of their living rooms might have influenced the performance of sound zones. “I think that there is a lot to it, because, well, how is the living room? What kind of materials are there, and so on” (3a), and “There is something with the acoustics in our living room. It has been a bit of a nuisance” (2b). Of course, several factors (including physical properties of the acoustic environment) influence the performance and, thereby, the experience of sound zones. Participant 2a described a situation where music from the neighbouring sound zone resulted in confusion as to whether it was part of the television or not. “Yeah, well, at some point we played music while watching something on the television. That was quite confusing. We thought that the music acted as background for the television content” (2a). Besides the influence of the acoustic environment, other factors relating to the sound zone properties might influence the experience. For instance, increasing the difference in sound zone volumes and content can deteriorate the separation between zones. However, since we provided the participating households with the freedom to mix and match content within sound zones, multiple participants found it particularly useful to listen to the same content in two sound zones. Participant 1b experienced that this particular scenario enabled him and his wife to listen at different volumes. “If you had to watch the same thing [on the television] and hear the same thing, then you could have different volumes. That is where we said, okay, at least that's where it started to look like some of the functionality that we could use” (1b).

5.2 Using Sound Zone Technology

In this theme, we illustrate how participants used sound zone technology in their everyday lives. Across all households, we identified particular uses because of their particular family composition. Below we describe the three sub-themes concerning the use of sound zone technology, namely: 1) Adopting Sound Zones, 2) Adapting Zones to Needs, and 3) Accessing Sounds from Others.

5.2.1 Adopting Sound Zones. Introducing new technologies into people's everyday lives intervene with daily practices and routines. Adopting a system like this obviously takes a period of time to get used to and incorporate into the participants’ existing media habits. This refers to situations where having sound zone technology available changed the households’ common practices. Participants found themselves in new situations where they needed to be aware that sound zones are present in the room. For instance, a member of the household stated that he used visual cues to see that people were in a sound zone only slightly noticeable to himself. “I can see that ’A’ is within a zone right now and immersed in something [...], and I need to be extra aware that I need to call a little louder” (2b). In this situation, as sound zones are able to focus sounds where needed, it can be difficult for others to understand the current sound level compared to if they had used a conventional sound system.

People would traditionally simply turn on their usual system and have sound everywhere in the room, it is now required additional consideration in where and how much sound they needed for their use case. “Now I simply bring the remote wherever I need the sound to be, for instance, if I sit in the corner of the couch then I bring it. That I have never done before, but it has an effect, something happens to the room, and it is another sound” (3a). But this was not necessarily seen as a nuisance, as the same participant continued: “When ironing clothes the other day, I brought the remote to the dinner table because then it would probably be less disturbing to others” (3a).

However, our participants noticed leakages outside zones, which are both perceived as a disturbance, but also as an opportunity for easily sharing something with others close by. “My son came and sat by me at one point where I asked whether I should make the zone wider, but as he sat right next to me, it wasn't necessary” (2a). Even though action was not required in this case, it still made the participant consider this situation as a time for modifying the sound environment and it also shows that the non-perfect separation of the sound zones may not be a hindrance but rather an opportunity. Some participants also explained they have changed how they sit in their living room. Participant 2B often works from home in the evening and explained how he found an optimal position from where he would receive the least sound from what others were doing. “I have found out where I need to sit, perfectly accurately. Where I feel the sound gets me the least.” (2b).

5.2.2 Controlling Sound Exposure. Participants were motivated by diminishing the audio exposure on other members of a household if they were the current user of the system. Without sound zone technology, members of a household could either decide to wear headphones or adjust the volume for both themselves and non-listeners. Listeners are now given the possibility to reduce sound exposure to others, without diminishing their own experience. Below we describe how people used the system to limit this exposure. For example, a participant wanted to decrease the exposure to others by moving the controller to the left of him intending to expose the other participant who was sitting to the left of him at the dinner table. “When sitting at the table I have often tried to move my sound zone as far to the left of me as possible, to shield her as much as possible” (2b). This exemplifies many situations across households where sound zones are seen as physically present entities, where a natural connection is to shield others by separating the listener from the non-listener and changing the position as well as increasing the distance. Additionally, participants found themselves asking the question “How small of a zone can I live with, in this situation?” (2a). And thereby ensuring that a sound zone is not imposed onto the rest of the room.

The participants disclosed how the responsibility of managing a sound zone feeds an expectation onto other people in the same space. As exemplified: “I think my challenge is that when I make a sound zone, I need to consider the others in the room. But sometimes I believe they are the problem. I can take care of them, but I can not control their sound zone” (3b). First, this shows how participants take on the responsibility towards one another, in recognising that a sound zone may still disturb others if it is not adjusted to conform with the current listening situation. Second, this points to the fact that the system is currently designed with a controller for each zone. And that a listener in one zone has no way of controlling the second zone used by another person unknowingly disturbing the first listener.

Having sound zones furthermore enabled the participants to expand on their current ways of watching television and listening to music. In times with only one person present in the room with the sound zone system, they could focus sound to enhance their experience while still not disturbing other people in the house as they otherwise would. This was especially pronounced in households where family members also enjoyed media in solitude. “You often watch TV in the afternoon (referring to the husband), and I don't think there is the same level of disturbance [...] and where I prefer to watch TV late in the evening where the others have gone to bed [...] this is especially where our behaviour have changed a lot while having the system” (3a).

5.2.3 Listening in on Zones. Sound zones allow the families to still be a part of listening activities but without immersing themselves. Family members would like to stay co-located but are doing other activities such as work or play, which before would lead to some family members retiring to other parts of the house. With the sound zone system, they could remain in the same room of the house without being prevented from their own activities. This even opened up the possibility for situations where, as the participants describe it, they could participate with “half an ear” (2b), and still be aware of what is listened to currently. Participants also described how the primary listener can actively take this into account to adjust the volume, position and width of the zone to accommodate the wish of the other family members. “I have had a wider zone when sitting on the couch, and then adjusted whether ’B’ wanted to hear something or not” (2a).

5.2.4 Use over Time. In this section, we report on the behaviours of the participants over time with the sound zone system. Our log of interactions did show slightly more adjustments at the beginning of the study, but no increasing or decreasing trends were discovered. Listening time was likewise stable over the study period with spikes on certain days, for instance, at weekends. While we do observe larger variations in time of day during weekends, use of the sound zones system on weekdays primarily occur in the late afternoon and evening. During the initial days of deployment, participants expressed excitement in terms of exploring system capabilities. However, multiple participants described how the system quickly became an integral part of their daily routines: “It was exciting for the kids in the beginning. However, we mostly watch and listen to the same type of content so over time it became normal” (4b). The novelty period reveals a trend in terms of user exploration and familiarisation with the technology. Participants found themselves exploring different listening situations, configurations, and settings to reach an optimal user experience which became apparent through experience sampling logs and a subject of inquiry during the mid-way interviews: “We did a lot of experimenting to find movies with good sound quality. And we have spent a lot of time fine-tuning and finding the optimal settings” (2b). However, as the novelty effect wore off, the emerging patterns of sustained use were characterized by an increased consistency in daily listening time, time of day, number of family members involved, and sound zone configurations. What is evident through the patterns of use is the increased awareness of sound exposure within the domestic environment. As described by one participant: “Before there was just sound in the entire room. Now I am bringing the sound with me. If I sit in the corner of the sofa, I place the controller in front of me because I am aware of the effect it has on the room” (3a).

5.3 Interacting with Sound Zone Technology

In the households, we provided two tangible controllers each controlling the spatial properties and the content within, as well as one ultra-wide visualisation display for showing the spatial properties. In this theme we describe how participants interacted with the system and their experiences on the interaction techniques for controlling and visualising the sound zones. We observed that interaction techniques generally provided allowed the participants to change and modify the system and its spatial properties as needed.

While adjusting volume has become a mundane task in conventional sound systems, the possibility of changing both the dimensions and positions of sound was new to all participants. The participants used our system to, e.g. , modify the width to account for the number of listeners as well as the zone position for where they were located, but as expected for any new system it required a certain degree of habituation. “I believe that this is a system that you will need to become accustomed to using it, it is not just like getting a new television, where you just turn down the volume, it takes a little bit of practice I believe” (2b). Especially in the beginning people relied on the visualisation display to understand the capabilities of the system. “We used it a lot more in the beginning [...] for instance to recognise which controller I am currently holding [...] but now I don't think that much about it anymore” (3a). Similarly, families used visualisation as a feedback mechanism to what they control. “When we are sitting together in the couch we use it (visualisation red.) to determine the width that we currently need” (4b). A participant even considered that the visualisation could be more anchored in the controller so that after getting used to the system the controller communicated the state of the sound zones. “Maybe it can be transferred to the controller, maybe on the small display, so you can see how your sound zone look like” (2b).

In general our participants appreciated the interaction techniques for modifying the spatial properties of the sound zones. “The way that the interface gives me the understanding of that I can widen the zone, so more people can sit under it. Or one can make it narrow and sit under it myself [...] it allowed me to find a lot of usefulness in the system” (1b). The anchoring of the positioning system in the controller worked well as a tangible control for where the participants placed their sound zones. “I enjoyed just taking the controller with me, knowing that the sound zone followed me, and I could just put it down wherever I am” (4b). However, making the tracking even more personal, and allow the tracking of individuals was suggested as an improvement for one of the participants. “If I could wear the tracking on my watch or something, that would be great” (5b).

In our analysis we encountered wishes and expectations from the participants of how and what can be interacted with for the next generation of a sound zone system. One of the households wished that the sound zone system not only included the option to change spatial properties, but furthermore be able to adjust the sound qualities of the zones, for instance, allowing them to increase the bass when listening to music, or focusing on the spectrum of speech when watching television. This desire was also driven by a wish to further interact and modify their sound environment at home. “Here you only have a few options to modify the sound [...] I am one of those people that like to tinker with my products, I don't like when the devices can only start and stop. Also, all people feel different about how things should sound” (3b), referring to how it is currently not possible to adjust spectral features of the sound. One of the participants also requested additional ways of interacting with a sound zone besides having controllers as “it can become a little difficult to run around with a controller all the time, especially if there are more people around” (2b), where the participant proposed a voice interface where he could simply command the system: “It is me, ’A’, who sits here right now, setup the volume to three and the width to two” (2b).

6 DISCUSSION

In this paper, we have contributed with the, to our knowledge, first empirical field study of sound zones. Our findings reveal that the use of sound zone systems in homes both change existing and support new listening practices and experiences. We extend prior research both within the area of sound zones and also in the broader area of sound in homes.

6.1 Sound Zones for Personal and Shared Listening

In our study, we show that sound zone technology allows users to control and manage sound exposure as envisioned by sound and acoustics research [ 4 , 20 , 21 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 40 , 41 ]. Particularly, we demonstrate sound zone technology is capable to support different types of situations within the five participating households, and furthermore, allowed participants to actively and deliberately control the sound exposure in their homes. Below, we highlight five areas in which our present research extends prior research within sound zones and sound in homes. These areas situate sound zones as an intervention and technology within the everyday listening activities and social relations of households.

Listening Situations for Sound Zones. Prior HCI research has proposed a framework of listening situations for sound zones technology through design ethnography. When comparing our empirical findings to the two-dimensional framework proposed by Lundgaard and Nielsen [ 26 ], we can corroborate the findings of the social end of the dimensions within both separate and connected activities. We found situations with multiple people listening to one sound source as well as multiple people listening to multiple sound sources. The private and separate quadrant of the framework describes an individual who does not wish to listen to any sound source. This became evident from and can be confirmed by our study as participants outside of the sound zones found themselves less exposed to sound compared to their daily media systems. The final quadrant of the framework describes a single user having multiple sound sources. This behaviour was not encountered in any of the households. We attribute this to the fact that even though we designed the system to play sound from several different sources, there are still sound sources that are not included, e.g. , conducting telephone calls while listening to other sources.

Increasing Wanted and Reducing Unwanted Sound. An important aspect of sound zones is how they can be instrumental in controlling digital audio. As a result of the prolonged use of the system, our families found new opportunities to stay physically together in their living rooms with the system than they could with their conventional media systems. One finding related to this is that participants became aware of their ability to shield others present in the room and intentionally modify their own sound zone to serve that purpose. As sound is spreading less into the surroundings, participants that would otherwise leave a room when others are listening, e.g. while watching television, could stay without feeling disturbed. This shows an example of how the sound zone technology expanded on their ability to remain co-located even during different activities [ 42 ] expanding on other social aspects of listening in homes [ 33 ]. But just as importantly, participants achieved the freedom to intensify their listening by increasing the volume more than before.

Differentiating Volume in Zones. Our findings showed that having the same content in two separate zones allowed each listener to adjust the sound level to their preference, whereas the family members differentiated their volume without compromising the experience for others. Homes may comprise people with different preferences [ 42 ] for sound as we observed from our study with two zones with the same content but with different volume levels. While this this allows individually adjusted sound levels based on preferences, it may offer essential volume differentiation for people hard of hearing. Therefore, investigating how this can aid in listening situations with people with hard of hearing and with normal hearing listening to the same content. A shared volume may prove too low for people hard at hearing and pleasant for others. On the contrary, where the volume may be adequate for the hard of hearing, but the audio is way too loud for comfort for others. Sound zone technology can without additional equipment ( e.g. [ 16 ]) or connecting hearing aids), selectively increase volume in zones and possibly even accentuate parts of the audio spectrum ( e.g. speech [ 47 ]).

Listening in on Sound. Sound zones are not yet able to create clearly distinct areas with sound, without also exposing the surrounding area to different degrees. This imperfection in the system can be seen as a limitation of the technology, but our participants also found it pleasurable to be able to hear (with half an ear) what others are listening to. This particular finding is similar, but different, to the findings of Oleksik et al. [ 42 ]. They describe how parents would like to e.g. keep doors open, to allow for lightweight monitoring of children's activities in the house. For sound zones, we have shown that participants enjoyed listening in on the sound activities of others allowing them to feel a sense of connectedness to the activity without participating fully. Furthermore, Oleksik et al. describe two conceptual ideas ( The Sound Window and The Appliance Cube ), that allow audio to be relayed from other locations to listeners in a room. However, sound zone technology can take these ideas a step further, as sound zone technology could focus these relayed sounds to specific listeners in a space. I.e. house appliance alarms are relayed directly to the people needing them, without reaching others in the same room.

Enabling Social Interaction when Listening. Sound zones can support interface-free personal listening without secluding listeners from others. Sound zones can be situated within “personal listening technology”, as it allows users to curate their listening experiences [ 19 , 28 ]. Headphones may allow for transparent audio, i.e allowing external sound to be heard by the listener [ 18 ]. However, sound zones do not necessitate wearing additional technology to be able to curate an individual listening experience, as it is interface-free and is independent of the number of listeners [ 4 ]. It allows for both personal audio while maintaining the possibility of social interaction [ 35 ]. With the system, we have enabled users to configure their listening activities and control how the sound should behave in their particular home based on what they were doing. We show that this not only supports existing listening practices, as discussed by, e.g., Leong and Wright [ 33 ], but furthermore changes how people socialise around listening to music or television.

6.2 Future Research for Sound Zone Technology

From the early demonstrations in 1967 [ 37 ] to the work on different sound zone technologies by Betlehem et al. [ 4 ], the vision has been to increase the separation between zones as well as the surrounding environment. Our findings reveal that sound zone technology allows control of the sound exposure by both listeners, but just as importantly for co-located non-listeners. For situations with only one active sound zone with one or more listeners, our participants found that the exposure to the room and the rest of the house had been noticeably reduced. This implies that, while sound zone technology has not yet matured to create (as our participants describe it) bubbles with little to no sound escaping around the people listening, merits of the technology are still present. Better sound separation would only increase and extend the scenarios for listening. For instance, having two sound zones with different content ( e.g. television and music) to a satisfactory degree. This needs to be improved through research into both hardware and software algorithms of sound zone speaker arrays. Specifically within HCI and interaction design several future research trajectories can be investigated. Below we put forth four concrete directions that can be pursued within human factors of sound zones.

Shared Control vs. Personal Interaction. We believe that there are opportunities for research on personal interaction techniques for sound zone interaction. We anchored the control of sound zones in a controller, hence, all interactions with the system were either performed through the controllers. Naturally, this can lead to issues of scalability. If users wanted several active sound zones simultaneously, the current approach would necessitate just as many controllers as zones. In the same line of thought, interaction with the controllers is shared, as with conventional media remotes. An interesting avenue of research for sound zone interaction could be to investigate personally anchored interaction technologies. Thereby, anchoring interaction onto personally owned devices like smart watches (as mentioned by some participants), with tracking and controls available on a personal level. This change in interaction puts the primary listener first but may make it less intuitive to provide sound zones to others such as children or guests visiting [ 23 ], compared to having a shareable controller accessible to everyone. Further research is necessary, and we speculate that it will change for different types of households, where a hybrid approach of both personal and shared interaction could be preferred.

Visualising Sound Zones. One of the main challenges of sound zone interaction is how to communicate the state and behaviour of sound zones to both listeners and non-listeners [ 34 ]. Our results show that visualising the sound zones through ultra-wide displays supports current listeners in their current exposure in the room (both for shielding and sharing), and for non-listeners to identify the sound zones of others to know who is currently listening. Prior research has shown that visualisations both increases efficiency and accuracy [ 23 ] as well as improve listeners’ understanding of how a sound zone behaves [ 25 ]. Extending this, our results show that participants over time got increasingly more accustomed to having sound zones, and visualisations assumed a more important role in the phase of familiarisation compared to later in the study.

Sound Zone Presets. Humans are creatures of habit. Many of the things we do every day, including interacting with digital devices, are likely to be some sort of learned behaviour. Participants did not express aversions against having to control all spatial properties of the sound zone system. We do, however, believe that future studies could investigate in what ways users can set up the system to accommodate common usage patterns in homes. For instance, having selectable presets for different listening situations found in homes [ 33 , 42 ] ( e.g. TV for one listener, TV for many with different volumes and music in specific areas), which could be further extended to the system being aware of which users are present and where in the room and embed automated system behaviours [ 15 ].

Expanding into other Domains. We have focused on the domestic context in this paper, but many believe sound zone technology poses great opportunities in a variety of settings. Controlling, focusing and managing sound is not exclusively of use in homes, as unwanted sound ( i.e. noise) has been linked to a plethora of negative effects [ 43 , 48 , 49 ] in a wide range of contexts. Sound zones may in public spaces allow for directed content ( e.g. information and entertainment) towards the intended listeners. For brevity, we will provide three examples. 1) In airports, the primary operators of sound zone technology could be service personnel dynamically focusing announcements to passengers. In this situation, passengers only have a transient relationship with the space and may not be aware. 2) In restaurants and bars, it is possible to play music according to the preferred content and loudness of individual guests and potentially decreasing the cocktail effect [ 2 ]. 3) We see great prospects for introducing sound zones into a critical domain, such as in hospitals. Sound zones can be used to e.g. provide focused musical interventions [ 14 ] for improving recovery, especially in multi-patient environments, as well as masking a variety of environmental noise [ 12 ] ( e.g. alarms, outside noise and other patients).

6.3 Limitations

We acknowledge that our empirical study was carried out with some limitations, and we discuss five of them in the following. First, the study was conducted in a western European country, which may have specific characteristics of consumption of sound and music in homes. We noticed that sound zone technology prompted certain use that extends how families already socialised or managed sounds in their homes. Therefore, the extensions we make to prior research are anchored in the household compositions and culture of participants. Further studies could expand our work to other cultural contexts. Second, sound zone technology is currently an expensive technology and not commercially available, limiting its spread into homes to a few people. However, the cost is expected to decline with more development. Because of this, we argue that this is an important time for studies such as the one presented here to highlight further design challenges and opportunities. Third, sound zone technology is still in its early stages of development which was reflected in our results. This meant that the opportunity of having two sound zones with different content was not utilised a lot in the households. As the technology matures, conducting similar studies will show additional knowledge about situations with multiple sound zones playing different sounds. Fourth, we deployed the sound zones system for four weeks. We acknowledge that while some listening practices have been changed or established, longer studies are necessary to understand how people may adopt this novel technology as their permanent media system. Fifth, we acknowledge that five participating families (19 participants in total) can lead to less generalisability, but we still argue that our study show important results for sound zone research.

7 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we have presented a four-week empirical field study with five households, investigating how sound zone systems support and change listening experiences. The presented findings illustrate how sound zones have provided participants with new opportunities for designing their sound environment at home. We contribute with three themes concerning sound zones in homes, concerning how this new technology was experienced, used and interacted with. The themes feed into how sound zones have the opportunity to impact both social situations and listening practices. Additionally, we contribute with empirical understandings of sound zone technology, and how the current technology is capable of supporting households in enhancing listening experiences while at the same time shielding other co-located members of the household. We believe sound zone technology to hold great promises for households, but it still needs further development to live up to its full potential.
