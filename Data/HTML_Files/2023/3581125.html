In this paper, we propose SwellSense, a fabrication technique to screen print stretchable circuits onto a special micro-capsule paper, creating localized swelling patterns with sensing capabilities. This simple technique will allow users to create a wide range of paper-based tactile interactive devices, which are mostly maintaining 2D planar form factor but can also be curved or folded into 3D interactive artifacts. We first present the design guidelines to support various tactile interaction design including basic tactile graphic geometries, patterns with directional density, or finer interactive textures with embedded sensing such as touch sensor, pressure sensor, and mechanical switch. We then provide a design editor to enable users to design more creatively using the SwellSense technique. We provide a technical evaluation and user evaluation to validate the basic performance of SwellSense. Lastly, we demonstrate several application examples and conclude with a discussion on current limitations and future work.

1 INTRODUCTION

Our living environments are always patterned or textured. 2.5D structures, from rough surface textures to more explicitly-designed patterns, are ubiquitous and are interwoven with 2D planar surfaces. These 2.5D textures assist human perception, enabling us to feel, understand, and discover additional environmental information [ 14 ].

Recognizing the importance of 2.5D structures, researchers have tried to reproduce or even invent new surface textures to help with human perception. Creating textures faces challenges of expensive and slow fabrication machines ( e.g., using high-resolution 3D printers [ 40 , 48 ]) or requiring special 2.5D pattern printers ( e.g., Casio Mofrel 2.5D printer). Also, most of the existing work focused on creating different forms of the 2.5D textures, while less attention is spent on enabling sensing in these fabricated textures to support interaction with the surfaces. Our work, therefore, aims to provide a highly-customizable approach to rapidly fabricate 2.5D surface structures with integrated sensing capabilities.

In this paper, we present SwellSense. Our approach makes use of an existing, special micro-capsule paper that expands locally on its surface when heated. By screen printing a stretchable conductive silver ink (the primary ink) and carbon ink (the supplementary ink) on the micro-capsule paper, we achieve both the heater to trigger the localized material swelling as well as the sensor to capture input signals as a user slides their finger over the textured surface. The interactive capabilities of SwellSense can provide assistance for visually impaired individuals, enabling audio augmentation through tactile interaction. It also opens up new design space to create various physical interfaces with informative and expressive tactile features.

The main contributions of this paper are:

  • We describe a new fabrication method to print stretchable circuits onto the micro-capsule paper to create the localized 2.5D swell. We further characterize the material and electrical properties of SwellSense to better guide users in adopting this approach.
  • We explore the design space of how to combine inks with high/low resistance to create different types of 2.5D tactile patterns with specific sensing capabilities, specifically mechanical switches and capacitive sensors.
  • We describe a tool to simplify the design process for the SwellSense technology and validate its usability through a design workshop with six participants.
  • We present eight different application examples (two from the workshop, six from the researchers) that demonstrate the newly enabled design space of interactive tactile surfaces.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Paper Electronics

The rich affordance and flex material property inspire using paper as a substrate or carrier for prototyping electronics [ 7 , 46 ] including sensors [ 8 , 25 , 42 ], displays [ 39 ] or actuators [ 36 , 38 , 49 ]. In order to fabricate electronic components on paper, researchers have also demonstrated how we can directly inkjet print conductive silver traces on photo paper [ 23 , 24 , 26 ] or transfer away the printed traces to other extensive mediums for rapidly prototyping versatile interactive devices [ 5 ]. The malleable property of paper supports adapting cutting or folding-based techniques to develop interactive kirigami [ 53 ] or origami devices [ 4 ]. Besides utilizing printed paper electronics for prototyping interactive devices, researchers also investigated how the unique affordances of paper electronics can serve educational purposes by bridging the expressive possibilities that paper enables [ 37 , 43 , 44 ].

2.2 Surface Texture Fabrication

Compared to 2D planar or 3D spatial interactive devices, the investigation on 2.5D surface texture fabrication is insufficient. Within the limited work, HapticPrint is a pioneer study that introduces a digital fabrication pipeline to enable users to easily design the feel aesthetics of an object using the commodity FFF 3D printers (Fused Filament Fabrication), and a recent work, Geodesy, advances the method by 4D printing patterns can self-rise into dome structures. [ 11 , 48 ]. Cillia also demonstrates utilizing a DLP (Digital Light Processing) 3D printer to prototype hair-like structures with highly customized fine surface textures that have tunable mechanical surface properties [ 40 ]. In addition to the techniques of direct 3D printing textures on surfaces, meta-material textures showcased fabricating textures within the 3D printed objects to embed intended tactile feedback [ 16 ]. While most of the existing work uses 3D printing as the fabrication approach, SwellSense seeks to provide a more rapid and accessible fabrication approach with inherent sensing capabilities without additional implementation. Traditionally, micro-capsule paper swells along specified printed black lines and diagrams when under a heat treatment, which requires a special fuser and ink to complete the process. Furthermore, there are no use cases where sensing capabilities are added.

2.3 Tactile information

Tactile information is crucial to support learning especially for those who are visually-impaired. There is plenty of existing work that provides the best practice for tactile media design [ 2 , 12 , 35 ]. Researchers have followed these guidelines and demonstrated various use cases such as editing spatial layouts through tactile templates [ 28 ], combining 3D prints with 2D tactile to make tactile maps for Orientation and Mobility training [ 15 ], improving 3D shape understanding [ 41 ] or mapping the tactile graphics with mobile devices to provide audio explanation [ 32 ].

Another body of related work comes from the devices that can provide haptic information, including using motors to create vibrations [ 17 , 18 ], soft pneumatic or phase transition actuators to deliver haptic cues [ 6 , 13 , 54 ], electrical muscle stimulation (EMS) to increase the dexterity [ 34 ], different internal compliant structures ( e.g., detent, bounce) to exert fine-tunable haptic effect [ 30 ], or like recently Chemical Haptics introduced an approach to deliver topical stimulants to the user's skin to render different types of haptic sensations [ 31 ]. Besides, many electro-tactile interfaces are also of particular interest [ 3 , 20 , 21 , 51 ]. Tacttoo provides a printed feel-through interface for electro-tactile output on the user's skin with a thin and conformal form factor [ 50 ]. Kato et al. introduce a more rapid approach to directly inkjet print tactile display that can provide both the electrical stimulus and the electrostatic force [ 22 ].

3 HOW SWELLSENSE WORKS

This project is primarily built on the commercially available Swell Touch Paper [ 1 ], also known as micro-capsule paper. This paper is heat sensitive, consisting of a heat-triggered micro-capsule layer on top of a paper backing. Black ink with carbon pigment is typically printed on the paper. When the paper slides through a heat fuser, the black ink will absorb the heat and cause the micro-capsule surface to rise up according to the drawing. In this way, printed images, such as braille dots become raised and tactile. Other methods including laser cutting [ 28 ], embossing [ 27 ], and thermoforming [ 47 ] are also common ways to create tactile graphics out of the Swell Touch Paper or other materials.

SwellSense introduces a new way of printing stretchable conductive inks directly on swell paper. The conductive inks will serve to directly heat the micro-capsule paper and support sensing along the surface. The overall goal for SwellSense has two folds: 1) to add the sensing capability to conventional tactile graphics/braille, and 2) to add tactile feedback to non-tactile interactive devices ( e.g., paper electronics). The thin sheet form factor together with the stretchable conductive ink enables SwellSense with unique properties including highly fold-able that can be constructed into 3D structures, diverse sensing capabilities ( e.g., direct/indirect capacitive sensing, contact-based resistive sensing), and meanwhile SwellSense supports real-time swelling effect. In Table 1 , we also compare SwellSense with other existing fabrication methods for creating tactile devices. It is worth noting that in this table, [ 28 ] utilizes the same kind of swell paper, but the sensing function relies on a large mobile device ( e.g., iPad), so we consider it to be sensing-capable but not foldable into 3D artifacts.

4 FABRICATION PIPELINE

Similar to several existing joule heating-based methods, SwellSense relies on the printed stretchable silver ink to trigger the actuation and provide the sensing capability [ 9 , 33 ]. The fabrication process of SwellSense consists of three main steps (shown in Figure 2 c ) : (1) preparing the screen printing stencils; (2) printing and drying the stretchable conductive inks; and (3) final swell triggering & assembling step.

4.1 Step 1: Preparation of the screen printing stencils

For screen printing, we used YRYM Stencil Vinyl as our main screen printing stencil and the HTVRONT Transfer Tape to transfer the vinyl stencil from the cutting mat to the swell paper. For cutting the stencils, the Silhouette Cameo 3 is used to make the pattern cutouts. Our ink choices are 1) highly conductive stretchable silver ink (DM-SIP-2002), and 2) supplementary resistive stretchable carbon ink (DM-CAP-2100S), which both are low-temperature sintering inks.

4.2 Step 2: Ink printing and curing process

After the stencils are transferred onto the swell paper, we used a screen printing squeegee to brush over the ink. We determined a empirical swell trigger temperature of about 83°C, and adopted a curing process of 75°C for 40 minutes. We also took out the swell paper from the oven every 10-15 minutes to cool the sheet down to room temperature to prevent overheating. In addition, the carbon ink printing normally requires the printing of the connecting silver traces on the bottom first, so the screen printing and ink curing process need to be repeated for both silver and carbon layers.

4.3 Step 3: Swell triggering and circuit assembling

Once the annealed conductive ink is removed from the oven, the vinyl stencil is separated from the paper, we use a DC power supply to trigger the swell. In terms of heating methods, one can choose to probe directly at the two ends of the target area or design a trace with resistance variance ( e.g., altering the width, adding carbon inks) to only trigger the high resistance areas within the same heating path. After the triggering process is complete, we connected the conductive traces with other electronic components. Most of the paper electronics connection methods can be applied to SwellSense. As shown in the connection methods section of Figure 2 c , the most direct way is to connect the circuits by directly using alligator clips. To build a more elegant connection, one can also apply silver epoxy (MG Chemicals 8331D) or conductive patches (Chibitronics Conductive Fabric Tape Patches).

5 MATERIAL PERFORMANCE

5.1 Resistance Change Before and After Swell

The first basic parameter that we investigated is the resistance change before and after the swell. For this paper, the tests are mainly centered around our primary silver ink, while we also report several key parameters for the supplementary carbon ink in a later section. For the silver ink, We have tested 12 samples with dimensions of 3mm by 30mm. We measured with a two-probe setup and converted to sheet resistance R s = R $\frac{W}{L}$ , where R s is the sheet resistance, L and W indicate the length and width of the pattern. We achieved sheet resistance of 0.05 ± 0.01 ohm/sq and 0.02 ± 0.01 ohm/sq for before and after swell respectively. It is noteworthy that the resistance drops because we did not adopt the best heating condition for both inks.

5.2 Swell Z-axis Thickness Measurements

The most important factor is how much swell our proposed technique can produce. It is reported for the Swell Touch Paper that we are using, the nominal height of braille dots is 0.5 mm on average, which has been taken as the main reference for this paper [ 19 ]. As shown in Figure 4 a , the plain Swell Touch Paper will generally have a thickness of 0.23mm, and after we print the silver ink, the thickness increases to around 0.28mm. After we provide the power to heat up the printed traces (3cm by 3mm for 12 samples), the trace will rise up to around 0.92mm. Another interesting finding is when we try to heat up the patterns with different trace widths, the swelling heights are not always the same. Summarized in Figure 4 b , the thinner trace will result in a smaller swelling height. We perform tests on 3cm long samples with various widths ranging from 0.5mm to 5mm.

5.3 Heating Speed Tests

As shown in Figure 4 c and d , we also investigated the time variance to heat up traces with different lengths. All the samples are 0.3cm wide and have lengths of 1cm, 2cm, 3cm, and 4cm. We heat them up with 1V, and we achieved a clear trend that for heating longer traces, the heating time will also increase proportionally. We have also tested using 1V to heat up the traces beyond 4cm but did not observe a successful swell, where we speculate the saturation temperatures are below the triggering temperature for these cases. Besides, we have also tested for heating up the samples of fixed sizes (0.3cm by 3cm) with different heating voltages. It is clear that the higher voltages we provide, the faster the heating speed we can achieve.

To better understand the heating performance (the saturation temperature and the heat rise time), we have listed the following governing equations below. We follow Newton's law of cooling, listed as equation (3): the heat conduction $\frac{dQ}{dT}$ [W] is proportional to the difference of the temperature between adjacent materials, T x - T r [K], where h [W/m 2 ·K] is the heat transfer coefficient that depends on both materials.

\begin{equation} P=\frac{V^2}{R} \end{equation}
(1)
\begin{equation} Q= Q_{conv} + Q_{cond} + Q_{rad} \end{equation}
(2)

\begin{equation} \frac{dQ}{dt}= hA(T_{x}-T_{r}) \end{equation}
(3)

Here, A , P , V , and R are the surface area, power, voltage, and resistance for the system's input. Q is the total heat dissipation through convection ( Q conv ), conduction ( Q cond ), and radiation ( Q rad ). The radiation and conduction parts are neglected because of the low temperature of our system and there are no high thermally conductive materials in contact with our system [ 29 ].

\begin{equation} Cm\frac{dT}{dt}= \frac{V^2}{R}-\gamma A(T-T_{0}) \end{equation}
(4)

\begin{equation} T=\frac{V^2}{\gamma AR}(1-\exp (-\frac{\gamma t}{Cm A})) + T_{0} \end{equation}
(5)

\begin{equation} T_{sat}= T(t=\infty)=\frac{V^2}{\gamma AR}+ T_{0} \end{equation}
(6)

Equation (4) specifically describes our current heat conduction model for SwellSense, and by solving equation (4), we achieved our final guiding equation (5). In this equation, T 0 , C , and m represent the ambient temperature, heat capacity, and mass for the heater respectively, and h is equal to γ . The response time of the heater is defined as the time required to rise to the saturation temperature [ 52 ]. For a given sample with width and total length of W and L , the total mass including the silver trace and the paper beneath will be m= W · L · ( ρ 1 · h 1 + ρ 2 · h 2 ), and h 1 , h 2 , ρ 1 , ρ 2 are the thickness and the density for the printed silver trace and the swell paper respectively where h 1 is 0.05mm, h 2 is 0.23mm, ρ 1 is 2.2g/cm 3 and ρ 2 is 0.86g/cm 3 . For the specific heat capacity for the system, we mainly consider the printed silver trace which contains around 65% bulk silver with C trace =0.55J/(g · K) [ 45 ].

Also, the saturation temperature T sat is when t approaches infinity, and for our design editor we implemented the voltage required for a specific pattern to reach its actuating temperature (83°C). For achieving γ , we have tested 10 samples with a size of 6cm by 3mm under 1V and achieved γ = 132.4 [W/m 2 ·K] on average. To further verify the heat conduction model for SwellSense (shown in Figure 6 a ), we have tested heating up 0.3cm by 6cm samples under different voltages of 0.2V, 0.5V, 0.8V, 1.1V and 1.4V (5 samples for each voltage), and compared the saturation temperature from experiments to the theoretical values (results summarized in Figure 6 b ).

This empirical model is later embedded into our design editor to further help users achieve proper heating parameters, which is especially important for preventing overheating/burning of the patterns. It is also noteworthy that through our observation and thanks to the great stretchability of the silver trace, even when we slightly over-heat the trace for several more seconds or chose a slightly higher heating voltage ( e.g., 1-2V higher than expected voltages), the silver trace will still maintain its conductivity and hold the entire shape. While shown in Figure 4 f , this overheating process might cause the swell paper to expand over its heating zone, so we recommend users follow the guidance of our design editor and testing results.

5.4 Bending and Touching Tests

After we obtained the swelled patterns, the sensing function is usually executed through physical touch to the swelled areas ( e.g., press, slide), where we are interested in how robust these areas are under these physical manipulations. We carried out two basic tests, bending test and touching test (summarized in Figure 5 a and b ). For the bending test, we measured the resistance change for a 10cm by 0.3cm pattern. The results show that when we bend towards the swelled pattern direction, the resistance will slightly decrease, and if we bend the pattern in the opposite direction, the resistance will slightly increase. We also built a simple servo motor-based setup to repetitively press the swelled pattern (2.5 diameter circular pattern) and read the resistance change every 30 touches, and we barely see any trend after touching the area 300 times, which indicates the robustness of the SwellSense approach is fairly stable.

5.5 Feature Size, Accuracy and Repeatability Tests

We have also included several other swell performance tests which are summarized in Figure 4 e-h . We first tested how long a trace we can trigger, and we successfully heated up samples from 1cm to 10cm. We would like to note that through our testing results and workshop feedback, we did not encounter design limitations due to trace length. However, users should be cautious when designing extremely long traces ( e.g., using serpentine traces to fill a 15cm by 15 cm square entirely). For these cases, we recommend breaking the long traces into shorter ones or heating up the entire trace sequentially. Similarly, the thinnest line that we can trigger is 0.5mm while the thinnest gap for the serpentine trace design is around 1mm. We have also briefly tested how repeatable SwellSense technique is and achieved very consistent swell results which can also be referred from the small variance of the Z-axis thickness tests.

5.6 Carbon Ink Tests

Carbon ink (DM-CAP-2100S) in this paper is an alternative ink that aims to provide an alternative way of creating swells with small feature size ( i.e., 1mm diameter), but for most of the scenarios, silver inks show benefits ( e.g., lower voltage requirement, lower resistance variance, better robustness). We first tested 12 samples (4mm by 4mm) and achieved the sheet resistance for the carbon ink of 24.18 ± 3.19 ohm/sq and 94.35 ± 54.35 for before and after swell respectively. Compared to silver inks, the resistance variance is much bigger, and we have observed some minor cracks at the surface of the carbon ink patterns that cause the dramatic resistance increase which does not happen to the silver ink. Other parameters that we would like to report are the swell height and pattern feature size. After testing 12 samples (4mm by 4mm), We obtained a 0.43 ± 0.04 mm on average for the after-swell height (including the paper thickness). The benefits of utilizing the carbon ink are to directly create locally high resistance areas without the need to alternate the trace width, which serves as a better option to make patterns with small feature sizes, and we have achieved squared patterns ranging from 1mm to 5mm.

6 SWELLSENSE DESIGNS

6.1 General Design Primitives

We have listed four main categories of SwellSense's geometric designs including basic line shapes (first row of Figure 7 ), more complex shapes (second row of Figure 7 ) including numbers, English letters, different basic geometries, or even drawings ( i.e., a radio). The third category is about how we can tune the line density of SwellSense to indicate extension tactile information like direction. For example, the last example (b4) in this category is a three-circle pattern, where the outermost circle has much fewer swelled units than the internal one, and we envision this pattern can be used to represent the hour, minute, and second dials as haptic hints for low-vision people. The last category is how we can utilize SwellSense to create repetitive surface patterns. We have exemplified this by leaving intended non-swell areas which are not covered by the conductive ink, and thus we can create various surface patterns. It is also noteworthy that for triggering the repetitive surface patterns, sometimes the irregular shapes will cause uneven heating conditions. For example, in the fifth pattern (c5) in this category with both bigger and smaller circles, if we directly probe and apply the voltages from two ends of this pattern, the current will not pass through all the areas uniformly since the silver trace is also covering this pattern unevenly (for a unit area, the smaller circle parts are covered with more silver traces than the bigger circle parts). For this condition, we recommend users heat up the pattern sequentially by triggering the smaller circle areas first and then the bigger circle areas.

6.2 Sensing Primitives

Sensing is another inherent benefit for SwellSense on top of creating the local heating by using the stretchable conductive traces on the micro-capsule paper. In this paper, the most frequently used sensing mechanism is capacitive sensing. As shown in the sensing primitive section of Figure 7 , we specifically implemented the active self-capacitive sensing, which is frequently used in many HCI papers, and categorized as loading mode capacitive sensing[ 10 ]. With this basic functioning mechanism, we also introduce two different types of self-capacitance sensing, one is when users directly touch the electrode, and the other one is through indirect contact. The measurements and implementation are carried out by using the Arduino Capacitance Library, and the data is captured by Arduino UNO and a 1 megaohm resistor is used in the RC circuit.

We first show a simple button pattern as a capacitive sensing unit, that is, when users directly touch the swelled area, we can use a microcontroller to detect the signal, which can also further be extended to other geometries like a slider or rotator (shown in the first row of Figure 7 ). For direct capacitive sensing, the dominating factor is the involved capacitance coupling between users and the ground. So, when one changes the touch position or pressure, the effect on the charging time of the entire RC circuit is very minimal and difficult to detect without other signal amplification mechanisms.

Another approach to better detect human touching pressure or position is to involve another capacitor in the system. We simply put common scotch tape on top of the swelled silver trace to add a dielectric layer between the silver and human skin to form an additional capacitor, which will form a new two-plates capacitor. As shown in the second row of the sensing primitives in Figure 7 , we can capture clear pressure stage differences when users press the pattern. We found this is particularly useful to avoid false triggers when using capacitive touch sensing as the sensing method. For another example, if the pattern is with diverse surface density, the normal areas between the finger to the bottom silver pattern will change when users are moving their fingers on top. The slider can be utilized as a directional slider with tactile feedback, which is a missing point for most of the printed electronics [ 5 , 49 ]. Also, since different density designs can achieve different signals, we introduce a single-pin multi-touch controller. This brings the benefits of reducing pin or wire numbers by allowing users to touch different density areas as individual inputs. Users can meanwhile feel the tactile difference of different density patterns when touching them. We also report the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) from the following equations:

\begin{equation} SNR_{dB} = 10 log_{10} \bigg [(\frac{A_{signal}}{A_{noise}})^2\bigg ] \end{equation}
(7)

where A is the root mean square (RMS) amplitude of the signals calculated by

\begin{equation} A_{signal/noise, RMS} = \sqrt {\frac{1}{n} \cdot \Big (A_{1}^2 + A_{2}^2 + A_{3}^2 + \cdots + A_{n}^2 \Big)} \end{equation}
(8)

We achieved 46.6dB for direct touch, 26.5dB/35.8dB/38.4dB for gentle/middle/hard pressure respectively, and 29.8dB for slide.

Besides capacitive sensing, we also introduce how we can use SwellSense to create mechanical contact resistive switches/potentiometers. We can fold the sheet to form a two-layer mechanical switch and only when the top wiper layer touches the bottom terminal layer, it will form a closed-loop circuit.

6.3 Design Considerations

Besides the design and sensing primitives, we do want to report three design considerations for SwellSense. As shown in Figure 7 , the first one is from our experimental observation that when heating a relatively wide trace ( i.e., 5mm-1cm), smooth corners will have better heating consistency than sharp ones. Secondly, in order to achieve swell patterns with very small feature sizes, one can either directly use the resistive carbon ink or if one wants to use the silver ink, the connecting traces have to be wider than the heating area width to form low resistance areas (shown in Figure 7 design strategies category). Lastly, one can also selectively heat up traces by alternating trace width. For example, to selectively heat up a 2mm wide trace, the unheated area has to be at least 1mm wider to form lower resistance areas. Also, the smallest gap width is around 4mm. With smaller gap width, it is harder to have the controllability to form clear distinctions between the swelled and unswelled areas.

7 SWELLSENSE DESIGN EDITOR

To further expand the design customization and support end users, we also developed a design editor (shown in Figure 8 ). The back-end implementation of this design editor is with Grasshopper and Rhino in C# and the front-end design interface is developed by using Human UI. The goals of this design editor are to help users 1) design, adjust, visualize the patterns and export the PDF file for the vinyl cutter, and 2) provide the heating condition parameters ( e.g., estimated resistance, heating voltage, and time) for prototyping. As mentioned in section 5.3, equation (5) gives the relationship of the needed voltage V and heating time t , given a fixed pattern dimension. We specifically implemented the following equation derived from equation (5):

\begin{equation} V= \sqrt {\frac{(T-T_{0})\gamma AR}{1-\exp (-\frac{\gamma t}{Cm A})}} \end{equation}
(9)

In this equation, Temperature T 0 is the ambient temperature which we set to be 20°C. T and R are the swelling temperature (83°C) and total resistance R = R s $\frac{L}{W}$ of the pattern which are achieved from our experimental testings. A is the pattern dimension with width and length of W and L . m is the total mass of the pattern. C and γ are the heat capacity and heat transfer coefficient for the system. It is obvious for a given pattern with a known size, we can achieve a relationship between V and t , and multiple V and t combinations can allow us to achieve the same temperature for the same patterns. So, as shown in the bottom of the design editor overview of Figure 8 , we created a scroll bar to allow users to choose different heating times they want, which will result in different recommended heating voltages. It is also noteworthy, after plugging in the values, the dominating factor for the equation is the length L of the pattern instead of the width W or heating time t .

In order to generate designs with our design editor, users should go through the following steps: (1) Upload a drawing file to the design editor. (2) Select the design type (singular path, filled shape, path of varying widths, or template library). (3) Adjust the design parameters ( e.g., pattern density, line width). (4) View the simulation results. (5) Adapt the recommended heating conditions ( e.g., heating time, required voltage/power). (6) Export the design and get ready for vinyl cutting.

This design editor currently requires users to have their own vector drawings in PDF format, which could be both a closed-loop design ( e.g., full circle) and an open-loop design ( e.g., half circle). For the closed-loop designs, our design editor will help users to create serpentine traces that could fill the entire pattern and users can adjust the density or width of the traces. For the open-loop design, users can either directly adjust the thickness of the design or if a selective heating task is required, users can adjust thicknesses 1 and 2 to achieve the desired pattern with selective heating capability. Afterward, the users will be able to see the simulation of the swelled 2.5D pattern, the design editor will also provide the recommended values as the heating conditions they can refer to. Besides supporting the main methods ( e.g., filled shape, singular line), we have also created a design library to include our existing designs of application examples for braille and surface pattern designs. By the end, users can directly export their designs as PDFs and get ready for the later vinyl-cutting task. We have also showcased all the essential grasshopper modules on the right side of Figure 8 b .

8 WORKSHOP AND FINDINGS

SwellSense aims to enable a highly-customizable design space of sensible tactile patterns while simplifying the fabrication process. The workshop goals are: 1) verifying the usability of the proposed method including our design editor and fabrication techniques; and 2) exploring the open design space. So, we carried out an IRB-approved workshop with eleven university students (all 20s, 5 women and 6 men). Among our participants, two have engineering backgrounds, and the rest are with industrial design backgrounds. All participants have basic experience and understanding of physical computing tools ( e.g., Arduino programming and basic circuit building), yet none of them have experienced topics related to printed electronics or tactile graphics.

8.1 Procedure

We conducted a workshop in a studio-like lab space and followed a 3.5-hour timeline with four sessions: (1) a 30-min introduction about the background of SwellSense, (2) 30-min design editor testing, (3) a 2-hr design and prototyping session, (4) 30-min discussion and post-survey. With the first research goal of testing the accessibility of SwellSense design editor and techniques, we have quantitatively and qualitatively measured the following four metrics: (1) fabrication time, (2) repeatability, (3) technical difficulty, and (4) confidence, to gauge the impact of using our design editor.

8.2 Results & Limitation

This section describes our findings centered around the usability of the proposed method including our design editor and fabrication techniques, and design possibilities by applying the method.

8.2.1 SwellSense design editor makes the techniques more accessible. One of the biggest challenges in SwellSense fabrication is estimating the heating conditions for different designs. In order to study the efficiency of the encoded heating information generated in the editor, we asked participants to heat up two same 3cm x 3cm x 1mm square patterns. For task one, participants were not given information on the resistance, heating voltage, or heating time for the pattern. Task two was similar, but participants were given the information generated by the design editor. We report the results in Figure 9 b. When referencing the heating condition provided by the design editor, all eleven participants successfully demonstrated heating up the pattern without causing any over-burn or under-swell. However, for task one, three participants used lower voltage and had to keep adjusting the voltage to trigger the swelling, six participants used too high voltage, causing their designs to burn, and only two participants chose the proper heating voltages and heated the pattern to the desired height. (shown on the right side of Figure 9 b). As reflected from the fabrication time for both tasks, and in the post-discussion session, participants mentioned that the design editor was critical to visualize their design, and more importantly, to figure out appropriate heating conditions for their designs.

We also tested the repeatability of SwellSense technique by comparing the heating height results for both tasks, where the swelling height results for task one (including 6 over-burning samples) are lower than task two results. Besides, our post-survey included a 5-point Likert scale for how difficult they think about SwellSense and how confident they feel by using our design editor and without using the design editor (5 for the most difficult/confident, 1 for the least). The results indicated that the participants found the design editor to be useful for quickly learning and applying the SwellSense technique with confidence ( Figure 9 a).

8.2.2 SwellSense's Design Insights. Seven participants attended the following design and prototyping session, where participants were asked to draw ideas for potential applications of SwellSense techniques. As a result, 15 ideas were proposed with five themes: touch feedback, sensible mechanical friction, sensing triggered swelling, assistive technology for visually impaired people, and emotion recorder. P3, P4, and P6 proposed scenarios about swelling patterns that can be triggered by touching the sensing areas on the same sheet with example applications such as a birthday card or an educational book for children. Another notable application theme was applying the SwellSense to create a device for emotional bonding. P5 discussed that the unique tactile interaction through the familiar and friendly materiality of paper inspired them to apply it to a device that supports capturing a special memory for a special moment with emotional bonding.

For the following prototyping, participants created a paper horn and a birthday card. The horn contains functionalities including music tone adjustment by the capacitive touch pads and sound volume adjustment by a resistive track on the side of the paper and a ring on the user's finger ( Figure 10 a2-a4 ). The birthday card supports an interaction that allows people to touch the sensing area on the left side of the birthday card to start the swelling of the heart pattern on the other side of the card ( Figure 10 b2-b4 ). These two prototypes leveraged the initial proposed design ideas and are summarized in Figure 10 . We also would like to note that the hardware implementation was assisted by the researchers due to the limited time and available skill sets.

After the show-and-tell presentation of the two projects, we had an open discussion on limitations that led to proposing a call for future work. Some participants (p2, p4) pointed out that the size of the paper (8.5 x 11") limited their design ideas. They discussed direct printing on everyday surfaces can be an interesting future work direction. Also, extending the swelling effect to both sides of the papers was discussed to broaden the design applications using the sheet. Further, one participant (p1) was interested in on-body interface possibilities and asked whether the swelling behavior can be created against the skin to deliver tactile information, and if this SwellSense technique can be embedded into fabrics. This likewise echoed the previous point that the current form factor of micro-capsule paper limited the design space.

9 APPLICATION

Besides two application examples from the design workshop, in this section, we further showcase six application examples enabled by SwellSense. These applications are carefully chosen and can be mainly categorized by two themes, 1) adding tactile information to printed electronics targeting at more general users ( e.g., makers) and 2) creating sensible tactile patterns which can bring benefits to the visually-impaired community. We also provide a mapping graph (shown as Figure 11 ) to emphasize the unique features that each application tries to highlight and which design and sensing primitives each application has utilized. For example, in the birthday card application (workshop application 2), SwellSense highlights the real-time swelling feature by combing the direct capacitive touch unit and density design patterns. Or in the interactive piano example (application 3), SwellSense creates multiple contact resistive piano keys to form the 3D interactive piano device.

9.1 Application 1 & 2: Adding tactility to paper electronics — Interactive tracks and phone extension

People have demonstrated many possibilities of using paper electronics for entertaining or educational purposes [ 43 , 44 ]. Many designs are intended for users to follow an interactive path ( e.g., interactive finger slider, car tracking games), but the printed circuits are usually completely flat without tactile feedback. In Figure 12 , we show 2.5D tracks to guide the directions of a metal ball. When the metal ball moves along the swelled tracks, it forms a closed-loop circuit. Different locations of the ball lead to different embedded resistance, which are mapped to different tones of a buzzer ( Figure 12 a,b ). Besides, this 2.5D swell can also add tactile feedback to users. The biggest drawback of printed electronics is the lack of tactility. We demonstrated a mobile device extension panel, which is enabled by the capacitance coupling between the conductive strips and the mobile device screen. The function that this specific pattern enables is when you scroll clockwise or counter-clockwise on the right side of the panel, you can scroll up or down the device screen.

9.2 Application 3: Rolling Paper Piano

This application utilizes the malleability of paper that one can easily fold or cut to transform its shape. We firstly fabricated a piano keyboard containing 7 natural notes that is connected to a buzzer through a microcontroller board, and the conductive traces of each note are disconnected as shown in Figure 13 c . A paper with printed silver traces representing the numbered musical notation of a song is fabricated and taped around the top roller. As the roller rotates, the music note block comes into contact with the corresponding note-sensing electrodes, completing an electrical connection that is sensed by the microcontroller, which in turn triggers the buzzer to play the note. Through this process, users can also experience the rugged feeling from the swelled areas as tactile feedback, and the inverted trapezoidal shape of the note block and sensing electrode ensures reliable electrical contact (shown in Figure 13 ).

9.3 Interactive Braille & Barchart

Nowadays, braille and tactile graphics education is largely limited to self-learning through instruction books or courses from schools. SwellSense can enable manufacturers or educators to rapidly customize different 2.5D swells for the letters or graphics with inherent sensing capability. Here, we first demonstrate a highly-customizable interactive braille, which can play the pronunciation of the letter when the corresponding raised dots are touched by the user (shown in Figure 14 ). For this device, the carbon ink and silver inks are printed separately (shown in Figure 14 a1-a4 ). SwellSense can also enable different surface patterns which can be utilized to represent different meanings. For example, we showcased an interactive tactile bar chart in Figure 14 c. The three different surface patterns (circles, crosses, rectangles) indicate different information in the bar chart.

9.4 Application 6: Augmenting visually-hidden surfaces for versatile purposes

There are many surfaces that are ubiquitously around us but visually-hidden ( e.g., inner side of the door handle, back of our phone, or bottom surface of the table). In this application, we would like to show how SwellSense can augment these everyday surfaces with different tactile information. We developed a tactile interactive hidden numeric keypad. As shown in Figure 15 c , we are using the keypad to sign in to Windows on the lock screen. The numeric keypad is placed on the underside of the tabletop so that visual obstructions can prevent the user input process from being visually captured. We also set a threshold to prevent the keyboard from being triggered when users are searching or accidentally touching the numbers. The user must confirm their input with a harder press for the number to be recorded. Moving forward, there are other visually-hidden areas that can be enhanced with interactive tactile functionality such as the back of a phone or tablet (shown in Figure 15 b4 ). Besides, we can also utilize SwellSense to create interactive extension panels for these visually-hidden surfaces, where in Figure 15 (b1-b3 ), we demonstrate how to easily customize sheets with interactive tactile information as phone input position extension, input operation extension ( i.e., zoom-in/zoom-out with different speeds and granularity).

10 LIMITATION AND FUTURE WORK

10.1 Fine Swelling Height Tuning and Height Reversibility

Currently, the heating process is open-loop without any fine-tuning capabilities for the swelling height. We believe that by involving a close-loop control ( e.g., temperature sensor, relay) or creating a thickness gradient within the same pattern, we can achieve finer control of the swelling height or height gradient within the same sheet. Also, SwellSense takes the advantages of the off-the-shelf micro-capsule paper, allowing users to rapidly fabricate sensible 2.5D patterns with arbitrary geometries. This great customizability compensates the non-reversible nature for SwellSense that users can always create new patterns and devices with new sheets, which can be further strengthened if SwellSense is reversible and can be reused. Additionally, in the future, we propose to re-heat up the units and applying pressure against their swelling direction which could potentially enable a reversible heating process for SwellSense, but this will require further investigation and testing.

10.2 Stretchable Carbon Ink

In this paper, the primary ink is stretchable silver ink (DM-SIP-2002). However, one limitation that we observed is for creating solid patterns with small feature sizes ( e.g., a sold dot pattern with a diameter of 1mm), which brings us to introduce a more resistive ink (DM-CAP-2100S). With this ink, users can fabricate solid fine patterns by directly making more resistive areas that will draw higher voltage along the same trace without the need of alternating the trace width or creating serpentine trace areas. While throughout experiments, the carbon ink is not as endurable as the silver ink. Certain areas might have a risk to break if overheated. Also considering the solid fine pattern can still be enabled by the silver ink, we only list the carbon ink as the supplementary ink in this paper.

10.3 Inkjet Printing As An Alternative Fabrication method

The screen printing approach is the main fabrication approach to fabricate the devices in this paper. The consideration comes from the stretchable conductive ink option in the market. Since we aim to provide a highly accessible and customizable approach, we only consider off-the-shelf ink. Most of the stretchable inks are fairly viscous ( e.g., 8-20 Pa.S), making them only compatible with screen printing fabrication methods. However, this screen printing fabrication approach is at the expense of involving a time-consuming and ink-wasting stencils preparation step. On the other hand, inkjet printing, as a direct ink deposition method, will help to improve the automation of the current fabrication routine, but the commonly used silver nanoparticle inks will not be compatible because of the extremely low stretchability of the ink, instead, low-temperature sintering PEDOT:PSS or silver/carbon nanowire inks could be the future ink substitution.

11 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we presented SwellSense, a simple and highly customizable fabrication approach to creating 2.5D surface textures with inherent sensing capability. By utilizing surface expandable micro-capsule paper together with printed stretchable conductive traces, we can locally control the 2.5D surface swell for various types of interactive devices. We have reported the detailed fabrication process and the performance testing results to better guide users to adopt the technique. In order to make SwellSense more accessible to broader users, we also build a design editor to facilitate users and verified its usability through a design workshop. Besides, we also showcased the design space and several application examples to demonstrate how SwellSense can create various interactive mechanical features or add direct sensible tactility to everyday surfaces.
