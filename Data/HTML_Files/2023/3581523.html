CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

In this paper we present LYDSPOR; a site-specific sound experience, created in Elsinore, Denmark, consisting of two physical installations and an app-based soundwalk, which together allow people to feel and sense in their bodies certain narrative fragments of the history of the city. LYDSPOR is the result of a 1,5 year-long research project which had the aim of adopting soma design methods by drawing on affective interaction design and on an understanding of bodies as always multiple, relational, and never only human. Through an analysis of the design process and its outcome, the paper contributes an in-depth understanding of how to combine somatic and affective design approaches when creating site-specific sonic augmentations for historical dissemination in public space.

1 INTRODUCTION

The question of how to design with and for the human body is a rapidly growing area of research within HCI. One might trace a genealogy going from Paul Dourish's introduction of embodiment [ 24 ] to the emerging area of soma design [ 50 ], in which designers have moved from acknowledging the importance of interaction as inherently embodied to exploring how we can understand and design for the body by incorporating the actual living, corporal body with its muscles and moving limbs into design processes. This development has also led to a broadening of the sensory capacities one might design for, moving from the purely visual to other sense modalities such as sound, touch, and smell [ 35 , 71 ]. Currently there is also a move towards expanding the notion of ‘the body’ in HCI from referring simply to designers’ and end-users’ bodies in isolation to the consideration of a plurality of entangled human and more-than-human bodies participating in design processes; this can include technologies, materials, or environmental elements such as animals or plants (e.g. [ 11 , 19 , 48 , 79 ]). Such approaches are influenced by theories of entanglement [ 34 ], posthumanism [ 29 ], or new materialism [ 6 ], amongst others. As suggested by Homewood et al., “ rather than framing the human as an independent actor manipulating the world around her, more-than-human perspectives highlight the co-constitutive role of non-human aspects. This reflects a relational ontology and a focus on the agency of more-than-human objects and entities such as things, spaces, people, and materials .” [ 49 :7]. We see a need to further explore these important conceptual and methodological shifts which take into account both human and more-than-human agents being present and participating in design processes and in their outcomes [ 93 ].

In our research, the particular context we have focused on for exploring this new research direction is the design of site-specific sonic experiences for the dissemination of cultural heritage. Designing for public space in the town of Elsinore, Denmark, in collaboration with the municipality and the City Museum, allowed us to engage with the affective, sensory and somatic qualities of urban space [ 75 ] and the multiple living and non-living bodies co-existing there, in the present-day as well as in the past. Our desire to focus on bodies and their relationality led us early on to the decision to work with sound and its capacity to move in-between as well as to connect different bodies and their environment [ 57 ]. We worked specifically with sonic augmentation, which for us meant using soundscapes and sonic vibrations to add an experiential layer to the existing environments. Our intention was to explore ‘embodied listening’ – the act of listening as a multi-sensory experience [ 3 , 23 , 57 , 65 , 76 , 92 ] and how this could lead to evocative experiences of historical scenes taking place in the city. This design approach fits well into the current development within the museum world and the trend towards more sensory, affective and immersive visitor experiences [ 53 , 56 , 78 ] and we believe it contributes to the HCI research within this field by promoting a focus on multiple bodies, both human and more-than-human.

The main research question driving our work has been: How can we develop site-specific designs with a focus on human and more-than-human bodies in the context of cultural heritage dissemination? We have also explored a subsidiary question: How can we use sonic augmentation and an embodied approach to listening to achieve this? To answer these two questions, we have engaged in a design process that lasted for about 18 months using methods developed within soma design, such as sensitising exercises, to become attentive to the designers’ and end-users’ bodies throughout the process [ 89 ]. Moreover, since our aim was to extend our focus on the human body to also include other, more-than-human bodies, we adapted soma design tactics by drawing theoretically on affective interaction design [ 37 ] which builds on Spinoza's [ 82 ] concept that bodies are always multiple, relational and never only human [ 41 ]. In this way, our work opens up the novel design research space of using soma design for designing site-specific experiences through extending the attention to ‘soma’ to include both human and more-than-human bodies related to the sites, as well as being present as part of the design process.

In this paper, we present the design research process leading to LYDSPOR (SONIC TRACES), a site-specific sonic experience that allows people to feel and sense narrative fragments of the past, interwoven with the present-day experience of human and more-than-human bodies co-existing in the city of Elsinore. The LYDSPOR experience uses sonic technologies and soundscapes to augment the existing space of the city with forgotten sounds and stories from the 16 th and 17 th centuries, and it consists of three parts: two physical installations, one at the harbour and the other at the City Museum of Elsinore, and one app-based soundwalk on the street Hestemøllestræde, leading up from the harbour to where the museum is located. The installation at the harbour ( Sanden , meaning The Sand) uses sound transducers incorporated into a physical structure to play a soundscape that can be listened to and felt as vibrations through the body when standing in or sitting on the installation. Through the felt and heard soundscape, it tells the story of the land and the diaspora community which emerged there, which existed for a hundred years before the settlement was demolished during the war against Sweden in 1658. The installation at the museum ( Karmeliterhuset – named after the building which currently houses the museum, and which historically was part of a Carmelite monastery) also uses sound transducers and embodied listening to convey both the human and more-than-human sounds that could have been heard in the building 500 years ago. Finally, the soundwalk along Hestemøllestræde (Horse mill street) uses location-aware technology and a custom designed app to introduce visitors to historical human and more-than-human actors and stories related to this particular street, unfolding different perspectives on the frictions between the belief system of magic, science and the Church during the 16 th and 17 th centuries.

Beyond the design process and the resulting auditory experience, we present how we invited 12 participants to experience our work by conducting structured test sessions. These were focused on deep, individual engagements with the experiences, eliciting rich qualitative data including our observations, participants’ reflections (in the form of recorded interviews) and their somatic/affective experiences as drawn onto body maps [ 5 ]. We present an analysis of this data to unfold insights related to site-specificity, bodily awareness, and the sensory and affective encounters with the more-than-human world of the past and the present. Based on these findings, we end by discussing our contributions, particularly our auditory and body-oriented approach to the dissemination of cultural heritage, as well as on how we have extended soma design to allow for a broader understanding of bodies including the more-than-human.

2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORKS

In this section, we sketch out the related work spanning across affective cultural heritage dissemination, soma design, affective interaction design, and sonic interaction design, which are domains core to our project, and thus help us situate our research contribution.

2.1 Sonic Augmentation to Foster Affective Heritage Encounters

In recent years, there has been a trend within the museum world towards the sensorial [ 53 ], the affective [ 78 ] and the immersive [ 56 ]. This development has been enabled (and sometimes propelled) by the introduction of new technology [ 85 ] as well as by the visitor-centred approach favoured by most museums today [ 28 , 42 , 52 ]. Andrea Witcomb, as a proponent of ‘pedagogies of feeling’ [ 95 ], argues that especially when it comes to dealing with “difficult” topics, such as histories of war and oppression, affective strategies are important [ 94 ]. Dianne Mulcahy [ 63 ] puts forward the idea that there is an untapped potential of affective museum encounters to bring forth new capacities for thinking, doing and being, which can activate visitors’ ethical and political imaginations. This also resonates with the striving of museum curators today to expand their domains by leaving the strict confines of the museum. This means taking advantage of technologies which offer the augmentations of pre-existing environments (through sounds, vibrations and/or visualisations), and in many cases it has also involved engaging in more artistic methods of production. Well-known examples of immersive historical experiences that make use of mobile technologies and which use evocative narratives and sounds to enhance public spaces are “A Hollow Body”, developed for the Museum of London in 2014 [ 18 ] and “Nightwatchers”, developed for The Tower of London in 2015 [ 4 ]. Another example worth highlighting is “Touched Echo” [ 36 ], which takes a more directly somatic approach in its attempt to foster affective heritage encounters in a public space. By imagining shielding one's ears from the noise from an air raid, and leaning on the balustrade, the sound of aeroplanes and explosions is transmitted from the swinging balustrade through the arms directly into the inner ear via bone conduction.

In HCI, researchers have, in recent years, taken a special interest in museum visitors’ engagement, participation, and play (e.g., [ 17 , 21 , 22 , 86 , 97 ]) as well as their affective engagements with cultural heritage (e.g., [ 12 , 30 , 47 ]) – taking place both inside and outside of the museum's walls. Here again we find examples of how sonic augmentations are used to evoke emotions and enable affective encounters. In the work by Spence et al. [ 81 ] and Ryding et al. [ 72 , 73 ], personal relationships, mobile phone technology and voice play important parts in fostering affective encounters with exhibited objects in the museum. Fosh et al. [ 30 ] and Hazzard et al. [ 47 ] used mobile technology to deliver interactive music and narratives in order to emotionally enhance a visit to a sculpture garden. A similar approach to evoke emotions in visitors is taken by Benford et al. [ 9 ] at the Munch Museum. Wodiczko et al. [ 96 ] take a political approach to affective heritage encounters by creating an interactive ‘Speakers’ Corner’ in Weimar's Theatre Square using visual projections and audio to give presence to un-heard voices of refugees in a site of German national cultural heritage. Also working with an installation in public space, Fritsch et al. [ 8 ] present visitors with ‘echoes of the past’ linked to a public square in Aarhus, Denmark. Designed to resemble a pre-radar listening device of the inter-war period, “Ekkomaten” lets people explore an 18th century soundscape consisting of fictional stories inspired by historical events in order to facilitate affective engagements with this time area.

The above-mentioned examples show how site-specific experiences have the capacity to evoke affective engagements with history. In our work with LYDSPOR, we take this approach a step further by drawing on soma and affective design in HCI to bring focus both to human and more-than-human bodies in the past and the present.

2.2 Soma and Affective Design

Soma design is a method for doing design work that has found increased popularity in the HCI research community lately (e.g., [ 67 , 83 , 90 ]) and many have used it for developing artefacts and systems which articulate how technologies impact the human body. It is both a theoretical concept and a practical methodological approach. It has its roots in the interdisciplinary field of Somaesthetics [ 77 ], where the soma refers to the integration of body, movement, thought, and emotion, and aesthetics to our ability to engage with and deepen our understanding of sensory experiences. When engaging with soma design methods, researchers use their own bodies and experiences to slowly shape an interactive artefact or system, taking on all the roles: that of the designer, the researcher, and the end-user [ 50 ]. Starting from testing and reflecting on how particular combinations of physical and digital materials impact their bodies, researchers share first-person experiences among a research group, and invite other people to experience the developed prototypes, oscillating between first-, second-, and third-person perspectives [ 51 ]. Some examples of designs developed using this method are the Soma Mat and Breathing Light [ 84 ], aiming to support a meditative bodily introspection through light and heat, the Breathing Wings [ 88 ] and Breathing Shell [ 90 ], wearable systems which both explore different approaches to sensory experiences through shape-changing materials touching the wearer's body, and the Squeaky/pain [ 1 ] body-extension which increases somaesthetic awareness of chronic pain through movement and sound.

In our adaptation of soma design, we started from the human soma and then continued by exploring ways of extending the notion of ‘bodies’ to include other, more-than-human bodies, both in the soma design tactics used throughout our process, and in the interactive experiences that LYDSPOR evokes. Our engagement with sonic interaction design together with soma design employed sounds, vibrations and embodied listening as a way of emphasising the body as relational and permeable. To deepen this understanding of bodies in relation to the more-than-human, we have drawn on the way affect has been conceptualised in the ‘Affective Turn’ within the humanities [ 20 , 60 ] and how this has been explored under the heading of Affective Interaction Design [ 37 , 38 ]. Here, affect is being conceptualised as emerging relationally as “ those intensities that pass body to body (human, nonhuman, part-body and otherwise) ” [ 43 :1]. This line of thinking builds on Spinoza, who defines affect as “an ability to affect and be affected” [ 82 ]. According to Deleuze, a ‘body’ in Spinoza's philosophy can be anything: “ (…) it can be an animal, a body of sounds, a mind or an idea ” [ 41 :127]. Inspired by this proposition, we entered a soma design process with an idea of bodies as being always multiple and relational, and never only human. This entailed, among other things, conducting sensitising exercises commonly used in soma design to cultivate the somatic sensibilities of designers, and which we extended to attune to both human and more-than-human bodies in public space. It also meant working with sound and its capacity to move through and between different bodies, human and more-than-human, as well as evoking affective relations among them.

2.3 Sonic Interaction Design and Embodied Listening

Sound-driven research in HCI and design has, in the last years, moved into extending real sound environments [ 61 ] and exploring sound as an interactive experience in itself [ 8 ]. This includes the creation of soundscapes, artistic sound interventions and app-based soundwalks (see [ 54 ] for an overview). We have also seen the emergence of the field of sonic interaction design (SiD), which is the study of sound as a medium both conveying information, and aesthetic and emotional meaning in an interactive context. Practices include installations, performances, and objects integrating elements such as sensor-activated devices, embedded technology, geolocation and context-aware technologies in prototypes and public art [ 14 , 69 ]. Through these practices, objects, prototypes, and listening experiences, investigations into how sonic gestures which “ promote expressive and creative experiences ” are made [ 55 :x]. Concrete applications can be seen e.g., in project Zizi [ 7 ] where an affective relationship to furniture is explored, and in Recycled Soundscapes [ 33 ] which investigated how to design a public platform for active listening, recording and re-composition of urban sounds in public space. Within SiD there have also been recent explorations recently on how sound installations can augment the ‘spirit of place’ in public places [ 58 ] and historical sites [ 91 ].

SiD emphasises sound as a rich medium of expression for creating affective and emotional experiences that not only engage the ears but the entire body [ 69 ]. Here ‘embodied listening’ has been conceptualised as a multimodal experience, where the whole body perceives sonic signals [ 3 , 16 , 23 , 57 , 65 , 76 , 92 ]. This has been explored in relation to e.g. bodily movements such as micro-movements, walking and running [ 27 , 31 , 68 ] to support bodily awareness [ 10 ] and to elicit learnings on bodily movements and somatic techniques [ 32 ]. A technology which accentuates listening with the whole body is the use of sound transducers which emit vibrations through the material you attach them to, turning the material into a loudspeaker. Examples of interactive designs which use sound transducers include ListenTree, an audio-haptic display embedded in the natural environment, where listeners can experience a tree turned into a living loudspeaker [ 26 ] and the Humming Wall enticing people to interact through a vibrotactile and vibroacoustic interactive artefact placed in a public park [ 62 ].

In the case of LYDSPOR, we have furthered these investigations in how to create embodied listening experiences by exploring technology for creating interactive soundscapes which can be experienced while walking, as well as vibrations passing through material and human bodies.

3 THE DESIGNED LYDSPOR EXPERIENCE

In this section, we will provide an overview of the overall LYDSPOR experience, consisting of the two physical installations Sanden and Karmeliterhuset and of the Hestemøllestræde soundwalk, which links the two physical installations together.

3.1 The Sanden Installation

The Sanden installation was placed in Elsinore harbour close to the waterfront and directly opposite to the small street, Hestemøllestræde, in which Karmeliterhuset is located. This location provides a great view of the harbour, the local culture house (and city library), of Kronborg (the castle) and of the sea. It is also on a popular route which people take on their way to e.g., the city library or to Kronborg (Figure 2 ).

The story which inspired our work with the installation was that of ‘Sanden’ - an area of land situated between the mediaeval town centre and the Kronborg castle. In 1574, large-scale renovations were initiated by the Danish crown to transform the mediaeval fortress into the Renaissance castle of Kronborg that we know today. For this work, many Dutch and German artisans were hired. It also led to Danish and foreign merchants moving to Elsinore attracted by the opportunities for trading. Many of the immigrants settled at Sanden, which quickly developed into the wealthiest and most cosmopolitan part of town, also known as ‘Little Amsterdam’. This settlement was, however, demolished in 1658 by the Swedish army during the Dano-Swedish Wars of that time.

The installation was designed as an asymmetrical open wooden box approximately 120 cm by 185 cm by 60 cm, its shape inspired by the shape of the settlement. There was seating space all around the box and a step to get inside. Underneath the floor of the box and under the seats we mounted several large sound transducers (Figure 2 ). These were connected to an amplifier and an MP3-player, also hidden underneath the floor. A four and a half minutes long sound file was continuously looping, creating vibrations in the wooden structure, and delivering a soundscape that could be heard from a few metres away. The insides of the box contained sandy soil and a few plants. In this way, people were invited to interact with the box using their bodies in different ways. They could sit casually on the side of the box or engage more by stepping inside of it. They could stand on it to get a good view of the harbour and the sea or they could lie down to rest and to watch the seagulls above. The surface of the wood, the sandy soil and plants all welcomed touch, which was also emphasised by the vibrations that travelled through them.

The purpose of the installation was not just to tell the story of the rise and fall of ‘Little Amsterdam’, but rather to tell the story of the land. This means that our focus was just as much on the time before the settlement as well as after its annihilation, as it was on its development and heyday. The soundscape started with sounds from nature, such as insects, birds and the sounds of waves in the background. After a while, it shifted into the first sounds of human intervention, which consisted of turning the land into gardens for the wealthy inhabitants of Elsinore. This meant sounds of digging and planting and that of domesticated animals such as pigs and goats. Then the sounds of the building phase of the settlement could be heard and more and more human voices speaking different languages. To represent the heyday of ‘Little Amsterdam’, music from the 17 th century was played. This was followed by the deep tones from a storm bell warning the inhabitants of the upcoming battle. You could now hear people fleeing with their children. Finally, the whole box would shake with the vibrations of the heavy bombardment leading to the demolition of the settlements. After this, sounds of nature would return.

Our intention with this soundscape was to create an experience of zooming out rather than putting too much focus on specific historical events. We wished to highlight that the human activity on this land (the settlement) was a brief intervention (lasting roughly 100 years) into a much larger narrative which is not centred around humans. This was further emphasised by the plants that we chose to place inside the box. Apart from the grass, this was garlic mustard, plantain, dandelion and yarrow – what we today would call common weeds, but which are medicinal plants that were just as common in this part of the country hundreds of years ago as they are today.

3.2 The Karmeliterhuset Installation

This installation was placed on one of the external walls of the building in which the City Museum at Elsinore is currently located, at Hestemøllestræde (Figure 3 ). The old building was, before the Reformation, part of Our Lady Monastery, a monastery formed by the Carmelite order. The story that inspired the installation; the soundscape, and the overall experience evoked when interacting with it, is tied to the history of the building, and the fact that it has been used for many different things throughout the centuries. In the early 16 th century, the building was used as a hospital run by the monks for the town's many foreign sailors arriving in Elsinore from all over the world (until 1536). Then it was used as a horse mill, and later, from 1587, as a nobleman's residence. Finally, from the beginning of the 17 th century the building became one of the country's poor houses, and it remained so for more than 300 years. We found this diversity of the building's uses quite fascinating, covering a wide spectrum of the city's history as it unfolded in and outside this building. This includes both dramatic sides of history connected to illnesses and the poor, the mundane and everyday life unfolding in a horse mill and, at the other end the everyday life of aristocrats, who, according to historical archives, were throwing parties in the building.

Aiming to develop a site-specific experience with a focus on human and more-than-human bodies of the past, we intertwined those different historical times and uses of the building. We achieved this by creating a peephole experience of listening to what was going on inside the building at the time of the 16 th and 17 th century. The physical part of the installation consists of three wooden boxes of 17 cm by 17 cm by 17 cm each, all attached to a vertical wooden surface that was fastened on the outside wall of the museum. Each box has a transducer inside, which plays a 3-minute soundscape, inviting one to listen closely to historical sounds from inside the building. To interact with this installation, one needs to put one's ear right up against the box. This was a design decision made for creating a personal and to some extent intimate somatic and affective experience of zooming in and listening to the past life of the building extending from the inside to the outside, through the boxes.

The highest box (170 cm above ground) plays a soundscape connected to the mouth and the head of living beings that could have resided within the building, such as breathing, mourning, sighing, speaking, praying, singing, coughing, or laughing. The soundscape listened through the middle box (110 cm above ground) consists of a sound collage imagined as following the middle part of a body including hands, or more broadly actions hands might engage in, such as surfaces in which things are placed (food, medical equipment), a table where people eat, monks praying with pearls in their hands. Finally, the soundscape of the lowest box (70 cm above ground) consists of sounds connected to the feet of people or animals throughout the 16 th and 17 th century, and actions happening at the meeting between living beings and floors. These include, ​horses stamping (horse mill), coins or pearls dropping on the floor (from the monks using praying beads), rats running inside the building, people walking and wooden floor creaking, doors opening/closing.

3.3 The Hestemøllestræde Soundwalk

The Hestemøllestræde soundwalk consists of an app developed specifically for the project and fourteen small boxes mounted on the ground at the sidewalk of Hestemøllerstræde (Figure 4 ). Each box has an iBeacon transmitter inside, which is triggered by a Bluetooth signal. When using an app on a mobile device one can determine when the device has entered or left the location, along with an estimation of proximity to an iBeacon.

In the soundwalk, each iBeacon has its own narrative or signal sounds creating a dynamic soundscape, which is played once a listener enters its sonic zone. All iBeacons are connected in an intricate system which makes the transitions between one iBeacon to the next very smooth so that the Bluetooth signals enabling the narratives and background sounds were not lost or glitching as the listeners followed the soundwalk. This was a design decision made to create an immersive sonic experience where listeners were invited to continue to move freely all the way up and down Hestemøllerstræde. In the soundwalk listeners were specifically invited to sit on the bench alongside the road, walk next to the church wall or look at a sculpture placed there. Each part of the narrative would only be played once, enabling a sonically different experience every time the listener entered an iBeacon's sonic zone.

Hestemøllerstræde has been one of the main streets connecting the harbour area with St. Mary's Church in Elsinore and Our Lady Monastery. The soundwalk conveys the atmosphere of the street around the 16 th and 17 th century, so that listeners can better imagine how it felt living, working, and walking in the street during that time. We found stories of people who had walked and lived on the street and included them in the soundwalk via monologues, dialogues, and background sounds. The soundscape includes fishmongers, sailors, monks, workers, children, and people running errands on the street. We also gave sonic space to seagulls, the storm bell, horses and rats, as well as historical figures who had been living close to Hestemøllerstræde: the aristocrat Sophie Brahe, a horticulturalist with knowledge of astronomy, chemistry, and medicine, who lived in Elsinore at the beginning of the 17 th century, Henrik Smid, author of medical and herbal books in the 16 th century, and Mette Ebbis, called the ‘crutch bitch’, who was considered to be a witch and was burnt as part of the witchcraft trials in Elsinore in 1625 to 1626. Their stories present the frictions at the time between the belief in magic, science, and the Church during the 16 th and 17 th century.

In this way, the soundwalk is inspired by total theatre, a work of art in which all elements come together contributing to the overall sensorial and emotional effect created through the sonic experience, where the listener is immersed in the performance space. The listener explores the fragmented but interconnected scenes that are played out around them, and which include human and nonhuman actors. Stories are being told, not of the historical figures and big historical events, but of the street and its people, animals and things living and existing in it.

4 DESIGNING LYDSPOR

LYDSPOR was developed in a project-collaboration between the AirLab at the IT University of Copenhagen with long-term collaborator CATCH – Centre for Art, Design and Technology in Elsinore, and with the Municipality and the City Museum of Elsinore. We (the authors) came with a research agenda to bring together soma and affective design in the process of designing site-specific experiences. CATCH acted as facilitator to the process. The museum's motivation for participating was to explore both how to move the museum out in the city, and how new technology could be used for disseminating historical knowledge. Finally, the municipality had an interest in providing new experiences for both people living in the city and its visitors.

In January 2021, the design process started, and it ended with the project being exhibited in Elsinore in May 2022. Throughout the process, the whole project group would meet regularly to engage in exercises, discuss and to share knowledge. For the participating design researchers (the authors), it involved moving back and forth between the actual city of Elsinore and our lab to make sure that the designed experiences were rightly situated and tailored for the actual physical, cultural, social, and historical context. Through an iterative process of reading and discussing historical archives and exploring different locations in the city, the project group decided together which locations to use for the designs. The project group was expanded in the end of the design process, as the project entered more into production mode, by a dramaturgist, a sound designer, a programmer and a carpenter who were hired to work closely together with the design researchers to finalise the prototypes.

In the following account of our design process, we focus on highlighting insights that were gained from steps in our process that were crucial in helping us address our research questions. This includes carrying out sensitising sessions at the chosen locations in Elsinore, articulating design directions, as well as engaging in explorations of sonic augmentation and embodied listening through somatic, technological, and material investigations in the lab.

4.1 Opening up the Design Space: Attuning to Locations

In the first part of the design process, our aim was to get to know the sites in Elsinore. We developed a set of sensitising exercises progressing from a first-person to a collective experience. This involved attuning to our own bodies and to more-than-human bodies, such as plants, animals, and buildings. We conducted three sensitising workshops over a time span of 3 months, through which we progressively fine-tuned and adapted the exercises (for more details see [ 74 ]).

When we would meet – in the research team or with stakeholders – we would first focus on attuning to our own body through a 5-minute-long guided breathing meditation to ground ourselves in the present moment. The second exercise , lasting for 20 mins, was focused on attuning one's body to other living and non-living bodies in the urban surroundings. In the first version of this exercise, we experimented with extending our breath into different bodies. However, as we noted that it was more intuitive to connect through touch, we changed the exercise the second time it was performed. We noticed how this brought a close attention to small details in our surroundings such as experiencing the lichen growing on a concrete wall. In this way, the exercise developed into an exploration of zooming in on different nonhuman bodies through touch and noting the somatic and affective impact this had on our own bodies (Figure 5 ). This included bending down or squatting to come close to the ground, as well as using our whole body to lean against or to lie on benches, walls, statues, and other material bodies in the surroundings. Finally, the third exercise, lasting also for 20 mins, focused, as a contrast to the previous one, on zooming out and becoming attentive to larger structures in the environment, such as buildings, by using one's body to mimic these material bodies as well as imagining how they would experience this location with its particular material configurations, such as the wind, the seagulls, cars and people passing by. All exercises ended with time to reflect on our first-person experiences and to document them on an adapted version of a body map [ 5 ] including more space around the outlined body, prompting us to put more focus on the surrounding environment. This was followed by sharing our experiences as a group (recorded on video).

4.2 Articulating Design Directions Based on the Sensitising Workshops

From the sensitising workshops, it became clear that the exercises we had developed worked to unfold different layers of existing spaces, such as their stories as well as their sensorial qualities. It also helped us to develop a shared, bodily understanding across all participants, something which was later used to further unpack the locations we were aiming to design for. Overall, this prompted us to consider telling the untold and forgotten stories of the city through the perspective of more-than-human bodies. How can different bodies tell stories (e.g., moss, an old building, or a piece of wood)? What stories would they want to tell about the past? These were the type of questions that would emerge after the sensitising workshops. Additionally, exploring the urban space through various senses (e.g., sound, touch, movement), through the second sensitising exercise for example, allowed for new, unexpected affective relationships to develop between our human and Other, more-than-human bodies. This included curiosity, playfulness, sadness, and a sense of care for these locations and the different bodies existing there. Based on these experiences, we (the authors) started to articulate a number of design directions, setting the ground for the material explorations and prototyping phases that followed. These directions concretised our aim to develop site-specific designs with a focus on human and more-than-human bodies in the context of cultural heritage dissemination:

  • Bringing attention to the body by inviting participants/visitors to explore different ways of using their body, and to engage with the intended experiences by using their different senses.
  • Bringing focus to human and more-than-human bodies and their relationality by facilitating both physical and affective encounters between these bodies (i.e., by prompting participants to engage with small-scale as well as large-scale bodies existing in the locations through listening and touching).
  • Enabling new reflections to emerge and affective relationships to develop by integrating narratives about the past into present-day locations and by telling these stories from a more-than-human perspective (i.e from the perspective of the moss, or a fountain).

4.3 Explorations of Sonic Augmentation through Somatic, Technological and Material Investigations

Next, we (the authors) started exploring physical and digital materials in our research lab with a particular focus on sonic augmentation. The aim was to sensitise ourselves to a range of auditory experiences and through this identify how we could work with sound and an embodied approach to listening in ways that would allow us to follow our design directions. We decided early on to explore sound transducers as a potential method of achieving an embodied listening experience by transmitting sounds through different materials. We combined sound transducers with different materials including wooden surfaces and boxes, plastic and metallic sheets and textiles, and tested and reflected on the experience of listening through these material surfaces. The sounds we used for this exploration included both ready-made soundscapes found online, but also our own developed collages of sonic elements that we imagined could suit each installation based on our knowledge of the physical site and the historical content provided by the museum. For example, in relation to Karmeliterhuset this included the sounds of coughing, footsteps, doors opening and closing.

The transducers attached to a material surface acted as contact speakers, spreading the sound throughout that surface. By touching the surface with different body parts, the sound can be felt as vibrations travelling through the body. When the volume is low enough, one needs to lean in and touch the surface with the ear to hear it clearly. When the volume is high, one can hear the sound coming through an artefact/material to which a transducer is attached, without touching the surface. But when touching it one can also feel the sound as a vibration on the material surface. We continued to explore how a range of high-pitched and low-pitched sounds could be heard through sound transducers of different sizes, when attached to different materials of varying textures and thicknesses. Throughout these explorations, we reflected on the affective experience of different sonic-material constellations (such as the sound of coughing being transmitted as vibrations through a piece of wood) in relation to the somatic experience of placing the ear in contact with the material surface when bending the body low close to the floor (Figure 6 ) or when stretching the body to reach higher up. We shared and discussed our experiences of listening to and “feeling” sounds through different materials, including plastic, textile, metal, articulating the impact of such explorations.

We found that wood had the strongest effect on “feeling” the sounds through a transducer attached to its surface, since its density and material qualities spread the sound as vibrations in an even way throughout its surface. We also noted that hollow shapes, such as wooden boxes, have the capacity to create a more intense and vivid experience of listening to, and feeling a material surface when your body is in contact with it, producing subtle vibrations that can be felt locally or as spreading across the body. We also noted that body posture and movement/non-movement played a significant role in evoking different kinds of affective experiences when listening through material surfaces. Additionally, assuming different body postures, such as bending the body lower or extending it higher or sitting or stepping on a wooden surface, altered the experience of listening through a material and feeling the sound transmitted through your body. Finally, we discussed how concealed or revealed layers of sound, experienced when interacting with material surfaces, could enable us to enter ‘poetic spaces’ of experiencing a site and its history. Overall, these reflections and lessons informed our understanding of how to materialise our design intentions and how to approach sonic augmentation and embodied listening in the way we wanted.

The design process related to the interactive soundwalk part of LYDSPOR, also started out as a technological and somatic investigation in our lab focusing on sonic augmentation. By exploring possibilities of tracking one's bodily movements on a local wireless network and via GeoTracking, we found location-aware technology to be a suitable technology to work further with. After testing different technological options, we decided to work with iBeacons - Bluetooth-based devices which act as transmitters to detect and track smartphones. Our aim was to use this technology to enable responsive and ever-changing experiences of the narratives presented in the soundwalk. We tested and found that 14 iBeacons were needed for covering the area of the whole street in order to make transitions smooth without jittering or losing Bluetooth signal (Figure 7 ).

Through the app and the backend system, we connected a set of sounds to each iBeacon station. To allow for free movements up and down the street, we then made sure that the different narrative fragments (in the form of monologues or dialogues) would not be repeated. However, when trying out the experience, we realised that to fully “get a feeling for” the sonic flow of the soundwalk, we needed to test all 14 iBeacons together. Especially the length of the signal sounds and the pauses in-between them were important to test in relation to a moving listening body passing the iBeacons at a walking pace. In the start, we had created too long signal sounds of e.g., seagulls screaming, and incorporated too long time for the randomised pauses in-between the signal sounds, because we had imagined people moving slower through the soundwalk. As we found that the pace of the listening bodies would indeed be faster, we had to make adjustments so that the listeners would be able to experience all the different layers of the soundscape. We also worked on the sonic transition between each iBeacon to make the overlaps between the soundscapes connected to each iBeacon mix into each other, creating a fluent transition, leading the listener onwards from one narrative/atmosphere to the next in the soundwalk.

Our aim with the soundwalk was to connect listeners with the site-specific history that included both stories of historical figures such as Sophie Brahe and Mette Ebbis as well as sounds such as from church bells and horses, in order to enable reflections about the different beliefs, conditions and atmospheres present in the street during the 16 th and 17 th century (which might still be felt in the present-day). When we moved the testing of the soundwalk from the lab to the site-specific location of Hestemøllestræde we learned that the embodied listening experience of the historical narratives and soundscapes were quite different than we expected; the interplay between the narratives and soundscapes in the soundwalk connected better to some places along Hestemøllestræde than others. In the soundwalk we e.g., proposed a historical imagery of the street as a place closer to the harbour than what it is today, but the fact that the actual present surroundings were disconnected from the harbour by a road with cars and trams, made the soundscape with sounds of the ocean and seagulls less convincing, whereas the soundscape with church bells connected well with the church building located at Hestemøllestræde. The experience of the historical layer of the soundwalk was, in this way, very dependent on matching the sounds with meaningful physical locations.

5 INVITING OTHERS TO INTERACT WITH THE DESIGNS

The two installations and the soundwalk could be experienced in Elsinore from the 12 th to the 31 st of May and again from early July to 1 st of August in 2022. During this period, many people passing by the three locations had the chance to freely interact and experience LYDSPOR. Although we did not formally count the number of people interacting, from our own observations and from those of our stakeholders in Elsinore, several hundred people of all ages explored the Sanden installation. We also know that 156 people downloaded the soundwalk app on their phone.

We primarily gathered structured feedback during the first three weeks of display. In total, we had 21 participants, who were invited to test our designs and reflect on their experiences. Out of those people, 9 were part of a focus group we conducted, and 12 were part of a testing procedure focused on deep, individual engagements. We consider this as a type of user testing, or evaluation of our designs, focused on eliciting rich qualitative data on how participants engaged with the sonic experiences.

Out of the 12 participants involved in the individual testing sessions, there was an equal number who were citizens of Elsinore, and who were visitors to the city (mostly Danish and a few from other countries). The participants had answered a call to take part in our test session, which was advertised through social media and through our personal networks. Three of the participants were connected to the City Museum and had extensive knowledge of the history of Elsinore. The rest had varying degrees of knowledge of the historical events of the city. When it came to the specific historical contexts of the installations and the soundwalk, they were in most cases explained to the participants prior to the testing process. However, on a few occasions we chose to wait until after the end of the experiential sessions to introduce them. The reason for this was that we wanted to see if there would be a significant difference in how participants engaged with the designs depending on if they had prior knowledge of the history or not.

In the following, we present our approach and process of running these experiential sessions, followed by an elaboration on three themes that emerged from analysing the data gathered.

5.1 The Testing Approach and Process

When planning and structuring the process of inviting others to explore LYDSPOR, our aim was to create a testing procedure which would allow us to come back to our main research question: How can we develop site-specific designs with a focus on human and more-than-human bodies in the context of cultural heritage dissemination?. This question pointed to three areas that we wanted to unfold further as part of the testing: 1) site-specificity, 2) a focus on human and more-than-human bodies, and 3) dissemination of cultural heritage. Based on these directions, we created a testing procedure consisting of five stages which we believed could help us gain more insights into what types of experiences our designs fostered in relation to our three focus areas.

In the first two preparatory stages, before engaging with the installations or the soundwalk, participants were first invited to do a short breathing meditation exercise for 5 mins, and after this, they were invited to engage in ‘deep listening’ [ 64 ] – a practice developed by composer Pauline Oliveros intended to “expand consciousness” and create an awareness and attunement to the whole body and the environment of which it is placed [ 64 :xxiv]. For our testing procedure, we adapted Oliveros’ score “Energy Changes” from “Sonic Meditation” [ 98 ], and invited each participant to do this exercise, listening to the body as well as to the environment, for 10 mins. The purpose of these two preparatory stages was to enable participants to attune to their own body as well as other human and more-than-human bodies present at the specific locations, before engaging with our designs. We believed that this would strengthen the attention to different bodies as well as the site-specificity during the next stage of interacting with the sonic experiences, thus helping us to gather more detailed data in regard to these aspects of their experience.

In the third stage, participants were invited to freely engage with one of the sonic experiences for roughly 10 minutes, without talking. Following this, they were asked to capture the affective and somatic trajectories they went through during that session by filling out (still in silence) an adapted body map (printed out), using coloured felt pens. The reason for using body maps was both to gather data on complex and non-explicit emotions and felt sensations of the body that are not easily put into words [ 5 ], as well as to help the participants to focus on their body. In our version of the body map, we used five body outlines in a row. The first and the last representing the beginning and the end of the experience, and the three in between representing what was going on during the experience. Underneath each body outline was space to take notes on body postures and other reflections connected to what had been drawn on, in relation to the bodily figure (Figure 8 ). We also prompted participants to use the blank space surrounding each bodily figure to capture aspects of the surrounding environment that drew their attention.

Finally, we conducted a semi-structured interview with each participant, lasting for max 30 mins. The aim of this last stage was to invite them to articulate their impressions and reflections, using their body maps as a point of departure, as well as re-enacting body postures or showing particular details, when deemed necessary. The topics covered by the interviews were related to how the participants experienced the site-specificity of the designs, the historical soundscapes, the dynamics of their bodily awareness including their own body as well as of other more-than-human bodies.

5.2 Data Analysis

The material that we base our analysis and reflections on consists of the interviews, notes of participants’ interactions, as well as the body maps which were filled in by the participants. Overall, our approach towards analysing this data is situated in a qualitative paradigm. The interviews were recorded, transcribed, and analysed by taking a reflexive, inductive and constructionist approach to thematic analysis [ 15 ]. A reflexive approach means that “ meaning and knowledge are understood as situated and contextual, and researcher subjectivity is conceptualised as a resource for knowledge production ” [ 13 :334]. In this sense, the coding, as well as the conceptualisation of themes, have been an organic process starting from the authors familiarising themselves with the data, discussing it and looking for patterns. We started off by identifying codes which were relevant to our research questions. One researcher then completed the coding of the data. The result of this process was then discussed in the research group (the authors) and some modifications were made. The next step was to create themes based on the coded data. This was done iteratively by taking turns to analyse the coded data individually and coming together to discuss in the group.

A known limitation with using body maps is that they do not necessarily contain explicit information that can be easily interpreted or analysed [ 5 ]. We therefore treated them as a complement to the interviews with the role to help us deepen our understanding of participants’ somatic and affective experiences. Similarly, we used the notes from the observations, as well as participants’ own notes on their body maps, to complement the interviews and specifically to analyse how participants had interacted using their bodies (such as taking different body postures).

5.3 Themes Conceptualised from the Analysis

As we engaged with the data, we ended up creating three themes based on the analysis which pointed towards different aspects of participants' experiences with the designs which related back to our main research question. These were a) connecting the past and the present in the locations, b) extending bodily awareness through space and time, and c) playful encounters with more-than-human bodies. For the presentation of the themes, we are not using the participants’ real names.

5.3.1 Connecting the Past and the Present in the Locations. By sonically augmenting the existing urban environment, the experience of the everyday life of the harbour, as well as the small street leading up to the City Museum, was modified and extended in different ways. Soundscapes depicting past events of the city would blend with the well-known sounds of people and cars passing by, the cry of the seagulls, and the occasional ring of the church bell. Being exposed, in this way, to narratives of the past in the middle of (more or less) busy street life, led, perhaps not surprisingly, to people making connections between the past and the present in these locations – both emotionally and intellectually. We see this as a significant finding as it points to how the designs fostered site-specific encounters with cultural heritage by incorporating present-day material configurations of the locations along with both current understandings of, and historical narratives about them. For example, Mina, one of the participants experiencing the soundwalk, very clearly experienced the church in Hestemøllestræd as a connection between the past and present when listening to the church bells ringing both in the soundwalk and in the street at the same time. Experiences such as this led participants to re-encountering the present-day space around them and seeing it with “new eyes”. Anna, for example, expressed surprise at how walking up such a small street as Hestemøllestræde, could once involve a major shift in atmosphere. She expressed: “ It is what, 150 metres long? It's funny. I never thought about that. I never thought that so little geographical space could separate what was going on in the city very significantly. There's a big shift in atmosphere from everyday life of commerce and drunken sailors singing and shouting, to the clergy and the church where one is being quiet ”.

Nico, one of the participants who experienced the Sanden installation, makes a connection between the historical soundscape related to the location and a present-day warship. As he articulated: “ I don't know if that warship is always there (pointing at a ship in the harbour) but I was looking at it when the sound of the cannonballs came and I kind of liked the connection although it is more modern than this story of the Sands. ”

Participants would also connect current world events with the historical narratives they were experiencing. Most noticeably, this happened in relation to the harbour installation as this soundscape told the story of the bombardment of the city during the mid-1600s. Britt-Marie says, “ I thought about the people living here and bombs falling in 1658 and Ukraine came up as well and all the pictures you've seen on the television ”. She continues, “ I felt the anxiety or terror that people most feel when the bombs are falling ”. This was particularly interesting to us, as it pointed to how these connections made between the historical soundscapes and the present-day locations would sometimes lead to strong emotional reactions including empathy. The encounter with people's suffering in different times seems to have been a common entry point to these affective experiences. Ulla, who experienced the installation at the Karmeliterhuset , expressed it this way: “ The combination of the sound is somehow gruesome. It is the coughing of people inside...Or I don't know if it is that, but it is something that makes me really think about the smell... ”.

5.3.2 Extending Bodily Awareness Through Space and Time. With the two physical installations, we deliberately worked with transducers to carry the historical narratives into the body of the visitors. In addition to this, we designed the installations to invite different body postures. When it comes to the soundwalk, people were invited to move their bodies freely up and down the street, to sit down on a bench and to go close to the church wall. From the gathered data, we could see that these strategies brought about different types of bodily interactions with the designs and that this would lead to an increased bodily awareness regarding one's own body but also extending to other bodies in the present-day surroundings as well as to human and more-than-human bodies of the past.

First of all, the vibrations clearly had a significant effect on people's experience as they brought attention to different body parts when interacting with the installations. From both the interviews and the body maps, we could see that this included hands, feet, legs, buttocks, and the head. Ari, a young woman who experienced Sanden , mentioned, “ I think from this experience I realised I really like to use my hands, because I didn't feel so much vibration through my feet or other body parts. So, I think I used my hands a lot to feel the vibrations and the texture of the installation ”. Thinking back on his experiences with the same installation, Nico reflected on the vibrations and the effect they had on his body. He said: “A t some point there was a very strong vibration. It felt very different on my body. My hands had a very strong and high vibration while on my feet it was a bit more stable and then went into my buttocks. That was very deep as I was sitting on the installation, using it as a bench with my feet inside of it in the soil. It was very strange because I never realised before how the vibrations feel very different in different parts of the body ”. Several participants also reported attention to the stomach and the chest area as they reacted emotionally to the installations. Clapping his stomach, Olle, a retired physician, said: “ I felt it in my stomach because I know a bit about the war at that time and that it was really, really ugly ”.

Reflecting on her experience during the soundwalk, Siv says: “You don't see that when you walk past it normally, but then all of a sudden you are standing right next to the wall and you think: Oh okay, that's actually a really tall building. It made me think, how did people actually regard the church at that time? I think people were feeling very small. That was the feeling I got emphasised, in relation to the church, I am a small being in relation to all this grandeur”. This example shows that being exposed to the site-specific historical soundscape also enabled participants to become aware of their own body in relation to other bodies in their present-day surroundings, such as the church building, and that this fostered an awareness of and reflections about people's bodily experiences in the past related to the same situation.

5.3.3 Playful Encounters with More-than-human Bodies. One of our design goals was specifically to invite users to bring their attention to the more-than-human world both of the present and of the past. Therefore, we deliberately worked with the sounds of animals, objects, the wind and so on. We also worked with wood, soil and plants as a tangible connection between the past and the present. From the gathered data, we saw that this led to participants encountering more-than-human bodies in playful, enjoyable, and sometimes confusing or surprising experiences. For example, Nico, after interacting with the harbour installations mentioned: “ I bent over to touch the installation and I felt a slight vibration from the sound in my hands. I liked the feeling of the wood. I put my ear on the installation. It gave me a slight feeling that it was a tree, because of the feeling on my ear and there were nature sounds playing, so it made me feel like I am in nature ”. He later continued, “ I really enjoyed the soil. I felt it was a break from the surrounding tiles and concrete and it felt like I was stepping outside the area as it is now, but I'm still present because I can hear sounds and everything that happens around me ”. For Elizabeth, however, the same soil made her confused as there was a mismatch between her visual perception of the surrounding harbour and her bodily experience of the soil inside the installation.

For Stine, the soil and the plants were an important component of the harbour installation as it brought about playfulness. She says that if the installation “ had only focused on the part about the war, she would have felt disrespectful to be there, explore, use her body. It would feel like walking into a memorial ”. This playfulness can also be seen in the stories from participants who had unexpected encounters with non-human bodies. Lone, who experienced the Karmeliterhuset installation, explained: “ With the middle box it was interesting because from my position I was like, looking down (..) and so I could see all the little insects on the ground and it just fitted with my imagination, where the sounds are coming from, the place, this poor house and insects running around ”. Another participant, Sandra, had a similar experience when listening to the three boxes on the wall. She explained: “ It is really interesting how it forces your perspective. The first thing I did was to close my eyes, but then I opened them to see what I was looking at. With the lower one I was looking at the ground and there was a little bug crawling on the side of the wall, so I was thinking about that. (...) listening to footsteps (from the boxes) and coins dropping, it was like I was getting that bug's perspective ”.

Overall, we could see from the gathered data that facilitating encounters with the more-than-human world, in the specific locations for our designs, enabled unexpected and playful perspectives to emerge as well as affective encounters to occur both in relation to the more-than-human world existing at present in these locations as well as in the past.

6 DISCUSSION

Here we reflect on our design process, its outcome, and the findings that emerged from testing the final designs. We discuss our contributions, focusing on our approach to cultural heritage dissemination and how we have extended soma design to allow for broader understanding of the body which includes the more-than-human.

6.1 A Sonic, Bodily and Site-specific Approach to Cultural Heritage Dissemination

Early on in our design process we took the decision to work with non-linear and experimental forms of storytelling, for example by using sonic collages inspired by total theatre and sounds that could be experienced as vibrations travelling through the body. In this sense, we drew on previous work within HCI which embraces ambiguity and expressions that are “ evocative rather than didactic, and mysterious rather than explicit ” [ 39 :235]. When it comes to site-specific design in the context of heritage dissemination, this is a different approach than taken for example by Udsen and Halskov [ 91 ] which aimed to create an ‘authentic’ experience of what an historical site would have sounded like in the past. We used a more ‘poetic’ approach, which for us meant that the aim of our sound design was on being evocative rather than strictly historically accurate. This was grounded in our intention to bring attention to the body and its relationality. One interesting example, we believe, of how our work enabled new affective relations to emerge through a sensorial and evocative experience related to the past, was when participants experiencing the Sanden installation connected the bombardment of Elsinore during the 17 th century with the ongoing war in Ukraine (in 2022). Another example was when one of the participants of the soundwalk became acutely aware of the building of St. Mary's Church (from the 15 th century), how small it made them feel, and how they related this bodily experience back to how small people must have felt in relation to the power of the Church during the Middle Ages.

Dourish suggests that technology can “ provide opportunities to re-encounter and re-imagine everyday space ” [ 25 :6]. This became evident to us as the people who engaged with our designs became aware of their present surroundings by listening, sensing and moving their bodies through the space, as well as experiencing a form of ‘presentification’ of the past [ 45 ] in these locations. In other words, our designs worked to defamiliarize the sites and their histories, and we believe this allowed for participants to ‘re-orientate’ themselves in relation to the sites. As feminist scholar Sara Ahmed describes it, “ the work of inhabiting space involves a dynamic negotiation between what is familiar and unfamiliar, such that it is still possible for the world to create new impressions, depending on which way we turn, which affects what is within reach ” [ 2 :7]. Our designs made it possible to create new impressions both of the current locations (seeing them with “new eyes”) and of their historical context. This was accomplished by inviting participants to attend to bodies, their own as well as others, human and more-than-human, past and present.

Re-orientation, in this way, we believe, can lead to moments of epiphany as well as an experience of “being lost”. This became apparent during the test sessions we conducted. Some participants seemed to value the experience of new impressions (relating to past and present), because of how it brought them a sense of curiosity, playfulness, new reflections and affective engagements. On the other hand, other participants became confused and unsure of what to make of the experience – in short, they felt a little lost. This can perhaps be seen as a problem in the context of the dissemination of cultural heritage. However, we believe that there is a value in “getting lost” sometimes, as “ ‘getting lost still takes us somewhere; and being lost is a way of inhibiting space by registering what is not familiar: being lost can in its turn become a familiar feeling ” [ 2 :7]. Cultivating a familiarity with being lost, can be seen as an important strategy in the striving to dismantle the Grand Narratives that have dominated our understanding of history for so long. From this perspective, it does not only matter which stories are being told, but equally important is how they are being told [ 46 , 70 ]. We consider this important for developing a type of auditory and sensorial experience of history, where the focus on different body parts, plants, animals, technologies, and so on, can contribute to making somatic and affective connections between “bodies in the past'' and “bodies in the present”.

6.2 Extending Soma Design: From Human to More-Than-Human Bodies

We have grounded our process, both from a design perspective and theoretically, in an extended notion of bodies, by combining soma and affective design methods and theories [ 37 , 50 ]. Soma design provided us with methods to sensitise ourselves both to the locations we chose for our designs and to the different materials and interactions we worked with during the design process. It also helped us to continuously reflect on and come back to the somatic impact of different technologies and experiences being developed throughout our process. The philosophical foundation drawn from Spinoza's concept of affect, on the other hand, prompted us to consider bodies as something beyond the human and to extend our understanding of what a body is, or could be, towards the relational, in-between and more-than-human [ 41 , 44 ]. Arguably, this is different from how the body is typically addressed in soma design, where the starting point is often the individual body and a first-person perspective [ 87 ]. Even if, in soma design, first-person perspectives are also complemented by second-, and third-person ones [ 51 ], it is not common to extend the approach to bodies beyond the human (i.e., to question what a body is) and to make openings for designing through focusing on relationality and collectivity.

We believe that our initial decision to work with sound helped to further emphasise our affect-oriented approach. Using transducers that emit vibrations was a way of exploring sound as a movement of energy that can move through and between different materials and bodies. Sound, indeed, is a form of ‘vibrational affect’ [ 40 ] that creates resonance. In our case, this resonance was not only experienced, for example, through the wooden surfaces when physically interacting with the two installations, but it was also something that connected our human bodies of the present to human and more-than-human bodies of the past. In other words, the designed soundscapes, which were listened to and felt as vibrations, enabled affective connections between bodies in a broader temporal spectrum, extending from the present to the past.

Prior soma design research has mainly focused on the impact of tactile sensations and interactions with materials (e.g. [ 71 , 80 , 88 ]). Sound has been considered as a modality that directs one's focus away from the body to the surrounding space [ 10 ]. However, our research indicates that the relational and vibrational aspects of sound have the potential to evoke strong somatic sensations as well as creating felt relations between human and more-than-human bodies. We see this reflected in participants’ reflections and in their filled-out body maps (Figure 8 ), where the drawings often simultaneously display intensified (human) bodily experiences, as well as the dissolution of the bodily boundary and the extension of it into the environment.

The more-than-human perspective also directed our attention away from dominant, person-centred narratives and instead towards living and non-living bodies. Following Oogjes and Wakkary, this is a practice of ‘designing-with’ the more-than-human, aiming to displace a human-centric approach [ 66 :1]. For example, we worked with soil and plants in one of the installations ( Sanden ), with the sensory qualities of historical buildings (soundwalk) and with natural elements such as stones, moss, and insects on the ground ( Karmeliterhuset ). From the findings, we see how these elements played an active part in shaping people's experience when interacting with LYDSPOR, resulting in new affective relations emerging across multiple bodies; human, material (physical and digital), sonic and others. On the other hand, we can also identify instances where the intention to open up more-than-human worlds and connections was not necessarily a part of the participants’ experience. For example, inviting people to experience the plants growing on the piece of land called ‘Sanden’ or directing the visitors’ gaze to the ground ( Karmeliterhuset ) left some participants confused or simply untouched. This calls for future explorations of ‘designing-with’ the more-than-human, and for finding better ways of displacing a human-centric approach in the design space.

To conclude, we have been inspired by Homewood et al.’s [ 49 ] suggestion to explore what bodies participate in the design process, and how to make openings for other, more-than human bodies. Our work to extend the notion of the human body in soma design, from an affect-oriented perspective, contributes to this call. We therefore believe that we, in this paper, offer an example of a project that explored ‘de-centering as a horizon’, which Livio and Devendorf [ 59 :10] articulate as “ an orientation (…) to guide movement that is concurrently understood to remain continually beyond reach ”. This means that, as design researchers, we should acknowledge and involve more-than-human entities in our work, at the same time as we continuously reflect on and question whether our projects have succeeded in de-centering the human perspective. In this context, experimental approaches which are not entirely successful in that aim can provide as much useful information as those which simply perform as expected. In our research, we have taken initial steps towards a more-than-human approach to soma design methods in HCI, which can now be built upon. We believe this is a future design orientation that has much to offer and which we believe should be further explored.

7 CONCLUSION

In this paper we have presented the design process and the final outcome of LYDSPOR, a site-specific sonic experience, including two physical installations and a soundwalk, developed for the city of Elsinore in Denmark. Our aim with this project was to create an experience that would allow people to get a bodily sense of historical narratives related to certain locations in the city; the harbour, a historical building and the street connecting the two. Moreover, we wanted to focus on the body as relational and more-than-human. In order to achieve this, we have built upon and adopted concepts and methods from soma and affective design as well as knowledge and practices from sonic interaction design and the design of cultural heritage experiences. This paper contributes to these different research trajectories within HCI, by giving insights from and discussing our design process, its outcome and the reception of LYDSPOR. We have offered this in order to prepare the ground for future experiments in and across HCI and design. As future work, we would like to further explore approaches to combining soma and affective design, aiming to attune to the more-than-human. We believe this could potentially lead to a deeper connection with urban spaces and city environments as commons, creating collective affect of care and a close kinship with the nature-culture.
