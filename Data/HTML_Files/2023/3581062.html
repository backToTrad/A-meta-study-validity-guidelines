At First Light: Expressive Lights in Support of Drone-Initiated Communication

Jessica R. Cauchard , Magic Lab, Department of Industrial Engineering and Management, Ben Gurion University of the Negev, Israel, jcauchard@bgu.ac.il

CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Drones are increasingly used in situations where they can assist a person. However, people are not familiar with drones approaching them. We propose an expressive light system embedded on a drone to convey its intention to initiate communication, which we present as a four-stage process: Drone-Initiated Engagement Model (DIEM) . We then describe the design and development of an LED-based prototype divided in two configurations with a total of 26 light animations, and report their evaluation in an online survey ( N = 156). We describe the suitability of different configurations, animations, and colors to convey each stage of DIEM . We finally validate our system in a user study ( N = 45) that showed that participants can perceive all four stages of approach via the drone movement and that expressive lights provide a more nuanced user experience. We contribute insights into the design of expressive light systems, as a stepping stone towards machine-initiated communication.

1 INTRODUCTION

Drones are being used in an increasing number of domains [ 31 , 34 ] , where they can assist, not only users but also bystanders. For example, in a public space a drone can provide people with information in real-time [ 44 ]. Also, in emergency response [ 2 ], and Search and Rescue (SAR) missions [ 53 ] in particular, drones can directly help victims, such as by establishing a communication network between them and the rescuers [ 39 ]. To do that, the drone needs to be able to initiate communication with a victim, since we cannot expect a victim or bystander to be the initiative party, due to a lack of ability, awareness, or intention. The field of Human-Drone Interaction (HDI) has started to investigate ways for an autonomous drone to communicate with a person [ 31 ]. However, these mechanisms traditionally focus on humans initiating communication.

To study how a drone should initiate interaction to establish communication with individuals, we build upon research in Human-Human Interaction (HHI) that modeled how people engage with each other [ 35 ] and subsequent works in Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) that proposed a recognition process between human and machine interactors prior to the interaction. Different methods have been proposed, such as using the robot's body orientation and gaze [ 4 ], however, these methods cannot be directly transferred to drones that present different features from ground robots and that are traditionally non-anthropomorphic in nature. Although the HDI literature presents some best practices in drone approach strategies [ 19 , 56 ] and in drone conveying acknowledgment and intent through drone gestures and movement [ 33 , 51 ], currently, to the best of our knowledge, no best practices have been proposed for a drone fully initiating communication. Based upon Kendon's 6-phase model for HHI [ 35 ], which is also widely used in the HRI literature [ 4 , 30 , 33 ], we define the Drone-Initiated Engagement Model (DIEM), which consist of four stages for a drone initiating communication: 1) Searching, 2) Spotting, 3) Approaching, and 4) Ready to Interact. To support these stages (e.g., when a drone needs to engage with a person), we investigate the use of expressive lights, a visual feedback modality. Expressive lights are animated light displays created by controlling both color and brightness of small programmable lights (LEDs). They have previously been used to express mobile robots and devices’ states [ 6 , 29 , 36 ], to help in anticipating the trajectory of a drone [ 52 ], and also to support collaboration between people and drones [ 17 ].

We describe the design process and implementation of At First Light: an Expressive Lights system to support drone-initiated communication (Figure 1 .a). We further created light animations ( n = 26) to elicit the four different states that the drone can be in while engaging with a person. We evaluated the correspondence of the designed expressive lights to the states via an online survey ( N = 156). Our results show that, for each of the four states of the drone, some animations illustrate the states better than others. At First Light was then validated in an outdoor user study ( N = 45) comparing two expressive light configurations with a baseline (Figure 1 .b) to add ecological validity to our initial results.

Our findings suggest that expressive lights on a drone can convey different pieces of information to bystanders. Additionally, we sought the optimal animation for each state and found strong and iconic animations as well as color preferences. We conclude with guidelines for the design of expressive light interfaces on drones. Furthermore, with an emphasis on the stages of initiating an interaction with a person, we demonstrate how expressive lights can be used to visualize the four different states of interaction. Our work contributes in the following:

  • Formalization of DIEM: a 4-state model for drone-initiated communication.
  • Design, development, and evaluation of At First Light: an expressive light system for drones.
  • Insights on the use of expressive lights to support drone-initiated communication.

2 RELATED WORK

The use of drones is becoming ubiquitous, and as their autonomy increases, people encounter them in an increasing number of situations, where drones may need to communicate with people. For instance, after an earthquake, a rescue drone can gather data by communicating with bystanders and those in need. In such situation, bystanders will most likely interact with a drone for the first time, and so the drone must be able to communicate its intentions and current state to prevent people from being scared of it [ 23 ]. We here focus on human-drone first encounters, and more specifically, on the drone initiating communication. Prior work shows that an interaction initiated by a robot might fail if no proper strategy is followed [ 43 ]. And so, HRI researchers have adopted human-human communication models to support interactions between humans and robots [ 30 , 43 , 46 ]. Yet, it is not clear whether existing HRI models can be applied to aerial robots that present different capabilities and form factors. According to prior work in HHI, communication begins with a definite structure [ 35 ] where proximity also plays a role [ 25 ]. We here describe engagement models used in HHI & HRI and then describe prior work in HDI.

2.1 Engagement in Human-Human & Human-Robot Interaction

An engagement between two agents corresponds to when “interactors start, maintain, and end their perceived communication with each other” [ 47 ]. The process of initiating interaction focuses on the start of the engagement, which is before, or just as, the interactors share the belief that the interaction begins [ 45 ]. Considering Figure 2 , it is the process of going from (a) to the start of (b). This process combines both verbal and non-verbal behaviors to support the perception of the connection between interactors. The non-verbal channel can be used to signal that they have seen each other and to provide information about what an interactor is intending on doing and evidence for their presence in the interaction [ 47 ]. Kendon [ 35 ] identified a series of greeting behaviors humans follow when meeting a person. This model was later used in HRI for initiating communication by a museum robot [ 58 ] and was also fully implemented on a humanoid robot [ 30 ]. A taxonomy was recently published [ 4 ] summarizing prior research on human-robot first encounters and compared robotic systems to Kendon's six phases model, which we describe below:

  1. Sighting and Orientation. The greetings procedure begins with either one of the interactors recognizing the other as someone they want to greet, and assessing their availability to interact. The decision is then made whether to initiate the greeting or not. If the target's attention has not been caught, the initiating interactor attempts to get it by either calling a name, coughing, making gestures, or turning toward them.
  2. Distance Salutation. Following the decision to greet, a signal is made to indicate that it has been initiated, such as: hand wave, head toss, nod, or smile. These can be combined or accompanied by vocalizations. If the greeting is only passing and there is no intention to further interact, the distance salutation ends the greeting process.
  3. Approach. During this phase, either both interactors move towards each other or just one. The approach might begin with a head dip. Then, during the actual approach, the interactors are likely to engage in several behaviors, such as looking away (moving their gaze away from one another), crossing their arms, and grooming.
  4. Final Approach. When both interactors are within approximately 3 meters of each other, their gaze focuses on one other and they might be smiling, tilting their heads, or presenting their palms.
  5. Close Salutation. When the approach reaches a < = 1.6 m distance, interactors greet each other verbally, non-verbally, or both. Examples include handshakes, kisses, hugs, and bows; depending on cultural customs.
  6. Transition. This final greeting phase is the transition to the communication itself. The interactors adjust their distance to each other, which is most likely determined by the familiarity between them [ 25 ].

In contrast to ground robots, aerial robots have wider maneuvering spaces. They can approach humans from above or hover in place, they also generate noise and usually have different visual properties and appearances than ground robots (e.g., propellers, no arms or legs). Thus, some elements of engagement might differ for drones, and we propose to adapt Kendon's model to HDI. Next, we describe the literature on drones initiating interactions.

2.2 Initiating interaction in HDI

To compare the current state of the art on drone-initiated interactions, we use Kendon's [ 35 ] model as a taxonomy by which we evaluate the work done so far. This approach is similar to the one presented in prior taxonomy on first encounters in HRI [ 4 ]. Relevant papers from HDI are summarized in Figure 2 . Jensen et al. [ 33 ] followed Kendon's model and implemented greetings for a drone. They focused on the first two phases (i.e., Sighting and Distance Salutation) and referred to them as creating an ‘acknowledgement’ between a drone and a person. By using the orientation and gestures performed by the drone through movement (e.g., nod), they were able to convey a feeling of acknowledgement to study participants. They found that the preferred distance to be acknowledged was 2m. Yet, people could also feel acknowledged from a 4m distance. Since their study featured drone gestures from both far (4m) and close (2m) distances, we also consider them appropriate for Close Salutation (phase 5). Monajjemi et al. [ 37 ] implemented a ‘wobble’ gesture to notify a user that waves towards the drone that is has spotted them, which corresponds to Distance Salutation. A follow-up study [ 38 ] featured a far-to-near situated interaction between a drone and a human (phases 2-4), using a LED strip on the drone to signal the person it was spotted. They further used different light animations during the approach and the interaction with the person (e.g., ‘timer’ animation when the drone took a selfie). Since the human is the one to initiate the interaction in this study, the light behaviors do not apply to Sighting (phase 1). Also, when the drone reached the person, it stopped and waited for them to perform a hand gesture, thus, not conveying a Close Salutation or Transition (phases 5 and 6).

In our literature search, we did not find prior work with drone approaching behaviors that are similar to what is suggested for approaching (phases 3 and 4). However, Szafir et al. [ 51 ] have used flight paths that succeeded in communicating drone's intent to approach a person and Cauchard et al. [ 15 ] showed how the drone's trajectory can convey emotions. Other strategies for approaching humans by drones have been investigated [ 56 ], describing the best approach angle (front), direction (straight), speed (0.5 m/s), and stop distance (1.2 meters away from the person prior to interacting). Similar proxemics preferences were also observed in another study [ 57 ] which used audio feedback at the end of the drone's approach. Kendon suggests that in Transition (phase 6), the interactors will adjust their final distance from each other based on their familiarity. Thus, these results can be used to determine the final distance of the drone, after it performs a close salutation. To conclude, we could not find prior work that addressed all stages of initiation of communication. The closest work for a full approach was the one by Monajjemi et al. [ 38 ]. However, their research did not focus on initiating the interaction by the drone, as the user is the one to initiate the interaction. Furthermore, there was no empirical test of what the participants understood from the drone's signals. Thus, we offer to bridge this gap in our work, by adapting Kendon's full greetings model to HDI research.

3 DRONE-INITIATED ENGAGEMENT MODEL

Heenan et al. [ 30 ] implemented non-verbal behaviors and gestures for a humanoid robot to initiate communication by simulating human greetings. Our goal is to implement a similar process on a drone. While their approach was to mimic the phases and gestures humans use when engaging with each other, we had to make adjustments for drone-initiated communication. Indeed drones are different in shape, form, and behavior from humanoid robots. Thus, drone's non-verbal vocabulary is more limited and may not enable the full range of expression required to describe all of Kendon's greeting phases. As such, we narrow down the spectrum of phases and derive the drone-initiated engagement model (DIEM) , a 4-state model for drone to initiate communication : Searching, Spotting, Approaching, Ready to Interact (Figure 2 ):

Searching . This state corresponds to a pre-engagement (phase 0). Since a drone cannot just be idle like a ground robot, this state has to be included in the greeting. As our working scenario is a search and rescue drone, the pre-greeting state in searching for a person. The searching state is when the drone is trying to track a missing person, or when looking for a bystander to obtain information from.

Spotting . The spotting state represents the first two phases of greeting: Sighting and Distance Salutation. Since drones also generate noise, we assume that people will be aware of their presence and thus combined Sighting and Distance Salutation together, as was also done in previous work [ 33 ]. This state is activated once the drone has identified a person. The drone then indicates to the person it has spotted them and wishes to communicate.

Approaching . The drone approaches its target. This corresponds to the third and fourth greeting phases: which are Approach and Final Approach. We believe the two approach stages are too hard to distinguish for a person looking at the drone from afar, and thus combined them into one state.

Ready to Interact . This state represents the final two phases of greeting. We merged Close Salutation with Transition since, in Human-Human and Human-Robot interactions, a close approach would happen within the private space and then move back out of it [ 30 ]. However, in HDI such interaction would draw safety concerns, and prior work has shown that people's comfort decrease when the drone gets too close to them [ 56 ]. Thus, we suggest the drone should always remain within a comfortable distance from the human and therefore combined the phases. In this state, the drone will indicate to the person that it has completed its approach and that it is ready to start communicating.

4 AT FIRST LIGHT - PROTOTYPE

Our goal was to implement a non-verbal signaling system in support of DIEM . To do so, we propose to research the validity of using expressive lights on a drone. As a preliminary step, we built a system and designed light animations that could convey information. We first discuss the design principles that we employed, and why we chose expressive lights as our solution. Then, we present our design and implementation process.

4.1 Design Principles

Here we present the key design features for a modality to convey the initiation states presented in the previous section.

  • Modality. The most important principle is the ability to convey information to novice users and bystanders. This means, we should assume that when a SAR drone will approach a victim or bystander, they may not be familiar with the technology and may have no prior knowledge of the drones’ abilities and intents. Drones are appearance-constrained flying robots, and are limited in their non-verbal communication options [ 10 , 49 ]. Thus, it is crucial to have a rather natural modality, that side viewers would be able to understand without any training.
  • Visibility. A SAR drone might be operating in harsh conditions. Thus, we looked for a modality that would perform well in varying lighting and weather conditions.
  • Directionality. Since drones fly and have a high degree of freedom in their movement, understanding and predicting their trajectory can be difficult. Jensen et al. [ 33 ] suggested making drone's front recognizable, since the drone's orientation can help convey a feeling of acknowledgment.
  • Integration. We investigated technical solution that could be fitted on most drones.
  • Lightweight. Small drones flight times vary between 10 minutes to no more than 1 hour [ 20 , 21 ]. Any additional weight added on to the drone could significantly reduce its flight time and stability. Thus, it is important to not overload the drones and investigate lightweight solutions.

Based on these design principles, we found lights to be the right feedback modality. We propose expressive lights, which are animated light displays created by controlling both color and brightness of small programmable LEDs [ 26 ]. Lights are being broadly used to convey all types of information. They are used as simple indicators such as if a device is on or off, to show a battery charge state, the braking of a car, and in many everyday devices. Thus, they should be familiar to all. Previous work in HCI and HRI has succeeded to convey information with LEDs without any training, such as the informational state of a mobile device [ 29 ], smart speakers [ 36 ], and mobile robots [ 6 , 41 ], with the usage of expressive behaviors of the lights and colors. Ibrahimov et al. [ 32 ] suggested using LEDs on drones to communicate information through light-paintings in the sky. Lights have been also used to convey an appearance-constrained robot's gaze [ 50 ], drone emotions [ 3 ], directionality [ 52 ], and also to support human-drone collaborations [ 17 ].

Most of the non-verbal communication between humans and robots is achieved through body gestures and orientation, and prior research has succeeded in using movement for HDI [ 11 , 15 , 18 , 23 , 24 , 33 ]. However, since drones are more compact, identifying only movement in bad lighting conditions or from a distance can be harder to recognize. A major advantage of LEDs is their ability to be seen during daylight as well as in the dark [ 54 ]. Furthermore, they are visible from a distance, which makes them more versatile than sound or other visual modalities such as movement or screen displays [ 7 , 9 ]. Considering the suggestion to make the front of the drone recognizable [ 33 ], lights could be configured accordingly. By putting the LEDs on the front of the drone, viewers will understand if the drone is looking towards their location or somewhere else. Additionally, Szafir et al. [ 52 ] work showed that a LED strip on a drone can help nearby viewers predict the drone's path direction.

4.2 Hardware Prototype

We designed and assembled two configurations of lights ( Strip and Rings ) mounted on the front of the drone using 3D printed parts (Figure 3 ). The strip was inspired by prior work with drones [ 38 , 52 ] and also from research with expressive lights in robotics [ 5 , 22 , 41 ]. Since previous work demonstrated the role of anthropomorphism in people's perception of drones [ 55 ], we suggest a second configuration that features two LED rings and resembling eyes. Our design was influenced by the importance of gaze in prior HHI and HRI works [ 1 ], as well as prior findings that gaze can improve drone capabilities to communicate [ 40 , 52 ]. We implemented the expressive lights system on a DJI Mavic 2 Enterprise Dual, a common commercial drone widely available and used in previous research [ 16 ]. We built a self-reliant system and embedded it on the drone without affecting its flight control. The system's total payload is only 87.4g for the Strip and 71g for the Rings configuration. We used Adafruit NeoPixels programmable LEDs 1 : The strip was positioned between two legs, resulting in a 30-LED strip; and the rings fit exactly around the front sensors without impeding the propellers’ movement, resulting in two 12-LED rings. These are controlled by an Arduino Nano IOT 33 micro-controller with a built-in Bluetooth communication module for the remote control of the light animations. A lithium battery (3.7V, 1200mAh) powers the system. As for the serial communication, the LEDs are connected to the Arduino's digital pins, with either the strip or each ring, connected to one pin. A 470Ω resistor is added between the data pin and the data input on the NeoPixels. For the assembly, the key features were to not interfere with any of the drone's sensors, spreading the weight evenly, and achieving a minimum weight of the total payload.

4.3 Software Prototype: Light Animations

Every single LED in the array (strip or rings) can be controlled individually through the Arduino in brightness and color (RGB value 0-255). We designed animations with different light patterns and speeds using C ++ and used a mobile application to switch between animations. Thirteen animations were implemented on both Strip and Rings, with minor differences between configurations, resulting in a total of 26 animations. Due to the complexity of the rhythms and speeds of each animation, we illustrate them in Figure 4 and open-sourced the code 2 . We aimed for a diverse set of animations to find suitable ones for all states. Some animations were taken from or inspired by prior work [ 5 , 29 , 38 , 52 ], others were generated by our team. Animations are divided into two groups: (I) All the LEDs act as a single light source (e.g., all blink together); (II) Different LEDs are lit to create motion (e.g., a small array of light that moves along the strip - Chase (Figure 4 )). Moreover, we designed a basic color display for both configurations, where every LED in the array is lit up in the same color. We implemented six colors (see Table 1 ) based on prior work [ 5 ]. We found it important to test color alone as prior work showed that color alone can convey different meanings of a robot's state [ 41 ].

5 ONLINE STUDY

We aimed to determine which animations, color, and LED configuration would be most suitable for each drone state.

5.1 Method

We ran an online study and used video and image stimuli for the evaluation. This enabled us to reach a large and diverse group of participants to determine which animations best reflect the drone's states [ 5 , 29 , 41 ]. For the rest of this section, we describe the recruitment and participants, stimuli, study variables, and procedure.

5.1.1 Recruitment. We used Amazon's Mechanical Turk crowd-sourcing platform and limited the study to English-speaking countries so that language would not affect our results. Workers were selected with a HIT Approval Rate $\ge 97\%$ and Approved HITs ≥ 100. Participation in the study took on average 15.5 minutes ( SD =13.3), and participants were paid up to $ 2. We implemented two reliability checks to ensure high levels of answer integrity: a control question during the study and a minimum time to ensure participants took the time necessary to answer the survey.

5.1.2 Participants. A total of 176 people took part in the study. Participants who failed at least one of the reliability checks were removed, which resulted in 156 participants: 73F, 80M, and 1NB participants, as well as 2 participants who preferred not to answer ( μ =43.4 y.o., SD =9.6) included in the analysis of the results. The large majority (97%) had seen a drone prior to the study and 12% had piloted one before.

5.1.3 Stimuli. We video-recorded the 26 animations while the drone was hovering outdoors. We kept the drone hovering and decided not to include movement as this could influence people's perception of the animations. All animations used the same brightness and blue color (0, 0, 255) as it is considered neutral [ 5 , 52 ] and as it should not have a strong connotation with the state of the machine [ 41 ]. All videos were filmed within an hour to ensure similar luminosity and weather conditions. They were then trimmed to be of a similar length in time, with periodic and constant animations lasting approx. 5 sec, and non-periodic animation videos trimmed to fit one animation cycle (4-10sec). Since we could not control whether the videos would be watched with or without sound, we removed the audio track.

5.1.4 Study Variables. We varied three independent variables: 26 animations were presented as video stimuli (Figure 4 ), representing thirteen animations in two configurations ; as well as six colors (Table 1 ) that were presented as still images. We opted to not only look for suitable animations for each state but also to uncover color preferences. We tested color separately from animations, building on prior work [ 5 ] and to avoid interactions between variables.

5.1.5 Procedure. Participants read and signed an electronic consent form. They were warned that the study contains videos of flashing lights at different speeds that may generate discomfort. Note that participants were not put at risk as a flash is not a potential threat unless it occurs at a frequency greater than 3Hz and occupies 25% of the screen at normal viewing distance or if it transitions from or to saturated red [ 27 ]. Since the LED display only occupies approx. 2.5% of screen real estate and we do not transition between colors, participants would at most experience discomfort. Participants then filled in a demographics questionnaire including questions about their previous experience with drones. They then watched two short video clips demonstrating human-drone interaction, one in a SAR context and the other showing a demonstration of the four DIEM states. The participants then reviewed 4 random animations of either Strip or Ring configuration and then another 4 animations (8 total) from the second configuration, the order was counterbalanced. The purpose of showing each participant 8 animations instead of all 26, was to keep the study short and avoid fatigue. This method has been successful in prior online studies [ 28 , 29 ]. The animations were presented one at a time and after each one, participants were asked to rate on a 5-point Likert-scale how strongly they agreed or disagreed that the animation corresponded to each of the four states of the drone. Participants were also given the option to elaborate on their ratings, especially if they ‘strongly agreed’ with one or more of the states. Note that the names of the animations were not displayed so as to not bias participants’ answers. After rating the eight animations, participants were asked to choose their preferred color of animations for each state of the drone. After that, participants were asked to rate their perception of the drone in each configuration [ 55 ] along 8 dimensions of perception.

5.2 Data Analysis

We performed a Shapiro–Wilk test to check if the correspondence means within each animation and states are normally distributed. Since this was not the case, we chose non-parametric statistical tests. To determine whether the correspondence means for each state in every animation is significantly different from every other state, we performed a Wilcoxon signed-rank test, with p -values adjusted using Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction to control for False Discovery Rates (FDR). All tests discussed as demonstrating statistically significant results have a p -value < 0.05.

5.3 Results

We here report the results of the study. We start with the results of the animations analysis, then present the results of the color preference, and lastly discuss the results of how the drone was perceived for the two configurations.

5.3.1 Animations – State Correspondence. To uncover the best animation for each state, we followed Harrison et al. [ 29 ]’s strong and iconic definitions for the representation of expressive lights. We looked for animations that met the following two conditions: 1. a mean score for correspondence to a state ≥ 3.5 out of 5 ( strong ); and 2. a significant difference with all other states, so that the animation is suitable for a particular state and not suitable for any other ( iconic ). In the rest of this work, we will use the terms strong and iconic to assess animations. Figure 5 shows a summary of the mean ratings of the 13 animations for each of the four states in both configurations. Table 2 summarizes all comparisons with a significant difference. Fourteen (14) of the 26 animations had a state with a strong result: 7 in the Strip configuration and 7 in the Rings configuration. Nine (9) animations had one state that was significantly different from all other states within the animation ( iconic) : 5 in Strip and 4 in Rings configuration. Eight (8) animations met both criteria of strong and iconic (highlighted in gray in Figure 5 ). The searching state has the highest number of both strong and iconic animations. In the Approaching and Ready to Interact states, no animations appeared as either strong or iconic. Next, we detail the results of each state individually. Figure 6 summarizes the results.

Searching . For the Strip configuration, 4 animations are strong and iconic: BackForth , Chase , Fade , and Decent with respective mean ratings ( μ ) of 4.3, 4.1, 3.7, and 3.6; and one is iconic only: ProgressBar . By definition, the strong animations are rated with the highest agreement for this state. For the Rings configuration, Chase , Switch , and BackForth animations are both iconic and strong, with respective μ of 4.1, 3.8, and 3.7.

5.3.3 Perception. We compared the results of the perception questionnaire for both configurations. The Shapiro-Wilk test performed showed that the data was not normally distributed, so we report a Wilcoxon signed-rank test, used to check for significant differences between the configurations for each dimension. All dimensions showed significant differences as presented in Figure 8 . Our results show that the Rings configuration was significantly rated higher than the Strip one in: friendliness, trustworthiness, intelligence, willingness to interact, and likability. Moreover, the drone with rings was perceived as significantly more animal-like, feminine, and younger than its strip counterpart. Additionally, we were interested in understanding how the added expressive lights affected the perception of the drone itself (i.e., plain without expressive light). To do so, we compared our questionnaire results to the ones of the DJI Mavic 2 drone from Wojciechowska et al. [ 55 ]’s research. Note that as the sample size of their study was different from ours, we could not perform statistical tests, and instead only present their results for reference. We find some interesting differences in the perception of the drone once expressive lights are added, so that adding light seems to lower the perceived intelligence of the drone. However, the Rings configuration seems to increase the perceived friendliness, trustworthiness, willingness to interact, and likeability overall.

5.3.4 Qualitative Data. This section discusses two topics resulting from the open-ended question: Mental models and differences between the configurations.

Mental Models . From the answers provided by participants, we noticed that some of their choices were influenced by previous experience and knowledge of technology, be it in real life : “The circles of lights made felt like loading a webpage, like it was thinking.” ( P 35 - Chase - Rings) or “It looks like a progress bar” ( P 109 - ProgressBar - Strip), or in popular culture: “this is common [...] in sci-fi settings to signal that a robot is searching for something.” ( P 55 - BackForth - Strip). In addition, people made sense of the light signals, such as a fast flashing behavior before slowing down which “communicat[ed] a sense of urgency or excitement” ( P 109 - Slowdown - Strip), while a blinking behavior could be interpreted as welcoming “it seems to flash ‘hello”’ ( P 156 - Morse - Strip). These suggest that the mental model can be drawn from prior concepts participants are already familiar with when interpreting the animations.

Strip vs. Rings Configuration . While our results do not suggest a major difference between the two configurations, one aspect was clearly contrasting, which is in anthropomorphising the drone when identifying the expressive lights as “eyes”. While this happened systematically in the Rings configuration: “It looks like its ‘eyes’ are searching” ( P 4 - Slowdown - Rings) or “The eyes make me think it's ‘seen’ and spotted someone” ( P 3 - Blink - Rings), it only happened for some participants with the Eyes animation in the Strip configuration “it looks similar to two eyes” ( P 5 - Eyes - Strip).

5.4 Initial Design Recommendation

Based on our findings, we can recommend 8 light animations in 2 configurations that emerged as strong and iconic, as well as appropriate colors for each state. For most states, we observed similar results between animations across the two configurations, suggesting that the configuration may play a less important role than the animation itself, which we will validate in the in-person study.

  • Searching: We found the following strong and iconic animations: BackForth and Chase in both configurations; Fade and Decent in Strip; and Switch in Rings. Colorwise, blue and red were both found suitable. We recommend the Chase animation which yields the highest rating for the Rings and was second best for the Strip. We further suggest to use blue color, since blue was only indicated as suitable for this state.
  • Spotting: One strong and iconic animation emerged from our study: Morse in Rings, as well as 6 additional animations that emerged as strong. Several participants described the fast blinking speed could signify that the drone is trying to get someone's attention. Red appeared to be the most suitable color. We therefore suggest the Morse animation in red to indicate to a person that the drone spotted them.
  • Approaching: None of the studied light animations emerged as strong or iconic; and only the color orange appeared suitable. We suggest OneDecent animation, as some participants mentioned that the decreasing size of the lights could indicate decreasing distance between them and the drone. We then recommend exploring OneDecent with orange color.
  • Ready to Interact: Our results did not reveal strong or iconic animations; and only the green color appeared suitable. Since prior work showed that color only can convey certain machine states [ 41 ], we propose a solid green color to indicate the drone's readiness to interact.

6 VALIDATION STUDY

To validate the findings of the online study and increase the ecological validity of this work, we conducted an in-situ study. Our goal was to investigate how the At First Light system performs in a close-to-real-world environment, with both Strip and Rings configurations compared to a plain drone without expressive lights.

6.1 Method

We used a between-subject study design with three conditions: a drone with no expressive lights (Baseline), At First Light with Strip, and At First Light with Rings; so that each participant only experienced one condition. This decision was made to support our focus on first encounters, which would be biased if participants experienced more than one condition. We used a Wizard-of-Oz (WoZ) methodology so that the drone appears to be autonomous throughout the study, as in prior work [ 14 , 56 ]. This method enables a controlled user study in a safe environment for the participant.

6.1.1 Participants. We recruited 45 volunteers using online advertising and word-of-mouth, 21F, 23M, and 1 participant who preferred not to answer, from 20 to 35 y.o. ( μ = 26.5 y.o., SD = 3.14). Almost all participants (93%) saw a drone prior to the study, 20% had piloted one before, and none of them owned one. Participants were compensated the equivalent of US $ 9 for their participation. Note that in the rest of the manuscript we refer to participants P1-P15 for participants in the Strip condition, P16-P30 for Baseline, and P31-P45 for the Rings condition.

6.1.2 Apparatus. We used the At First Light system as described in section 4.2 . To compare the Strip and Rings configurations, we used the same animations and colors in both, following the initial design recommendations presented in section 5.4 . We made sure to select a different color for each state. Note that after a 3-person pilot and inputs from members of the research team, we refined the RGB values of the Blue color to (75, 75, 255) to make it easier to perceive in bright light. Our final choice of animations and colors is summarized in Table 4 . The drone's flight path (Figure 9 ) was pre-programmed with the DJI pilot mobile application and the At First Light system was pre-programmed to support the WoZ protocol. In both Strip and Rings conditions, the LEDs system changed animation and color based on the drone's current state. The drone flew at a constant speed of 2m/s at 8m height. During the drone's approach toward the participant, the speed was lowered to 1m/s, which is the lowest possible speed in this pre-planned flight mode. The drone also lowered its height to 2m and stopped at a 2-meter distance in front of the participant, before going up again and flying back to its takeoff position. The drone approach speed, height, and final distance were chosen based on prior work on human-drone proxemics [ 42 , 56 ]. Note that due to the deviation of the drone's GPS, the researcher manually adjusted the drone orientation on final approach when needed. The drone's built-in vision system was used to make sure the drone wouldn't come closer than 1.8 meters to the participant.

6.1.3 Procedure. Participants were first welcomed, explained about the study, and asked to sign a consent form. They were then described a search and rescue scenario and asked to think of themselves as if they went alone on a hike and got injured. They then watched a short video clip of a hiker in such a situation to help them put their mind to it. Participants were then directed outside by a large quiet grassy area (Figure 9 ) where the experiment took place, and where they had to sit and wait at a marked location. Once the participant was in place, the experimenter launched the drone from behind the scene. The drone took off and started along its pre-planned trajectory while the participant's unique task was to observe the drone. After landing the drone, the researcher brought the participant back to the lab where they conducted a short structured interview regarding the participant's experience and understanding of the situation (as in prior work on designing expressions for appearance constrained-robots [ 48 ]). The participant was asked to describe what the drone was doing and to elaborate on their understanding of it. Regardless of their answer, they were then asked whether they thought the drone wanted to communicate some information and what. Finally, they were asked to specify which actions contributed to this understanding. The participant then had to answer several surveys. Self-Assessment Manikin (SAM) [ 13 ] – to rate participants’ emotional experience with the drone approaching them on a 9-point Likert scale for valence and arousal. States Assessment – a purpose-made 5-item questionnaire related to participants’ understanding of the drone's states, as well as its intentions, and the information communicated. Participants had to answer along a 7-point Likert scale (from 1-Strongly Disagree to 7-Strongly Agree) the following statements: 1. The drone was looking for something or someone; 2. The drone acknowledged my presence from a distance; 3. The drone was approaching me; While hovering right in front of me: 4. the drone seemed like it wanted to tell me something and 5. the drone was trying to tell me something. Godspeed III, IV, and V [ 8 ] – assessed by 7-point Likert scale questionnaires and used to measure participants’ perception of the drone on likeability, intelligence, and safety. Demographics – included questions related to prior drone experience and ownership. Finally, participants received their compensation for participating in the study, which took 35 minutes in average.

6.2 Data Analysis

The data analysis was divided into quantitative data from the questionnaires and qualitative data from the interviews.

6.2.1 Quantitative Data. We performed a Shapiro–Wilk test to check if the Godspeed questionnaire variable's means are normally distributed and tested homogeneity of variances with Bartlett's test. Then, we used one-way ANOVA to test the effect of the configuration. For the SAM and states assessment variables, and the Godspeed sub-variables, which are ordinal, we used the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test [ 12 ].

6.2.2 Qualitative Data. Participants’ comments to the open-ended questions were coded into four categories: perception of the drone searching, perception of being spotted by the drone, perception of the drone approaching, and perception of the drone is ready to interact [ 48 ]. Furthermore, we coded descriptions of the colors and animations within each category. Descriptions were categorized to searching if participants used words such as ‘scanning’, ‘looking’, ‘searching’; to spotting if they used words such as ‘detected’, ‘recognized’, ‘saw me’; to approaching if they used words such as ‘moved closer’, ‘went down’; and to ready to interact if they positively answered when asked about getting explicit or in-explicit information from the drone. Descriptions containing words such as ‘blinking’, ’moving light’, and ’lights getting smaller’ were coded into the animation sub-categories within each category, and descriptions containing colors were coded into the color sub-category.

6.3 Results

The three conditions (Baseline, Strip, Rings) were first compared by the dependent variables and sub-variables’ results (Tables 5 and 6 ). A significant difference was found in the perceived responsibility of the drone. The Kruskal-Wallis test revealed a significant effect of configuration ( χ 2 (2)=5.8, p =0.05). We ran a Mann-Whitney test with p -value adjusted by BH correction among the configurations and found a significant difference between the Strip and the Rings, with the drone in the Strip configuration considered to be more responsible than the drone with the Rings ( p =0.024). Other variables did not reveal a significant difference between conditions. 6.3.2 Understanding Expressive Lights. From the interviews, it seems that the color of the animations had a bigger effect on the participants than the animation itself. When talking about Communicating, where we found a significant difference between Strip configuration to baseline, when asked about the factors that contributed to their understanding, 12 out of 13 participants mentioned the green color, and only 1 mentioned the animation. A similar result was for the Rings configuration, with 11 out of 11 participants mentioned the color and only 1 mentioned the animation. In Spotting, where the animation was periodic without steady light, we also noticed some difference between with or without expressive lights, with 12 out of 12 participants in the Strip configuration mentioned the red color and 7 mentioned the animation, with 6 mentioned the blinking light and one the lights getting smaller ( OneDecent animation). For Rings configuration, 11 out of 13 participants mentioned the red light and 1 mentioned animation.

In terms of understanding colors, the red color was referred to as intimidating or communicating an error or a miss-detection by 33% of the participants who saw the Strip or Rings configuration. The green color on the other hand was considered relaxing, assuring, and communicative by 77% of the participants who saw the Strip or Rings configuration. The red and orange colors were sometimes mixed, with some participants failing to recognize the difference between them and only talked about red or orange colors before the light turned green.

7 DISCUSSION

This section further presents a discussion of our findings and lessons learned throughout this project, as well as our take aways on the use of expressive lights and on DIEM .

7.1 Lessons Learned

Expressive Lights Animations . The goal of this project was to investigate the use of expressive lights for drones to initiate communication with a human. We used a search and rescue drone fitted with At First Light in two user studies: online and in-situ. Our initial implementation was tested in an online study that led to the identification of 8 light animations that were both strong and iconic [ 29 ], meaning that participants were able to associate these to specific drone states. In particular, participants mentioned that some animations reminded them of everyday user interfaces, such as progress bars, this highlighting the importance of existing mental models in the design of new technology. These results were further confirmed in the in-situ study where for example, P5 said: “ The ongoing blue strip made me feel it was searching” ( Chase animation) While these initial results are promising in the use of expressive lights to support drone-initiated communication, they present limitations as some states are more often recognized than others (e.g., Searching and Spotting). Moreover, when validating these results in-situ, participants described not being fully attentive to the animations, possibly because of the distance between the drone and the person, or because of the other environmental factors (e.g., sunlight, movement of the drone).

Differences between Expressive Lights Animations and Baseline . Our in-situ study questionnaires did not reveal significant differences in how participants perceived the drone between the At First Light conditions and the baseline condition. We further found that the movement of the drone itself enabled communicating information, so that participants in all conditions recognized that the drone was Searching by observing its trajectory's grid-like pattern (Figure 9 ) “it was searching around in certain places, it looked scanning moving left right” (P4) and “The drone seemed to be flying in a certain search pattern” (P28) . Similarly, the Approaching state appeared clear to all from the drone's movement and behavior “Then he came closer to understand what is my condition” (P17) .

While the results of the SAM questionnaire did not reveal a significant difference in participants’ arousal and valence after the study, the baseline condition appeared to generate more stress to participants “When the drone initially came down in my direction I didn't quite understand what it was doing. I was a little nervous because I didn't know exactly what would happen with him and I felt the adrenaline rise” (P33) than the expressive light conditions “At first I felt uncomfortable when he started coming down in my direction and then the moment the light started to decrease I realized he was getting closer to me” (P1) . These correlate with our findings that the participants understood the expressive lights as the drone's expression of intent. For instance, in the Ready to Interact state, participants in the expressive light conditions understood that the drone approach was over and believed the drone was being communicative, such as “I saw lights, green color. Came very close, said: ‘recognized, help coming”’ (P38) .

The interview revealed further differences between the two At First Light conditions and the baseline condition in how participants described their experience, not only in the Ready to Interact state described above but also in the Spotting state. Indeed, in the expressive light conditions, the large majority of participants described being spotted by the drone at a distance “there was a blinking of light, the color change, and it seemed that it saw me” (P11) , while in the baseline condition most participants felt spotted only when the drone was close to them “it came down to me and looked at me, then I understood that it is taking my photo” (P20) . In addition to the expressive lights, participants often described how colors supported their understanding of the situation.

Use of Colors . In the first study, we opted to investigate appropriate colors for expressive light separately from animations that are single-color lights. This choice was made to limit the number of possible combinations, as 24 animations in 6 colors would have led to 144 combinations; and as colors present cultural connotations that could potentially reduce the generalizability of the results. Yet, this is an important aspect of light patterns that we wanted to address right from the start. Our initial results showed significant differences between colors for each state and enabled us to recommend specific colors for each state.

We then validated these choices in our in-situ study and found that during the interview, all colors were mentioned by participants. This showed that all of them were seen and noticed, although some colors were mentioned more often (red and green over orange and blue). Participants often described the change of color when discussing changes in the drone's state (Spotting and Ready to Interact), more often than they discussed the light animation itself “saw a light like eyes in red, felt recognized, then moved closer to me, focused on me, lights turned to green” (P43) . As such, we found the light color to be a key factor in the interpretation of the expressive lights, and we suggest that a color change may be more easily noticed than a change between animations.

We however note that some participants made mistakes when describing colors “There were red [orange] lights that became smaller” (P5) and “I saw red and yellow [orange] colors, the colors were a bit intimidating”(P41) . This color mismatch could be due to the color perception itself or to people not remembering what they experienced precisely a few minutes later in the interview. In addition, despite the popularity of red for both the Searching and Spotting states in our online study, in the in-situ study, red was sometimes interpreted in a negative manner “The first time when it was red I thought of negative sign” (P13) . We suggest that red should not be used for the drone initiating interaction and highlight the importance of choosing colors further apart on the visual spectrum when marking a change of state.

Configurations and Design . Through the two user studies, we compared three drone configurations (i.e., visual designs), a drone fitted with an LED strip, one fitted with LED rings, and a plain drone (baseline condition). One main difference between the Rings and Strip seems to be the level of anthropomorphization where the LED Rings are often described as eyes by participants regardless of the animation. Such results are consistent with prior work on drone design [ 55 ]. Further, our results revealed differences in participants’ perceptions of the drone between the conditions. While we could only calculate significance between the two At First Light conditions, the graph points to differences in perception between these and the baseline condition on several dimensions. For instance, we observe that in the Machine-Animal scale, the three conditions could be classified into different average groups (Figure 8 ). Our results revealed that in the Rings configuration, the drone appeared more animal-like, friendly, trustworthy, intelligent, and likable than in the Strip condition. Participants also reported a higher willingness to interact. The Rings configuration seems to also outperform the plain condition, except for perceived intelligence. In the in-situ study, we observed a significant difference between the configurations in the perceived responsibility, where the Strip outperformed the Rings configuration. Interestingly, we did not find further significant differences, such as in perceived likeability and intelligence, between the three configurations in the validation study. This could be a result of different methodology (within vs. between study design), an insufficient number of participants ( N =156 vs. N =15 for each condition), or other environmental factors present in the in-situ study but not online. With regards to the configuration in Strip or Rings, both configurations led to light animations that induced consistency in their interpretation of specific drone states. For example, participants chose the BackForth and Chase animations as representative of the drone's searching state in both configurations. Given the number of arguments in favor of the Rings, we recommend using At First Light with the Rings configuration.

In the next section, we describe how well the four different states of the drone initiating communication were recognized and present our findings to support future design of drones that can initiate communication.

7.2 Towards Drone Initiating Communication

In this work, we defined DIEM - a four-state greeting model for a drone to initiate communication with a human, using expressive lights. We below describe our findings related to the use of At First Light to support each state of DIEM. These findings are presented for the Rings configuration given its performance (see section 7.1 ) .

Searching . We opted to include a pre-engagement phase to contextualize the role of the drone in the scenario, i.e., prior to Kendon's Phase 1 of initiation (Sighting). Since our user study was based on a search and rescue scenario, our pre-engagement phase corresponded to the drone searching for a missing person or passersby who could provide information. Searching is the state for which the highest number (7) of strong and iconic animations emerged. Participants described that several animations reminded them of searching patterns that they were familiar with. In addition, the colors red and blue emerged as the best fit for Searching. We suggest the use of the Chase animation which yield the highest rating, in blue color, since blue was only indicated as suitable for this state.

Spotting . This corresponds to Kendon's first two greeting phases (Sighting and Distance salutation). One strong and iconic animation emerged from our online study, as well as 6 additional animations that emerged as strong. Several participants described the fast blinking speed could signify that the drone is trying to get someone's attention. Although red was preferred for this stage in our initial study, we found it to be interpreted as a negative signal in the in-situ study. Green, however, was the second highest rated color for this state and was interpreted as reassuring and communicative. We then suggest the use of the Morse animation in green color to indicate to a person that they have been spotted.

Approaching . This corresponds to phases 3 and 4 (Approach and Final approach) from Kendon's greeting model. While our initial study did not reveal light animations as either strong or iconic for this state, and only the color orange appeared suitable to represent it, the results of our in-situ study showed that all participants understood the drone was approaching from its descent toward them. Moreover, the decreasing size of the lights in the OneDecent animation seemed to have assisted several people in understanding the drone's intention, therefore avoiding confusion or even stress. We then suggest to combine the approach strategy with the OneDecent animation in orange. We further advise a bright orange shade so that the color will not be perceived as red, which appeared intimidating at times.

Ready to Interact . This final state corresponds to the last two phases (Close salutation and Transition) of Kendon's greetings model. As for Approaching, our initial results did not reveal strong or iconic animations for this state, and only the green color appeared suitable to represent it. Yet, our in-situ study showed promising results in participants understanding the completion of the approach and perceiving the drone's intent to communicate with only one iteration of the Blink animation followed by a solid green color. We suggest that once a drone completes its approach and stops at an appropriate distance to a person [ 56 ], a transition to green, such as through the Blink animation, may be sufficient to indicate the drone's readiness to communicate.

8 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

We acknowledge several methodological limitations to this work. First, in our online study, we explored 26 different light animations and there are undoubtedly designs that we did not consider that might reveal to be strong and iconic for particular states. Furthermore, the choice of running an online video study, which is on par with prior work [ 29 , 41 ], had the advantage of presenting consistent stimuli to all participants. However, it may have created idealized conditions (e.g., lighting conditions, distance to the drone, drone movement), which may not be representative of all interactions in dynamic environmental conditions. While more research should investigate additional conditions, results were strengthened by the findings of the in-situ study. Another limitation refers to the level of realism of our in-situ study. While we did our best to balance a replicable controlled and safe study with a realistic scenario, our participants were not exposed to the conditions that they would encounter in a real SAR situation. Still, the study was run outdoors and participants were given limited information about the situation they were going to encounter so that we would gain in ecological validity. Overall, we believe that this work provides promising findings and useful insights as a strong base for future research.

Future work could moreover consider wider sets of expressive light animations, including different patterns, configurations, or colors. We suggest that an important next step will be to study the effect of color alone against animations and against a combination of both. We suggest this as color change was often described as a main element to the participants’ understanding of the state change. An additional aspect to consider could be studying the visibility of light animations and colors, and the effect they have in combination with distance, angles, and lighting conditions on human perception and understanding. We further propose to combine light animations with specific drone movements (as in Bevins and Duncan [ 11 ]’s work). We propose that additional work could investigate the use of expressive lights on drones to convey additional states, behaviors, and intentions throughout different use cases. Finally, future research should consider adding additional salutation signals, such as a vocal greeting – as in prior HRI research (e.g., [ 4 ]).

9 CONCLUSIONS

This work explored the use of expressive lights to convey the intention of a drone to initiate communication with a person. We described DIEM - the process of a drone initiating communication and divided it into 4 states: Searching, Spotting, Approaching, and Ready to Interact. We then designed and developed At First Light: an expressive light system using LEDs embedded on a commercial drone in two configurations (Strip and Rings). We further implemented 26 light animations for which we solicited the interpretation ratings from 156 participants. This evaluation uncovered 8 animations that are legible to represent two of the four states, meaning that minimal to no training would be required for bystanders to understand the Searching and Spotting states of a drone. We then validated at First Light in an in-situ user study with 45 participants where we compared its two configurations with a baseline condition. Our results show that the 4 states of approach are recognizable in all conditions and that the expressive light system provides a more nuanced understanding of the drone states and communicative behaviors. Our findings highlight differences between light configurations and LED colors to represent each state, as well as the importance of the color change matching the state change. The paper concludes with insights for future design and research in the field. While this work could be expanded in many directions, it provides a starting point towards the use of expressive lights for drone-initiated communication.
