Orthoses with electronic functions have emerged as a promising medical product in response to the increasing demand for rehabilitation training, therapy assistance, and health monitoring. However, fabricating this “smart orthosis” often requires long development cycles and exorbitant prices. We introduce E-Orthosis, an integrated fabrication approach with construction toolkits for healthcare professionals to quickly embed electronics in off-the-shelf orthoses with customized functions cost-effectively and time-efficiently. Specifically, we develop components with magnets and pogo pins to support rapid attachment and sustainable use, and textile-based electrodes with snap installation to improve the wearing experience. We also provide a circuit iron tool to apply circuit traces on complex surfaces of orthoses directly and a hot punch tool to embed magnet ports and electrodes. Three application examples, technical evaluations, and expert reviews demonstrate the functionality of E-Orthosis and the potential for democratizing rapid-developed and low-cost smart orthoses for patients.

1 INTRODUCTION

Orthoses with sensing and stimulating functions, hereon referred to as smart orthoses, provide real-time feedback on treatment effects and unpredictable changes or emergencies. They can also provide physicians with accurate health data to facilitate precision medicine for long-term health recovery processes and rehabilitation training. However, despite the growing demand for intelligent orthoses, it remains challenging to personalize and popularize them outside the laboratory. The reasons can be listed as follows:

  • Integrating electronics into orthoses requires professional electronic experiences that most clinicians do not possess. Existing research on the integration of electronics on textiles [ 22 , 27 ], skin [ 23 ], 3D printed objects [ 36 ], and daily things [ 45 ] have been conducted. But they share no compatibility with orthoses due to the particular uses being medical devices: orthoses generally require different stiffness and thickness for various treatment and rehabilitation plans, which is impossible with these existing methods.
  • During optimal rehabilitation, health monitoring is needed to prevent patients from developing conditions such as abnormal bone growth or swelling bruise. However, customizing smart orthoses is typically time-consuming, due to which patients may miss the golden period of rehabilitation.
  • In daily use, especially for rehabilitation at home, it is crucial to ensure the accuracy, reliability, and durability of smart orthoses. However, common functional electronic attachment, such as adhesive gel-based electrodes [ 42 ], requires regular replacement and repositioning, which is difficult for non-skilled patients to operate themselves.
  • Orthoses usually have short service life due to varying rehabilitating circumstances [ 37 ]. However, most existing works on customizing smart orthoses mainly focus on function realization and disregard the high cost of adding customized electronics, which hinders the promotion of smart orthoses.

In response, we advocate for improving off-the-shelf orthoses to personalized, ready-to-use, low-cost smart orthoses to democratize smart orthoses for healthcare equity. There are a few criteria that E-Orthosis meets:

  • We use pogo pins that can adapt to various orthoses with different thicknesses and curvatures and use magnets to attach and detach quickly for iterative reuse and cost reduction.
  • We develop a snap-fit method of attaching textile electrodes to orthoses for effective and comfortable long-term monitoring at home.
  • We develop an iron tool for applying circuit traces directly to the 3D surface of orthosis and a heat punch tool for embedding magnet ports and electrodes.

E-Orthosis construction kit works on orthoses with diverse materials (e.g., thermoplastic, foam, fabric), varying thicknesses (1.6 mm - 4 mm), and complex geometries such as curves and corners, which demonstrate its adaptability and robustness. Three applications of augmenting conventional orthoses into smart orthoses demonstrate their utility and manufacturability. We also evaluated the mechanical and electrical stability of E-Orthosis by shaking it over 20,000 times and subjecting it to a five-day, 12-hour-per-day, non-laboratory wear study. Expert interviews with orthotists and rehabilitation physicians demonstrate the accessibility and potential for expanding the use of wearable computing in healthcare. We hope that in the future, E-Orthosis will be affordable and widely accessible in hospitals, studios, and households.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

  • A novel fabrication pipeline for augmenting orthoses rapidly. This pipeline simplifies the typically complex and time-consuming assembly of wires and active electronics that is usually required to customize smart orthoses.
  • The design and realization of a toolkit system with easy-to-attach components, customized textile electrodes with snap accessories, and a series of iron-on and hot punch tools.
  • Three applications of augmenting off-the-shelf orthoses, technical evaluations, and an expert review demonstrate the stability, compatibility, and usage potential of smart orthoses.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Smart Wearables for Rehabilitation

Smart wearables, as a form of ubiquitous computing systems, have been used in health and rehabilitation fields for a long time [ 5 , 35 , 40 ]. Orthosis, widely used in orthopedics and rehabilitation, is a potential healthcare wearable computing device. Smart orthosis enables various functionalities, such as physiological monitoring [ 25 ] and conformity sensing [ 38 ]. Wearing smart orthoses allows patients to monitor their health conditions and seamlessly access smart home assistance [ 20 ], thereby optimizing the experience of patients during rehabilitation and increasing their willingness to wear orthoses [ 2 ]. Moreover, smart orthosis provides clinicians with patients’ objective health data [ 18 ], enabling them to adjust treatment plans accurately and efficiently, minimize side effects, and achieve remote instruction.

In addition to the high cost of customizing smart orthoses, several technical obstacles have impeded their development and application. First, embedding electronics, including welding and wiring, is complicated and necessitates both electronic expertise and manual techniques. Second, the customization process of smart orthoses typically takes a few days. While awaiting the completion of orthosis customization, patients may miss the optimal window for rehabilitation. Although some research investigated eliminating the installation of electrodes through printed electrodes on orthosis [ 6 ], the circuit requires soldering or inserting cables and electrical components, which is cumbersome, time-consuming, and error-prone. Third, traditional adhesive electrodes for EMG recording are uncomfortable and unsuitable for daily wear. Although some research has been conducted to improve the comfort of electrodes, it does not support continuous work when orthoses are worn (two weeks to several months) [ 11 , 31 ]. Therefore, most EMG recordings are still performed in laboratories.

2.2 Personal Fabrication of Healthcare Products

Personal fabrication technologies increasingly used in healthcare products are mainly 3D printing and computer-aided design [ 3 , 7 , 8 , 13 , 14 ]. They respond to the extensive demand for customization for rehabilitation and have been applied to the rapid customization of prostheses [ 34 ], orthoses [ 41 ], and assistive devices in daily use [ 16 ]. Furthermore, these increasingly democratized technologies enable healthcare professionals to play the roles of designers or makers and use their clinical experience to customize healthcare products [ 4 , 26 , 43 ]. Prior researchers have explored various methods, such as workshops [ 1 ], courses [ 30 ], and co-design [ 15 ], to assist healthcare professionals in utilizing personalized fabrication technologies with the motivation of reducing global healthcare inequality [ 9 ].

However, during the customization of smart wearables in healthcare, the relevant electronic techniques are still challenging for healthcare professionals. In most cases of smart wearables customization, medical professionals just decide the data type to record [ 28 ] or assess the intelligent system [ 39 ] as a consultant rather than a maker. There is limited research regarding the further inclusion of healthcare professionals in developing smart wearable technology. In one existing work [ 12 ], healthcare professionals are encouraged to create paper prototypes to express their needs. It remains an open opportunity to enable healthcare professionals to develop smart wearable products for actual use.

2.3 Fabrication Approach & Construction Toolkits of Electronics

Recent works in Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) have proposed electronic fabrication methods for creating smart wearables. For example, BodyStylus [ 32 ], SkinWire [ 19 ], and ElectroDermis [ 29 ] showcase the design and fabrication of on-skin interactive interfaces with functions such as hand gesture recognition, touch sensing, and temperature measurement. ExoForm [ 33 ] utilizes 4D printing and offers a semi-rigid wearable system that is semi-autonomously assembled. Oh, Snap! [ 36 ] presents a pipeline that uses magnets to connect 3D-printed objects to sensing electronics. KnitUI [ 27 ] provides a fabrication example and an interactive design interface to assist users in constructing a machine-knitted textile user interface with pressure sensors.

Previous studies integrate fabrication tools into construction toolkits for electronic construction. Toolkit research is crucial to the field of HCI as it allows users or new audiences from other fields to participate in the design and implementation of interactive systems. Well-designed construction toolkits provide novice users with the advantages of emerging technologies and inspire them to develop new solutions [ 24 ]. Researchers have developed electronic construction toolkits to assist amateurs in embedding and functionalizing electronics in diverse objects. Some research focuses on the improvement of daily objects [ 45 ], fabrics [ 17 , 21 , 22 ], and skin [ 23 , 44 ], particularly for health monitoring [ 44 ]. However, existing electronic construction kits cannot meet the needs of augmented smart orthoses. Augmenting orthoses with digital function requires double-sided electrical connections between the outer and inner surfaces of orthoses for sensors installed within (e.g., heart rate, body temperature). In addition, orthoses have complex shapes and various thicknesses and hardness. Therefore, kits for smart orthoses require components and fabrication processes vastly distinct from those of existing works (Figure 2 ).

3 DESIGN GOALS

Based on our observation and interviews with orthotists and rehabilitation physicians, we have established the following design objectives, guiding this effort to develop rapidly augmenting orthoses.

Reducing learning costs for healthcare professionals. We choose healthcare professionals, such as rehabilitation physicians and orthotists, as our target users due to the following considerations: 1) rehabilitation physicians are familiar with the requirements of smart orthoses and the optimal placement of crucial sensors such as electrodes and pressure sensors; 2) orthotists have fabrication expertise in customizing thermoformed orthoses, including adding accessories such as buckles through punching holes (see Figure 3 ). Therefore, converting the customized electronic installation operations into what orthotists are familiar with will reduce the learning cost, thereby increasing the efficiency of customizing smart orthoses with a single appointment between physicians and patients.

Ensuring home-use wearability for patients. E-Orthosis should allow non-skilled patients to use smart orthoses, ensuring reliability, durability, and aesthetic acceptance. For instance, circuits should be incorporated into the orthosis to prevent it from being obtrusive or overhanging; circuits should be stable during the possible slight deformation of the orthosis during insertion and removal. In addition, the electrodes should not require daily installation.

Improving compatibility of electronics with various off-the-shelf orthoses. Our method must be adaptable to orthoses of various materials and thicknesses to accommodate the vast array of orthoses on the market. For instance, the EMG electrodes or the heartrate sensors require close contact with the skin to ensure high-quality signals for data acquisition; therefore, the influence of varying thicknesses must be considered when fitting various orthoses. Existing orthoses have diverse geometries and are composed of various materials with different thicknesses due to varying functional requirements and manufacturing conditions. Therefore, electronics should be seamlessly integrated and installed in numerous orthoses currently in use.

Allowing reuse for affordability. Considering limited life cycle requirements and variation requirements of orthosis during rehabilitation, our method should allow cross-orthosis and cross-client sustainable use of electronics to reduce the capital cost of smart orthosis for an individual patient.

4 OVERVIEW OF E-ORTHOSIS

Based on the above design goals, we propose E-Orthosis, a design and fabrication pipeline and toolkit for augmenting orthosis with electronic functions from the perspective of rehabilitation physicians and orthotists (we call users in the following of this section).

Integrating hardware to a high degree is essential for smart orthoses outside the laboratory. Our pipeline primarily contributes to the low-cost and efficient assembly of hardware modules on orthosis. Beginning with orthotists obtaining the functional requirements of the orthosis from healthcare physicians, our method provides a basic circuit configuration to assist users in mastering the electronic components and connection methods (Figure 4a and Section 5.1). Users can design component layouts and circuit routing using our aiding tools (Figure 4b and Section 5.1). Using our hot punch tool, users can punch holes in the orthosis to embed and secure magnetic connectors (Figure 4c -d and Section 5.2). Our circuit ironing tool can assist users in ironing the circuit trace on the curved surface of the orthosis (Figure 4e and section 5.3), ensuring conformity and stability during daily activities without obtrusive wiring. After that, users only need to attach the electronic modules with magnets to the magnetic connectors on the orthosis to sandwich the circuit traces, and the assembly of the hardware modules is completed (Figure 4f and Section 6.2). The magnetically connected electronic modules are detachable and reusable in other orthoses (Figure 4h ).

Our tools and pipeline can support the rapid assembly and recycling of electronic components by professional physicians and orthotists, and ensure the functional stability and aesthetic acceptance of smart orthoses during daily patient use. Our method is compatible with orthoses comprised of versatile materials (e.g., thermoplastic, foam, fabric), varying thicknesses (1.6 mm - 4 mm), and intricate geometries such as curves and corners. Additionally, it is compatible with orthoses worn on numerous body parts. Besides working on static orthoses that support and stiffen specific body parts, our toolkit can embed electronics in the stable portion of dynamic orthoses, allowing clients to move joints. The E-Orthosis construction kit includes (1) customized, easy-to-attach components, such as modular electronic components, connectors with magnetic ports, and textile electrodes with snap-in installation structures, (2) one hot punch tool for punching holes in the target orthoses to embed components, and (3) one trace iron tool for the rapid installation of circuit traces. The following sections will elaborate on the detailed content.

5 PIPELINE AND TOOLKIT

This section presents details of the pipeline and toolkit to allow users to utilize our method.

5.1 Functional Planning with Modular Electronic Components

E-Orthosis enables users to create smart orthoses with digital healthcare capabilities through repurposed components. Determining specific digital functions for rehabilitation and selecting electronic components with corresponding capabilities, such as pressure sensing, are the initial steps in the process. A typical circuit includes a power module, controller, and sensor module. Moreover, output modules (light and vibration) as reminders are optional and can be wired together using a serial communication bus with the same frequency of flashing or vibration (Figure 5 ). After selecting electronic modules, users place the modules temporarily on the orthosis referring to circuit architecture (e.g., power → controller → pressure sensor). More details of location planning operation will be explained in the next section (Section 5.2). There are two ways to visualize and utilize the signal from the sensor: (1) transmitting monitor results to a PC or phone via Bluetooth, and (2) displaying the monitored physical state through the lights on the sensor module itself or additional reminder modules such as vibration. The feedback can be set to serve patients, patient's families, physicians, and orthotists. Orthotists can obtain the preprogrammed controller module. If additional functionality is required in the future, the software of the controller module can be modified through micro-USB and be reused in another orthosis.

Except for general module types (power, controller, and output as a reminder), we customized a series of electronic component modules, including sensors and actuators needed in rehabilitation. As shown in Figure 6 , we provided healthcare professionals with a guideline for their potential usage in rehabilitation. The sensors and actuators are categorized by their installing positions: installed on the outer surface of orthoses individually, the inner surface of orthoses, or the outer surface with extended elements such as electrodes and pressure-sensitive film.

Each module is a custom printed circuit board (PCB) with magnets, allowing for simple assembly and replacement of components by healthcare professionals without electronics experience, and can be reused for various orthoses throughout the rehabilitation. Informal magnetic force testing revealed that the neodymium N52 magnets (remanence of ∼ 0.29 Tesla), each measuring Φ3 × 2 mm, provided a sufficient magnetic force to attach the board to the connection port, even with 6 mm of orthoses material in between.

The pins of PCB boards are customized according to the assembled position of electronic components. As shown in Figure 7 , modules to be installed on the outer surface of orthoses (such as power, controller, and accelerator) are embedded with pogo pins for connecting copper circuit trace, whereas modules to be installed on the inner surface have hole pins for connecting long pogo pins. Each module's pins are arranged in the middle of the board to ensure a stable connection to the magnet connectors on the orthosis. In addition, each module is labeled with a function name, a user-friendly icon, and a directional arrow for locating the direction on the bottom layer.

Technically, most sensing modules use I2C for communications except pressure and EMG sensors, which read electrical signal change directly. Light and vibration modules are controlled through PWM signals from the microcontroller module. Seeed studio XIAO is selected as the controller due to its built-in BLE radio, small size, low power consumption, and ability to support extended orthosis wear time. The battery module (3.7 V lithium battery, 100 mAh), whose micro-USB is an external charger, can supply the smart orthosis with power for an extended period and can be swapped out for recharging.

5.2 Embedding Components with Hot Punch Tool

To attach magnet ports and electrodes to orthoses, particularly to achieve detachable connection and to ensure electrical connection through the outer surface and inner surface of orthoses with diverse materials and thicknesses, we customized a hot punch tool with two replaceable hot punches (Figure 8 ). Two replaceable modular punches heads match the contours of magnetic ports and electrode metal buckles ( ${\rm{\Phi }}$ 3 mm). Two modular punches that share a single heating source can reduce costs. The punch heads of sufficient length (greater than 18 mm) can penetrate orthosis materials of varying thicknesses. One hole matches the shape of magnet ports with single-row pins, whereas two holes next to one another match the shape of magnet ports with double-row pins. The installment structure of magnet ports and the operation of embedding magnet connectors will be explained in Section 6.2.

Considering the modular length of circuit traces, a soft ruler with holes helps to determine the positions of punches for magnet connectors and the location of electronic components. The ruler's hole is slightly larger than the magnetic port, allowing the punch's head to pass through. Components do not need to be precisely aligned because the soft ruler of the template has the same elasticity and turning radius (63 mm) as the ironing circuit traces. Note that adjacent modules can be spaced apart by multiple holes, and modules with single-row pins can be placed on either row of the ruler's double-row pins. We advise planning the location of vital sensors (e.g., heart rate, acceleration) first and then other components.

Technically, a soldering iron was modified by removing the original connector and retaining the temperature control heating rod. As replaceable modular punches, customized metal heads are assembled to stainless steel sleeves, which are connected to the body of the soldering iron via a stud bolt. The handles of modular punches are protected from heat by Teflon tape, two layers of aluminum silicate refractory fiber felt with a 5 mm thickness, and heat shrink. With protection, the surface temperature can remain below 70 °C after 20 minutes of heating at 250 °C, which is a standard setting for practical applications.

5.3 Fixing Circuit Traces with Trace Ironing Tool

To assist healthcare physicians and HCI makers in attaching circuit traces to the surfaces of orthoses efficiently and securely, we have developed a tool (Figure 9 ) that can simultaneously iron and distribute circuits. This tool is inspired by the circuit ironing tool on textiles [ 22 ]. Furthermore, it allows ironing on curved surfaces of orthoses.

The operation of ironing traces is efficient and user-friendly. After aligning and ironing the starting point of the circuit trace with the magnetic connector on the orthosis, users move the tool on the surface of the orthosis, adjusting the angle of the ironing head flexibly and pressing the soft ironing head to iron the circuit traces on the curved surface. Next, users slide the cutting blade at the stamp holes of the circuit trace and iron the end of the circuit onto the magnetic connector.

Technically, the device is derived from a temperature-adjustable soldering iron with no additional PCB customization. A custom-shaped metal plate replaces the original soldering head and is attached with an M4 screw to the soldering iron. The ironing head is covered with soft thermal rubber pads (2 mm thick) and Teflon tapes to prevent adhesion to low-temperature thermoplastic materials. To avoid the deformation of 3D-printed ABS (Acrylonitrile Butadiene Styrene) shells, the inner heater is surrounded by thermal insulation materials (5 mm-thick aluminum silicate refractory fiber felts). Approximately a 5 mm gap exists between the shell and the thermal insulation materials to improve thermal insulation.

The circuit tape made of copper wires and TPU sheets is wound onto replaceable spools, allowing for simple installation and replacement. When moving the soldering head on the surfaces of orthoses, the tape is unrolled and heated to fix. TPU becomes viscous at temperatures between 120 to 150 °C to adhere the circuit on orthosis. Note that the temperature and heating time should be regulated to avoid overheating the surface of the orthosis and altering its original shape.

After ironing the circuit traces, users can connect the magnetic modules with the magnetic connectors on the orthosis to sandwich the circuit traces and complete the augment to orthoses. The details of the connection structure and installation operation will be elaborated in Section 6.2. Programs with different functions can be loaded on the controller module via micro-USB after the installment of the controller.

Additional accessories in the E-Orthosis construction kit, including circuit trace stacks, magnet connectors with patches, and textile electrodes with snap fasteners, will be explained in the next section.

6 TECHNICAL DETAILS

This section presents extensions to our basic pipeline to ensure robustness and durability.

6.1 Circuit Trace Stack

The circuit stack maintains a balance between flexibility and durability. Each circuit trace consists of three layers:

(1) Conductive wire layer. We use copper with a thin conductive adhesive. The serpentine trace allows circuit stretching to accommodate curved surfaces and slight deformations of orthosis during wearing and removal.

(2) Heat adhesive and protective layer. We use a thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) membrane with a hot melt adhesive to bond stability to the base surface of an orthosis. This layer can adhere securely to common orthosis surface materials such as ABS, PCL, foam, and fabric. Additionally, it increases resistance to the intrusion of water and dirt and withstands the wear of extended daily use (e.g., the friction of nylon belts).

(3) Carrier layer. To prevent the adhesive layer from adhering to the ironing head during ironing, we add a release paper at the top. Parallel slit patterns (Kirigami) are optional for circuits that need to turn (Figure 9c 2). Thus, the ironing traces allow turning with a radius of 63 mm. Each subsection of the circuit can accumulate 29 degrees of turning angle.

To reserve interval conductivity for components above the circuit trace, we laser-cut holes in the TPU layer at intervals. In addition, lines of slit stamp holes are dispersed to facilitate smooth cutting with the ironing tool. Thus, the circuit trace length (current version) is adjustable in increments of 3.5 cm.

For the fabrication technique of circuit trace, copper wire is cut with a laser marking machine; the TPU layer and carrier layer are fabricated by laser cutting and bonded with ironing.

6.2 Connection Structure Design

The connection mechanism of E-Orthosis is crucial for ensuring compatibility, flexibility, and durability. E-Orthosis is comprised of three types of electronic connection: (1) Connecting PCB modules and wires on the outer surface; (2) Connecting PCB modules on the inner surface and circuits on the outer surface; (3) Connecting extended (such as electrodes) on the inner surface and circuits on the outer surface.

6.2.1 Connection on the outer surface. Magnetism is utilized for detachable connections, automatic alignment, and connection stability. As shown in Figure 10 , a magnet connection port with a patch is embedded in the orthosis to provide magnetism for the rapid installation of electronic components such as power, controller, and acceleration sensor modules on the outer surface of orthoses. Electronic component modules (custom PCB boards) are equipped with magnets and short pogo pins that allow for simple attachment and detachment for recycling or maintenance while preventing unintentional detachment. The circuit trace ironed onto the surface of the orthosis is sandwiched between the electronic component and magnet connection port by the force of the magnet and pogo pins of PCB boards (Figure 10a ). The resilient and long-lasting pogo pins compensate for minor surface irregularities on the orthosis.

6.2.2 Connecting PCB between the inner and outer surface. For sensors (e.g., temperature and heart rate sensors) that must be installed close to the body on the inner side of the orthosis, they should be electronically connected to the outer surface of the orthosis. Therefore, we incorporate a magnetic connection port with long pogo pins within the orthosis (Figure 11b ). Through orthosis with varying thicknesses, hardness, and a slight curve, the long pogo pins can reach the through-hole pins of the internal sensors, thereby establishing an electrical connection between the outer and inner surfaces (Figure 11c ). A controller module or additional magnetic connection port with pogo pins is magnetically attached to the clamp circuit trace on the outer surface to ensure electronic connecting stability (Figure 11d ).

6.2.3 Embedding magnet connection ports in orthoses. Magnet connection port is incorporated into orthosis for two purposes: (1) to provide magnetism for easy attachment and recycling use of PCB modules, and (2) to provide potential electrical penetration between the outer and inner surface. We decided to use commercially available magnetic connection ports with pogo pins due to their alignment accuracy, stability, affordability, and accessibility. Long pogo pins (compression ranges from 3.8 mm to 5.3 mm) are embedded in connection ports to facilitate electrical penetration between the outer and inner surfaces of orthoses with variable thicknesses. Two connection port units for PCB boards with two rows of pins are combined (e.g., microcontroller). To secure the magnet connection, a patch larger than the magnet connection adheres to the upper surface of the magnet port and the outer surface of the orthosis.

Our primary considerations for the attachment method of the magnet connection port are (1) mechanical and electrical connection stability on orthosis with various materials and heights; and (2) operation simplicity and efficiency. Thus, we considered and tested the three attachment methods for magnet connection ports on orthoses.

Direct sticking and insert. We investigated the direct adhering of the port to the surface of the orthosis using conductive tape, with pogo pins penetrating tiny holes in the orthoses to connect with corresponding pins. The magnet connection port protrudes from the surface of orthoses, resulting in an unattractive partial hang of PCB boards on ports and an increased risk of rubbing off during daily use. In addition, it creates difficulties for ironing circuit traces because the padded circuit trace at the bottom of the connection port cannot guarantee connection stability without the pressure of magnets and pogo pins.

Heat pressing. The alignment of the magnet connection port with the surface of the orthosis is made simple and stable by applying heat to the port. We evaluated this method's viability using a customized mini heat pressing tool. Heat pressing a port into a common orthosis material (low-temperature thermoplastic materials, or LTTP) requires continuous pressing until the material cools, which is time-consuming. In addition, this method is incompatible with specific orthoses, including those made of foam, textiles, and plastic with a higher hardness.

Hot punching holes. Embedding the port into the hole of the corresponding shape through heat punching fits our use cases well. This method applies to various orthosis materials, such as thermoplastics, foams, and textiles, that can be heated and punched quickly and easily. In addition, a patch larger than the magnet connection can be added to help secure the magnet port by adhering firmly to the magnet port's upper surface and the orthosis’ outer surface.

We considered all three approaches but ultimately determined that hot punching holes were the most appropriate method based on our criteria. We developed a hot punch tool and described it in Section 5.1.

6.3 Installment Structure of Textile Electrode

The embedding of electrodes on orthoses should consider two aspects: (1) conformity, accuracy, stability, and patient comfort during wear; and (2) ease of installation for orthotists.

Currently, the gold standard for clinical and laboratory EMG monitoring is to collect data using adhesive, single-use electrodes. These electrodes frequently irritate the skin and are undesirable for long-term use because the conductive gel dries out over time, compromising signal quality, which is neither ergonomic nor aesthetically pleasing to wear during daily activities. Although a tattoo electrode is highly compatible with the skin, it can only be used once. Textile electrodes are gaining in popularity due to their high contact area, structural stability, and variety of design options [ 35 ]. Using textile electrodes for sensing and measuring biosignals and other on-body interactions has gained increasing attention. However, installing fabric electrodes is a time-consuming process that requires sewing. Therefore, we designed an installation method and a structure of textile electrodes for orthoses enhancement.

The E-Orthosis electrode comprises silver fiber conductive textile (purchased from Yumokemao Co., Ltd. (Jinan, China) ) and thin foam padding. It allows for a comfortable, irritation-free fit on the skin for extended periods. We use conductive fasteners to connect because their operation is consistent with the existing method of orthosis accessory assembly that orthotists have mastered. Two installation methods (screw and snap) are compared for ease, comfort, mechanical stability, thickness compatibility, and electrical contact efficiency.

A flat-head screw was initially chosen as the connection method (Figure 12a ) due to its ability to adapt to materials of varying thicknesses by compressing the foam pad between screws. However, it was discovered that tightening the screws on textile electrodes decreases the quality of the electrical contact. The concave surface of the fixed electrode is inferred to reduce its contact area with the body.

The second iteration (Figure 12b ) employs a conductive snap fastener to adapt to varying thicknesses by striking at the desired interval distance, thereby maintaining a flat surface with adequate contact area. To enhance conformity and contact with the skin, we wrap a snap fastener cap and a piece of foam beneath the conductive cloth. With the aid of a copper patch, the snap fastener socket on the exterior of the orthosis is connected to the circuit trace.

Electrode installation is a straightforward process for orthotists. The orthotist begins by punching a small hole at the end of the trace at the desired location. Then, the orthotist can attach a copper patch and a snap fastener socket to the exterior of the orthosis, a textile electrode to the interior, and tap to secure them.

E-Orthosis enables effective long-term monitoring of daily rehabilitation by incorporating textile electrodes. Patients can wear and use smart orthoses at home without assistance from medical personnel.

7 APPLICATIONS OF AUGMENTING ORTHOSES

We augment three orthoses as examples of smart healthcare to demonstrate the potential versatility of E-Orthosis applications. The modified orthoses were derived from two sources: (1) collecting discarded orthoses shaped on-site by orthotists in hospitals and (2) online and offline purchases.

7.1 Pressure Monitoring Wrist Orthosis

A customized wrist orthosis for wrist bone fracture is embedded with a pressure sensor for monitoring the wearing state and duration (Figure 13 ). A pressure-sensing film is affixed to the inner surface of the bony prominence where excessive pressure tends to develop. A flashing light on the orthosis will alert the wearer when the device is too tight or too loose. It prevents the obstruction of blood flow to pressure ulcers. When the edema in the area of the initial fracture decreases after a period of use, the light on the orthosis alerts the patient to return to the hospital to have the orthosis adjusted or replaced. Under Velcro straps, a circuit trace with an adhesive layer can remain stable. During rehabilitation, the electronic components module can be quickly detached and replaced with the next orthosis.

7.2 Electrical Stimulation Splint

When the lower limb is fractured, prolonged use of a fixed splint may result in muscle atrophy due to prolonged muscle inactivity. Adding electrical stimulation to a foam orthosis (Figure 14 ) assists in preventing muscle atrophy. Patients can get electrical stimulation in small amounts and multiple times per day in their own homes while continuously wearing the orthosis. They are not required to travel to the hospital and remove the brace to receive electrical stimulation therapy. Internally embedded within the dense foam are customized conductive fabric electrodes with large contact areas. The power module is easily removable and replaceable for recharging. The stability of the circuit traces is unaffected by the slight deformation of the foam splint.

7.3 Dynamic Knee Orthoses

A dynamic knee orthosis embedded with accelerometers and EMG detects and alerts the patient of their range of motion and the degree of muscle fatigue. It prevents secondary injury caused by excessive exercise and provides a simple and rapid assessment of the knee joint mobility of the patient (Figure 15 ). Thus, patients can independently, promptly, and safely complete daily rehabilitation training without on-site physician supervision.

8 EVALUATION

8.1 Compatibility of E-Orthosis

The tests in this section evaluate the compatibility of our method with orthosis produced using various manufacturing processes, materials, thicknesses, and geometries.

8.1.1 Circuit ironing on different surface geometries. Circuits can be ironed onto orthoses with complex geometries due to the flexibility of the ironing angle and the shape-adaptability of the ironing head, which is equipped with deformable rubber. Figure 16 illustrates how the ironed circuit traces conform to organic body shapes, such as on the center of the palm (Figure 16a 1) and around the ankle (Figure 16a 2). This method can also work on orthosis with variable thickness (Figure 16b ) and small protruding features (Figure 16c ).

8.1.2 Attaching to versatile materials. The materials used in orthoses vary depending on the structural or tensile strength requirements and the manufacturing method. Since common materials used in orthoses can be hot-hole punched and both the circuit tapes and magnetic connection port patch have adhesive properties, our method enables the installation of electronics in a variety of materials for real-world applications. We evaluated the functionality of embedding connection ports and ironing circuits on orthoses made from various materials collected from clinics and stores and 3D printing the DIY community's shared model. As illustrated in Figure 17a , the circuits are compatible with different orthosis materials, such as low-temperature thermoplastic (PCL), PLA, ABS, foam, and fabric.

8.1.3 Internal and external connections of different thicknesses and shapes. Due to the interval tolerance of magnetic attraction and long pogo pins, the electrical and mechanical connection between the interior and exterior of orthoses can accommodate (1) varying heights of substrate material, (2) varying heights of possible soft padding, and (3) slight gaps to substrates due to the organic warp shape. Figure 17b depicts the components connected through the substrate material with a typical thickness range between 1.6 mm and 4 mm. As recommended by the experts in Section 7.1, an extra three mm-thick custom foam pad with a hollow is adhered to the inner surface of the orthosis to smooth the component's edge. The directly acceptable thickness range is determined by the maximum length and compression limit of the pogo pins on the magnetic connection port. Our pogo pins have a total length of 5.3 mm and a maximum compression of 3.8 mm. As demonstrated in Figure 17b , when the substrate material is thinner than 3.8 mm, the foam pad will cover the gap between the connection port and PCB board, ensuring a comfortable fit without bulging while maintaining electrical and mechanical connection. For substrates thicker than 5.3 mm, such as dense foam, we can use longer pogo pins to increase the applicable thickness range and add magnets between the connector and PCB if a stronger magnetic attraction is required.

Figure 17d illustrates that components are mechanically and electrically connected via orthoses with a surface radius greater than 6 mm. We do not advise installing electronic components within a surface with a smaller radius, as this may affect the surface's conformability to wear.

With the adaptability of snap fastener and foam wrapped in the conductive textile, the textile electrode can also adapt to different thicknesses (1.6 mm - 4.0 mm), as shown in Figure 17c .

8.2 Robustness and Durability of Electrical Connection

To evaluate the robustness and durability of mechanical and electrical connection, we apply a robot arm (Figure 18a ) to swing the smart wrist orthosis (application example in Section 5.1) back and forth for 120° every 3 seconds, simulating the patient's movement during long-term use. According to the time-lapse video, the components worked well until the 50 mAh battery ran out after approximately 3 hours and more than 7200 swings. To recover the work electronics, we then magnetically detached the power module and replaced it with a battery with a greater capacity. It maintained mechanical and electrical stability for over 8.5 hours and 20,000 swings, sustaining the support of the work.

In addition, we conducted a five-day wear test outside the lab (Figure 18b ) to evaluate the mechanical durability and electrical continuity under real-world conditions. A co-author was required to wear the application example (described in Section 7.3) during his daily activities for five consecutive days (12 hours per day) without action limit. Before the wear study, we pretested that the power consumptions of the controller module, the accelerometer module, and the EMG sensor are about 40.7 mW, 12.8 mW, and 29.7 mW, respectively. A 400 mAh 3.7 V battery module is utilized to power the system. The controller module transmits data via Bluetooth every day for 12 hours to monitor the stability of the physical connections. Each day's worth of wear began with the magnetic replacement of the charged power module. Throughout the evaluation, the participant was engaged in normal daily activities and replaced the power module.

The outcome demonstrated that the smart orthosis remained electrically functional and collected IMU and EMG signals via BLE without interruption, yielding 38.8 million data points (92.7 MB). All electrical connections, including magnetic component modules, electrodes, and circuits, remained solid even after the power module was replaced. In addition, none of the components detached from the orthosis during the five-day experiment conducted outside the laboratory.

We also summarized some lessons about durability. For example, excessive pressing to the circuit boards installed on textile braces may cause the depression of copper wires under the extrusion of Pogo Pins. This irreversible deformation in extreme cases may lead to poor contact in subsequent use. Future iterations may solve this issue by improving the structural strength of the wires at contact points or patches. In addition, the straps of the orthosis covering the circuits help protect the electronic modules from being rubbed off due to intense slip friction.

8.3 Electrode Contact

EMG signals are sensitive to variations in electrode properties, such as changes in contact impedance. Therefore, we compared textile electrodes to commercial pre-gelled electrodes to evaluate whether the textile electrodes make sufficient contact with human skin (Figure 19 ). For our experiment, we placed both the EMG textile electrodes (20 mm in diameter) and the commercial pre-gelled electrodes (30 mm in diameter, with a hydrogel part of 16 mm in diameter) mounted on a wrist hand orthosis at the same locations on the human skin. We used an automatic LCR data bridge to record the contact impedance over a frequency range of 10 Hz–10 kHz.

Our experiment demonstrates that the textile electrodes are appropriate for our E-Orthosis applications because their contact impedance is comparable to that of commercially available pre-gelled electrodes, and they provide greater user comfort and convenience.

8.4 Efficiency and Cost of E-Orthosis

As we seek to make E-Orthosis widely available, its cost is a crucial design consideration. We estimated the cost of each application based on the manufacturing of 1,000 electronic components of E-Orthosis. This cost estimate in Figure 20 includes magnetic connectors and circuit traces, as well as the electronic component modules (including the BOM cost of circuits, the PCB assembly cost, and the cost of sourcing and shipping). Since testing and fabrication of circuit traces occur in the lab and are difficult to calculate, they are omitted. Due to the cross-client reusability of magnet electronics component modules, the capital expenditure for a single patient can be significantly reduced.

9 EXPERT REVIEWS & CO-DESIGN ITERATION

To gain insights into the suitability of E-Orthosis in the field of clinical orthoses and rehabilitation, we conducted a hands-on review with a professional orthotist and an interview with a rehabilitation physician. These healthcare professionals play crucial roles in the process of customizing orthoses, with the physician prescribing the specific requirements and the orthotist fabricating the orthosis accordingly.

9.1 Orthotist Expert

To evaluate the learning cost of E-Orthosis construction kits for orthotists, we conducted a hands-on review and a semi-structured interview with a professional orthotist who is the (director of the orthosis department of a rehabilitation studio and has 16 years of experience in customizing orthoses). We took our kits, samples, and application prototypes to his workspace and invited him to augment an orthosis using the given kits. To minimize disruptions to the orthotist, the study consisted of two sessions conducted on two separate days. Two researchers facilitated both sessions. (1) In Session 1, we briefly introduced the orthosis fabrication process using our kit. Then the orthotist practiced the following steps using our kit: punching holes, embedding ports, ironing traces, and snapping modules and electrodes. This allowed the orthotist to gain hands-on experience with our kit and its various components. (2) In Session 2, We assigned the orthotist the task of augmenting an orthosis according to instructions: augmenting a lower limb splint (same as the application example in Section 7.2) with electronics of pressure sensing (referring to the application example in Section 7.1). We encouraged the orthotist to express his thoughts during the whole process. After the study, we applied thematic analysis to flesh out the observed user behaviors and the interview transcript. The study lasted approximately three hours, with each session lasting 1.5 hours. We present unique findings afforded by the evaluation.

The orthotist expressed confidence in the potential of wearable electronic technologies for orthoses but also pointed out that existing alternatives are inadequate. He emphasized the significance of developing smart orthoses, including research and commercial innovation, such as pressure-monitoring smart scoliosis orthoses for parents of teenagers. However, he also noted that physicians and orthotists traditionally lack technical expertise in circuits and programming, stating that “there are few orthotists capable of creating smart orthoses like me. He was excited to experience E-Orthosis, which has the potential to empower him to build smart orthoses independently, and commented that “it is fresh and surprising for me to build smart orthosis in person.”

Overall, the orthotist found the kit easy to use and completed hole punching, port embedding, circuit ironing, component snapping, and electrode screwing. He also completed augmenting an orthosis with pressure monitoring in 30 minutes (as shown in Figure 21a ). The electronics worked well when the orthotists transferred the components modules from our original application prototype sample to the orthosis he augmented. During the practice, he spontaneously adjusted the angle of the circuit iron-on tool and quickly learned to control the heating temperature and speed. He also understood that the Teflon tapes wrapped around the head were used to prevent the ironing head from sticking to the circuit trace, as these tapes are commonly utilized in the heating process of thermoplastic material during orthosis customization. The orthotist appreciated the easy-to-attach magnet components with signal lights for their rapid visual feedback on the work state, adding, “I hope to save time and cost for future clients by reusing these components.”

When asked about the challenges and recommendations for the fabrication process, the orthotist reported that the main issue was the occasional error in the punching location. To address this, he suggested optimizing the punch head by adding a long positioning pin in the middle to improve punching precision, citing using a similar drill head for prostheses fabrication in his workplace (as shown in Figure 21c ). Another recommendation is to improve the efficiency of the fabrication process further. The orthotist proposed a fabrication pipeline that may simplify alignment and ironing work. This could involve aligning and magnetically combining the magnetic connectors, circuit traces, and component modules to form an entire circuit bandage on a flat operator table in advance, then embedding the electronic bandage on the pre-punched orthosis with corresponding holes. After detaching the component modules, the entire circuit can be ironed. Other suggestions include marking the installation direction of the magnetic connectors to aid in the assembly process.

When asked about patients’ acceptance of the augmented orthosis, the orthotist reported concerns about the robustness of magnetic connection in clinical situations, stating that: “The magnetic component modules may easily rub off when patients wear them in bed, which may interrupt monitoring." He recommended adding a protective shell or layer to component modules. He also suggested optimizing the patient's interaction experience with our modules by adding easy-to-use buttons or knobs and a digital screen that displays more information.

The orthotist exceeded our expectations by exhibiting maker identity and actively optimizing our tools using crafty action and fabrication resources in his workspace. He was concerned about the discomfort when patients wear the orthosis because of the internal sensors. He proposed punching holes in a foam layer, typically used on the inner surface of orthoses, and embedding the internal sensor in the hollow of the foam pad. Furthermore, he proposed adding a customized foam plaster to orthoses without a foam layer to provide a transition edge for the internal sensors. As shown in Figure 21d , the orthotist beveled a small piece of foam with a sander in his workspace to achieve a smooth and conforming surface.

9.2 Rehabilitation Physician Expert

We interviewed a rehabilitation physician with nine years of experience in a hospital's rehabilitation department to gain a deeper understanding of our approach from a medical standpoint. We introduced the functionality and purpose of E-Orthosis and demonstrated the functionality of modules, pipelines, tools, evaluation results, and application examples during the hour-long session.

The rehabilitation physician emphasized the promising potential of smart orthoses. He proposed that our smart orthoses will improve patients' quality of live during rehabilitation, particularly those with unconsciousness or hemiplegia. “Currently, we rely merely on the subjective scale assessment report or patient behavior tests to assess muscle strength and fatigue. It would be very welcome if the patients can quantitatively perceive the progress of their muscle through an app and correspondingly adjust their rehabilitation training in their daily life.” However, he had no practical experience with smart orthoses in his current hospital. When asked about barriers to the wider adoption of smart orthoses in clinical practice, he reported the potential high labor and time costs for doctors and capital costs for patients. However, he added that the low-cost, easy-to-use modular construction kit has promising potential to change this context.

Regarding the augmenting effect, the physician praised the modules’ small size and convenience for daily use. In addition, he expressed concern about the cost of each application and praised the use of magnetic electronic components sustainably.

Finally, we discussed the positioning assistance roles for sensors and electrodes if E-Orthosis is utilized in clinical practice. Physicians will apply their clinical expertise, “We (physician) will assess which muscle of the patient to monitor based on the diagnosis, and ask the orthotist to arrange sensors to the corresponding positions, which can be easily integrated into the existing communication process of orthosis customization.”

10 DISCUSSION

In this section, we discuss the limitations of our methodology and the real-world impact and prospects for E-Orthosis.

Minimal board size. While the connection port and ironed circuit conform to the majority of orthoses’ curved surfaces, the component edges may not adhere to extremely curved subsurface. Future iterations can resolve this problem by further miniaturizing the size of electronic components and corresponding magnet connection ports. This will also reduce the impact of punching on the strength of the orthosis structure.

Fabrication efficiency and mass manufacturing of construction kit. Most kits E-Orthosis provided (such as magnetic connection port, patch, PCB boards, iron tool, and hot punch tool) are easy for scalable production. In addition, industrial laser marking machines with a roller can increase the circuit stack's production efficiency. The scalability of these modules and tools enables the deployment of E-Orthosis to more clinics and rehabilitation facilities.

Power management. Currently, electronics are powered by rechargeable commercial batteries, and the power module can be quickly replaced. We will offer additional battery modules with varying capacities based on demand. In addition, we may investigate alternative energy supply methods, such as piezoelectric energy collection from vibration and thermoelectric energy collection from heat transfer.

Operation safety. Hot punching is a common operation in orthosis customization, but certain materials, such as PP, may produce slight fumes when heated. To prevent inhaling these fumes, we advise orthotists and other operators to install a fume hood or other exhaust equipment in their workspace.

Wearability and user acceptance. Currently, we understand the requirements of patients from orthotists and healthcare physicians. In the future, we intend to directly contact patients who regularly wear orthoses and further evaluate the comfort, appearance, and compliance of smart orthoses augmented by our approach.

Codesign in clinical practice. As stated in the expert review, healthcare professionals proposed ideas for developing new smart orthoses based on application examples and function lists for component modules. In the future, we intend to further leverage the clinical expertise of healthcare professionals by incorporating actual client-clinician interactions into the E-Orthosis fabrication process. For example, we will collaborate with physicians and orthotists to design more smart orthoses for their clients. This will help us understand the advantages and limitations of our method and equipment in a clinical setting.

Software toolkit for hardware programming and visualization. Due to the reusability of the embedded programming in the electronics controller for patients with similar illnesses, a code library of hardware configurations and wireless visualization will facilitate the rapid customization of smart orthoses. Hence, there is a possibility of developing a software interface for healthcare professionals to select and configure the function and visualization of E-Orthosis-manufactured smart orthoses.

Compatibility with wider types of orthoses. While our approach works well for attaching circuits to regular static orthoses or stable regions of dynamic orthoses, we do not currently support soldering circuit traces across different parts of dynamic orthoses connected by coils, springs, or ropes. Additionally, the placement of electronic components should avoid metal materials such as steel hinges, as it can be challenging to punch holes in metal.

Expansion of electronic function. The current version of the controller module can only connect a single sensing module, necessitating the use of multiple controller modules for orthoses requiring multiple sensors. Because existing sensor modules contain only a sensor chip with a fixed I2C communication address, preventing them from being connected in parallel. In future work, a small microcontroller should be embedded in each sensor/actuator module, such as the electronics shown in [ 10 , 21 ]. Moreover, there is a potential to expand the function of component modules for orthosis augment. For example, mechanical modules such as pneumatic actuators and corresponding control modules can be integrated to assist the users of dynamic orthoses in moving their impaired limbs. And flexible stretch sensing modules can be added to measure the traction force during rehabilitation.

CONCLUSIONS

We introduced E-Orthosis, a novel fabrication method for augmenting off-the-shelf orthoses with advanced digital capabilities in healthcare. A collection of modular electronic accessories with magnetic and spring connections facilitates rapid attachment and cross-client use. Snap-integrated textile electrodes enable long-term EMG monitoring at home with greater repeatability and precision without needing medical personnel. In addition, we offer a hot punch tool with replaceable modular heads for embedding magnetic connection ports and electrodes, as well as an iron tool for quickly and securely installing circuit traces on the 3D surface of orthoses. It has been demonstrated that our method is compatible with curved surfaces, versatile materials, and substrates of varying thicknesses. Repeated swinging tests and a five-day field wear study showed the robustness and durability of mechanical and electrical connections. We further demonstrated the versatility of our toolkit by implementing healthcare computing applications for upgrading orthoses. An expert review identified the low learning costs and clinical practice potential.
