Becoming a Speleologist: Design Implications for Coordination in Wild Outdoor Environments

Eleonora Mencarini , Intelligent Interfaces and Interaction, Bruno Kessler Foundation, Italy, mencarini@fbk.eu
Tommaso Zambon , Department of Computer Science and Engineering, University of Bologna, Italy, tommaso.zambon3@unibo.it

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581545
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Learning outdoor sports entails acquiring physical skills, managing gear, and coordinating with others. We investigated how speleologists are trained to explore underground caves. We interviewed 15 instructors and 10 trainees to understand the main problems that may occur during training cave trips. Our findings show that stressful situations are linked to beginners’ difficulties applying new gestures and procedures - on which their progression and safety depend - and coordinating with others when they are out of sight. It emerged that group awareness and communication are pivotal for their tranquility. Yet, the underground environment makes communicating very hard. This study led to the elaboration of design implications for technology supporting awareness, communication, and coordination in speleology training, which draw from and enrich previous literature on coordination in the wild, as it may happen while performing outdoor sports or during search-and-rescue operations.

1 INTRODUCTION

Speleology is an exploratory activity based on the discovery and study of underground cavities for the purpose of scientific knowledge. By exploring caves and investigating their formation is possible to understand the hydrology, geology, and geography of a certain area. However, exploring caves is not an activity within everyone's reach. Having been formed by water erosion of rock, caves are hostile environments: they are narrow, dark, humid, isolated, and almost devoid of vegetation or animals. Due to their inhospitality and isolation, they require to be visited by groups of people properly trained. In fact, several people are needed to carry the necessary gear to proceed into a cave (such as ropes, drills, bolts, and emergency kits) and call for help in case an accident occurs.

In this paper, we analyze the coordination, communication, and awareness problems that groups of speleologists face during a cave trip. In particular, we focused on the difficulties faced by beginner speleologists. This subgroup was chosen as we assume their unfamiliarity with the practice may amplify problems and perceptions that would happen to expert speleologists too, when under pressure or in difficult situations (e.g., during difficult passages or physical ailments after spending many hours underground). We attended the theoretical lessons and interviewed participants and instructors from two courses for beginner speleologists. Our findings show that the isolated and harsh nature of caves is, on the one hand, one of the main sources of pleasure and motivation to practice speleology and, on the other hand, the main reason for danger. Caves’ shape leads cavers to alternate on ropes and in narrow passages; therefore, groups split up, and often beginners find themselves alone while performing movements and procedures on which their progression and safety depend. For this reason, group awareness and instructors' presence, in terms of physical distance and feedback frequency, are pivotal for trainees' tranquility and thus learning to move safely and be autonomous.

Despite being a niche practice, speleology lies at the intersection of various human activities such as outdoor sports, citizen science, team coordination, and rope access. Since we focused on beginners’ acquisition of skills and procedures for underground progression, we build upon and aim to contribute to HCI research on the role of technology in experiencing the outdoors [ 21 , 25 , 33 ], learning outdoor sports [ 20 , 22 ], and supporting group coordination in the wild [ 9 , 13 , 16 ]. Our study shows the difficulties of learning new skills in a challenging and dark environment and offers a series of design implications for digital devices supporting the awareness, communication, and coordination of speleology groups with different levels of expertise. In our view, the results of this work can set the basis for the investigation of all those situations where learning in a wild environment and coordination of a sparse group are required, e.g., rope work practices such as forestry, rock scaling, and work-at-height.

The paper is structured as follows: in the second section, we provide background information about the practice of speleology; in the third section, we outline the related HCI work in the outdoor sports and coordination in the wild fields; section 4 describes our study procedure and results in the form of four themes that emerged from the interview analysis; section 5 discusses the findings and proposes a few implications for the design of technological devices supporting group coordination in the wild among people with different levels of expertise. The paper concludes with a summary of the main contributions provided, limitations, and future direction of this work.

2 CASE STUDY BACKGROUND: SPELEOLOGY

Speleology (or 'caving' as it is often called) is a serious-leisure outdoor activity [ 8 ] practiced by amateurs who explore caves and aim to discover new ones for scientific purposes. Since finding new caves (or branches in the already known ones) requires enduring physical performance and mastery of specific tools and progression techniques, speleology has been defined as a sporting science [ 6 , 23 , 28 ]. The 'sports' part refers to the physical performance needed to pursue the knowledge generation goal, while the 'science' part refers to speleologists’ willingness to know more about how caves formed and influence the surrounding area, e.g., by providing water to the local villages. Speleologists carry on the scientific purpose of their activity by organizing regional, national, and international meetings where they share their findings with other speleologists and academics in the fields of geology, hydrology, archaeology, underground biology, etc. For this reason, it can be considered a form of voluntary and self-organized citizen science practice [ 23 ] where the people practicing it are local experts who set their own objectives, collect and analyze data, and disseminate findings [ 3 ]. Because developing speleological knowledge depends on exploration, and the greater the explorers' physical abilities, the greater the likelihood that more caves will be discovered and surveyed [ 28 ], newcomers are trained for both the physical endeavor and the scientific competencies. In this study, we focus on the sports aspects of speleology.

The great physical effort entailed by caves is due to their cold, narrow, and dark environment, which requires much stamina, energy, and personal equipment to be crossed. All cavers need to wear a harness with shoulder straps, a helmet with a headlamp, boots, and a specific suit (Figure 1 ).

Furthermore, to move up and down in vertical traits (pits), speleologists use alternative tools along ropes: when descending towards the bottom, they use a device called 'descender', while, when ascending to go back to the surface, they use a hand and chest clamps, plus a sling as a stirrup for the foot (see Figure 2 ). The ascent is especially physically demanding because it comes at the end of the trip when most of the energy has already been spent approaching the cave and exploring it, and cavers need to push with their foot in a foot loop to gain every meter up along the rope.

Finally, for organizational and safety reasons, speleology needs to be done in a group. Organizational aspects are that cave explorations or visits require bringing a lot of shared equipment such as ropes, bolts, measurement tools, and cameras and, thus, enough people to carry all the gear is needed; safety aspects refer to the procedure to follow in case of accidents. In Italy, according to the national mountaineering association that regulates the speleological practice, caves should be visited by groups of 4 people minimum so that in case one gets injured, another speleologist can stay with them while the other two go out of the cave to call the rescue service together, as one of them could get injured too due to stress or hurry.

3 RELATED WORK

Besides being a niche practice, speleology is an under-investigated activity in HCI. To our knowledge, only two HCI works investigated it: Schuchardt et al. [ 31 ] conducted a study where 24 cavers assessed the effectiveness of Immersive Virtual Environments in providing a spatial understanding of the complex structures of caves, while Mencarini et al. [ 23 ] investigated the practice from the experts' point of view, highlighting the twofold nature of speleology being both a sport and a scientific activity. For this reason, we decided to frame our work within the boundaries of HCI research in related fields, i.e., HCI and outdoor adventure sports, learning outdoor sports, and coordination and awareness in the wild.

3.1 HCI and outdoor adventure sports

Most HCI studies in the outdoor adventure sports domain have shown that these sportspeople are not keen on introducing technology in their practice since they see their immersion in nature as a liberation from the frenzy of modern life, of which technology is part [ 14 ]. Investigating several outdoor sports, Ahtinen et al. [ 1 ] noticed that the most appreciated function in apps for tracking physical performance is that of personal archives because they allow sportspeople to monitor their performance and set new goals over time. In the case of mountaineering, technologies easing environmental challenges and reducing the technical skills required to tackle them are perceived as cheating and thus strongly discouraged since the feeling of mastery over natural elements motivates practitioners and helps them comply with the rules of their community of practice. In such a context, technologies for recording performance are better welcomed because they allow peer recognition [ 7 ]. Similarly, Mencarini et al. [ 21 ] found that the will to gain all the necessary competencies to face an outdoor adventure is at the root of the reluctance to use wearable devices in climbing. Climbers fear that these devices may contradict the core values of the climbing practice, i.e., self-efficacy, mutual trust between partners, and the ability to manage the unexpected events that an adventure may bring along. These authors found a possibility for technology bridging the communication gap between climbers and belayers or beginner climbers and instructors.

Sometimes, the investigation of sports practices leads to unexpected and counterintuitive findings. For example, Tholander and Nylander [ 33 ] noticed that, to some extent, outdoor sportspeople enjoy the annoying feelings of pain and fatigue that these sports entail and suggest considering those sensations and the overall sportspeople's personal lived experience in the design of technology. Following this approach, the same authors [ 27 ] have proposed an app for people practicing orienteering based on real-time GPS data to provide information about the deviance from the planned track. The idea is to use real-time sports data based on specific factors, skills, or techniques to enrich the sport experience. In this way, athletes can appropriate the technology into their sporting practice, and the technology can renew some aspects of the sport. In the same direction goes the work of Posti et al. [ 30 ], who developed an app suggesting solitary paths to ensure solitude for hikers.

As for speleology, the work of Mencarini et al. [ 23 ] shows a different attitude towards technology: thanks to the scientific vocation of the practice, cavers are very much interested and open to using technology to survey and report their underground discoveries.

These works show the importance of carefully understanding the outdoor sports practice before designing a technology since it could be seen as a change in the sports’ nature and, thus, what practitioners search for when practicing it. Building upon these findings - but also encouraged by those of Mencarini et al. [ 23 ], which show an interest in technology by the speleology community - we undertook a qualitative investigation to gain a thorough understanding of the practice.

3.2 Learning outdoor sports

In outdoor sports such as climbing, surfing, and skiing, immediate contact between trainer and trainee is not always possible, and this distance prevents using classic teaching techniques such as imitation or modification of the trainee's posture, as may happen in other sports such as dance classes or indoor team sports. HCI research has addressed the topic of outdoor sports learning by exploring how technologies could provide augmented feedback, i.e., feedback from an external source rather than the internal feelings of the sportsperson, or feedforward, i.e., instructions about what to do rather than the assessment of something already done. A few works have compared different sensory modalities, i.e., audio, visual, and haptics, to prove the most effective to communicate augmented feedback in a specific sport. Results showed that audio feedback performs worst in the case of swimmers, requiring the longest reaction time from the athletes to adjust their movements [ 4 ], while luminous feedback can help swimmers keep the right direction when swimming in open waters [ 18 ]. Conversely, sound was very convenient to sonify skiers' center of gravity and helped them understand their balance [ 15 ]. As for climbers, visual feedback is hard to perceive if shown on a wrist-worn wearable [ 19 ]. These studies show that the suitability of augmented feedback is strongly influenced by the sports, the environment in which it is performed, and where the devices are placed.

A modality more successful than others is tactile instructions, usually given through vibrating motors attached to the sportsperson's body. It has been used with cross-country skiers [ 26 ], snowboarders [ 32 ], and climbers [ 21 ]. Tactile feedback or feedforward can be distributed along the body areas that need to move, modulated in frequency and intensity, and does not hamper the perception of the environment since it does not occupy hearing or sight and usually is easily perceived.

Although several studies have acknowledged that outdoor sports cannot be reduced to motor skills learning since they consist of communities, rituals, symbolism, and emotional involvement [ 10 , 29 , 37 ], very few HCI studies have included these aspects in their investigations. One example is Mencarini et al. [ 20 , 22 ], who considered how emotions affect climbing performance and proposed a wearable system that bridges the communication between instructor and trainee. By allowing instructors and trainees to communicate over a distance, Mencarini et al.'s work distinguish itself not only for designing to support the psychological aspects of sports but also because the feedback the trainee receives comes from the instructor rather than being automatically triggered by sensors, thus strengthening their relationship of trust.

In conclusion, all these studies offer an inspiration to address the problem of distance between instructor and trainee - which is present in speleology as well - from different angles.

3.3 Coordination and awareness in the wild

Part of HCI and CSCW research addressed the challenges of group coordination during leisure activities or emergencies outdoors. As for leisure activities such as sports, by comparing the attitude of trail runners, climbers, and skiers, regarding information sharing, Fedosov et al. [ 12 ] found that the more social and dispersed the activity is, the higher the need to share information among sports partners. Unlike runners who typically go alone and, even when in a group, do not need much coordination, and climbers who necessarily practice in pairs since they need a belayer, skiers need to coordinate with partners the most since they may go at different speeds or on different slopes and, thus, the group may easily split. Skiers need to share information about their location, status, and hazards they encounter with the rest of the group for safety and organizational reasons. Indeed, most HCI works on coordination and information sharing outdoors focused on skiers. Weilenmann and Holmquist [ 36 ] prototyped a wearable device that sensed the presence of other similar devices within a range of 100 meters of distance and was used to locate skiers belonging to the same group. Fedosov et al. [ 11 ] elicited skiers’ information exchange preferences finding that personal location, photos, and videos recording the experience and contextual information was what interested them the most. Subsequently, Fedosov et al. [ 13 ] developed an Augmented Reality Head-Mounted Display where ski friends could share personalized content on a ski resort map.

Other HCI and CSCW works have investigated coordination in wilderness emergencies, which may have severe consequences due to geographical isolation. Mueller and Pell [ 24 ] articulated the possible roles that technology can take in remote environments in relation to expected or unexpected events. These are the ‘coach role’, guiding for a better performance during foreseen situations; the ‘rescuer role’ providing emergency services during unexpected events (as avalanche beacons do); the ‘documentarian role’ for recording expected events with a high experiential value; and finally, the ‘mentor role’ supporting the user's reflection on the felt experience during unexpected events. Other works focused on supporting the coordination of Wilderness Search and Rescue (WSAR) teams, where collaboration and promptness are fundamental. For example, Desjardins et al. [ 9 ] investigated avalanche rescuers, which are co-located, while Alharthi et al. [ 2 ] addressed sparse WSAR team coordination focusing on the centrality of geospatial information and, thus, of digital and physical maps. Jones et al. [ 16 ] highlighted the need for shared implicit communication and awareness to maintain a shared mental model of the operations’ progress and, in a subsequent study [ 17 ], they designed RescueCASTR, an interface for WSAR field teams to stream live video and photos periodically through wearable cameras to support the command's contextual awareness.

These studies highlight that, although people engaging in outdoor adventure sports are not inclined to use technology because they enjoy adventure uncertainty, things change in situations of danger. In emergencies, people wish to be in control and are open to relying on prompt technology providing the right information to recover from a crisis. We build upon the literature presented here to address the design challenge of communication between people with different experience levels in dispersed groups and isolated places.

4 STUDY

With this study, we investigated how new speleologists are trained to gain an understanding of the main difficulties entailed by the speleological practice amplified by the unfamiliarity of newcomers who have not yet developed personal strategies to cope with the physical and emotional challenges provided by the underground environment. As for the methodology, due to the explorative nature of this study, we opted for semi-structured interviews, which we deemed more appropriate to offer an in-depth view of participants’ motivations, impressions, and lived experiences.

4.1 Participants’ recruitment and procedure

We contacted two speleology groups that organize courses in the Italian province of Trento. Each group was affiliated with one of the two national speleological associations: the Società Speleological Italiana (SSI) and the Scuola Nazionale di Speleologia of the Italian Alpine Club. For the whole duration of the courses, we took part in the theoretical lessons as we wanted to understand technical terms and discourse about progression techniques, tools, and the underground environment, as well as the key teachings and values conveyed to the newcomers. These lessons represented an opportunity to gather the necessary information to communicate effectively and relate with the participants during the interviews and deepen our insights during the analysis phase.

Then, we conducted semi-structured interviews with instructors and trainees to explore the caving, teaching, and learning experiences from both perspectives. Among the course attendants, 15 instructors (3F, 12M; average age = 40 years, Standard Dev.= 14.3) and 10 beginner spelologists (6F, 4M, average age =34 years, Standard Dev.= 13.19) accepted to be interviewed (the detailed demographics of our informants is illustrated in Table 1 and Table 2 ). Within the instructors' category, we included three persons (whose anonymization is marked with an E before the number) who were not certified instructors but were expert cavers and supported the instructors during the outdoor trips (e.g., by rigging the cave before the others started descending it). Moreover, we highlighted the participation of some instructors in the Cave Rescue Corps as we thought this expertise may have influenced their capabilities in stress management and risk prevention.

4.2 Analysis

Three main phases characterized the interview analysis process: transcription, coding, and comparison.

Transcription . Overall, almost 18 hours of interviews were recorded. On average, instructors’ interviews lasted 1 hour and 15 minutes, while trainees’ ones lasted 40 minutes. All interviews were transcribed integrally without correcting or censuring colloquialisms, dialects, or common sayings because, even though conversation analysis was not employed in this study, some of its elements were kept into account. Given the key role of emotions in beginners’ caving experience, the conversational nuances, forms of expression, pauses, and laughter have been considered to preserve the integrity of the narrations.

Coding . For the analysis, the Thematic Analysis [ 5 ] method was chosen since it allows finding links between the experiences, opinions, and ideas within the studied group and, thus, identifying relationships between concepts and data. Following this method, the two authors read and analyzed the transcripts independently in search of accounts of the challenges and key aspects of teaching and learning how to progress in the underground environment. They marked quotations of interest with open codes using the Atlas.ti 8 software. After conducting the open coding, the authors developed the axial codes, i.e., they clustered the open codes into 'families of codes'.

Comparison . Then, the second author reviewed the generated macro-categories of codes and discussed inconsistencies in interpretations with the first author. Inconsistencies were mainly related to the choice of labels to express certain concepts and whether the codes belonged to one or more families of codes. In total, 142 open codes and 6 axial codes were identified. The definition of the four main themes explaining the newcomers’ difficulties and the group management strategies emerged from the final discussion among the authors.

It is worth specifying that the first author of the paper has no direct experience of speleology but has experience of mountaineering, while the second author has attended a caving course himself in-between the interview analysis and the paper writing. The knowledge the second author gained during his course served to double-check the interpretation while writing.

4.3 Results

Both courses took place in Spring, as it is a good moment to visit caves in mountain areas where snow may cover entrances in Winter and floods may happen in Summer. They were structured in a very similar way since they followed the national guidelines. Typically, instructors and trainees would meet twice a week: on a working-day evening for theory lessons and at the weekend for field trips. Before entering caves, both courses offered an intermediate step to learn the progression procedures, movements, and gear handling on a rock wall during the day (Figure 3 ). There, trainees had the chance to experience techniques required to move along ropes in bright light and with an instructor following them step-by-step on a parallel rope. Cave trips started the weekend immediately after, while the theoretical lessons were still going on, and they unfolded progressively by starting with horizontal caves and moving towards more complicated and vertical ones.

We organized the findings from the interviews into four themes that articulate the needs and difficulties of beginner speleologists: i) ‘Venturing in a parallel world’, which explains the role of isolation as the main driver for practicing speleology as well as the main cause of danger and speleologists’ relationship with technology; ii) ‘A sparse group’, identifying the progression modalities at the basis for the need for awareness, communication, and coordination; iii) ‘Emergencies and special communication needs’, collecting the different types of communication that are exchanged during a cave trip with beginners; iv) ‘Teaching styles and learning needs’, where the different preferences around support and autonomy expressed by beginners are reported.

4.3.1 Venturing in a parallel world. Despite cold, dark, damp, and sometimes very narrow, speleologists are fascinated by caves. Their fascination is due to caves being such remote and challenging places, and their ability to master underground exploration makes them feel privileged: few people can go there and come back safely. This fascination has been reported by the trainees who took part in our study and had experienced non-touristic caves for the very first time as well:

“Just the fact of going where most people can't because you need to know how to move along the rope, you need all the gear... Those are places that remain a little... Indiana Jones' style” (T03);

“We explore unknown places that are the last bastions of the unknown since nowadays every centimeter of the Earth is monitored by satellites” (I04).

This feeling of adventure resonates with Mencarini et al.'s findings [ 23 ], in which some expert speleologists affirmed to feel like astronauts exploring the last unspoiled frontiers on Earth. The comparison with space explorations fits the almost complete absence of flora and fauna and the strong presence of darkness, silence, and rocks typical of caves. However, such charming isolation can be a major obstacle to problem-solving in case of accidents. No WI-FI, GPS or smartphone signal can reach inside caves because the rock walls prevent signals from propagating.

"[...] Maybe [caves] are the only place where nobody can hear you. I mean, the best thing probably is that you finally find a place where nobody can reach you for no reason, but at the same time, this could be a problem because if something happens down there, how can you tell those above the surface? So yes, on the one hand, it is pleasant not to be found, but on the other, it can be potentially lethal" (T03).

In fact, once inside the cave, they need to be self-sufficient because there is no way to communicate with the outside. If an accident happens, the group must split, and some must return to the surface to call for help. As a result, knowing the procedures, handling gear properly, and caring for each other are essential for the speleology practice: inside the cave, the group becomes a close-knit team.

The pleasure derived from exploring isolated places, sometimes for the first time, leads speleologists to be heavy users of technology for documenting, measuring, and mapping caves to share their experience with a wider public. Participants in our study mentioned using tools and digital devices borrowed from other fields, such as endoscope cameras typically used by plumbers to see if a crack leads to a big tunnel, and 3D laser scanners (called Disto™X) typically used by architects to measure the cave. Furthermore, they enjoy experimenting with different ways to document caves, such as taking photos and videos with cameras or drones.

“I can't take good pictures underground, but I enjoy taking videos” (I09).

Regarding the progression, they were more cautious in welcoming new technology because this type of simplification needs to be balanced with the risk of encouraging not adequately fit and trained people to go into caves and then not be able to go back to the surface on their own in case technology fails. Nevertheless, they have a history of self-made and borrowed gear also in this domain:

"The descending gear we use is inspired by the safety equipment used in works at height" (I10).

"Bolts were not invented by free climbers, but by the building industry. Then, speleologists [spread them]" (E07).

And, regarding digital devices, they wished to have a technology showing them their localization in the cave:

“It would be useful to have a digital map of the cave and know where you are on the map. I don't know on what technology such a device could be based since GPS does not reach inside caves, but still, it would be nice to have it” (T01).

4.3.2 A sparse group. Going through vertical passages requires specific techniques that set the pace of cave progression. Long pits are rigged with several ropes for various reasons: i) there might not be a rope long enough to descend the whole pit; ii) the rope should not rub against the rock (otherwise, it risks breaking); iii) ropes can be climbed by one person at a time. By securing several consecutive ropes to the rock, it is possible to have multiple people ascending or descending, each one on a different rope segment, called rebelay .

Moving along these vertical traits is one of the beginners’ main difficulties because it requires skillful use of gear to be safe and move efficiently (see Figure 4 ). For this reason, initially, progression up and down the ropes is experienced where instructors can easily rig two parallel ropes and accompany the trainee side by side. However, caves’ shape does not always allow trainees to be accompanied side by side by an instructor. In those cases, instructors and trainees alternate on rebelays so that each trainee is looked after by two instructors, one above and one below them.

Coordination . To give way on the rope to the next one, caving partners use the command ‘free!'. The second most used command used is “rock!”, which is shouted every time something falls and may hit the people below.

“The most important command beginners need to know is the ‘free!’. Once you overcome an anchor point or reach the end of the pit, you must shout 'free' to let your partners know that they can proceed. If you don't say it, the next one will stand there dazed, waiting who knows how long. The ‘free' command is fundamental. Then, one thing I learned […] is that it is impossible to hear words properly when you are in deep pits, so sometimes you end up saying just the vowels […]. If I hear the sound ‘ee’ I can say ‘ok, good. He has arrived’” (I04).

Besides coordinating in the turn-taking on the rope, cavers need to coordinate at a much higher distance. Groups of cavers tend to split up because often there is not enough space to host the entire group; the longer people wait, the cooler they feel; and every person has their own pace due to their fitness level or the weight to transport (e.g., carrying more gear, water, food, etc.). Especially when climbing up to return to the surface, the progression tends to show the major differences between the individuals, the ascent being the most physically demanding part of the activity. To avoid these problems, everyone tries to proceed constantly, keeping their own pace, and the group scatters. Still, a general sense of the others’ location must be maintained:

"Attention should always be paid to how dispersed the group is; this doesn't mean everybody should walk 3 meters away from each other. The rule I follow is that the last person should never be left alone. The last could be slower because of a heavier backpack" (E03).

Communication. Communication happens only by voice when people are close to each other because, often, the cave structure presents meanders in all directions, waterfalls or water drippings, and echoes, which can hinder sound propagation. Lately, the use of walkie-talkies has become popular in many mountaineering activities, but, once again, in caves, they result in almost no utility since their signal cannot propagate easily. The instructors we interviewed used walkie-talkies only a few times during some specific activities, such as taking pictures for dissemination purposes because they work only in specific conditions, that is, in wide spaces free from obstacles:

“I used them once on a photo shooting trip. We were 4 [people], but we had only two walkie-talkies. I stood at the top of a pit and had a walkie-talkie, while another person at the bottom had the other one. In that case, they were very useful because we were on an 80-meter-deep pit, and usually, in those situations, when you shout, the other hears only ‘uououo’, which means nothing, or you go with viva voce” (I05).

Awareness. Knowing where the others are and seeing and hearing each other is important to know that everything and everybody is ok and let the tension drop. Since caves are dark, the others' presence is perceived by hearing their noises or seeing the headlamps' light. Hearing voices is enough to know that someone is near and available if needed and can improve the mood while underground:

R: "How did instructors support trainees practically?

T07: Well, with their voice... They used their voice a lot.

R: What does 'with their voice' mean?

T07: That they speak all the time. Sometimes, they even shout to make people hear their presence. […]

R: So, they make you feel their presence, their closeness...

T07: Yes, exactly. And I think that's important. You can see, but hearing helps you a lot. Hearing that somebody is there all the time... For example, I12 keeps singing all the time.

R: Really? Does he sing? What songs does it sing?

T07: Whatever! He makes up the songs. He sings from the beginning till the end. It's nice... Well, it can get annoying after a while, but still, he keeps company. He is always there. You know that I12 is always present. […] In my opinion, silence can cause uneasiness and might be scary in a cave, [...] it can trigger fear. Caves are closed places, silent, without animals, without nature (unless you consider rock nature), and I think that hearing people's voices can reassure you”.

Seeing each other headlamps helps to feel reassured of not being alone too: “[In Lamar cave] The way down is a spiral and, since I was one of the last ones to go, I could see the headlamps of those already on their way down, and it looked like a torchlight procession medieval tower” (T06).

4.3.3 Emergencies and special needs for communication. The problems beginner speleologists can encounter during a cave trip are physical, such as getting hurt or exhausted, or psychological, such as feeling fear or discouragement. Trainees’ most common physical problems are getting tired or weak due to dehydration or hunger. Saving energies is pivotal because caving has a peculiarity: the most demanding part of the activity comes at the end, i.e., descending into the cave is not as physically tiring as going back to the surface. Besides physical fatigue, other physical problems can be caused by the environment and cannot be controlled. For example, getting stuck in narrow passages, hurt by a falling stone, or hit by sudden water flows. When something like this happens, communication is spread by word of mouth until it reaches the emergency bag carrier. The emergency bag contains food, water, and a first-aid kit and is carried by an expert speleologist who occupies one end of the row: when going down, they follow the group, while when going up, they lead it so that, in case of need, they always must descend and not climb up to reach the others, which is more tiring.

Typically, physical and mental problems are intertwined, meaning that, often, physical exhaustion brings mental distress, i.e., fear, willingness to stop, and fear of not making it to the surface:

“I have noticed that often beginners’ psychological difficulties are due to not feeling at ease in the cave or being physically tired and starting to think 'why the heck am I here? I want to get out'. Then, this ripple effect occurs: the tired one slows down, wants to climb the least possible, and all the events chain leads them to become demoralized” (I11).

In the beginning, mental distress may be caused simply by the unfamiliarity with the environment and the activity:

“A [moment of] fear was due to the feeling of void. I am not familiar with that sensation because I don't have many occasions to go climbing, and I was wary of the gear (chest and hand rope clamps). I mean, rationally, I knew I should have trusted them, but my subconscious did not fully agree. […] The second type of fear [I experienced] was fear of fear itself. What I mean is that, when entering a cave, I am always anxious to encounter my limit at an arduous point such as a narrow passage. An unexpected claustrophobia attack in the middle of a hundreds-meter-deep cave would clearly have been a problem. That's why I was a bit anxious. Similarly, I was worried I could feel dizzy while descending pits. Then, slowly, with the experience, the anxiety has disappeared” (T09).

Instructors know these problems can happen and try to prevent them by keeping the morale high. Then, the strategy instructors adopt to contain moments of discouragement and fear before they become unmanageable is to talk to and reassure the stressed trainee:

“Well, think of it: if I am in a situation not familiar to me or a moment of crisis; I do not have self-confidence, I could want to delegate this confidence to someone else, but if this someone else is far from me and I can't see them, I feel abandoned and won't get out of the crisis. So, the first fundamental thing to do is to go close to the trainee in difficulty and have eye contact. Once you are close, either you show the person that they are safe and cannot fall, slide, or go anywhere, or - if they are not in a safe condition yet - you put them in it. Then, you ask open questions like 'What's up?' And probably they would say 'I don't feel good, I'm tired'. You need to let them vent. Only by doing so, they would realize the real motivations of the panic, and often these motivations are not much grounded. You cannot really calm them down. There is little you can do; you stay there until the person calms down” (I12).

Furthermore, managing the equipment is very stressful for beginners because their safety depends on the gear's correct use. Unfortunately, being at a distance does not help instructors realize when a trainee is having a moment of difficulty nor make it easy to provide instructions about what to do.

“[…] because of the long climbing segments, [the instructor] had to go first [and hang on the rope ahead], but at that point, we were so far apart that he could not hear me. So, I was on my own as he was way too far to intervene. At some point, I even made a serious mistake and fell for more than 5 meters and hit my elbow so hard I had bruises for two weeks” (T04).

And even when instructors notice and try to help, voice instructions are their only tool.

“The teacher cannot approach you to help you. So, you are always distant from your teacher, and they can just tell you by word what you should do, [like] 'pull this and move that'” (T10).

At times, trainees' stress level is so high that they cannot process instructions. In those moments, they are already thinking by themselves about what to do and reach a point of saturation where further information only confuses them. T10 had a moment of panic in one of the deepest pits they visited:

“The cave was huge, and people at the bottom were chatting, so the voice went back and forth; plus, there was water dropping, so even if [the instructor] spoke to me, I could not catch a single word. And I was moving wrong! [laughing]. She told me the same sentence so many times that eventually I understood what she meant. But even after that, I still thought I was doing it right. Then, I stopped for a while, focused, and asked myself: 'Ok. What does the sentence mean?' [and in the end] I could understand what I was doing wrong”.

All problems share the unpredictability factor. Instructors can leverage their experience to foresee and prevent moments of difficulty, but the darkness and the LED headlamps do not make it possible to see trainees' facial expressions because the strong light pointed at their faces makes them close their eyes. Therefore, trainees need to know themselves and tell their difficulties when they are still manageable.

4.3.4 Teaching styles and learning needs. Before entering the cave, every trainee is paired with an instructor. Pairs are not fixed; they might change on every cave trip, allowing trainees to experience different teaching styles. The relationship between instructor and trainee in the same pair tends to be informal to facilitate trust and communication. For example, often, instructors use irony or conversation to ease the stress provoked by the hostile and unfamiliar underground environment. During the first field trips, instructors try to be as close as possible to the assigned trainees to check how they are and whether they are doing the procedures well. Then, as time passes, instructors become less present either because caves do not allow them to stay close or because they must gradually leave the trainees alone to make them gain autonomy. Teachers' level of presence or absence - in terms of physical distance and feedback frequency - is the hardest aspect to manage in this dynamic towards trainees’ independence. The learning pace may differ depending on the trainees’ personalities, and empathy is necessary to find the right attunement. Nonetheless, instructors are volunteers with no educational background: “you become an instructor because you are technically good, not because of your empathy or teaching skills” (I01).

From our interviews, it emerged that some trainees preferred to be guided by instructors in what they had to do, while others wanted to try on their own first and often perceived instructors as too pressing. This latter cluster of trainees expressed the need to practice and improve their progression techniques by trial and error in hostile cave sections, even if it would have taken longer. Since they do not embody that specific know-how yet, they need to reflect on every action before putting it into practice. This type of trainee wishes for independence and prefers receiving feedback at the end of their attempt as 'knowledge of results' [ 38 ], that is, to receive feedback about what they have done once they have completed it. An example is provided by T01 when he states:

“If I did something stupid, you [the instructor] should stop me when I am about to go. Don't stop me before I make a mistake or when I haven't finished my attempt yet, because I might still have to go through the check procedure to see if everything is in place... I mean, let me finish!”.

Conversely, other trainees do not appreciate receiving feedback at the end of a procedure since they feel under exam. This type of trainee prefers to receive feedback during the execution of the procedure, as a 'knowledge of performance' [ 38 ], i.e., feedback that informs about the correctness of a sequence or a gesture:

“[Letting trainees try by themselves and then, only after, give feedback] is okay from time to time, but not too often. I noticed that it tends to make trainees nervous because they feel under pressure. Trainees are observed in silence, they make a mistake, and it is pointed out only at the end. So, you get the impression to be under examination. Conversely, if the instructor watches you and when you make a mistake, he immediately alerts you by saying, 'be careful, you are doing something wrong; you should do it in this other way’, it would look more like a friendly relationship, a simple matter of care for each other. It would give a less professorial impression. In my opinion, using irony and checking over what trainees are doing in a relaxed way would be better” (T09).

At the end of this continuum between autonomy and guidance, we can find trainees wishing for total guidance by instructors. These trainees wait for instructions (or feedforwards). Knowing that their safety is strictly linked to the correct execution of procedures that they do not embody yet pushes them to seek experts' guidance and confirmations:

"I do whatever instructors tell me to do because [caving] is so dangerous... I have no experience and am hanging on a rope attached to the wall with a bolt; if I do something silly, I will fall. The instructors are the expert, so I reset all my instincts and rely completely on what they tell me to do" (T05).

In conclusion, balancing teachers' level of promptness is hard. Trainees’ individual preferences for guidance or autonomy alternate, depending on both their level of experience and their personality. During the first field trips, instructors and trainees care to be as close as possible and check on every passage; then, with time passing, this relationship loosens at different paces for each trainee. Nevertheless, the goal of becoming an autonomous speleologist is common for everyone.

5 DESIGN IMPLICATIONS

Speleology is an exploratory activity based on the discovery and study of underground cavities for scientific purposes. Caves are wild environments that require to be visited by groups of trained people for safety reasons due to their inhospitality and isolation. When speleologists enter a cave, they are isolated: no communication means (smartphone, walkie-talkies, radios) can put those inside the cave in communication with the outside. The rock walls of the cave hinder every kind of signal propagation. In fact, during rescue operations - when communication is essential - rescuers unwind a reel of telephone twisted pair, which is secured where it does not hamper movements and can be broken anywhere along its length to connect a telephone system and communicate [ 23 ]. This condition of isolation entails that the group of speleologists must be as self-reliant as possible, meaning that it must have all the necessary equipment for the progression, food, water, and an emergency kit.

In this paper, we explored which challenges beginner speleologists encounter while learning to move underground. A series of interlinked difficulties have emerged, such as the stress of being in an unknown environment and having to use gear correctly to both move forward and keep safe, the need to coordinate to manage the turn-taking on ropes, and the difficulty of communicating due to the distance and environmental noises.

Among other possibilities, the difficulties of beginner speleologists open the way for a design space for technology-mediated communications. Here, technology offers an opportunity for more direct, clear, and efficient communication since it would allow cavers to send direct messages to the chosen recipient without dissipating in the environment. However, the challenges the underground environment entails cannot be solved easily but require the identification of communication means and protocols that could work in narrow rocky tunnels.

Speleology shares some of its most salient characteristics with outdoor sports and search and rescue teams operations. It requires physical effort and is practiced in groups, thus requiring coordination in wild terrains where voice or standard technology-based communication is ineffective. Building upon our findings and previous literature on teaching outdoor sports and coordinating outdoors, we elaborated a series of suggestions for the design of technology aimed at facilitating the awareness, communication, and coordination of a group of speleologists with different levels of expertise during a cave trip. These design implications wed the idea expressed by Mencarini et al. [ 21 ] of a technology that poses itself as a facilitator of the expertise exchange between the actors involved rather than a substitute. Still, in the case examined in this paper, we do not address the coordination of a dyad as in the case of climber and belayer or beginner climber and instructor, but a group consisting of at least four people. Considering larger units, we build upon previous works on dispersed groups of skiers [ 11 , 36 ] and WSAR groups who need coordinate in wild areas devoid of telecommunication signal coverage [ 2 , 16 ]. 5.1 Explore alternative communication protocols

Caves' shape hampers voice communication: because of narrow and crooked tunnels, impermeable rock walls, and water dripping, sound propagation is hard. Yet, cavers communicate by voice when they need to coordinate, either by shouting or passing the message by word of mouth. To this purpose, technology may offer an opportunity for clear and direct communication by delivering messages directly to the designated recipients. Nevertheless, signal propagation encounters the same difficulties as sound propagation. In fact, it is not easy to bring a Wi-Fi phone or internet signal inside a cave from the outside. Therefore, we encourage HCI researchers and designers to explore alternative communication protocols to enable technology-mediated communication underground. At this stage, a possibility is using mesh networks, i.e., networks consisting of radio or infrared modules (e.g., Zigbee, Adafruit feathers) that can send, receive, or pass over messages if they were not the meant addressee. Still, to work properly, mesh networks need each device to have at least a reachable partner device (other than the original sender) to pass the message over. If reaching another network node is not possible, the communication chain will be interrupted. Albeit not flawless, mesh networks have already been suggested by Jones et al. [ 16 , 17 ] to help WSAR teams coordinate in isolated places, and we believe that they could be exploited in leisure activities too.

5.2 Ensure the possibility to coordinate with the right person

Besides hampering sound propagation, the underground conformation sets the rules and the pace of the cavers’ progression. In fact, for safety reasons, in vertical traits, speleologists proceed alternating on ropes one by one, and when one has reached the top, they notify that the next can start climbing by shouting the command “free!”. This is the most common type of coordination speleologists face, but the one-by-one progression leads the group to split into smaller subgroups and scatter, and sometimes speleologists need to coordinate with partners far from them. For example, they may need to send a message to all the instructors to coordinate the whole group or to the emergency sack holder in case of an accident. In fact, during an underground trip, cavers have different roles, each with different communication needs. There are experienced cavers, instructors and trainees (if it is a trip with a training purpose), the emergency sack holder, and the next-to-go on the rope. Following these findings, we recommend providing the possibility to choose the addressee of a message when designing a technology to support interaction and mediate communication in caves, for example, by navigating a list of contacts or through a more visual selection process, such as picking avatars on the screen. Choosing the intended addressee is a feature all everyday communication tools (phone, email, instant messaging) have; nevertheless, given that those communication tools cannot work in caves, we think it is important to remind researchers and designers to include this feature when setting up new communication protocols and devices, not because communication needs to be private but to ensure that messages achieve the desired addressee.

5.3 Ensure awareness and communication beyond coordination

The different kinds of coordination present in caving, i.e., with close partners for the usual activity or with more distant ones in exceptional cases, show the need to find a way to communicate both simple coordination messages, like the 'free!' command, as well as more complex ones. Furthermore, during training trips, more nuanced communication across the group of cavers is needed. Often, beginner speleologists experience tension or discouragement because of their unfamiliarity with the activity or physical problems such as weakness and the tiredness of being in a dark, humid, and cold place for a long time. When these moments occur, instructors intervene and support trainees by listening to them, reassuring them, and helping them recover from physical ailments. However, to prevent reaching a moment of breakdown, it would be useful for beginners to know that they are not alone but rather part of a group engaging in a shared activity. From these findings, a design space emerges for a technology that makes group members feel connected and raises awareness about their presence. A technological system based on a mesh network could be making explicit the level of distance and closeness among them by leveraging the time taken by a message to reach the next mesh device and, drawing inspiration from Hummingbird, the prototype developed by Weilenmann and Holmquist [ 36 ], make it perceivable through subtle and implicit communication such as sound frequency or the LED lights intensity.

5.4 Design for beginners’ gradual autonomy

During the course learning path, trainees pass from being accompanied step-by-step by the instructors who suggest to them all the aspects of progression (e.g., the best posture to hold on the rope, how to heat up when they feel cold, checking when they move the gear from a rope to the next one), to be autonomous. Progressively, instructors become less present: they keep higher distances from trainees and interfere less with their activity. This path towards autonomy follows a different pace for each trainee according to their personality, preference, and learning speed. These findings indicate the opportunity for a technology accompanying this gradual detachment. If, on the one hand, the communication channel between instructor and trainee should be always open, on the other hand, it would be possible to think of a device that progressively limits it or which allows passing from explicit communicative modalities, such as audio, to more implicit and subtle ones, such as lights, sound, and vibrations. Drawing inspiration from the ambiguous design for learning new motor schemes described in Mencarini et al. [ 21 ] and Toups et al. studies on zero-fidelity simulations for firefighters [ 34 , 35 ], we suggest designing a technology that would not confine trainees to passive learning. In fact, such a device should guarantee trainees tranquility since it ensures the existence of an open communication channel with the instructor and, at the same time, empower them in their actions. Of course, while transitioning from explicit levels of communication to more implicit ones, the instructor's decision about which communication channels to use should consider safety the most important aspect to preserve.

By combining the findings from our study and HCI literature on communication in wild environments, we have elaborated these implications for design, which define the how, who, what, and why of technology-mediated communication underground. These design implications emerged from the qualitative investigation of an under-investigated domain in HCI, i.e., speleology, to confirm and enrich other HCI works on technologies for outdoor group coordination. Furthermore, being speleology at the intersection not only of outdoor sports and team coordination in the wild but also of sharing tools, procedures, and progression techniques with works-at-height, we believe this work can be a starting point to investigate work activities in the wild areas or rough terrains such as forestry and rope access.

6 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

Since the study took place in a geographically limited area, results cannot be fully generalized, and future works could verify whether learning speleology happens similarly in other parts of the world.

Then, the next step for this work is to conceive the design of a prototype that materializes the principles outlined in the design implications of this paper.

7 CONCLUSIONS

Learning can be seen as the process leading novices to do something autonomously and efficiently. In the case of outdoor sports, learning entails acquiring physical skills, managing gear, and coordinating with others to tackle the natural environment. Our investigation of how speleologists are trained has shown that beginners’ difficulties lay in learning new gestures and procedures - on which their progression and safety depend - and coordinating with others when they are out of sight. The relationship with the other group members becomes pivotal since, while inside the cave, the group is completely isolated from the rest of the world (neither phone nor GPS signal reach devices inside caves), and awareness and communication are the basis of beginners' tranquility. Following these findings, we elaborated a series of design implications for technology supporting awareness, communication, and coordination in speleology groups whose members have different levels of expertise. These implications aim to find a compromise between instructors being present and trainees working autonomously by enhancing communication while encouraging the learning experience. Finally, since speleology shares some settings, practices, and tools with outdoor sports, team coordination activities in the wild, and works-at-height, we believe this work can be a starting point to investigate other activities in the wild areas.
