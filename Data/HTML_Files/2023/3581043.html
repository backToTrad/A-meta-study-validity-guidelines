The amount and quality of mathematical language in the family are positively associated with promoting children's mathematical abilities. However, mathematical language in many families is poor. Through need-finding investigation, we developed MathKingdom , a voice-agent-based game that helps children aged 4–7 learn and use rich, accurate mathematical language (e.g., mathematical expressions related to measurement, sequence, patterns). The game has four flows, in which users can wake up, transform, decorate, and perform as their avatars, as well as practice basic mathematical vocabulary, mathematical single sentences, coherent mathematical statements, and free expression. We refined the system design through wizard-of-oz testing and then evaluated it with 18 families. The results showed that MathKingdom effectively engaged children, enhanced their mathematical language skills and mathematical abilities, and encouraged parent-child conversations about math.

1 INTRODUCTION

Mathematical ability is important to children's development, affecting their performance in a wide range of subjects (e.g., STEM development) [ 13 , 40 , 63 ] as well as health and personal well-being [ 32 , 46 ]. Of note, children's mathematical abilities vary widely before they enter school, and a key contributor to this variation is children's language skills, especially mathematical language [ 34 , 40 , 41 , 42 ].

Mathematical language is used to define math concepts and express math ideas (e.g., to describe a mathematical pattern) and contains a variety of words that are highly content-specific to math (e.g., “more,” “less,” and other quantity words). Children acquire mathematical language from early childhood, and the quantity and quality of mathematical conversations in the family lead to individual differences in children's mathematical language competencies [ 5 , 9 , 43 ]. In the field of human-computer interaction (HCI), technologies to support children's early mathematical learning have focused on enriching the experience or enhancing a specific skill. However, to our knowledge, no technology has been designed for mathematical language at home.

To further understand the opportunities and challenges of children learning mathematical language at home, we conducted a formative study with 11 families (Section 3). We found that children were often passive recipients of mathematical activities at home and lacked opportunities for conversational practice. The lack of guidance resulted in a single type of mathematical language in the home. At the same time, children had barriers to attention and participation. Through our formative study, we identified four main design goals: 1) encouraging children to speak the mathematical language; 2) creating opportunities for parent-child mathematical dialogues; 3) covering various concepts in the home mathematical language activity; and 4) giving children some initiative in activities to support participation.

Many voice agents in the HCI field are widely used in children's homes to support various learning goals [ 16 , 20 , 44 , 71 , 74 ]. For example, StoryBuddy is a system that helps parents create an interactive storytelling experience [ 74 ]. Parents can choose to communicate with their children in person using only questions extracted from the system or have a voice agent lead the children through the story and answer questions when they are busy. Studies have demonstrated the advantages of voice agents for early education in the family environment. First, children's reading and writing skills are fitted and literacy as a barrier to preschoolers’ participation is avoided [ 16 , 20 ]. Second, voice agents can act as facilitators to a certain extent to promote smooth activities and reduce the burden of parental guidance [ 20 , 39 ]. In addition, voice agents construct a context for conversations to take place, which has the effect of facilitating communication, and children can learn new words to extend their communication skills [ 20 , 44 , 71 ]. In this study, we used the voice agent to create conditions for mathematical dialogues and explore how they work in families.

Based on previous works and our formative research, we designed and developed MathKingdom , an interactive voice-agent-based game for children aged 4–7 years. This game guides children to use mathematical language through four flows: 1) Wake Up. Children answer questions with basic mathematical language to ‘wake up’ a geometry-composed character; 2) Character Transformation. To change the appearance of the geometry-composed character, children will learn to use a single sentence in mathematical language to describe mathematical features of specific shapes; 3) Character Decoration. To decorate the character with a decorative object, children will be guided to use coherent sentences in mathematical language to describe the mathematical features of graphic decorative objects; 4) Storytelling. Children are encouraged to introduce their new character with decoration to their parents in mathematical language. The geometry-composed character in the game is the child's avatar, which would map the child's movements after being ‘waked up’ and act as the visual focus of the game to engage the child.

This paper makes the following contributions.

  • A formative study of families with preschool-aged children revealed that families should be provided with a rich variety of mathematical language and opportunities for mathematical dialogue to increase children's engagement through their initiative in play.
  • MathKingdom , a voice-agent-based game for children aged 4–7 years, allows children to learn and practice mathematical language through mathematical dialogues.
  • A user study evaluated the effectiveness of MathKingdom on children's mathematical language competencies and early mathematical skills and explored how MathKingdom promotes parent-child interaction through quantitative and qualitative methods.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Early Mathematics Education

Significant individual differences have been observed in preschool children's mathematical abilities, and these differences predict later academic achievement [ 18 , 29 , 62 ]. Moreover, the preschool period is an important period for developing subject preferences when environmental moderation can play a greater role [ 8 , 18 , 27 , 38 ]. This has prompted interest and discussion about the impact of children's early environments on their general cognitive and specific skills. An accepted conclusion is that early mathematics education in natural learning situations, such as play activities, is more in line with child development and subject requirements [ 22 ]. The effectiveness of learning in the early years is greatly influenced by engagement. Van den Heuvel-Panhuizen described learning in the early years as “a process that occurs primarily ‘from inside’... driven by the child's natural curiosity, its urge to find out how things fit together” [ 60 ]. Early learning in kindergarten often takes the form of playful activities that give children the opportunity to discover mathematical concepts and solve mathematical problems in context. In addition, children's learning experiences in informal settings are an integral part of early education, helping them learn new concepts and make connections between different concepts [ 14 , 29 ], and playing an important role in enhancing children's engagement with subjects and building interest [ 27 ]. At the same time, successful mathematical activities are based on effective mathematical communication [ 10 , 12 , 51 ], which is significantly related to children's logical and mathematical cognitive development [ 26 ].

There have also been many attempts in HCI to support children's early mathematical learning through technology [ 1 , 4 , 30 , 36 , 61 ]. For example, augmented reality (AR) technology has enabled children to discover mathematical concepts in familiar, ordinary objects and engage with math problems in meaningful contexts [ 30 , 36 , 61 ]. The combination of embodied theory and media technology creates opportunities for children to explore mathematical concepts through physical movements [ 1 , 56 ]. However, most of these studies focused on enriching children's learning experiences or enhancing mastery of a particular mathematical concept, without promoting mathematical communication or mathematical expression as the main goal.

2.2 Mathematical Language Development

For mathematics education, speaking about mathematics is inevitable important and central to mathematical activities. Effective oral expression can provide background information about mathematical problems and help participants construct meaning [ 2 , 3 , 67 ]. In contrast to passive reception of knowledge, speaking and discussion have a higher degree of cognitive engagement [ 11 , 54 ], and oral expression is important for low-ability students to acquire new knowledge and activate the integration of relevant prior knowledge [ 11 , 15 ]. Mathematical language refers to the keywords and concepts required for mathematical activities [ 58 ], which is the basis for children's effective mathematical communication [ 24 , 25 , 42 , 48 , 58 ]. Indeed, children begin to learn mathematical language from their surroundings at preschool age, and children's mathematical language skills are significantly correlated with their mathematical abilities [ 43 , 62 ]. Studies have reported that in early childhood, mathematical and language difficulties often coincide and that inadequate language preparation affects the quality of children's mathematical communication and the development of mathematical ability [ 10 , 12 , 51 ].

By developing a curriculum or reading interventions, some researchers have demonstrated the ways children can improve their early mathematical skills through learning mathematical language [ 28 , 42 ]. However, no technology has been designed to support the learning of mathematical language in the home environment. Home is an important site for mathematical language learning, where the quantity and quality of mathematical conversations provided by parents are related to children's mathematical knowledge [ 5 , 9 , 43 , 65 ]. In addition, parents have an irreplaceable role in children's early mathematical language learning; they can adapt their mathematical talk to their children's current skill level to help children continue to hone their mathematical skills, compensating for the difficulty of accommodating individual differences in schooling [ 75 ]. In this paper, we attempt to enrich math conversations at home through technology-supported math language activities.

2.3 Voice Agents in Children's Education

Voice-based technologies have increasingly been adopted by children. This technology offers a new interaction method that is not limited by children's literacy skills [ 16 , 20 ] and alleviates the social isolation of children by interactive devices, which keep them from interacting with the physical world and others [ 39 , 59 ]. Researchers have recognized that conversational agents have the potential to provide a powerful new mechanism to foster children's information recall, learning, and engagement [ 49 , 53 , 68 ].

Many existing studies have focused on exploring how voice-based agent systems can guide and accompany children's learning [ 7 , 57 , 73 , 74 ], as well as the benefits in specific subject areas [ 16 , 64 , 72 ]. Some research has also paid attention to the potential for voice agents to act as third-party mediators to complement parenting tasks [ 20 ], and has begun to investigate how to deploy voice-based technologies at home [ 35 , 55 , 69 , 74 ]. Xu et al. [ 69 ] developed science videos with the main character powered by voice agents that engage children in contingent conversation by asking them questions and providing responsive feedback to enhance the interactive experience of children watching science programs. There has been discussion on how conversational technology can be used to better support children's active learning and parental involvement in video viewing. Zhang et al. [ 74 ] introduced the chatbot StoryBuddy into a parent-child storytelling activity, which allows parents to collaborate with the robot to create an interactive Q&A for children. The user study showed that StoryBuddy could provide parents with flexible engagement and control while effectively keeping children active during storytelling. The results of these studies indicate that conversational agents can promote the sustainability of learning activities at home [ 55 ] and reduce the burden of parental guidance for young children [ 20 , 39 ].

MathKingdom aims to apply voice-based conversational agent technology to the practice of mathematical language. We focus on home conditions and provide opportunities for responsive, diverse, and abundant mathematical conversations for young children and their parents.

3 FORMATIVE STUDY

As detailed above, mathematical language is important in the development of children's mathematical abilities. Therefore, we performed a formative study to gather information on 1) parents’ attitudes toward the mathematical language, 2) families’ daily practices of mathematical language, and 3) design opportunities that target the correct challenges.

3.1 Method

We recruited and interviewed 11 families with at least one child aged 4-7 (M = 5.64, SD = 1.23; 4 females, 7 males) from two different provinces in China, and all of these participants were native Mandarin speakers. First, we explained the meaning of mathematical language to parents and gave examples. After ensuring that the parents had understood, we started the interview. Each interview session followed a one-on-one semi-structured format, in which we asked parents about their perceptions of mathematical language; the frequency and duration of mathematical language occurring in the home; and the scenarios in which they generated mathematical conversations with their children. We began the interview with broad questions that put fewer restrictions on the participants’ responses and then asked more focused follow-up questions to probe parents on certain topics. The interviews lasted 25 minutes for participants and were carried out via video conferencing. Finally, each participant was compensated with a gift worth $ 5 .

All data were recorded and analyzed with the participants’ consent. We employed thematic analysis [ 6 ], where data were iteratively examined and reviewed to identify themes and patterns. Interview transcripts were coded for parents’ perceptions of mathematical language and how they were using mathematical language in the home. We expected to determine whether children and parents become blocked or encounter engagement issues in mathematical conversations. Two researchers developed and revised codes individually and over several meeting discussions to converge the findings into themes.

3.2 Key Findings and Design Implications

3.2.1 Children Typically Engage in Math Activities through Verbal Input. We found that watching animations with early math instruction was the most common activity related to mathematical language among the 11 families, followed by reading mathematical books or illustrations together. However, these two activities were language inputs, meaning that children were exposed to mathematical language by listening or watching more often. According to previous research (section 2.2), learning by talking allows children to be actively engaged in learning activities [ 11 ]. Also, active learning outcomes are better than passive learning [ 52 ]. However, in our survey, less than a quarter of families engaged in activities that supported their children's mathematical expression. To improve this situation, we wanted our design to encourage children to speak in mathematical language.

3.2.2 Lack of Opportunities to Initiate Math Dialogues. For many students, engaging in mathematical conversations led by the teacher is accessible. This is because the learning environment in class and teachers’ instructional strategies support their conversational activities. However, this environment of mathematical learning may be difficult to create in informal settings. Furthermore, more than half of the parents said they were not confident in their ability to guide their children in mathematical conversations. “I'm not sure I'm doing the right thing,” one parent said. In fact, time is fragmented for parents, and few can spend a lot of time studying educational materials. Parents are therefore more likely to teach their children from experience. Although they often told stories, they did not specifically emphasize math-related content.

Mathematical dialogues only occasionally appeared in parent-child communication. A parent mentioned, “The knowledge (coins and clocks) taught in kindergarten is common in everyday life, but I didn't teach her before. Now she learns very slowly.” Based on previous research and our research, our system should create a meaningful context for mathematical conversations.

3.2.3 Poor Mathematical Language. Away from the formal instructional context or teacher guidance, children's mathematical language at home was incomplete. This narrative was often “piecemeal” and unstructured. For example, one parent described the language her child spoke in math: “Sometimes when I ask him a question, he only answers me with one word. He rarely speaks a complete sentence, not to mention a series of related sentences.” We also found that parents lacked guidance on diverse and accurate math language practice for their children. For example, most parents described their math conversations with their children as being mainly about counting and recognizing geometric shapes without calculating, measuring, or the concept of patterns. However, previous researchers have shown that the breadth of mathematical language has beneficial effects on children's mathematical abilities. Based on these comments, we aimed to 1) provide a rich variety of mathematical language for families and 2) guide children toward more coherent and precise expressions.

3.2.4 Low Engagement. Parents commonly mentioned the issue of child engagement, as math activities in the home are initiated and led by parents. Math dialogues between children and parents often start with the parent asking questions and end with the child answering. However, children's motivations are not high in this type of question-and-answer dialogue. In addition, as passive recipients, children are easily distracted by irrelevant stimuli; “I don't quite understand how to bring up his interest, and obviously he prefers playing games to participating in these.” However, the preschool years are an important interest-building phase; we also wanted to avoid overly rigid direct instruction and provide more fun for children's mathematical experiences. To this end, one of our goals was to give children more space to explore to increase their agency.

3.3 Design Goals

Based on previous research and our findings, we propose four main design goals: 1) Make children speak math. Our system uses voice agents to guide children in speaking mathematical language. 2) Promote parent-child dialogue in mathematical language. 3) Mastery learning of mathematical language. Our system covers different mathematical concepts and guides children to organize rational, coherent mathematical sentences and use more accurate mathematical language. 4) Sustained engagement. Our game tries to increase their interest and engagement by employing embodied interaction. Specifically, the main character in the game is an avatar of the child and can map the child's body movements.

4 SYSTEM DESIGN AND DEVELOPMENT

4.1 MathKingdom

The game background is the mathematics world. In this world, the child has an avatar that is composed of geometric shapes. Through the first three flows of the MathKingdom , children can ‘wake up’ the avatar, transform its appearance, and decorate the avatar. To cover various mathematical concepts in MathKingdom , we refer to children's learning trajectories in the research-based early mathematics assessment (REMA) and incorporate different concepts in the first three flows (Table 1). The fourth flow is designed to encourage free mathematical dialogues between parents and children. All these four flows are guided by our voice agent, and users should press the space key before speaking.

4.1.1 Game Flow. ‘Wake Up’ . The ‘Wake Up’ flow is designed to encourage children to use basic concepts and terms of mathematical language, which are frequently used in later flows.

In the beginning, the game asks children to recognize basic geometric shapes (including a circle, triangle, rectangle, rhombus, square, and trapezoid). Specifically, MathKingdom displays a human-like avatar composed of basic geometric shapes, which have black outlines without a fill color. The voice agent then asks children to tell all the shapes they can recognize in the geometry-composed avatar. The shapes being successfully spoken by the child change in color, providing clear visual feedback. Shapes that cannot be recognized are displayed individually, and audio introductions of them (e.g., ‘A square is a shape with four equal sides and four equal right angles’ for the shape square) are delivered. Parents can talk with children about the shapes until they want to continue the game.

After all the shapes have been recognized, the voice agent asks the child three questions related to the features of the geometry-composed avatar. Specifically, children may be required to answer questions, as shown in Table 1.

Avatar Transformation . After all the questions in the first flow have been answered, the geometry-composed avatar is activated and starts mapping children's body movements. In the Avatar Transformation flow, children can use a single sentence in mathematical language to customize the appearance of the avatar by changing its shape elements. To form these sentences, children are required to identify and classify different geometric shapes and compare their spatial positions and sizes. For example, children can change their hair shape by saying “change the biggest triangle into the circle.”

The Voice agent reminds children of using mathematical language to organize a single sentence. Children can keep changing the appearance of the avatar until they signal that they have finished making changes.

Avatar Decoration . In the Use Coherent Sentences flow, children can choose one specific graphic object from three, and then the voice agent guides children to use a series of coherent sentences in mathematical language to describe this object. To complete this description, children are exposed to the math concept of a pattern, which is a higher-level concept than concepts involved in previous flows. Moreover, to make sentences coherent, children are required to use connectives (e.g., first, second, in total, from left to right) of mathematical language, which are mainly related to the concepts of sequence and spatial position.

Specifically, children select one graphic decoration object and describe its features with mathematical language. After the correct description has been given, the avatar wears the decoration and continues mapping the children's body movements.

Storytelling . Finally, in the Storytelling flow, children can describe the customized geometry-composed avatar to their parents. The voice agent encourages children to use coherent sentences with math connectives that have been used in previous flows.

4.1.2 Voice Agent Design. We used a voice agent to navigate all the flows of the game, introducing it as a member of the mathematical world. In addition, we choose a cartoon-like voice for it and make it more like a game companion for the children.

The voice agent has four types of utterances: introduction utterance, question utterance, feedback utterance, and mathematical language utterance. Taking the first flow as an example, the introduction utterance introducing the game and its flows is “Let's wake up your figure in the MathKingdom!” This follows the question utterance of “What shapes could you find?” The feedback utterance compliments the expected answer using “It's great!” or “Good!” It asks, “Could you find any other shapes?” when the child leaves out some shapes. After the child has named all the shapes, the mathematical language utterances of “We have the circle, the triangle, the rectangle, the rhombus, the square, and the trapezoid” are broadcasted as a well-organized mathematical language sentence or sentences, which aim to expose children to richer and more accurate mathematical language.

4.2 Wizard-of-Oz Test

To iterate and refine our voice-based interface, we conducted the Wizard-of-Oz (WoZ) [ 31 ] test. In this test, we focused on (1) whether the utterance of the voice agent could help children understand flow tasks; (2) whether the utterance of the voice agent could engage children in the flow; and (3) whether the visual clues could be understood and help children organize mathematical language.

4.2.1 Test Method. In the WoZ version of our game, all the flows were developed, except the process of these flows should be controlled manually and all visual clues are not included. Note that the second flow can map children's movements, but the shape transformation of the geometry-composed avatar appearance should be controlled by the researcher. To identify the validity of visual clues and iterate them later, we printed them on cards and observed participants’ reactions to different clues. Moreover, we designed the voice agent's conversation flow as scripts for the WoZ experiment, which was transformed into speech through the Tencent Cloud text-to-speech library 1 and broadcasted to users at the corresponding conversation nodes.

We recruited 8 pairs of participants, with each pair consisting of one child aged 4–7 (M = 5.63, SD = 1.40; 3 females, 5 males) and the child's parent. All of them were native Mandarin speakers. Participants were brought into a private room in a library as pairs, and they interacted with this system believing they were interacting with a functional game. During the experimental process, one researcher sat near the child and parent to observe users’ behaviors and conduct necessary interventions, while another researcher controlled the game flow remotely. The experiment followed a semi-structured interview, aiming to understand participants’ behavior and collect their suggestions about the game experience.

4.2.2 Findings. Through this WoZ experiment, we obtained three findings to make our design iteration of MathKingdom more usable.

Real-time feedback. We noticed the importance of the voice agent giving complimentary feedback for children's correct answers or mathematical sentences and encouraging children to try again for undesirable answers (see Figure 2).

Additional visual clue. In the third flow, coherent sentences are required. These sentences should include the key details of the graphic object and mathematical connectives, which is too demanding for children to organize without additional support. Thus, we aimed to provide additional visual clues for children to notice mathematical features (see Figure 3). Specifically, the main features of the decoration are displayed in disassembly, and the key connectives are provided as well. In addition, visual clues should be simple and frank given the limited reading comprehension of preschool children.

Suggestions for parents. We found that parents were willing to discuss mathematical language with children, but sometimes they did not know how to give children proper suggestions. To this end, our game displays suggestions for parents to help children organize mathematical sentences. For example, in the second flow, some frequently used mathematical words should be displayed during the whole process.

4.3 Implementation

We developed the game in Unity, using the MMPose for pose estimation, the Baidu AI Cloud speech-to-text library 2 for capturing children's speech, and the Tencent Cloud text-to-speech library for speech synthesis of our voice agent. We chose Tencent TTS because it has a cartoon-like voice, which is more user-friendly to children. The pose estimation algorithm catches the data of body key points, which is sent to Unity through UDP and mapped to the geometry-composed avatar in real-time.

5 USER STUDY

To evaluate how well MathKingdom meets our design objectives of engaging children in speaking the mathematical language, we conducted a short-term user study to examine the following research questions.

  • RQ1: Do children find MathKingdom engaging?
  • RQ2: Can children successfully navigate the voice flow?
  • RQ3: Do children improve their mathematical language skills with MathKingdom ?
  • RQ4: Can children improve their mathematical skills with MathKingdom ?
  • RQ5: How do children and parents have mathematical dialogues in MathKingdom ?

5.1 Participants

We recruited 21 pairs of participants for this user study through the authors’ social media accounts. Each pair consisted of a parent and a child aged 4–7, and all these participants were native Mandarin speakers. Three pairs of participants did not complete the study and were therefore not included in the analysis, leaving 18 valid data sets(M = 6.22, SD = 0.97; 7 female, 11 male). We labeled these 18 children as numbers C1 to C18.

5.2 Procedure

This short-term evaluation consisted of three sessions and was conducted in 6 days. The interval time between every session was 1 day. Because this study focused on mathematical language activities in the family, we conducted the short-term evaluation in the participants’ homes. For the game part of every session, the child should navigate through the game with their parent sitting close to them. Parents were encouraged to interact with children during the game process. We recorded the whole process of the evaluation and obtained a total of 34 hours of audio and 13.5 hours of video, the audio data were transcribed.

5.2.1 Session 1: Game and Pre-Assessments. In the first session, we first built rapport with the child before conducting two pre-assessment tasks (described in 5.3.3 and 5.3.4): mathematics assessment and mathematical language assessment. After finishing these two assessments, we introduced the MathKingdom game to the parent and the child before starting the game. Then, the participants could play the game freely within 15 min.

5.2.2 Session 2: Game. In this session, participants were required to play the game.

5.2.3 Session 3: Game and Post-Assessments. In this session, after finishing all flows, participants filled out a post-questionnaire to rate their engagement levels during their interaction with MathKingdom and their preferences for each flow. A post-assessment of the children's math and math language skills was then conducted. In addition, we conducted a semi-structured interview with each pair of participants about the user experience and expectations. Each pair of parent and child was given compensation worth $ 15 . The procedure for one pair of participants is shown in Figure 4.

5.3 Measures

5.3.1 Engagement Questionnaires. Because our formative investigation revealed that engagement was at a low level in children's home mathematical language activities, we argue that enhancing children's engagement is a key design objective of our game. To evaluate children's engagement during the game, we utilized the Giggle Gauge self-report instrument [ 17 ], which has been validated with children in our target age range and assesses engagement in terms of perceptions such as challenge, aesthetic, feedback, and endurability, on a four-point scale (4 is the highest). In addition, to assess children's willingness for future engagement and preferences for each flow, we applied the Smileyometer and Again-Again table [ 45 ].

5.3.2 Usability Assessment. To navigate each user flow, the child must use specific mathematical words or organize mathematical sentences. Therefore, to evaluate children's successful application of mathematical language in each flow, we captured their amounts and types of mistakes in voice input (e.g., speech-to-text machine comprehension errors or invalid input errors), and the fluency of help that children received from parents and research assistants. Moreover, participants’ success rate in the assigned tasks is related to mathematical language competencies; thus, we used this usability assessment for the children's first and last time playing our game.

5.3.3 Mathematical Language Assessment. Based on the conceptualizing method in the mathematical assessment mentioned above, we combined the measure developed by Purpura [ 41 ] and the measure of language competencies to evaluate children's mathematical language competencies before and after our short-term user study. In this assessment, children were required to describe figures that contained mathematical concepts, and we coded their descriptions by mathematical keywords. These keywords covered different mathematical concepts, and each keyword was equivalent to one point. Notably, as the geometric domain contains more concepts than the number domain, the scores of the geometric domain tended to be higher than those of the other one. Additionally, the third flow of our game requires coherent mathematical sentences, which can reflect the change in children's mathematical language skills. In this flow, the children's mathematical language skills are measured with the fluency and accuracy of mathematical language. Fluency was indicated by the time taken to speak all sentences required in the third flow. We counted the time from when the children started to speak the mathematical sentences to when they finished. Accuracy referred to the number of valid keywords of mathematical language for organizing these sentences. For example, “There are four layers in total” contains two mathematical terms (‘four’ as a number and ‘in total’ as connective) and counted two scores.

5.3.4 Mathematics Assessment. To assess the change in children's mathematical skills before and after the short-term evaluation, we utilized the REMA-SF [ 66 ], which is a short form of research-based early mathematics assessment (REMA). In this assessment, learning trajectories were conceptualized within two broad domains: number and geometric competencies. Specifically, the number constructs included verbal and object counting, subitizing, number comparison and sequencing, connecting numerals to quantities, number composition/decomposition, adding and subtracting, and place value. Geometric constructs included the recognition, composition/decomposition, congruence, and construction of shapes, as well as spatial imagery, measurement, and patterns.

Two research assistants first independently developed an initial codebook by opening the coding data of the game process. After that, the two researchers worked together to discuss individual code and then performed a second round of individual coding with the emerging codebook. Then, these two research assistants discussed again to resolve disagreements, clarify coding details, and determine the codebook. Finally, two researchers split the study data, which were independently coded. Researchers coded these data using NVIVO over several meetings to converge the findings into themes. All codes had interrater reliability of over 90 $\%$ .

6 RESULTS

6.1 Children engaged in MathKingdom actively

This theme emerged from our observations of children using our system and what attributes supported their engagement. On the last post-activity questionnaire, most children indicated having an interest in MathKingdom . Moreover, 17 out of 18 children answered 4 (M=3.94; SD=0.23) to the question “I enjoyed using MathKingdom ,” and 15 children answered 4 (M=3.83; SD=0.37) to the claim that “ MathKingdom was hard in a good way.” In addition, children were asked whether they would like to participate in each flow again. Children answered 3.61 (SD=0.60) for the first flow, 3.94 (SD=0.23) for the second flow, and 3.83 (SD=0.37) for the last one. Other metrics achieved moderate levels. Four children shared negative reactions about the first flow, three of whom were on the older end of our age range, at 7 years old. For example, C15 commented, “I think the first flow is too easy.” C1 reported that he thought the introduction utterance in the second flow was too long. Further work is needed to identify how to set the difficulty of the game to help older learners use MathKingdom .

We observed that the avatars composed of transformable geometric shapes appealed to children. Most children actively explored what could happen to them and how they could turn them into their imagined images. Specifically, they would try to change the geometry-composed avatar by changing different shapes and making different gestures. For example, C8 changed the avatar's tummy from a square into a rectangle and exclaimed, “Hey, a rectangular tummy, that's funny.” C10 explored how his body was mapped to the avatar. Specifically, he spread his hands and found that the avatar also did that, and then he became excited to try to make the avatar dance by wiggling his arms.

6.3 Using MathKingdom to develop children's mathematical language

To calculate the fluency and accuracy of the third flow, we focused on marking the data of children who chose the same decoration during the game for the first and third sessions. (See 5.3.3 for the description of these assessments.) A total of nine children's data were valid. The analysis revealed an increase in the fluency and accuracy of these children's mathematical language. Compared to the first gaming time, six children used a shorter time for organizing coherent sentences. Particularly, one child reduced the time from 61 seconds to 13 seconds. One main reason for the above is that children made fewer unsuccessful attempts and used fewer meaningless conversational words (e.g., ums, uhs) or repetitions, which imply thinking and organizing mathematical language. In the last experiment, five out of nine children could use more proper connectives to organize sentences. Specifically, four of these children improved two points, and one child improved one point in the accuracy assessment. Overall, children were able to use more coherent and accurate mathematical language to describe mathematical features after our evaluation process.

Besides, as shown in Table 4, we conducted a paired samples t-test of the mathematical language measure. For the number domain in mathematical language, we found a statistical difference (t (18) = 3.688, p = 0.002 < 0.01) pre- (M = 5.17, SD = 0.79) and post-experiment (M = 5.61, SD = 0.92). As for the geometric domain, we found a statistical difference (t (18) = 3.183, p = 0.005 < 0.01) pre (M = 11.5, SD = 2.28) and post-experiment (M = 12.4, SD = 2.64). That is, children improved in both domains of mathematical language before and after the three experiments. However, the mean score increase in number is lower than in the geometric domain, likely because our game uses geometric graphics, and children receive more visual stimulation and verbal practice in it; this inspired us to include more narrative content about numbers in the game.

In addition, most parents agreed that MathKingdom improved their children's math language skills. The parent of C11 said, “I think the game improved the verbal expression and logical thinking skills of my child.”

6.4 Using MathKingdom to develop children's mathematical abilities

We used REMA to assess children's mathematical abilities before and after our user study (See 5.3.4 for the description of these assessments). The data from the analysis of measurement before and after the three experiment sessions are shown in Table 4. The results show that for the number domain in mathematical abilities, we found a statistical difference (t (18) = 3.321, p = 0.004 < 0.01) pre (M = 9.18, SD = 1.72) and post-experiment (M = 10.06, SD = 1.55). As for the geometric domain, we observed a statistical difference (t (18) = 2.582, p = 0.019 < 0.05) pre- (M = 9.59, SD = 2.77) and post-experiment (M = 10.46, SD = 1.78). During the interviews, all parents and children agreed that using the system improved mathematical skills. C13’s parent told us that she taught her child the concept of left and right in the first experiment session and that she found out that the child had acquired the taught concept.

6.5 Promoting Mathematical Dialogues between Parents and Children

In MathKingdom, the four flows are designed for using basic mathematical words, organizing mathematical single sentences, organizing mathematical coherent sentences, and free storytelling. The mathematical dialogues between children and parents in each flow are different.

In the first flow, a common conversational pattern is for parents to ask children to answer. Parents tend to introduce children to the shapes in the picture and guide them to practice repetition by asking questions. For example, a parent of a 4-year-old (C13) helping her child understand triangles said, “Look, 1, 2, 3, there are three angles, what is this shape?”

The discussion takes place more in the second and third flow. The geometry-composed avatar raises a common concern and leads to discussions about how to make the avatars more beautiful/interesting. At the same time, parents provide sentence templates for the children's descriptions and remind them of their accuracy. For example, “Look at this trapezoid. It's... right, the biggest one, so you should say...”, “Can you use the sentences you just learned? First, it is... and second, it is...” In addition to following parental advice and guidance, children also offer and insist on their ideas. C3 said to her mother, "I think the circle-shaped mouth is better." Similarly, when the parent suggested altering the character's head, C15 insisted on changing the shape of the hair to make the character look like a panda. We found that interaction between children and avatars could also promote and maintain communication. When the parent asked her 5-year-old child (C14) which part he wanted to change, he paused for a long time and then raised his left hand to indicate that he wanted to change the shape of his left hand. Finally, they continued their conversation. Parents also encouraged children to find solutions when they encountered problems in expression. A 7-year-old boy (C8) had difficulty describing the spatial features of the left-hand shape. His mother suggested changing the spatial feature, so he raised his hand over his head. Then, he successfully described the target shape as “the highest” one.

In free storytelling, children with weaker language skills tend to describe shapes and count quantities. For example, C6 said to his mother, “It has six rhombuses on its head and a circle at the bottom.” In contrast, the more verbal children focus their narratives on the spatial relationships of the individual figures and the characteristics of the avatar. They associate certain shapes with other similar-looking objects to support their free storytelling. For example, C18 chose the third decoration for his avatar, calling it a ‘crown made of rhombuses’. This was because he thought that the avatar he created in the second process looked like a superhero and that ‘the hero should get a crown’.

Significantly, in free storytelling, parents ask their children questions that they have previously answered incorrectly. For example, three children confused left and right in the second flow, and without exception, their parents verified the children's knowledge of orientation again. In general, parents and children were able to communicate spontaneously using mathematical language in the system.

7 DISCUSSION

The results of our evaluation demonstrated that MathKingdom could effectively engage children to actively interact with the voice agent during play. Our quantitative analysis revealed that children's mathematical language skills and mathematical abilities improved through the game. In addition, we observed that MathKingdom promoted mathematical dialogues between parents and children. In this section, we discuss the specific effects of MathKingdom on parent-child mathematical conversations, as well as the limitations of our study and future work.

7.1 Mathematical Communication under Multimodal Interaction

We provided a multimodal interaction for MathKingdom in which children could interact with the system through language and physical movements. We found that embodiment provided an additional scaffold for children's problem-solving and effectively promoted active participation.

First, when younger children faced barriers to expression, they used body movements as a supplement to communicate ideas and get help. For example, children would point to a shape they did not know and repeat the name of the shape as they received the answer. Some children also used body movements in more descriptive ways (see section 6.5). As evidenced by educational research, designs that include body activities are more appropriate for those in early childhood, who enjoy movement and play [ 33 , 56 ]. The physicality of the body plays a central role in social meaning-making activities, particularly as children use body movements as a guiding function for vocabulary and meaning construction needs [ 37 , 47 ].

Second, embodied self-relevance promotes children's active participation. It has been shown that there is a gradual increase in children's cognitive engagement from entirely educator-led passive reception to communication guided by students’ questions and ideas [ 54 ]. Strong motivation is necessary to engage in mathematical conversation and make it work, where a shared visual focus can be an effective mediator of communication[ 52 ]. In our evaluation, as the image mapped by the child was the common focus of the play and was completely controlled by the child, children had a heightened sense of ownership and motivation. When the mapping began, children were excited to look at the screen while exploring the actions that the avatars could take. During the image transformation session, they thought carefully about their goals for the transformation and how to achieve them, exchanging ideas with their parents. At that point, the parents became facilitators, and the children took the lead in giving the final transformation instructions. This is rarely the case in common educational activities that are entirely parent-led.

7.2 Role of Voice Agents in Family Education Activities

In previous research on voice agents designed for families, an important topic is how to define the role of agents in activities [ 21 , 74 ]. On the one hand, voice agents can be designed to act as third-party mediators of parent-child communication [ 70 ]. On the other hand, children who have stronger prosocial relationships with media figures can learn more effectively from them [ 23 ].

In MathKingdom, the voice agent is set up as a member of the Math Kingdom, inviting children to come and play games. We chose a cartoon-like voice for it, and although it still acts as a guide for the game, it is more like a companion for the children. One child in our evaluation referred to it as “Teacher!”, while the others interacted with it in livelier ways. More than half of the children actively responded to the voice agent, calling out its name before the game began or discussing its visual image as resembling a ball or the "sun of Math Kingdom". Parents use the voice agent more as an assistant or surrogate teacher. The voice agent shared the burden of parental guidance by asking questions and providing follow-up feedback. In addition, the agent provided a rich variety of math conversation topics and mathematical language application examples suitable for preschoolers.

7.3 Communication Patterns and Preferences of Different Families

As a form of early education that can be flexibly adapted to different topics, games are effective in engaging children in informal learning and creating a background for meaningful conversations [ 19 , 75 ]. In MathKingdom , we provide rich mathematical elements in the picture and clearer suggestions for parents to guide their children in organizing sentences (see section 4.2.2) to support users in mathematical communication. The evaluation revealed that parents and children could engage fluently in MathKingdom and generate play-based mathematical conversations. Moreover, different communication patterns and tendencies were presented depending on the children's prior abilities.

In contexts where parents and children are involved together, parents can further enhance their child's potential achievement through appropriate scaffolding. This leads to a greater increase in the child's sense of self-efficacy and facilitates productive and intimate interactions [ 50 ]. Specifically, parents rarely intervened when children expressed fluency in the evaluation, and more parent-child mathematical communication occurred in the free discussion of transforming images. Conversely, if the child's verbal or mathematical skills are lower than expected, the parent should support their participation in play in a way that is more adapted to the child. For example, one child was guided to repeat word by word, pointing to shapes and leading the child to count. Some parents also used the rich mathematical elements of the geometry-composed character to question children spontaneously, such as asking children to point to a shape during breaks.

Children's spontaneous mathematical talk showed that children of different abilities favored different mathematical topics. Children with lower mathematical ability tended to identify shapes and count quantities; higher-ability ones tended to look at the picture as a whole and associate it to form images similar to theirs.

7.4 Limitations and Future Work

In this section, we discuss several limitations of this work and possible directions for our future study. First, we set different levels of difficulty in expressing mathematical language for the four flows of the game; some children may feel a lack of novelty in their repeated participation with the problems of the first level, as well as require more shapes and more decorations when changing and decorating images. Second, the final storytelling session was provided with a barebones framework as a free discussion session. However, the evaluation showed that the content of some parent-child conversations was thin and repetitive. Third, excessively long voice guidance reduces children's patience when the experiment was repeated. Thus, we need to adjust the voice flow to better accommodate children's preferences. These limitations revealed some directions for improvement; we can further adapt the flows to accommodate more children. Options for skipping the guide and using more changeable graphics could be provided to support sustained engagement. The final storytelling flow should also provide a more contextual setting to help participants discuss the mathematical features of the avatar.

Some of the parent-child performances in the evaluation also showed us new design directions, one of which is to provide families with more free and flexible learning tools, allowing parents to customize learning tools according to their children's abilities. For example, specifying modular conversation voice packs allows parents to switch different levels of conversational difficulty depending on the child's learning process. It is predictable that mathematical games involving parents and children will also have an impact on the daily math conversations at home. We plan to develop a more flexible MathKingdom system to accommodate children's rapidly growing cognitive levels and explore its long-term impact on children's mathematical language abilities and mathematical dialogue at home. In addition, related to children's cognitive engagement, subject interest, self-efficacy, and emotional development, how rights are distributed in educational games is also a meaningful direction to explore.

8 CONCLUSION

We presented MathKingdom , a voice-guided game to support preschool children's (age: 4–7 years) mathematical language learning at home. In this work, we (1) developed a formative survey to further understand the challenges faced by preschool families and identify design goals; (2) refined the game design through a WoZ experiment; and (3) conducted a multi-day user evaluation that demonstrated the effectiveness of MathKingdom in engaging children and supporting mathematical language learning at home. We reported how MathKingdom promoted interaction in mathematical language contexts, validated the potential of using technology to support mathematical language learning, and identified improvements to the system and directions for future work.
