We present the insights from participatory design work that explores notions of Diversity Computing (DivComp) and how HCI can meaningfully engage with designing technology around diversity without resorting to tokenistic approaches. A future goal overarching the initial findings in this paper is to design technologically mediated, physical spaces (DivComp Spaces) within a school context where children meet, experiment and learn the complex dynamics of othering. We report on a series of nine workshops with 48 children. Based on a thematic analysis, we present four themes — Technology as Utility and Authority, Individual and Collective Place-Making, Staged and Emerging Conflicts, Belonging to the Group and Self-Expression — which we use to inform three design directions for developing DivComp Spaces specifically in the context of school. Finally, we critically reflect on our design practice and the difficulties of designing not only for , but also with diversity meaningfully embedded into design processes.

1 INTRODUCTION

In increasingly diverse societies, the question of how we live together, avoiding destructive conflict while engaging with the diversity of the human condition, is arguably one of the biggest challenges of our time. While diversity is generally seen as beneficial in many contexts — e.g. in fostering creativity — it can also lead to challenges and tensions [ 59 ]. Harmer and Lumsden [ 41 ] argued how digital technologies play key roles in framing diversity and facilitate processes of tensions or othering [ 78 ], i.e. the process of constructing social boundaries which not only serve “to mark and name those thought to be different from oneself but also is a process through which people construct their own identities in reference to others” [ 94 ] cited in [ 45 ]. With an inherent tendency for quantification and categorization [ 23 , 24 , 36 , 62 ], digital technology exerts normative and discriminative power that significantly shape these processes of othering.

Beyond acknowledging that technologies frame diversity and processes of othering, the question arises in which environments technology is designed to do so. Despite an advancing awareness in HCI of the centrality of diversity in addressing inequalities [ 15 , 24 , 33 , 43 ], some approaches applied in HCI towards diversity issues have been criticized, for example, as being tokenistic [ 24 ], i.e. when “people are brought into a team specifically to represent their group but are not given the power and influence to make a difference” [ 33 ]. Dankwa and Draude argue that if approaches towards diversity issues become tokenistic it risks leading to othering of users and that in these approaches there is “nearly no considerations for the intersections and complexities of user identity and context in technology design and development” [ 24 ]. Therefore, considering diversity needs to be pervasive in both the outcome and the process of designing technologies. To address e.g. tokenistic approaches towards diversity issues in HCI, Dankwa and Draude call for “setting diversity at the core of HCI” [ 24 ] by: confronting existing systems of HCI practice; questioning methods, systems of knowledge and practices; and advocating for empowerment of both researchers and participants to make changes to organizational structures [ 24 ].

In this paper, we engage with the question of how to confront the above-mentioned issues of othering in diverse settings by exploring the notion of Diversity Computing (DivComp), as proposed by Fletcher-Watson and colleagues [ 33 ]. DivComp encourage an alternative framing of technology as opposed to current tendencies of normative quantification and categorization by instead suggesting interactive technologies that mediate diversity in meaningful ways. To this end, DivComp seeks to re-frame processes of othering as agonistic [ 58 ], i.e. non-violent, respectful and constructive ways of being different together.

As a specific setting for exploring a DivComp framing of technology, we focus on unstructured, in-between spaces for children in educational settings like school yards, aisles, break rooms or after-school care – i.e. contexts that bring together diverse groups of children and involve complex dynamics of othering [ 9 ]. Our long-term aim is to explore possibilities of augmenting such physical spaces with technology that creates opportunities for diverse groups of children to have meaningful, shared experiences without normative expectations. In this paper, we present and discuss the results from the initial phase of this long-term research project.

In questioning methods and reflecting [as proposed by 24 ] on how to set diversity at the core of how to design DivComp Spaces, we facilitated nine participatory design (PD) workshops over four months with two classes of 48 eleven to thirteen year old children in an austrian middle-school, with at least two researchers participating in the facilitation of each workshop. Our goal was to better understand the context, how diversity and existing processes of othering unfold, and what role technology may play in these processes.

We make the following contributions: We first report on the design and implementation of the nine PD workshops in a school as our empirical case. Second, we present the results of a thematic analysis of the data collected from these workshops. Third, we use the resulting four themes to develop three design directions for DivComp Spaces. Finally, we critically reflect on our methodological choices and discuss implications for designing for and with diversity.

The paper is structured as follows: The next section presents related work on diversity, othering, space and place-theory, and PD with children. In section 3 , we describe the method including the specific setting, how the workshops were organized and how data was collected. We then report on the analysis of the data and present the resulting four themes. Drawing on these four themes and insights from related literature, we develop the three design directions for DivComp Spaces and discuss potentials and challenges of doing PD in the context of DivComp. Lastly, we discuss limitations of the study and how future research may address these.

2 RELATED WORK

Our research interest in DivComp Spaces touches on the intersection of several research fields, including diversity; spaces and place-making; and PD with children, which we review in turn.

2.1 Diversity and Othering

2.1.1 Diversity. The increased awareness of the importance of diversity in HCI [ 15 , 24 , 33 , 43 ] has manifested in several different contexts, for example in HCI education [ 71 ]. For HCI research, the topic of diversity has involved studies including marginalized voices, such as the inclusion of Black women in the design of game-based second language learning [ 67 ], people experiencing homelessness [ 70 ] or displacement because of war [ 1 , 60 ]. However, engaging with diversity in HCI is not only a question of who is included, but also how the complexity of identity is represented in terms of data collection and reporting [ 75 ]. One way which HCI research has explored to design for diverse users is through multimodal interfaces which can “greatly expand the accessibility of computing for diverse and non-specialist users” [ 64 ]. Multimodal interfaces can, for example, learn and adapt to different sensory, cognitive and emotional preferences and capabilities of users [ 26 ].

The notion of diversity is ambiguous because it can be understood as “both finite categorical differences and infinite noncategorical otherness” [ 59 ]. However, understanding diversity as categorical differences may destroy “creativity by reducing diversity to sameness” [ 59 ]. Instead, we frame diversity as infinite noncategorical otherness which “does not homogenise diversity, but instead acknowledges otherness, [this] is the creative aspect of diversity” [ 59 ]. This reflect the notion of diversity in the context of DivComp, where diversity is described as “inherent in the living world, existing within groups and indeed within individuals, as well as between them” [ 33 ]. This definition of diversity moves towards an understanding of differences between people which not only operates on “known diversity dimensions—for example, gender identity or race—but can also depend on mood, health, recent experiences, and personal goals” [ 33 ].

Challenges in relation to diversity “often arise because people are likely to assume that differences are in conflict” [ 59 ]. For example, topics related to diversity and inclusion such as “feminisms, women's health, trans rights, LGBT+ inclusion, neurodiversity, and accessibility in all other forms” [ 81 ] may be seen as challenging or even polarising. Despite this, Strohmayer and colleagues encourage researchers to work towards the inclusion and justice of misrepresented groups, and encourage questioning the existing social order [ 81 ], in line with Dankwa and Draude's call for diversity in HCI [ 23 ].

As described in the introduction, DivComp seeks to reframe processes of othering as agonistic and thereby as allowing space for constructive conflicts while at the same time avoiding to reduce diversity to sameness.

2.1.2 The Challenges and Potential of Othering. The concept of othering originated in the context of post-colonial studies in the social sciences [ 78 ]. As a process of social differentiation and identity formation, othering is multi-dimensional and relates to the concept of intersectionality [ 21 ], that originated in feminist studies to describe the interconnected nature of social categorizations such as ethnicity, class, and gender as a source for systematic privilege, discrimination and oppression.

Within the context of digital technology, most related work on othering targets online identities and social media, see e.g. [ 41 ]. But the entanglement of computing in our lives make this by far not the only arena in which technology shapes power structures and contributes to polarizing processes of othering. Spiel [ 77 ], for example, points out the role of fitness trackers in creating normative boundaries around healthy behavior and who counts as healthy. Wherever sensors and algorithms measure and analyze human life, technology inevitably enacts boundaries and categorizations with systematic normative tendencies.

DivComp envisions the scaffolding of human interactions by supporting diverse peers in making sense of each other [ 33 ]. The theoretical underpinning is provided by the concept of participatory sense making [ 27 ], which describe how we create meaning only in interaction with others. While acknowledging that othering can be polarizing, and at the same time embracing an understanding of diversity as infinite noncategorical otherness, we emphasize othering in the context of the DivComp framework as processes with potential to being agonistic , i.e. non-violent, respectful and constructive ways of being different together.

2.1.3 Design Examples of Agonistic Othering. There are a few projects that target such participatory sense making and ways of being different together through design work. Roberson and Nardi [ 70 ] discuss how providing digital cameras to homeless people in a neighborhood of Los Angeles allowed them to participate in Art Walks and exhibitions, creating meaningful, shared experiences within a larger, predominately housed, community interested in photography. Participatory practices around digital media production also formed the common theme of a sustained collaboration between researchers at the Malmö Living Lab and a local grassroots hip-hop community: Bjögvinsson and colleagues [ 5 ] describe how they involved a wide range of stakeholders and communities, such as immigrant youth from different parts of the city, media production companies and public transport providers, to design systems such as BlueBus (bluetooth sharing of music on buses) or UrbLove (exploration game of diverse urban neighborhoods). Bjögvinsson and colleagues draw on Mouffe's notion of agonistic spaces , allowing a “polyphony of conflicting voices which, despite their opposition, respect each other and are united by passionate engagement” [ 58 ]. The above examples demonstrate the potential for technology to create spaces in which otherwise polarizing othering can be re-framed as agonistic.

From this perspective, DivComp Spaces can be defined as boundary objects and infrastructures [ 79 ] that create agonistic spaces [ 58 ], i.e. they provide a common frame for encounters and modes of participation that enable constructive, respectful and non-violent controversy, in contrast to antagonistic othering that might be normative, delineating or polarising.

2.2 Spaces and Place-Making

Introducing with a quote from Massey: “Spatial organization is integral to the production of the social and not merely its result” [ 56 ] as cited in [ 57 ], McLeod describes how the spatial turn has inspired a growing interest in the notion of space and place in “the experience of schooling – across teaching, learning, pedagogy and curriculum” [ 57 ]. The notion of space and place is generally “conceptually loaded and carry different meanings” [ 20 ]. Therefore, in this subsection we outline how we frame space , place and place-making in relation to school settings — focusing on the unstructured in-between spaces — and in relation to relevant HCI research.

2.2.1 In-Between Spaces and Places. The notion of space and place has roots in research fields such as geography [ 85 ], sociology [ 80 ] and psychology [ 16 ], and has also inspired research in the field of Computer-Supported Cooperative Work (CSCW), see e.g. [ 42 ], and HCI and interaction design, see e.g. [ 19 ] and [ 20 ] for a theoretical overview.

Where space entails the physical environment and setting, “place is space with content” [ 37 ] as cited in [ 69 ]; i.e the physical environments contribute to shaping our experiences and activities [ 20 ]. Place is constituted by the physical attributes of a given location, the activities that occur there and the conceptions people have of that setting [ 16 ] as cited in [ 25 ].

Vardouli and colleagues describe the notion of the in-between space as “a negotiable, ambiguous and incomplete space, meaning a physical configuration without a clear or predefined identity” [ 89 ]. Fataar and Rinquest explore the in-between in the context of school where they are demarcated by being ‘out-of-classroom’ and where children are “interacting based on friendships, camaraderie, segregation, tension, fellowship and competition...These public school spaces then become places where people spend their time, partly because they have to, but also because they choose to” [ 32 ]. Children's interactions in the in-between and out-of-classroom places are essential to their lived experiences of school in general [ 32 ].

We therefore see in-between school spaces as having potential for exploring how technology can be designed to mediate children s’ interactions and facilitate a sense of place, thereby contributing to better understanding how we may design future DivComp Spaces.

2.2.2 Place-Making. Creating a meaningful place — i.e. not only building a physical space— which can then “provoke emotional and intellectual responses in people who inhabit them” [ 69 ], is referred to as place-making . It should be emphasized that it is not the space itself that is important, it is people's experiences — their emotional and intellectual responses — of the place which are meaningful and contribute to place-making [ 54 ]. One example on a study on place-making practices, explored how displaced people living in a refugee camp transformed the spaces into homes by decorating them and discussed how technology could empower these place-making practices [ 60 ]. Similarly, in our study we explore how technology can be designed to facilitate place-making.

Place-making practices in the context of designing DivComp Spaces can be framed from a temporality perspective: In general, through the design process of creating spaces designers enable places rather than directly making places [ 25 ]. Hence, place-making can refer to both 1) the design process of creating spaces, where they are enabled as potential places by designers, and 2) to the ongoing place-making after a space has been created and when the occupants of the space embed meaning into the space. In addition, the ongoing place-making can be directed or framed actively to create or highlight certain identities of places [ 55 ]. Place-framing refers to how people make sense of events, for example in a way that “highlight a collective set of values, beliefs, and goals for some sort of change” [ 55 ]. Creating DivComp Spaces can then be understood as the design process of creating spaces with the potential of making places which are framed with values of the DivComp framework [ 33 ].

The question is then, how can we involve participants in the design process of transforming a space into a place, which simultaneously supports and frames the ongoing place-making after the design process has ended?

2.2.3 Qualities in School Spaces for Potential Ongoing Place-Making. While focusing on learning environments from a holistic perspective — thereby including both formal and informal school spaces — Mäkelä and Helfenstein's design principles [ 53 ] may serve as an inspirational lens for how a DivComp Space in the context of school may be designed. While their design principles do not mention place-making specifically, many of Mäkelä and Helfenstein's design principles build on aspects which may contribute to the kinds of emotional bonds necessary for people experiencing a space as a place [ 53 ]. Summarising, the most relevant design principles for creating the prerequisites for place-making in school spaces, which align well with our research focus are the following six:

  1. Balance socializing and collaboration with privacy and individuality.
  2. Consider both a shared vision as well as differing views and personal preferences.
  3. Comfortable and spacious environments as well as indoor and outdoor environments which promote physical wellness.
  4. Elements of nature and time and space for leisure and rest.
  5. Balancing novelty with conventionality in terms of tools and space solutions.
  6. Functional and flexible space for easy modification to respond to varying needs and requirements [ 53 ].

With the presence of technology in spaces — such as “[u]biquitous and pervasive technologies, ambient media and tangible computing technologies” [ 19 ]— additional qualities for place-making emerge: technology “can modify the spaces we inhabit, create new places or new forms of presence, and make physical presence itself a way of interacting with the system” [ 19 ]. In other words, technology embedded into spaces can continuously shape and mediate the activities and experiences happening there [ 19 ].

2.2.4 Designing the Qualities for Facilitating Ongoing Place-Making. While the above design principles concern the qualities for how spaces can become places for people in schools and may serve as guidelines for what needs to be present in the resulting space, the design process itself behind this outcome — i.e. place-making practices — is still not widely adopted in e.g. professions creating spaces, such as architecture and planning [ 25 ]. This is because “there exist huge complexities about the differences of perceptions among the participants in planning and design; between professionals and people, and between people and people themselves” [ 25 ]. Place-making cannot solely be undertaken by professionals such as designers and architects, as it is exactly the occupants of places who transform a space into a place [ 25 ]. Several have therefore argued for the importance of participatory approaches in order to involve people in the place-making, see e.g. [ 19 , 25 , 69 ] and, specifically for HCI, see e.g. [ 19 , 22 ]. Engaging people in designing for place-making is not only a question of how , but also of who ; as Rigolon argues “children's participation in design processes is seen as an effective way of creating places that fit their individual and collective identity” [ 69 ]. In the next section, we present related works on designing with children participants in PD.

2.3 Participatory Design and Children Participants

PD emerged as a topic in the field of information technology in the workplace in the 1970s [ 2 ]. Since then, the discourse of PD has been greatly influenced by questions of e.g. politics of participation, interaction and power dynamics between users and designers [ 84 ] in addition to how the mechanisms of PD are performed today, i.e. to “probe participants and encourage engagement, prime the participants for immersion in the design process, gain an understanding of the user's current experiences and context, and generate ideas and concepts” [ 72 ] as cited in [ 84 ]. However, “democratic participation and empowerment of users in PD is non-trivial to achieve” [ 84 ]. As an example, Thinyane and colleagues observed barriers of: language , cultural factors , power dynamics and personality traits which impaired democratic participation and empowerment in their PD case [ 84 ].

Adding to these PD challenges is the participation of children [ 29 ]. Psychology and neuroscience research literature show that the behavior of children in adolescence (young adolescents are defined as aged 11–14 years [ 6 ]) is characterized as a developing period of “heightened self-consciousness, mood variability, novelty seeking, risk taking, and peer orientation” [ 6 ]. The characteristic behavior of adolescence in the context of PD may then be expressed differently from the participation of adults: While a close relationship between designers and participants is generally important in PD, it is even more essential with children participants who “may take more time ‘warming up’ to strangers, but also to create a ‘safe zone’ in which participants are comfortable enough to freely engage with the session setting” [ 47 ] citing [ 4 ]. Van Mechelen and colleagues describe six challenging co-design dynamics in the context of designing with children aged nine to ten, [ 87 ]: (1) unequal power among children, (2) free riding, (3) unwillingness to take tasks serious, (4) dysfunctional conflicts, (5) apart together, i.e. “working individually and only combining results superficially” [ 88 ], and (6) groupthink, i.e rushing too quickly towards consensus.

Van Mechelen and colleagues argue that balancing these dynamics are key to configuring the participation of children in PD processes [ 87 ]. In their words, conflicts “can be a positive force when it creates more energy around sharing diverse information and viewpoints. The challenge is to move groups from dysfunctional to constructive conflicts” [ 88 ]. Therefore, one of the central tasks of researchers is to organize dialogues with children and between them and to ensure that conflicts transform into meaningful design concepts [ 87 ]. The challenging dynamics reported by [ 87 ] would also occasionally occur during our workshops. During our workshops we developed strategies for how to accommodate for these challenges based on developing familiarity with the children. We elaborate on this in section 3 .

Integrating place-theory, place-making and PD with children in the context of planning school spaces, Rigolon discuss how to involve young children in this kind of planning, which is essential because while “designers usually consider the big picture, children have a finer knowledge, paying more attention to the small details, and both levels are important” [ 69 ]. Rigolon describes seven stages involving children in PD: (1) Problem recognition, (2) Briefing and process planning, (3) Analysis, (4) Design, (5) Construction, (6) Evaluation, and (7) Ongoing modification. Here we mainly consider the (3) Analysis and (4) Design phase, as these were the focus of the case study so far:

  • (3) Analysis: of the existing situation “focusing on how children perceive and use places” [ 69 ] citing [ 82 ]. For the data collection, drawing is discussed as a widely used technique to get information from children and can be done by them while discussing with a facilitator. Photography is also mentioned as a way for children to express ideas about places. In section 3.3 , we outline the design process where similar techniques were applied. Rigolon further discuss content analysis as an appropriate analysis technique. For our study, we applied a similar technique; thematic analysis. We motivate this choice in section 3 .
  • (4) Design: is the stage where new scenarios for the physical environment is proposed. Here, ‘warm-up’ activities to enhance children's imagination is suggested, so that they “understand that design is creating something different from what they see in their everyday lives” [ 69 ]. Subsequently, ‘real’ design activities can take place. For example, design games “can make the process more interesting for children because they can act out situations” [ 69 ] citing [ 73 ]. We applied this in the form of a kind of Inspiration Card Workshop [ 39 ] with the children, described in section 3.3 . One issue in this stage is to establish a language for communicating between designers and the children. For this purpose, visual tools is suggested. In our case, we have facilitated lo-fi prototyping together with the children, described in section 3.3 .

The subsequent phases, (5) Construction, (6) Evaluation, and (7) Ongoing modification, are considerations for our future research. An important aspect is (7) the ongoing modification, which reflect how a school space may support ongoing place-making: “it means leaving part of the canvas incomplete, designing spaces that are significantly flexible and that have a strong integration with furniture, so that children and teachers can modify their settings according to the different activities and the atmosphere that is searched” [ 69 ].

Ultimately, navigating and exploring the intersection of diversity (section 2.1 ), place-making in in-between spaces (section 2.2 ) and children participants (section 2.3 ) offer a complex but interesting area of exploring and developing our understanding of how to design future DivComp Spaces. The next section describes how we approached this complex area methodologically.

3 METHOD

In this section we first describe the setting of the case study before describing the nine PD workshops and the data collection.

3.1 Setting

We collaborated with a local middle school (children from ten to fourteen years old) in a socially and culturally diverse area of Salzburg, Austria. Due to its location and its status within the national education system, the school include a large proportion of children from ethnically diverse backgrounds, see section 3.2 . After obtaining informed consent from the children, their parents and teachers, we conducted nine workshops fortnightly from February to June 2022. The workshops took place during term, in lieu of lessons in other subjects and thus lasted 50 minutes each. This fortnightly rhythm allowed us some time to document and evaluate our workshops after completion and plan the upcoming ones.

3.2 Participants

The participating children from the two classes were aged eleven to thirteen and the majority came from ethnically diverse backgrounds: 2B, which focused on creativity and design school subjects, consisted of 25 children (19 girls with 1 girl undergoing gender transition, 6 boys) with the following first languages: Albanian (2), Austrian (2), Bosnian (1), Croatian (3), Dari (1), Serbian (4), Slovakian (1), Spanish (1), Turkish (9), and Ukrainian (1). Four of these children had special educational needs, with two having ADHD. 2C, which focused on natural sciences school subjects, consisted of 23 children (7 girls, 16 boys) with the following first languages: Afghan (3), Austrian (4), Bosnian (3), Brazilian (1), Chechen (3), Cuban (1), Hungarian (1), Macedonian (1), Serbian (1), Slovenian (1), Turkish (2), Romanian (1), and Ukrainian (1). Five of these children have special educational needs, with one having ADHD and one with autism spectrum disorder.

Because two different classes participated in our workshops, we would repeat the facilitation of a workshop format twice on the same day, so both classes of 2B and 2C participated in the same process of nine workshops each. The teachers of the two classes would usually participate or observe during the workshops.

Returning to the argument by Fataar and Rinquest, in-between school spaces already present diverse social dynamics in terms of “friendships, camaraderie, segregation, tension, fellowship and competition” [ 32 ] and with the added diverse backgrounds of the two classes described above, we found the studied setting to be fitting for exploring the framework of DivComp with children along various dimensions of difference, including: ethnicity, gender, neurodiversity, and social-economic backgrounds.

3.3 Data Collection: Nine PD Workshops

We planned a longer series of nine workshops for several reasons: As children participants may take time to “warm up” to strangers [ 47 ], a longer engagement with the participants would enable us to build rapport with them [ 31 ]. The nine workshops also enabled us to structure activities in alignment with a standard design process. The in-built rhythm of organizing a workshop every other week was furthermore a way for us to enable some experimentation in the planning of the workshop: as we got to know the different children, we could reiterate our planned workshops accordingly.

The collected data from the workshops was in the form of: 75 drawings, 39 written feedback notes from the children, 264 pictures, 241 minutes of audio recordings, and 233 minutes of video recordings. To support our reflexivity during the facilitation and organization of the workshops, each facilitator maintained a research diary after each workshop. The research diaries included reflections on how a workshop went, concerns regarding e.g. how to facilitate the children's participation, how to improve data-collection, ideas for future workshops etc. Later, for the thematic analysis described in 4 , the research diaries helped us in guiding us to interesting points in our data. In the following, we describe the workshops in terms of themes, goals, activity, and data collection:

3.3.1 Workshop 1 (WS1): Introduction. The first workshop with the children was an introduction of the researchers and the research project. To start building a relationship with the children, we performed a playful social ‘icebreaker’ activity, by asking them five different questions around preferences (e.g. which pizza topping they preferred) and family relations (e.g. how many siblings they have). The goal was to illustrate and discuss differences and similarities among the children. This short ice-breaker questionnaire is included in the appendix. The data collected from this workshop consisted of written feedback on the activity from the children.

3.3.2 Workshop 2 (WS2): Cognitive Maps. Before involving the children in changing their environment, it is essential to gain an understanding of how they perceive and use their environment [ 25 , 69 ]. We approached this by dividing the children into groups and let them draw floor plans based on their memory of the space, a method called cognitive maps [ 86 ]. To further explore their perceptions of the floor plans, the children annotated the drawings with additional qualities for specific spaces (see figure 1 ). For example, the children were asked “where do you like to be alone” and “where do most conflicts emerge?” The data gathered from this workshop consisted of the groups’ annotated floor plan drawings.

3.3.3 Workshop 3 (WS3): Imagining Technology. In order to learn more about how the children conceptualize technology, we organized an activity where they had to imagine future and improved versions of current technology. First, we asked the children to list all the every-day technology they use and meet during their morning routine from waking up to getting to school. Next, the children were asked to choose one of these every-day technologies and were then handed a paper template representing a “future newspaper”, with an empty space for drawing. The children then drew “advertisements” for their chosen technology on the paper template, including annotations of the technology could do and how it could improve the children's lives (see figure 2 ). The annotated drawings and audio-recordings of contextual interviews with some of the children were gathered for the data-collection.

3.3.4 Workshop 4, 5 and 6 (WS4, WS5, WS6): Technology Exploration. Building on the theme from WS3, we dedicated WS4, WS5, and WS6 to technology exploration. This approach is similar to participatory bootstrapping , where “designers reveal the capabilities and characteristics of technology and technical infrastructure to end users in intelligible ways [...] and end users try out and explore the possibilities for such technologies to enhance their practices [...]” [ 17 ]. We divided the children into three groups. Each group had to design ideas to improve an area near a couch in the common hallway, which was often used by the children in their breaks. The children built small-scale lo-fi prototypes using one of the three different technologies, introduced by the facilitators: Calliope mini combined with LEGO (fig. 3 ), Makey-Makey with various craft materials (fig. 4 ) and an e-textile prototyping kit with the Arduino LilyPad (fig. 5 ). In the subsequent two workshops we rotated the groups, meaning that by the end of the sixth workshop all children had tried out all three types of the technologies. The data-collection from the three workshops consisted of audio-recordings of contextual interviews from the three groups of children and pictures of their prototypes. Additionally after WS6, we conducted a 36 min. long interview with the main teachers of the two classes. We asked the teachers about their perspective on the workshops and the children's experiences with the workshops.

3.3.5 Workshop 7 (WS7): Bodystorming. As a way to combine the previous workshops’ foci on the children's conceptualisations of school spaces and technology , we planned the activity of WS7 to revolve around bodystorming . Bodystorming is a kind of brainstorming while physically present in the relevant space [ 63 ]. The children were divided into three groups with one facilitator per group. Each group was led to one of the three floors by the facilitator (see figures 6 , 7 , and 8 ). While on their respective floors, the children were asked to point out interesting in-between spaces on that floor, discuss what usually happens there, and how this space could be improved. Additionally, we asked the children to stage these typical activities and their ideas for improvement and take pictures of the staging. The data-collection from this workshop consisted of audio-recordings of the facilitated discussions, and the staging pictures.

3.3.6 Workshop 8 (WS8): Designing for Others. WS8 spanned two school hours, in contrast to the previous workshops which were facilitated during one 50 minutes session. We facilitated a design activity involving phases of ideation, prototyping and user testing, building on the insights from the previous workshops. Inspired by the Inspiration Card Workshop — i.e. a “collaborative method for combining findings from domain studies, represented in Domain Cards , with sources of inspirations from applications of technology, represented in Technology Cards (see figure 9 ) to create new concepts for design” [ 39 ] — we facilitated the children's ideation phase using a set of domain and technology cards. We prepared domain cards based on the children's discussions on likes, dislikes and ideas from WS7. Similar to WS7, each group of children worked on one of the three school floors. The groups stayed the same as in WS7, but rotated floors. The design activity started with the groups choosing one domain card of a space they would like to work with. The likes, dislikes and ideas on the domain card formed the starting point of the design task, which was to generate ideas for prototypes in the space. We started this ideation process by introducing all technology cards and discussing them with the children. Next, we asked the children to combine technology cards in order to form new ideas for technology applications in ways which would meet the likes and dislikes from the domain cards. These ideas were then prototyped using the provided lo-fi prototyping materials (for an example see figure 10 ). The prototypes were presented by the children to a couple of children from the group who originally had discussed likes and dislikes in the space, and who acted as an “outsider perspective” providing feedback. The feedback was used to improve the prototypes, and, lastly, the groups would record videos of enactments of the prototype in “use”. The data-collection of this workshop consisted of video-recordings of the design activity and the prototype enactments.

3.3.7 Workshop 9 (WS9): Presentation and Celebration. The last workshop was planned as a presentation of the video-recordings from WS8 of the prototype enactments to the children's class, and as a celebration of what the children had accomplished over the course of the semester. No data was collected during this workshop.

4 ANALYSIS

In this section, we first describe the thematic analysis method used for analyzing the data-collection described above. We then present the results of the analysis in terms of four themes.

4.1 Thematic Analysis Method

Thematic analysis (TA) [ 12 ] offers a flexible way of analyzing qualitative data, which may consist of both “textual (e.g. transcripts of interviews and focus groups) and visual (photographic and video) data” [ 14 ]. While TA acknowledges that analysis often uses a combination of inductive and deductive approaches, one approach tends to predominate [ 12 ]. The analysis was mainly driven by our research interest in how children conceptualize topics on diversity, conflict, socio-spatial relations and technology (as outlined in section 2 ), and the themes described in section 4.2 reflect this focus.

The two authors who took part in facilitating all workshops collaborated in analyzing the data while following a six-phase TA approach as described by Braun and Clarke [ 12 ]: They (1) had familiarized themselves with the data , as they had been part of collecting the qualitative data (described in section 3.3 ) as well as looked, listened, and read through the data before systematizing it and preparing it for further analysis. Next, they (2) generated initial codes independently from each other, and then (3) generated a first draft of themes . They (4) reviewed the potential themes by comparing their independently generated themes. They consulted the other two authors to finally (5) define and name the themes collectively. Through (6) producing this paper , the themes have been further defined and refined, resulting in the four themes described in the next section.

In applying TA to our data, we sought to be critically aware of the methodological choice and how it shaped our findings. In generating codes and themes, we initially were frustrated by their seemingly disconnected nature. Only when we started to see them as inter-connected differences and tensions rather than containers for sameness, our insights became meaningful and conductive for developing them into design directions. Rather than framing our themes as single-aspect categories, we developed our analytical approach further, resulting in themes characterized by their inner oppositions. This enabled us to frame the themes as complex entities, entailing contradictions and ambiguity. We reflect on this perspective to more traditional TA in our discussion below (section 5.2.2 ) and link it to wider notions of performativity and diffractive analysis.

4.2 Themes

We identified the following four themes, which all aim to reflect the main dimension of difference in their title: (1) Technology as Utility and Authority, (2) Individual and Collective Place-Making, (3) Staged and Emerging Conflicts, and (4) Belonging to the Group and Self-Expression. The themes capture the children's experiences of and struggles with topics like technology practice (current and future), spatial relations and social dynamics, stemming from issues connected to dimensions of diversity.

4.2.1 Technology as Utility and Authority: While discussing technology and its possible roles with the children, we observed notions of envisioned technologies ranging from understanding technology as utility to technology as authority. Both notions were expressed in the children's inventions and observed in their reactions towards recording technologies. Technology as utility describes technologies that can be controlled, offering support, comfort or even superpowers to people. Technology as authority takes on a role of a controlling and regulating instance, including surveillance of people's behavior.

In WS3 the children imagined future technologies. Most of their ideas concentrated on useful or supportive technology, helping the children in overcoming problems often in utopian ways. For example, an idea described a “Robot technology [which] when you lose body parts you can easily get them replaced. And you can also completely turn into a robot and you get a free flying [skateboard].” Other ideas included a neurochip for restoring sight, a bottle reminding you to stay hydrated, and a talking clock for people who cannot read the clock.

Some children also described ideas for technology for comfort and companionship. For example, a child described a robot in WS3: “It is a little friend for life. It can protect me. It has good skills with which it can save your life.” This idea was also expressed in WS4, where the activity revolved around enhancing couches in the corridor with technology. As an example, a child described a smart couch: “Our couch should have AI, which can give a massage, it should be human-like.” Other children extended the couch with conversational AI: “You can talk to Siri or for example some robot, it doesn't have to be Siri, just if you are lonely and if you have some questions, or when you need something, for example a massage. I know that exists but I really want to put it there.” The different ideas for support described by the children concentrated on physical as well as psychological well-being.

As we learned from interviews with teachers conducted after WS6, the children had their first proper year at school after covid-related restrictions were eased, and thus were only starting to discover school spaces and each other. One of the teachers told us: "Everything is open and unknown, there is a lot of the unknown in this meeting with other classes, and I think there is a lot of insecurity for children." Loneliness and ideas for technology as a companion could potentially be attributed to the children trying to navigating the school space, finding their own places and people in a very diverse and crowded environment.

As an example on the perspective on more authoritarian technology, one child described an idea for a drone with an AI system which kills people who do "bad" things:

Child: “It is a drone with integrated AI, which detects illegal things. [...] If there is a person running amok, it recognizes it and eliminates the person by flying there and exploding. [...] Everybody who is saying or doing bad things loses their heads”.

Interviewer 1: “And what if YOU say something bad?”

Child (in a convincing tone): “It is an AI! It ensures justice! And I would use it for good things”;

Interviewer 2: “And how do you know which things are good and which bad?”

Child: “Evil things are evil, and good things are good;”

While this seems like a rather violent vision, the described idea is interesting as it speaks to a perception of technology as having power and authority to enforce rules in an objective way. We hypothesize that this idea of having an external regulatory power to solve emerging conflicts could be seen as a strategy for coping with insecurities related to navigating the social dynamics and tensions of the school spaces.

Related to the perspective on technology as authoritarian, during the workshops we observed the children behave differently depending on the social context. When we as facilitators documented the workshops by recording video, audio and taking pictures of the children, the children would often be hesitant despite being reminded that the data would be anonymized and only viewed by us, as described in the consent form. In WS3, one group requested to re-record audio as they were gossiping in the background and felt uncomfortable about the recording. This would often happen when we controlled the documentation technology (e.g. audio or video recorder). On the contrary, we often observed that the children enjoyed and were more comfortable if they were controlling the audio or video recorder. For example, for two groups in WS7 the children were instructed to take pictures of their acting classmates and seemed to enjoy posing for pictures. Similarly, in WS8 we observed that if the documentation technology was e.g. fixed to a wall and thereby not visibly operated by us, the children were less hesitant to participate in the workshop and being recorded compared to when we visibly operated the technology. In this situation, the children were comfortable enough to act silly and make funny faces in front of the camera, especially when we were not nearby. Summarizing, we observed that the children would behave hesitant towards our documentation technology when the technology seemed to be visibly operated by an authority (us) and seemed more comfortable with “neutral” technology (e.g. when fixed to a wall) or when the technology was operated by their peers.

4.2.2 Individual and Collective Place-Making: We observed how place-making — where the children developed emotional connections to places — happened both individually and collectively in groups. These individual and collective place-making practices would sometimes overlap and cause tensions between different children, especially when the same spaces were the target of both individual and collective place-making practices at the same time.

During the activities in WS4-8 the children were engaged in making ideas for improving in-between school spaces. Many ideas revolved around spaces which could cater to individual children's needs, e.g. through controllable light settings and massage-giving furniture, embedded entertainment systems, or spaces for taking naps on a couch, without any technology involved. These ideas connect to having places for individuals to retreat to, related to design principle (3) for place-making in schools which describes environments that promote physical well-being [ 69 ].

The following is an example on a combination of collective and individual place-making, which is related to the first design principle for place-making in schools; i.e. the balancing of socializing and collaboration with privacy and individuality [ 69 ]. In WS7 and WS8 a group of girls told us that they often gather in the restroom to hang out together. They did not necessarily use the restrooms for their primary purpose, but as a place where they found the privacy for e.g. spending time in front of a mirror, using it to make videos or to re-apply make-up. For this group of girls, the restrooms was a central place for bonding. Another group of girls, however, felt excluded from this space and did not want to use the restrooms in the school anymore at the same time as the first group of girls. As one girl commented: “I would rather die, than pee there.”

Even more than just a safe space for same-gender encounters, restrooms also seemed to be the rare touch points between same-gender children of different age groups, adding another layer of diversity tensions. From our interview with the teachers, they explained: "during covid, the only opportunity to meet somebody from another class was at the restroom. And then they would often arrange specific time when they were about to meet during lessons, because they wouldn't be allowed to meet during the breaks. So from their time at school, they don't know each other that well. Maybe more from extra-curricular activities, but not from school."

In general, we observed gender to be one of the the major dividing forces in both classes. Especially in WS8, the girls were sitting together while keeping a clear distance from the boys and exchanging comments like: "We don't get along. They [boys] function differently." And "they are perverted" . While the boys on the other side complained the girls were "so bitchy" and would "always scream around" .

As an idea for facilitating meaningful encounters between individual and collective place-making practices, a group of children in WS7 suggested re-structuring the hallway area into smaller areas dedicated to children's interests and hobbies, e.g. a music area, a “chill area”, and a sports area dedicated to teaching each other different skills: "You can tell what you are doing there and how you have learned it. For example, [refers to another girl] makes a split, because she can do this, and then she has to explain how she did it." This quote shows how overlapping individual place-making practices not necessarily may lead to conflict, but could be beneficial for fostering collaboration among different children.

4.2.3 Staged and Emerging Conflicts: As pointed out in the above, an overlap of individual and collective place-making practices would sometimes lead to tensions, turning places of retreat into spaces of conflict. In WS2, WS6, WS7, and WS8 we discussed where and when conflicts were happening with the children. Based on these discussions, we decided to differentiate between two kinds of conflicts: Staged conflicts : everyone participating would engage in the conflict voluntarily, as it was more playful in nature. Emerging conflicts : This kind of conflict emerged when, for example, some children wanted to occupy a space and use it for something different than other children already using that space as a place for retreat, thereby intruding into their place-making practice.

For engaging in staged conflicts, the children preferred spaces where the teachers would not interrupt. In WS7 and WS8 two groups of children who explored the school's library spaces, described a system for play fighting that would warn the children about teachers approaching the space. A boy elaborated this system, explaining that he like to engage in fights for fun, however he wanted to avoid being discovered by the teachers: “Some technology, for example, cameras out there, so that when we are here, and we also have fun and fight, for example, so that we can see when teachers are coming.” .

In WS2 we found that many conflicts happen in in-between spaces: mostly around a couch area in one of the classrooms of the two classes and at a couch and a table in a common hallway area. At the same time, in WS2 and WS4 the children pointed out these same spaces as their main retreat places in which they like to be on their own or chat with friends. To understand the tensions of these places, we asked what would happen if another child wanted to use the retreat place for themselves. “A dispute would emerge of who is allowed to sit here and who is not [...] And then there's the couch ban from the teachers.” This is an example on an emerging conflict, where the children involuntarily engaged in a conflict about the space. To resolve this particular conflict, teachers enforced a “couch ban” for all the children, whereas the children themselves suggested having more couches in the space as a possible solution.

Emerging conflicts may be rooted in the insecurity around previously uninhabited school spaces and other classes due to the covid-regulations. The teacher of 2B told us in the interview: “Because they were restricted to the use of their classrooms for such a long time because of covid, it's only now that they are starting to negotiate who is allowed to go where during the break, who is allowed to sit on the couch or who is not. So children are approaching all this cautiously. They are looking for their places, but they haven't found them yet, I would say.”

4.2.4 Belonging to the Group and Self-Expression: The conflicts emerging during the workshops did not only revolve around spatial limitations but also around group dynamics within each class. We noticed a tension between the children wanting to belong to a group and at the same time wanting to express themselves.

Wanting to belong to a group was most visible in WS1, where we during an ice-breaker exercise grouped the children based on their answers to different questions. For example, when some children expressed their dislike of one particular pizza topping, the rest unanimously agreed on this. The children seemed to enjoy having a particular preference that united all of them. As one child commented: “That nobody likes pizza with pineapple was the best.”

In contrast to this almost conformist behavior, we also observed individual expressions that aimed at standing out. For example, in WS1 we asked the children to describe themselves with one adjective, and this turned into almost a competition for some children in describing themselves with the most negative adjective (e.g. “I am impatient” , “I am arrogant” , “I am annoying” ). Reflecting the related work on children by Blakemore and Mills [ 6 ], we observed the ice-breaker exercise and describing themselves with one adjective as a balancing act for the children of wanting to belong to a group of peers, while at the same time trying to stand out and express themselves, perhaps in order to get recognition.

Standing out can also happen unintentionally and against one's will. For example, when we asked about first and second languages spoken at home, only one girl explained that she spoke exclusively German at home. She openly addressed this as an issue in front of the class, complaining that the other children had an additional language to talk behind her back and that she felt excluded. This prompted a discussion on language diversity in the class and what it entailed on a day-to-day basis, e.g. rules like not switching to your second language during school time. We return to this particular situation in section 5.3 .

5.1.1 Negotiable Spaces for Retreating. As described in the themes Individual and Collective Place-Making and Staged and Emerging Conflicts , we observed that children would go to certain spaces in order to feel secure enough to relax and chat with their friends or just be on their own. At the same time, we found that these same spaces and their boundaries were contested, producing conflicts about who could and could not be in the space, thereby interrupting these spaces’ character of being places to retreat to. Related to these observations is design principle (4) discussed by Mäkelä and Helfenstein [ 53 ], about creating spaces for leisure and rest (see section 2.2 ). Another facet of spaces for leisure was an interesting idea proposed by a group of children, which is described in the theme Individual and Collective Place-Making : They suggested dedicating areas of the communal school space to children's interests and hobbies and for teaching each other these different skills.

How can we design spaces which enable children to retreat to a private environment dedicated to leisurely activities or engage in interests or hobbies, while at the same time facilitating constructive negotiations of who is allowed in? We do not argue for a techno-solutionist approach — i.e. that technology may solve all conflicts about who uses the space for rest — instead, we wish to explore how these conflicts may be negotiated in constructive ways facilitated by technology, i.e. scaffolding agonistic struggles while keeping antagonistic confrontations at bay, as suggested by DivComp.

In line with Mäkelä and Helfenstein's [ 53 ] and Rigolon's [ 69 ] emphasis on flexible spaces for easy ongoing modification (for details see the related work in sections 2.2 and 2.3 ) we may then ask: how can we design a space for retreating in the in-between spaces of school? Furthermore, how may technology allow for ongoing but respectful negotiation of who gets to be in such a space? The different children had diverse ideas of how to occupy the in-between school spaces, and a related question is to consider how more marginalized voices of these children may be heard in such negotiations. To approach these considerations, we may turn to research on multi-user interaction design: The conflicts described in the theme Individual and Collective Place-Making relates to differing opinions of how a space can be used and who can be there. Related to this, Niemantsverdriet and colleagues discuss how to approach conflicts from a multi-user interaction design context [ 61 ]. Niemantsverdriet and colleagues argue how “by providing visibility of information, to create awareness about the other users in the interface at the moment of interaction, people can better estimate what impact their adjustments will have on others” [ 61 ] in the context of multi-user lighting systems. For future research exploring the development of DivComp spaces, a resource for inspiration on how to approach multi-user interaction design is Shavitt and colleagues’ model of eight factors which affect how people use multi-user systems [ 76 ]. For example, they discuss the factor of Proximity — i.e. the user's position and orientation compared to each other and to e.g. a device — as a way of handling multi-user conflicts [ 76 ]. While considering e.g. the proximity factor is relevant in multi-user interfaces, we should also consider diverse modalities to include different preferences and capabilities in order to facilitate constructive use and negotiations of spaces [ 26 ]. For instance, some children may enjoy using their bodies to interact with a system while other children may enjoy more tactile experiences using their hands to feel the interface.

Taken together, we can envision a DivComp Space which informs children in a certain space about how other children wish to use a space as a retreat, thereby potentially encouraging behaviors which better suit a space for privacy in groups or for individuals, leisurely activities, and perhaps facilitate turn-taking of the space.

5.1.2 Spaces Scaffolding Playful Staged Conflicts. While Negotiable Spaces for Retreating revolve around conflicts regarding who gets to be in retreat-like places, the design direction we discuss in this section relates to conflicts which are consensual and more playful in character. Specifically in the theme Staged and Emerging Conflicts , some boys described how they engaged in physical fights for fun. From the research literature this kind of play fighting, or staged conflict, is described as fulfilling a variety of purposes: “the very point of play's paradoxes are that they permit us to communicate such subjectivity to others in a disguised fashion. They permit us to say the opposite of what we mean in order to mean the opposite of what we say... Being weak we (child or adult) play at being powerful in order both to disguise that we feel weak and yet to reveal to any other that weakness is our problem” [ 46 ]. While this kind of play at being powerful, or play fighting, is considered important and constructive in children's play, it may, however, not always be benign [ 68 ] citing [ 11 ]: “children also use play to terrorize each other. That is to say, they often move between pretending and actually being aggressive [...] the recipients of such conflicting information have difficulty in predicting what will happen next and become uneasy” [ 46 ] cited in [ 68 ].

Continuing this line of thinking from the perspective of developing future DivComp Spaces, these ordered conflicts are acknowledged and not necessarily something which should be prevented, suppressed or solved. However, we should aim for designing technology that can accommodate for the ambiguity of such ordered conflicts and give way for mutually satisfactory play fighting. The question is which role technology can play in facilitating these conflicts constructively in DivComp Spaces?

Future design directions in the context of designing spaces for constructively facilitating playful conflicts may look towards multi-user interaction design[ 61 , 76 ], similar to the design direction Negotiable Spaces for Retreating . Additionally, we can look towards the field of game design and playful interactions, which often incorporate conflict as an essential part of design, see for example “Games as Systems of Conflict” in [ 83 ]. The value of playfulness as a way to bring diverse people together have been argued for and explored before, see e.g. Fonseca and colleagues’ work [ 34 ]. Another example relevant to the design of DivComp Spaces in this design direction, is the work of Paasovaara and colleagues who outline a design space for exploring playful interactions mediated by interactive technology in public spaces [ 65 ]. They identify roles and levels of playful interaction systems, which may serve as inspiration for how to design DivComp Spaces facilitating conflicts. As an approach to designing for staged conflicts in the form of competition [ 83 ], we may engage with asymmetrical game design (see e.g. [ 38 , 95 ]) in addition to leveraging multimodal interface design. In considering asymmetrical game design for this design direction of DivComp Spaces, we are not only considering diverse preferences (in terms of multimodal interfaces) but also for diverse capabilities of different children who engage in playful and staged conflicts.

We can envision DivComp Spaces designed to facilitate playful interactions in encounters of children, where they can engage in competitions to engage in staged risk-taking behaviour [ 6 ] such as in play fighting [ 68 ], although ambiguous participation should be accommodated for.

5.1.3 Self-Conscious and Peer-Orientated Spaces for Performing. As described in the theme Technology as Utility and Authority , we observed interesting behaviors among the children in regards to being documented by technology, spanning from skeptical hesitance to silly and playful clowning in the face of a camera lens. This observed behavior is also described by research literature which explain how young adolescent children is in the process of developing increased self-consciousness and peer-orientation, as well as engaging in risk-taking and novelty seeking [ 6 ]. While this behavior was sometimes challenging during workshops and when recording data, we frame it as a potential for designing DivComp Spaces.

As a potential inspiration for such a design direction, we turn to the work of Long and colleagues, who have engaged in several projects designing co-creative AI for public spaces [ 49 ]. They argue that while AI is becoming increasingly prevalent in our everyday lives, there are still many misunderstandings of AI. In this perspective, public spaces can “serve as alternative venues for AI literacy initiatives” [ 49 ]. They further argue for creating co-creative AI systems in such spaces, as the “social, open-ended nature of co-creative experiences also make them well-suited for engaging participants in free-choice informal learning environments” [ 49 ]. Considering the children's awareness of and interest in AI and robots, as described in the theme Technology as Utility and Authority , together with the children's own socio-cultural development of self-consciousness and peer-oriented performance, we argue that such co-creative AI systems in DivComp Spaces could potentially serve two purposes: 1) co-creative AI systems could be trained and interacted with by the children themselves, as a way of staging performances of actions - similar to what we observed from our recordings of the children making funny faces to the camera or the group of girls making videos in the restroom. 2) co-creative AI systems could potentially develop the children's AI literacy [ 50 ].

As creation is closely related to self-expression[ 44 ], we can further shape the design direction based on our observations from the theme Belonging to the Group and Self-Expression . In this perspective, how may co-creative AI systems in DivComp Spaces allow children to express themselves without making them feel excluded or contributing to negative othering? A potential inspiration for extending the design direction could be to turn towards gamification strategies: López-Faican and Jaen propose a circular and iterative empathy development model based on gamification strategies in the context of designing interactive systems. They argue that users of systems applying this model thereby “are exposed to affective, cognitive, reflective and social experiences that encourage the expression of behaviors of a prosocial nature” [ 51 ]. For example, for promoting prosocial decisions, López-Faican and Jaen suggest including: (1) “Challenges/Tasks/Missions (individual and group-based)” and (2) “Emotional rewards (individual and group-based) that increase the degree of social binding between group members” [ 51 ]. Particularly in the combination of co-creative AI systems which includes AI avatars and story-telling elements, the concept of perspective taking is interesting [ 51 ]: “Role-taking allows the player to identify with a character, which leads the participant to get involved in the story, therefore avatars and feedback are elements that help promote perspective taking, generate feelings and understand the story” [ 51 ]. An interesting example for inspiring this design direction of DivComp Spaces is The Storytelling Machine, a multi-user interface including story-telling elements where people's drawings are transformed “into animated characters that roam the artwork's video worlds. The machine delivers a randomly generated collective story created from public content” [ 74 ].

Summarizing, we can envision DivComp Spaces designed with co-creative AI systems exploring children’ self-conscious and peer-oriented development [ 6 ] and which incorporate gamification strategies that focus on encouraging expression of prosocial behavior.

5.2 Participatory Design for Diversity

5.2.1 Designing for Others. HCI research has engaged with diversity issues by, for example, including and empowering marginalized voices, cf. section 2.1 . Giving voice to children in PD, however, is a political challenge [ 18 ]. The aim for the designer is to not assert their own ideas but instead to enable the children to “contribute to the design process, through the possibilities and limitations of their capabilities” such as through linguistic symbols, embodied interactions or other forms of constructive exchange[ 18 ]. While acknowledging that such PD dialogues with children is interpretative by nature [ 18 ] — i.e. we as researchers bring values of diversity and inclusion with us through the DivComp framing and therefore interpret the design process through that lens — we aimed at tailoring the workshop series to the children's capabilities and interests while still keeping the DivComp framing in mind.

As a case in point, during WS3-7 we observed how the children would often generate ideas for technology which fitted their own needs and wants. While it is not necessarily an issue to let children design for themselves — as is common when engaging children in Maker movement activities [ 90 ] — PD methods emphasize designing for and with others [ 90 ]. Since DivComp is about designing technology which mediates meaningful experiences of people being different together, we wanted to sensitize the children towards considering others than themselves in their design ideas. We therefore organized WS7 and WS8 to approach this challenge: WS7 was all about eliciting the children's feelings of likes, dislikes, and possible improvements to certain spaces, whereas WS8 was designed to let these likes and dislikes direct the design ideas of other groups of children. Some approaches for evoking empathy in children designing for others include letting children conduct user or field studies, or thought exercises, storytelling and reflection [ 90 ]. Designing for others is, however, difficult, as we also experienced with our participants in WS3-7: “Despite our careful preparation of the process and material for the projects, the participating children had difficulties in understanding who they were actually designing and Making for” [ 90 ].

During our study, we found that using domain cards that distilled the likes and dislikes discussed in WS7 had potential in orienting the generated design ideas in WS8 towards others’ perspectives rather than the children's own. We argue that there may be potential in further exploring the involvement of children in designing their own empathy tools — i.e. “physical or digital artifacts designed to help people understand the lived experiences of others” [ 66 ] citing [ 8 ] — to support them in designing for others in PD processes. This may further contribute to developing ways of enabling children to contribute to design processes through the possibilities and limitations of their own capabilities.

5.2.2 Differences that Matter. As is often the case in participatory design, analyzing the data collected in the workshops proved challenging. TA is naturally guiding researchers to look for categories, patterns, recurrences and links within data, which in our case only offered a very disparate picture of seemingly random directions. In multiple, reflexive iterations of working with the data [as in 13 ], however, we developed a more process oriented perspective that became sensitive to differences and tensions, rather than sameness and categories. For example, after identifying "belonging to a group" and "needs for self-expression" as initial themes, we realized how these two processes were often linked in opposition to each other, reflecting the tension between wanting to blend in and a risky desire to stand out. Thus, relevant insights were found less in traditional categories, but in trade-offs, tensions and differences.

Designing for diversity, it seems natural to arrive at this point, but there is a larger argument to be made that ties in with recent developments in HCI that seek to interpret relational and performative ontologies for HCI [ 35 , 91 , 93 ]. Understanding diversity as located in attributes of people reflects a reductive representationalism that offers little insight or guidance for intervention. From the vantage point of a performative ontology, we were able to see processes of othering as actions that get continually (re-)enacted within particular socio-material configurations. It shifts our attention from attributes of people to the qualities and structure of relations 1 .

Barad and Haraway, both central figures in this kind of posthumanistic thinking, borrow language from optics to describe this shift in knowledge work [ 3 , 40 ]. In contrast to the common practice of mirroring (i.e. seeking representations of reality), they argue for diffraction as the main mode of generating insight, i.e. generating knowledge from the way reality, when probed, produces patterns of difference. In HCI, such diffractive approaches to analysing data were used e.g. by Lazar et al. in the context of ageing [ 48 ] or Devendorf et al. in autobiographical storytelling [ 28 ].

This kind of diffractive thematic analysis , has given us access to the ambiguities and "differences that make a difference" [ 3 , p72] - visible in the four themes, or better tensions, discussed above. In Technology as Utility and Authority a spectrum of difference in technology between magically useful and powerfully deciding over good and bad became visible. In Staged and Emerging Conflicts the difference that matters lies in the struggle that is required for finding places for retreat. In Individual and Collective Place-Making configuring collaboration matters and Belonging to the Group and Self-Expression the difference between daring to be extravagant or playing it safe and conformist is reflected. In all of these themes or tensions, trade-offs are constantly being negotiated. Thus, implementing Diversity Computing may mean to design for the differences that matter.

5.3 Limitations and Future Research

5.3.1 Additional Design Directions. As the insights discussed in this paper are based on the initial phase of a long-term PD project, we are planning another series of PD workshops with the same children and engage them in co-designing prototypes which explores the design directions described in section 5.1 . As this is an initial design space the design directions discussed above are not exhaustive and other design directions could be explored. For instance, we can envision future research exploring design directions revolving around the children’ ideas for technology providing companionship or supporting well-being.

5.3.2 Configuring Participation. In adhering to the formal school hours which were allocated to our workshops, the time we had to develop rapport with the children and knowing what was important for the children was somewhat limited. While our workshop activities aimed at eliciting the voices of the children e.g. in terms of likes and dislikes, we brought a certain research interest and focus with us when facilitating these activities, as discussed in in section 5.2.1 . Our focus may then entail a kind of biased perspective in the sense that our research goals may not align with the goals of the children. This touches on the important and critical discussion of who benefits from PD and how , which has been a recurring topic in the PD research discourse, see e.g. [ 7 , 10 , 92 ]. This was, for example, clear to us in WS1 where a girl felt excluded because she was the only one speaking German at home, see section 4.2.4 . Encountering this tension made us reflect on our own role as researchers and how aspects of diversity can, unintentionally, become a tool for singling children out, so we planned future workshops to avoid running into similar situations. As a result, we shifted our focus towards the children's design choices, preferences and capabilities rather than situations they were born into. Additionally, we took a more subtle approach by not talking about tensions stemming from diversity explicitly, but rather observing them as they emerged in the context of workshop activities, and initiated dialogue around different aspects of diversity when it seemed suitable for the context. While subsequent workshops were organized with this in mind, future research need to be careful to not conduct unintentionally and excluding othering practices in the pursuit of embracing diversity.

Furthermore, in future research we plan on facilitating a more condensed participation during e.g. one week, as we experienced that the children would sometimes forget which activities they had participated in and why after a couple of weeks had gone by. In order for future research to further align goals and ensure mutual learning between researchers and participants, Eriksson and Torgersson have suggested strategies for formulating learning goals and aligning design activities with these [ 30 ]. Another way of aligning goals of both participants and researchers is to dedicate time and space for identifying the children's personal interests and incorporate them into the design activities, as discussed by Kanafi and colleagues [ 52 ].

6 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we present the insights from the initial phase of a long-term PD project which explores the notions of DivComp and discuss how HCI can meaningfully engage with designing technology around diversity. We report on a series of nine workshops with 48 children. Based on a thematic analysis, we present four themes: Technology as Utility and Authority , Individual and Collective Place-Making , Staged and Emerging Conflicts , and Belonging to the Group and Self-Expression . Drawing on these four themes and related work, we discuss three promising design directions for developing DivComp Spaces specifically in the context of school: Negotiable Spaces for Retreating , Spaces Scaffolding Playful Staged Conflicts , and Self-Conscious and Peer-Oriented Spaces for Performing . Finally, we critically reflect on our design practice and the challenges of designing not only for , but also with diversity meaningfully embedded into design and research processes.
