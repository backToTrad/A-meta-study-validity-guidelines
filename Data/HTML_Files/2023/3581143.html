Using Geometric Features of Drag-and-Drop Trajectories to Understand Students’ Learning

Jungwook Rhim , Cognitive computing Lab, Seoul National University, Korea, Republic of, rhim8668@snu.ac.kr
Jiwon Kim , Cognitive Computing Lab, Seoul National University, Korea, Republic of, tyg03136@snu.ac.kr
Gahgene Gweon , Seoul National University, Korea, Republic of, ggweon@snu.ac.kr

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581143
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Herein, we present two studies on how students’ Psychological State of Decision difficulty (PSD) relates to two aspects of learning, i.e., guessing behavior and learning achievement. To measure PSD, we extracted geometric features from trajectories of drag-and-drop touch interactions collected while students aged 7–10 played a math game on a tablet device. In the first study, we explored whether eight geometric features extracted from 97,303 trial trajectories could be grouped to understand students’ PSD. In the second study, we examined whether the two aspects of learning could be predicted using the data collected from 187 students with geometric features indicating their PSD. This work provides empirical evidence that geometric features can be grouped into two types of PSD in the context of learning, including conflict and uncertainty. Moreover, our results demonstrate that data on students’ PSD collected from drag-and-drop trajectories can be used to predict learning.

KEYWORDS: Drag-and-drop trajectory , Decision difficulty , Guessing behavior , Learning achievement

1 INTRODUCTION

In an educational context, trajectory data has been used to capture students’ learning, such as guessing behavior and learning achievements [ 53 , 58 ]. Trajectory data, as highlighted as a red line Figure 1 , is a movement path produced when a user operates an interface using a touch gesture on a touchscreen device or using a mouse on a computer. The use of trajectory data has advantages in that high-resolution temporal user data can be extracted from a continuous trajectory [ 32 ]. In addition, using trajectory data is cost-effective and noninvasive compared to human observations, because data can be collected from devices that learners are already using in the educational process [ 52 ]. Given such advantages, researchers have extracted geometric features, which are properties of space such as distance or shape, from trajectory data to infer a student's learning, such as hesitation level during problem-solving tasks [ 52 ], cognitive workload [ 47 ], and level of achievement in a given subject [ 53 ].

Analyzing trajectory data can also help in understanding students’ psychological state in decision-making situations, such as problem-solving tasks. As accompanying motor responses are activated according to mental processes during decision-making [ 29 ], trajectory data collected from these motor responses can reflect a student's psychological state during the decision-making process [ 28 ]. For example, psychological states, such as a psychological conflict between opposing evaluations of ambivalent options, have been inferred from trajectory data [ 56 ]. Although additional interpretation of a subject's psychological state can be obtained from qualitative data, collecting such data may not be feasible in certain situations. For example, in our study, data were collected from participants who were located remotely and wished to remain anonymous. Therefore, collecting qualitative data was not possible. However, based on the existing literature, we propose a cost-effective method to infer students’ psychological states during the problem-solving process by utilizing trajectory data.

In this study, by building on existing research on the application of trajectory data, we aimed to understand how students’ Psychological State of Decision difficulty (PSD) relates to two aspects of learning, including guessing behavior and learning achievement. We define a novel term, PSD, to be a certain mental state that a user engages in when he experiences difficulty while making a choice between multiple options. This may occur owing to the characteristics of the decision context, such as the level of risk of the options, information uncertainty, and the trade-off dilemma [ 10 ]. In psychological and business contexts, trajectory data have been used to infer a user's PSD in the context of gambling or consumer choice [ 16 ]. The inference of such a PSD has been shown to be helpful in understanding users’ decision-making process. Similarly, in an educational setting, capturing a user's PSD using trajectory data would help understand students’ difficulties with problem-solving tasks. Therefore, we conducted two studies to explore the impact of PSD in an educational setting in which students played tablet-based math games. In Study 1, we explored whether geometric features could be grouped to understand students’ PSD. Thus, our first research question is stated as follows.

RQ1. Can individual geometric features of drag-and-drop trajectory be grouped to understand Psychological State of Decision difficulty (PSD) in the context of learning?

To this end, we collected eight geometric features from the drag-and-drop trajectory data that were related to PSD in non-educational domains. Table 1 shows the findings obtained from the existing literature on psychology and business [ 15 , 16 ] in which geometric features of trajectory data were employed to analyze PSD. The two main PSDs that are relevant to the context of our educational study, which have been examined outside of the context of education include conflict and uncertainty. Conflict is a psychological state associated with concerns about the consequences of choices, such as gain and loss, whereas uncertainty is a psychological state reflecting the confusion between the given options occurring due to the similarity of the options [ 16 ]. These types of PSDs occur when a user encounters a situation in which they must make difficult decisions [ 15 ]. In the context of learning, students would face similar PSDs when faced with a challenging multiple-choice problem. Therefore, we utilized five geometric features [ 16 , 53 ] that were previously adopted to understand the PSDs of conflict and uncertainty, as shown in Table 1 . In addition, we also considered three new features derived from the five existing features. Thus, a total of eight geometric features were extracted from touch trajectory data generated in drag-and-drop interaction events collected from the KitKit School 1 , which is a tablet-based math game.

In addition, to understand PSD in the learning context, we explored how PSD is related to two aspects of learning, i.e., guessing behavior and learning achievement. Guessing behavior refers to solving a problem using a learning system's traits without investing a significant deal of learning effort [ 2 , 6 ]. We examine guessing behavior in addition to learning achievement because research has consistently shown that such behavior correlates negatively with learning [ 42 , 62 ]. In particular, Baker et al. found that guessing was the only negatively correlated behavior among various off-task behaviors, such as off-task conversation, off-task solitary behavior, and inactivity [ 2 ]. Thus, in Study 2, we explored whether students’ guessing behavior and learning achievement could be predicted using the eight geometric features related to PSD. The research question in Study 2 is stated as follows.

RQ2. Can students’ guessing behavior and learning achievement be predicted using the geometric features that compose Psychological State of Decision difficulty (PSD)?

The key contributions of this work are summarized as follows. 1) We present empirical evidence on grouping geometric features of drag-and-drop touch trajectory data to categorize two types of PSD in the context of learning environments, including conflict and uncertainty. 2) Moreover, we demonstrate that geometric features related to these two PSD types can be utilized to predict the two aspects of students' learning, including guessing behavior and learning achievement.

2 KITKIT SCHOOL

Our data 2 was collected using an educational tablet-based game called KitKit School , which was developed for and won the XPRIZE global learning competition 3 . The goal of the competition was to deliver an open-source tablet-based learning software application that can be used by children from regions with insufficient educational infrastructure. KitKit School has been deployed in over 50 countries since 2016. Its target users are preschoolers to second-grade students. The data consists of log from KitKit School that were used in field studies conducted by the developer in 29 Tanzanian villages from May 2018 to January 2019. A total of 187 students aged 7–10 participated in the field test. There was no protocol during the period of use, and therefore after the initial guidelines on app usage, the students freely used the KitKit School without further instructional guidance. We contacted the developer 4 in 2021 to acquire the data, which was the accessible by request per XPRIZE competition policy.

The KitKit School provides math games that consist of multiple course levels of varying difficulty. Initially, the easiest level of math games provided are presented in multiple types of games. Here, students can select a type of game to play. More types of games become available as students’ progress. Different course levels are also provided within a specific game type. For example, Figure 2 shows two different course levels for a game called Mango Shop . The learning goal of the game Mango Shop at Course Level 4 is to master the task of summing single-digit numbers. In contrast, at Course Level 6, the learning goal is to master summing two-digit numbers, which is a more complex and sophisticated concept. Students can move on to the next course level only when they pass an in-app test presented at the end of each course level.

From the KitKit School data, we collected a total of 97,303 logs of trial trajectories. A trial trajectory comprise data recorded during a touch interaction when a student inputs a response to a given problem using drag-and-drop gestures on a touchscreen. The trial trajectory was collected in the form of pixel coordinates, as shown in Figure 3 . Three types of information are collected from the trial trajectory, including trajectory coordinates, response information, and time duration. First, the trajectory coordinate information is a set of x-y coordinates of a given trial trajectory produced. The coordinates were recorded in units of pixels. We utilized coordinate information to measure eight geometric features listed in Table 1 . Second, the response information of a given trial trajectory includes the value of the student's response and its correctness. Third, time duration is defined as the difference between the time at which a student began a given math problem and the ending time of the associated trial trajectory. Successive trial trajectories were calculated by computing the difference between the ending time of the previous and current trajectories.

We collected logs from three math card games played using a drag-and-drop interface. Figure 4 shows screenshots from the three games: Missing Number, Mango Shop , and Number Train . As shown in Figure 4 , the user interface of all three math card games was similar. The locations of the candidate cards, which are cards with numbers that may be potential answers to a given problem, are provided at the bottom of the screen. In addition, the location of the input box, which are slots for the answer cards, are located above the number cards in a horizontal section. To solve the given math problem, a student must perform a drag-and-drop motion by dragging a selected number card from among the candidate into the input box.

In the Missing Number (Figure 4 ) game, a student should fill in a number for the egg with a missing number, so that the four eggs would have a complete arithmetic number sequence. To solve the problem, the student needs to drag a number card from the 0-9 candidate cards and drop the card on the egg with the ‘?’ mark. The Mango Shop (Figure 4 ) is a game where a student inputs the answer to a given addition or a subtraction math equation by dragging a card from the candidate cards ranging from 0-9 and dropping the dragged card to the slots with ‘?’ marks, which are on the right side of the given math problem. The Number Train (Figure 4 ) is a game in which a student is asked to provide the correct order for a given number list. A student has to drag a number card from the candidate cards that contain a list of numbers and drop the selected card on the correct train compartment.

3 RELATED WORK

3.1 Using Geometric Features of Trajectory Data to Understand the Difficulty of Decisions

The geometric features of the trajectory data can be utilized to understand the psychological state in which a user made a given decision. Traditionally, psychological states involved in decision making have been measured using subjective self-reported assessments [ 41 ]. However, this traditional approach involves some limitations because bias may occur owing to the subjective nature of the assessment [ 56 ]. Alternatively, indirect measurement data, such as response time, have also been adopted to infer psychological states linked with decision making. Yet, response time cannot sufficiently capture the decision-making process while the decision is being made, thus leaving the decision process as a black box [ 56 ]. Alternatively, trajectory data derived from motor behavior can be analyzed to measure users’ psychological states. Because motor behavior is known to be associated with the psychological state of actions via neural mechanisms [ 17 ], geometric features calculated from trajectory data, which reflect motor behavior, can help infer psychological states, such as decision difficulty.

Table 1 summarizes geometric features known to be related to decision difficulty that have been examined in previous studies. For example, Koop and Johnson [ 37 ] analyzed how the Maximum Absolute Deviation (MAD) and Average Absolute Deviation (AAD) of mouse trajectories were related to a decision conflict in selecting a preferred photo. They also found that MAD and AAD were affected by the outcome and risk of options in another experiment on gambling. Another study [ 23 ] analyzed the relationship between participants' difficulty making decisions and Area Under Curve (AUC) while users selected different options according to varying timings and reward amounts. Specifically, the more a participant reflected on choosing among different options, the higher the AUC of the mouse trajectory produced by that participant. In addition, the distance traveled by the mouse trajectory was related to the decision difficulty in classifying the animal species [ 19 ], and X-flip was used to measure the instability of selection, which is related to decision uncertainty [ 37 ].

Recent studies [ 15 , 16 ] have proposed grouping geometric features to capture PSD. For example, rather than interpreting individual geometric features separately, grouping multiple features into meaningful groups was found to be more informative in understanding decision difficulty in the context of a web survey [ 27 ]. Moreover, previous studies have shown that PSD can be understood further when geometric features are grouped into conflict and uncertainty [ 15 , 16 , 49 ]. In this study, we extend the existing research from fields other than education to understand aspects of students’ learning that reflect PSD.

3.2 Guessing Behavior and Learning Achievement as Aspects of Learning

To evaluate the impact of educational games on learning, investigating students' off-task behaviors in addition to their learning achievement is important. Student off-task behavior occurs when students engage in behavior in which learning is not the main goal [ 2 ]. Guessing behavior is a representative off-task behavior that can be observed when students play educational games [ 58 ]. The literature shows that although certain off-task behaviors, such as off-task conversation or inactivity, do not exhibit a negative correlation with learning, guessing behavior has been known to negatively impact learning [ 2 ]. According to the time-on-task hypothesis [ 11 ], as students spend less time understanding the learning materials, the chance of gaining the intended knowledge decreases. Therefore, engaging in guessing behavior, which involves less time spent on learning materials, can lead to less learning. To this end, examining both guessing behavior and learning achievement as aspects of learning is essential to develop a more complete understanding of how students learn.

Therefore, detecting guessing behavior, which hinders student learning, is essential in achieving maximal learning goals [ 7 ]. Traditionally, such detection has been performed through observation [ 5 , 6 , 65 ] and self-administered surveys [ 34 , 42 ]. Although such methods provide detailed high-quality data, they are time-consuming and costly, and can interfere with students’ learning during data collection. To supplement the limitations of observational or survey methods, research has been conducted utilizing log data, such as response time and response value, to detect guessing behavior [ 3 , 43 , 58 , 63 , 64 ]. In addition to such data, recent studies have utilized geometric features to detect guessing behaviors [ 33 , 58 ]. For example, Horwitz [ 33 ] showed that by using geometric features of mouse movements, the process of answering a survey was revealed in more detail than when using previously applied features, such as response time.

In addition to guessing behavior, students’ learning achievement is also important. For the assessment of learning achievement, data on students’ learning can be collected at different times, including before, after, and during learning. Data collected before and after learning includes pre- and post-test scores and demographic information, such as age and gender [ 39 ]. Data collected during learning include the number of attempts to solve a given problem, the correctness of a respondent's answers, time spent in solving a given problem [ 25 , 35 ], and the number of hint requests [ 26 , 54 ]. The results of previous studies have shown that data collected before and after learning may be expected to prove more helpful in providing assessments compared to those collected during learning [ 25 , 54 ]. However, despite these advantages, collecting data, such as student pre-test and demographic information, may be difficult outside of an experimental setting. Therefore, we propose a method to discover implicit data that can be collected during learning to provide helpful information to assess students. To this end, more recent work [ 1 ] has utilized real-time trajectory data that can capture the dynamic characteristics of students’ performance. For example, the length, chord, time duration, and interval time of users’ mouse trajectory during the problem-solving processes were used to understand students’ learning achievement in an interactive online learning system [ 61 ]. Additionally, geometric features of trajectory data collected from an educational game were used to understand learning achievement [ 53 ]. In this study, we extend this line of work by using data collected during learning as an aid in assessing students’ learning.

4 STUDY 1: GROUPING GEOMETRIC FEATURES TO UNDERSTAND PSD

In Study 1, we grouped eight geometric features to understand PSD. An exploratory factor analysis was conducted for this purpose, and the experimental methods and results are described in detail below.

4.1 Method

This section describes the measurement and analysis process used in Study 1. First, we explain the measurements of the eight geometric features of a trial trajectory. Second, we present the exploratory factor data analysis method used to address RQ1.

4.1.1 Measurements. To answer the first research question on using geometric features to understand students’ PSD, we collected eight types of geometric features from the trial trajectories. The eight features are listed in Table 1 . Five of the eight features—Maximum Absolute Deviation (MAD), Average Absolute Deviation (AAD), Area Under the Curve (AUC), distance, and X-flip—were adopted from existing psychophysiological research that examined actions that can be used to infer task execution difficulty [ 20 , 24 , 46 , 51 , 60 ]. In our study, we added three additional geometric features, including Y-flip, P-flip, and inflection. The Y-flip and P-flip features [ 49 ] were added to capture the possible axis orientations that can be used to move objects in the context of an educational game. The inflection feature [ 57 ] was added to capture the holistic aspect of a flip pattern that could not be captured with single X-, Y-, and P-flip features. After calculating the eight geometric features, we conducted standard scaling normalization so that the eight geometric feature values were comparable to each other. During this process, we excluded outliers using a distance-based multidimensional outlier removal process [ 44 ]. Consequently, 7,796 outliers were removed from the original 105,099 trial trajectories, resulting in 97,303 trial trajectories.

4.1.2 Analysis. To group PSD-related features, we conducted Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) by considering geometric features with a factor loading of 0.5 or higher. The factor_analyzer Python package was used to perform the EFA [ 9 ]. The preconditions of EFA were met: the p-value from Bartlett's test of sphericity was lower than 0.001, and the value of Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin was 0.769, which naturally exceeds 0.6. Note that in our experimental setting, the factor loading values resulting from the EFA analysis can be outside the range of -1 to 1 because we used promax-based oblique rotation. In contrast to orthogonal rotation, where factor loading values are correlation coefficients, the factor loading values are the regression coefficients in oblique rotation. We conducted oblique rotation instead of orthogonal rotation because orthogonal rotation requires the assumption of independence between factors, whereas oblique rotation does not require an independence assumption [ 50 ]. In the context of our study, because the derived factors are related to psychological states that occurred within an identical context, i.e., problem-solving tasks, we adopted rotation methods used in a similar context where the assumption of factor independence was not satisfied [ 50 ].

4.2 Results

The results of the EFA showed that individual geometric features can be grouped to acquire two types of PSD, including conflict and uncertainty, as presented in Table 2 . The first PSD consisted of MAD, AAD, AUC, and distance, which are reported to reflect decision conflicts. Thus, we label the first factor ‘conflict’. The factor loading proportion of the geometric features of the conflict pattern was 26.62% for AUC, 25.14% for MAD, 24.78% for AAD, and 15.08% for Distance, and the four main features accounted for 91.62% of the total factor loading. The second PSD consisted of the X-flip, Y-flip, P-flip, and inflection measures. X-flip is related to uncertainty [ 24 , 36 , 37 ], and Y-flip, P-flip, and inflection were all newly added in this work. Therefore, to the best of our knowledge, no works in the relevant literature have reported on how these three features reflect students’ psychological states. Because all the newly added features are variations of the X-flip, they are also related to uncertainty. Thus, the second factor is labeled as ‘uncertainty.’ The proportion of the factor loading of the components of uncertainty was 30.28% for P-flip, 22.86% for Y-flip, 22.85% for X-flip, and 18.11% for inflection. The factor loadings of the four geometric features accounted for 94.09% of the total. We referred to these the two PSDs as conflict and uncertainty, as used in psychological literature [ 15 , 16 ]. Conflict and uncertainty accounted for 39.38% and 28.65% of the variance in our data, respectively. As summarized in Table 2 below, we observed that each PSD consisted only of features that were similar in terms of psychological states.

4.3 Discussion

For RQ1, we examined whether individual geometric features of drag-and-drop trajectory can be grouped to understand PSD in the context of learning. The results showed that individual geometric features can be grouped into two interpretable PSDs. Specifically, our data indicate that PSDs in learning have two subconstructs, including conflict and uncertainty. The four geometric features that were grouped together for each behavioral pattern accounted for 91.62% and 94.09%, respectively. Thus, this factor supports a high validity for the construct of conflict and uncertainty.

We can interpret the psychological meaning of PSDs based on the decision-making literature [ 15 , 16 ] as mentioned section 1. Conflict is known to occur when a person concerns the result of decision-making [ 15 ], or, in our context, values the result of problem-solving. Such a decision conflict occurs when a reward (gain) or penalty (loss) is expected by the decision-maker [ 23 ]. Thus, understanding the level of conflict may be used to provide teachers with information on how much a learner values the problem-solving results. The second pattern of uncertainty occurs when a person is confused between candidate options [ 15 , 16 ]. In a learning context, such uncertainty can arise when a student is confused about which of the options is the correct answer. Knowledge of students’ uncertainty among candidate answers can help teachers understand areas in which students encounter difficulty during problem-solving tasks.

In summary, our results demonstrate that two types of PSDs, conflict and uncertainty, can be grouped using the eight geometric features to understand their psychological meaning in an educational context. In the next study, we explored whether the geometric features composing the two types of PSDs can be used to predict students’ learning, which is crucial in supporting learning. Specifically, two aspects of learning, guessing behavior and learning achievement, were measured and predicted using the eight geometric features.

5 STUDY 2: PREDICTING TWO ASPECTS OF LEARNING USING EIGHT GEOMETRIC FEATURES RELATED TO PSD

In Study 2, we addressed whether guessing behavior and learning achievement could be predicted using the geometric features. Specifically, we first examined how geometric features and PSD correlated with the two aspects of learning. In addition, we also investigated whether PSD-related geometric features can be used to build machine learning models to predict students' guessing behavior and learning achievement. Each of these sub research questions that constitute RQ2 are as follows:

RQ2 a . How are students’ geometric features and Psychological State of Decision difficulty (PSD) correlated with guessing behavior and learning achievement?

RQ2 b . Can students’ guessing behavior and learning achievement be predicted by a machine learning model using geometric features that compose Psychological State of Decision difficulty (PSD)?

5.1 Method

In this section, we first introduce the four measurements used in Study 2. Second, two types of analysis methods by which we answered the sub research questions are explained.

5.1.1 Measurements. To answer RQ2, we measured two independent variables using two scores, including (IV 1 ) as a geometric feature score and (IV 2 ) as a measure of conflict and uncertainty. Two aspects of student learning, which are our dependent variables, were also measured, including (DV 1 ) guessing behavior ratio and (DV 2 ) learning achievement. Unlike Study 1, we set a single student as the unit of analysis in Study 2. For this purpose, we aggregated 97,303 trial trajectories from 187 students. Each student produced an average of approximately 520 trial trajectories with a standard deviation of 80.7. Using aggregated data, we calculated the following four measurements.

IV1. Geometric feature score. We define a geometric feature score for each of the eight features. The geometric feature score for each feature was defined as the degree to which a specific geometric feature appeared in the student's trial trajectory. For each student, the average value of each geometric feature per trial trajectory was computed from all of their learning sessions. For example, student $i$ ’s AAD score, $AA{D}_i$ , was computed using Equation ( 1 ). $AA{D}_{ik}$ is the value of AAD in $k$ th trial trajectory of student $i$ , and $n$ is the number of trial trajectories produced by student $i$ .

\begin{equation} AA{D}_i = \frac{{\mathop \sum \nolimits_{k = 1}^n AA{D}_{ik}}}{n}\ \end{equation}
(1)

IV2. Conflict and uncertainty scores. Conflict and uncertainty scores indicate the extent to which each student experiences conflict and uncertainty during problem-solving. To acquire conflict and uncertainty scores, we analyzed the trial trajectories using a two-step process. First, the factor scores for each trial trajectory were calculated. We calculated the factor scores using weighted sum scores [ 21 ]. The weighted sum score method has the advantage that features with the highest factor loadings on the factor are considered the most influential when calculating factor scores. The factor scores for the conflict and uncertainty, ${C}_k$ and ${U}_k$ , were computed using Equation ( 2 ) for a given trial trajectory $k$ . In Equation ( 2 ), ${X}_k$ is a vector of the eight geometric features of a given trial trajectory and ${B}_C$ and ${B}_U$ are vectors of the eight factor loading values of the eight geometric features. Factor scores are composite variables that indicate the extent of conflict and uncertainty for a given trial trajectory.

\begin{equation} \begin{array}{@{}*{1}{c}@{}} {{C}_k = {X}_k{B}_C}\\ {{U}_k = {X}_k{B}_U} \end{array}\ \end{equation}
(2)

For the second step of the trial trajectory analysis, we computed the average factor scores of all trial trajectories produced by a student using Equation ( 3 ). ${C}_i$ and ${U}_i$ denote a student $i$ ’s conflict and uncertainty scores. ${C}_{ik}$ and ${U}_{ik}$ are the factor scores of conflict and uncertainty in $k$ th trial trajectory of student $i$ , and $n$ is the number of trial trajectories produced by student $i$ .

\begin{equation} {C}_i = \frac{{\mathop \sum \nolimits_{k = 1}^n {C}_{ik}}}{n},{U}_i = \frac{{\mathop \sum \nolimits_{k = 1}^n {U}_{ik}}}{n}\ \end{equation}
(3)

DV1. Guessing behavior ratio. The guessing behavior ratio refers to the degree to which a student exhibits guessing behavior. We measured a student's guessing behavior ratio as the percentage of the number of trial trajectories labeled as guessing behavior to all the trial trajectories generated by a student. To decide whether a trial trajectory should be labeled as guessing behavior, we adopted a rule-based approach [ 18 ], a two-step labeling method that was used by Muldner, et al. [ 48 ]. In the first step, the sample data were hand-coded using knowledge from prior works. Figure 5 shows the two rules used to label a student's guessing behavior. In the second step, the remaining data were labeled automatically using these two labeling rules. The rule-based approach was adopted in our study as in [ 48 ], where the authors first manually analyzed a fragment of logs and then automatically labeled the remainder of the data using a computational rule. We chose this approach because it has already been applied in an educational context [ 48 ] and is suitable for large-scale data analyses, as in our study.

In this work, prior to automatically labeling the entire dataset, we conducted an analysis to confirm the validity of the labeling rules. Specifically, we compared the labels obtained by human coders with those obtained by an automatic labeling system that used the two rules described in Figure 5 . Two graduate-level researchers with backgrounds in learning science manually analyzed 2,404 logs in the human coding process. As a result, a satisfactory kappa score of 0.705 was observed between the labeling rule and the criterion standard. Given this validity, the remainder of the data were automatically labeled based on the rules shown in Figure 5 .

The automatic labeling rules involve identifying two types of guessing behavior, including rapid guessing and systematic guessing. Rapid guessing behavior is defined as a student attempting to input an answer multiple times, in an excessively short time period to find a correct answer [ 30 ]. The rapid guessing behavior was labeled using the following two assumptions and calculation methods. First, if the response time for a question is too short, a student is more likely to have answered the question before fully understanding it. The second assumption is that the accuracy of the rapid guessing behavior can be the same as that of the random guessing behavior. Based on these two assumptions, we defined the ‘response time threshold’ as the time at which the cumulative accuracy of a student's trial in a specific problem became equal to the accuracy of random guessing behavior for the first time. If the time duration of a trial trajectory was shorter than the response time threshold, the trial trajectory was classified as a rapid guessing behavior. Systematic guessing behavior is another type of guessing behavior in which a student may try all the options successively to get the correct answer by chance rather than solving the problem [ 2 , 6 ]. Therefore, we classified a trial trajectory as a systematic guessing behavior when a student inputs successive responses, such as ‘1, 2, 3’ or ‘9, 8, 7’, in the three consecutive trial trajectories.

DV2. Learning achievement. Learning achievement indicates how well a student understands what they have learned after playing an educational game. We measured each student's learning achievement using app-level tests. Unlike the three games mentioned above, the in-app level test was not designed for gameplay; rather, it was used to evaluate students’ learning achievement. The test was given after each course level which contained approximately 688 questions on average. The questions were based on concepts presented during the course. Figure 6 shows an example of screenshots of the in-app level test.

We computed learning achievement as the average rate of correct answers on the in-app level tests, which consisted of ten questions. For example, if a student provided seven correct answers, the rate of correct answers was 0.7. The formula used to calculate a student's learning achievement is shown in Equation ( 4 ), where ${L}_i$ is student $i$ ’s learning achievement and $c$ is the course level.

\begin{equation} {L}_i = \ \mathop \sum \limits_c \frac{{number\ of\ correct\ answer}}{{number\ of\ questions\ given\left( { = 10} \right)}}\ \ \ \end{equation}
(4)

5.1.2 Analysis. This section presents two types of analyses used to address RQ2. First, for RQ2 a that examined how each PSDs and geometric features correlated with the two aspects of learning, two types of Pearson's correlations were computed, including (1) PSDs and students’ learning, as well as (2) geometric features comprising the two PSDs and students’ learning. Note that a correlational correction method, such as Bonferroni correction [ 12 ] is not necessary in our context. We calculated the coefficient of bivariate correlation between paired variables, such as conflict and guessing behavior ratio, rather than the coefficient of multiple correlations [ 8 ]. The coefficient of multiple correlations, which measures how well a given dependent variable is predicted using linear regression of the independent variables, is based on the assumption of causality between dependent and independent variable. However, in our context, one cannot assume the causality between student learning and PSD/geometric features. Therefore, we used bivariate correlation for correlational analysis.

Second, for RQ2 b , we trained four different machine learning algorithms using the eight geometric features. As in an existing work [ 59 ], we selected regression- and tree-based machine learning models, including full regression, coefficient adjustment regression, feature selection regression, and tree-based models. We also generated a baseline model by constructing a dummy regressor that predicted the median value of the target variable. For the full regression model, we trained multivariate linear regression using ordinary least squares (OLS) as an estimator. Ridge regression was applied for the coefficient adjustment regression model. The penalty coefficient (λ) was set to a maximum value of 1. For feature selection regression, we built a forward stepwise selection model. Finally, for the tree-based model, a random forest regressor was implemented with 200 decision trees.

To prepare the testing and training data, we chose user-independent cross-validation [ 13 ] over mixed cross-validation. ‘User-independent’ indicates that a new user who was not included in the training dataset is tested. Thus, we expect that the problem of overfitting, which may occur when the performance of the model depends heavily on a given target user, could be mitigated by user-independent cross-validation. Using a user-independent validation setting, we employed a five-fold cross-validation for each of the four machine learning algorithms. The median absolute error (MAE) and mean squared error (MSE) were calculated to measure the accuracy of the models. An F-test was performed to confirm a statistically significant difference in MSE between the baseline model and each machine learning model [ 14 ]. We assume MSE as the standard deviation of each model's performance and performed an F-test to compare two variances.

5.3 Discussion

To address RQ2, which examines whether students’ guessing behavior and learning achievement can be predicted using the geometric features that compose PSD, we conducted two analyses: correlational analysis for RQ2 a and machine learning analysis for RQ2 b . Below, we discuss the implications from each analysis results for each of the two sub-research questions in order.

5.3.1 Understanding conflict and uncertainty in relation to learning. Prior works have explored the relationship between students’ psychological states and learning, such as frustration and guessing behavior [ 5 ]. This study extends the results of previous research by showing how conflict and uncertainty in problem-solving are associated with learning. Specifically, the conflict and uncertainty scores showed 1) a positive relationship with the guessing behavior ratio and 2) a negative relationship with learning achievement. First, the positive relationship between the two PSDs and the guessing behavior ratio suggests that students tend to engage in guessing behavior more often with increasing psychological difficulties. These results are consistent with those of existing works. Specifically, according to research by Baker et al., guessing behavior is observed when student frustration occurs due to the presentation of difficult problems [ 6 ], or a lack of the academic or cognitive abilities required to solve the problem [ 42 ]. Second, the negative relationship between PSDs and learning achievement builds on previous research that has examined the relationship between task difficulty and performance. The relevant literature on psychology states that tasks require more effort and elicit more fatigue when task performers experience greater task difficulty, which typically results in lower performance [ 40 ]. Similarly, presenting a game task that does not meet a suitable difficulty level (i.e., a task that is too difficult for a student) was also negatively related to learning achievement [ 55 ]. Likewise, our study results extend existing findings by showing that a negative relationship between task difficulty and performance can be observed from drag-and-drop trajectory data alone.

In comparing conflict and uncertainty scores, we observed that although both comprise useful informative, a higher correlation coefficient was reported for the conflict score (r = 0.318) than for the uncertainty score (r = 0.171) for the guessing behavior ratio. This higher correlation between students’ guessing behavior ratio and conflict compared to uncertainty, may be attributed to some of the identified causes of guessing behavior for conflict and uncertainty. Research suggests that two possible causes of guessing behavior include students’ 1) performance-oriented goals and 2) lack of knowledge [ 62 ]. Based on the results of Study 1 and the literature on decision making [ 15 , 20 ], we can link performance-oriented goals to conflict and lack of knowledge to uncertainty. More specifically, conflict could be interpreted as a student feeling conflict because they value solving the problems and are thus performance-oriented. In addition, uncertainty occurs when the given options are too similar and are thus confusing. Therefore, in a learning context, if the cause of a given student's guessing behavior is ‘having a performance-oriented goal’, the student may be expected to show a higher degree of decision conflict. On the other hand, if the cause of a given student's guessing behavior is a ‘lack of knowledge’, the student will lack the knowledge to distinguish the correct answer from similar options. Thus, student may be expected to show a higher degree of uncertainty. The result of our experiment reports a higher correlation between guessing behavior ratio and conflict compared to uncertainty. Considering the literature on decision-making, this result suggests that guessing behavior may be attributed to students having a ‘performance-oriented goal’ rather than a ‘lack of knowledge’.

5.3.2 Potential for using PSD-related geometric features to predict learning using machine learning models. Given the significantly better prediction errors of tree-based and regression models over baseline machine learning model, we observed that geometric features that compose conflict and uncertainty can be used to infer the ratio of students’ guessing behavior and their learning achievement. Below, we propose three points, including explainability, collinearity issues, and designing tailored interventions, to consider when using PSD-related geometric features for machine learning in learning systems.

The advantage of using PSD-related geometric features when predicting learning is that they can improve the model's explainability. The explainability of a predictive model is defined as the ability to explain the prediction model in terms that could be understood by a human [ 22 , 45 ]. This explainability can be enhanced using comprehensible and intuitive features, such as those related to PSD [ 61 ]. When using individual features that only have geometric meanings to build machine learning models, interpreting the role of such features in the context of learning is difficult. However, the proposed method for understanding how PSD-related geometric features reflect a student's psychological state can help to interpret machine learning models that use PSD-geometric features more intuitively.

Although the use of PSD-related geometric features is recommended in building machine learning models owing of the improvement in explainability, one should be aware of potential collinearity issues that may occur between selected geometric features. Our data show that the prediction errors for the models without feature selection, i.e., the full regression model and the coefficient adjustment regression model, were higher than those of the other three models with feature selection. One possible explanation for the higher prediction error in models without feature selection may be the inclusion of features with high collinearity that capture similar geometric patterns. For example, because distance can reflect the complexity of the overall trajectory, it is likely to be a linear combination of individual features, such as AAD and MAD. Therefore, techniques that adjust for collinearity, such as feature selection or principle component analysis, can be used to reduce such collinearity issues.

Based on our findings, we suggest a design strategy that can be used to understand students' learning processes in detail. By utilizing PSD geometric features to infer decision difficulty, which is an important psychological aspect of the learning process, a learning system can provide tailored intervention that can also respond to students’ psychological needs. For example, building a system with both PSD geometric features and traditional features, such as the correctness of answers and response time [ 31 ], can provide adaptive interventions, as follows. For students who provide a correct answer, a high level of PSD implies a need for repeated exercise, whereas a low level of PSD can indicate a need for more challenging questions. For students who answered incorrectly, a high level of PSD can be a sign that this student needs easier questions or additional training, whereas a low level of PSD might relate to guessing behavior. Such guessing behavior can occur because of conflicts or uncertainty. For students who engage in guessing behavior caused by conflict, the reward system can be adjusted, such as by reducing the penalty for an incorrect answer, so that students’ concerns about the consequences of their decisions can be minimized. Meanwhile, for students who guess answers due to uncertainty, questions with a lower number of distracting options should be offered to mitigate confusion.

6 CONCLUSIONS

We demonstrated that PSD derived by analyzing the drag-and-drop trajectory data could help infer learning in the context of an educational game. Particularly, our results showed how conflict and uncertainty related to students' learning, and we presented an approach to advance the understanding of learning using these two PSDs. Our approach could be applied to the design of educational games, such as providing tailored interventions that respond to students’ PSD. We aimed to develop a noninvasive and cost-effective method that used large-scale trajectory data to infer students' PSDs when qualitative data collection was impossible. However, although our proposed method for understanding PSD from drag-and-drop data can be replicated easily, it may not be applicable for games in which drag-and-drop actions are not the main interaction types. Therefore, our method should be further developed to establish stronger external validity by conducting massive online experiments for different types of interactions.

This study involves two main limitations. First, the demographics of the students were limited as they were from a single country and data were only collected from children. Therefore, the correlation between conflict and uncertainty, guessing behavior, and learning achievement may exhibit different patterns in different populations. Second, we only examined a subset of guessing behaviors, namely rapid guessing and systematic guessing, which were accessible with our data. However, additional reported guessing behaviors, such as hint abuse [ 4 , 5 ], should also be examined to fully understand PSDs and guessing behavior.

Despite these limitations, this work provides two primary contributions. First, we introduced the application of PSD, conflict, and uncertainty in the context of learning. Second, we demonstrated an application of drag-and-drop trajectory data, which related to PSDs in predicting guessing behavior and learning achievement. We also provided five discussion points based on the results of the two studies, including the psychological interpretation of the two PSDs in a learning context, the relationship between PSDs and learning, the advantage of utilizing PSD-related geometric features, collinearity issues, and design strategy for a tailored intervention to respond to students' PSD.

In future studies, a more varied setting should be considered. This study only analyzed drag-and-drop interface games in which trajectories could be collected. This approach was only achievable for a specific interface in which the starting-point (drag) and ending-point (drop) of decision-making could be defined. However, in addition to drag-and-drop, touch devices allow different types of touchscreen interactions, such as tapping, pinching, and sliding. Additionally, other types of devices can collect more diverse interactions. For example, when students use a mouse, they produce more diverse interaction types, i.e., click, double-click, left-click, and click and hold. Given that different types of interactions have distinct functions and purposes, the psychological state that can be inferred by analyzing such interactions may also differ, which has not been discussed extensively thus far. Therefore, inferring related psychological states from diverse interface types requires further exploration.
