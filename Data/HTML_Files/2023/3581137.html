The tactile dimension: a method for physicalizing touch behaviors

CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Traces of touch provide valuable insight into how we interact with the physical world. Measuring touch behavior, however, is expensive and imprecise. Utilizing a fluorescent UV tracer powder, we developed a low-cost analog method to capture persistent, high-contrast touch records on arbitrary objects. We describe our process for selecting a tracer, methods for capturing, enhancing, and aggregating traces, and approaches to examining qualitative aspects of the user experience. Three user studies demonstrate key features of this method. First, we show that it provides clear and durable traces on objects representative of scientific visualization, physicalization, and product design. Second, we demonstrate how this method could be used to study touch perception, by measuring how task and narrative framing elicit different touch behaviors on the same object. Third, we demonstrate how this method can be used to evaluate data physicalizations by observing how participants touch two different physicalizations of COVID-19 time-series data.

1 INTRODUCTION

Touch is an essential modality for interacting with the world. Developmentally, we see, hear, smell, taste, and touch our environments at least a year before we can express our reactions verbally, and how we gesture, point and touch objects is a primary means of early communication [ 3 ]. Touch remains a fundamental way of exploring and communicating throughout our lives, reflected by the strong inclination to touch objects [ 32 ], to communicate through hand gestures [ 40 ], and to learn and remember [ 29 ]. Touching the physical environment affects our thinking [ 17 ].

Physical models and tangible data representations are common in science, design, and education. They expand the visualization toolbox beyond conventional screens [ 27 ] and serve as discursive objects in museums or collaborative settings [ 13 , 22 , 24 ]. Tangibility is a central concern in interface design, HCI [ 14 ], and Human-Robot Interaction [ 34 ]. Gestures are used as inputs for virtual environments, sometimes accompanied by haptic feedback. Tactile information displays for people who are visually impaired are also an active research area [ 6 ].

However, compared to visual and auditory systems, research on tactile cognition remains understudied. Experiments typically measure performance when touching, but rarely measure touch behavior directly. For example, Lederman and Klatzky ask participants to touch objects and match them to templates and show that constraining hand movements reduces performance [ 21 ]. Pont et al. ask subjects to touch test objects and judge their curvature [ 33 ]. Hutmacher and Kuhbandner show that touching an object provides better object recognition memory than simply viewing the object [ 12 ].

Recent interest in data physicalization has brought touch into the focus of visualization research [ 13 ]. Beyond the use of tangible interfaces for visualizations [ 47 ], researchers explore the use of texture to convey data [ 48 ], compare touch interactions with data representations in the physical world and in virtual reality [ 4 ], and consider the socio-political implications of "feeling your data" [ 23 ]. Jansen et al. observe that being able to touch a physical data representation leads to better understanding of the underlying data than just looking at the physical data representation [ 15 ]. It is, however, not clear why and how touching a physicalization might provide such an advantage, and how a physicalization should be designed to this end. To investigate this, a method to capture and record touch patterns on the surfaces of physical objects might be beneficial.

2 CHALLENGES IN MEASURING TOUCH BEHAVIORS

Measuring touch behavior on real-world objects is especially difficult because they involve arbitrarily shaped surfaces in various materials. Three approaches are possible: 1) instrumenting the object (e.g. with capacitive sensors), 2) instrumenting the environment (e.g. video or IR cameras in the room), and 3) hybrid approaches that combine multiple systems.

The first approach works well on flat surfaces with homogeneous materials; touch screens offer good spatial and temporal resolution. Agostinho et al. examine finger tracing gestures employed by school children when interpreting temperature charts on an iPad [ 1 ]. Touch screens are also useful for measuring non-instrumental qualities of touch interactions including the early stage motor impairments of Parkinson [ 39 ], and touch screen overlays could be used to examine touch interactions with static paper charts and maps.

Beyond flat and smooth surfaces, bespoke sensing solutions are needed. When successful, they add interactivity to physicalizations [ 9 ] but also add time and complexity to the development process. Capacitive sensors can be embedded into 3D surfaces such as the grip on a pen to document how people hold tools [ 10 ] but doing so can affect the user experience. The achievable sensing resolution varies: some approaches only capture whether a particular area on the model was touched but do not offer more information about the touch. Heat-sensitive materials such as thermochromic inks can capture touch traces but may only work well in particular temperature ranges. Pressure-sensitive films also have limited sensitivity and are not easily adapted to complex surfaces. Motion sensing can help researchers make inferences about interactions but does not directly register touch [ 11 ].

Optical solutions are relatively unobtrusive, but touch events can be difficult to resolve due to occlusion. Computer vision often cannot precisely capture whether, or where, an object or surface has been touched, but can nevertheless provide valuable information on the gestures people employ when interacting with data physicalizations [ 38 ]. LIDAR offers more precision when resolving where a finger or hand is pointed, but as with video, cannot record the shape of the actual touch on the surface. Depth-sensing cameras [ 43 ] have been used to capture multi-finger touches on non-flat surfaces, but the depth information is not sufficient to capture exactly where the hand connected with the surface. High-resolution forward-looking infrared (FLIR) cameras can directly capture the ephemeral heat signatures of touch interactions [ 19 ]. However, these cameras are expensive and require a complex setup and the quality of the outcomes depends on the heat-conductivity of the material.

Combining sensing approaches may provide a richer understanding of touch behaviors and interactions with objects, including when objects are touched, the touch gestures used, and how a participant interacts with the object [ 10 , 20 , 26 ]. The method presented in this paper fills a gap in current sensing approaches by providing a low-cost unobtrusive method for recording touch that can be used in combination with other methods to establish a holistic understanding of touch behaviors.

3 RESEARCH GOALS AND CONTRIBUTIONS

Our goal was to create a method that provides a physical record of touch that:

  1. creates an accurate and persistent record of the area where the object was touched
  2. works with arbitrary shapes, not only flat surfaces
  3. is applicable to a range of materials and can be applied to objects in everyday settings
  4. does not significantly interfere with the tactile experience (e.g. feel unnatural or require extensive instrumentation)

We present an analog method that fulfills these criteria, involving a powder tracer substance that is nearly invisible in white light but strongly fluorescent under ultra-violet (UV) light. Its particles are either transferred from the hand to the surface (additive) or were previously deposited on the surface and removed or displaced during touch (subtractive). The additive approach generates traces of accretion, such as muddy footprints that reveal where someone has walked or where people deposit trash in the environment [ 42 ]. The substractive approach creates traces of erosion, such as the wear patterns on outdoor sculptures like the Juliette in Verona. Such self-inscribing (or autographic) phenomena can be regarded as a material form of data [ 30 ], physicalizing where the user has touched the object. Learning to correctly infer the behaviors that create a particular trace requires triangulation with other data sources, such as observations, interviews, and other sensing methods [ 41 ].

We use this method for capturing touch traces on the surfaces of 2D and 3D objects in different shapes and materials. Touch traces can be seen under UV light and enhanced using image processing methods (Figure 1 ). The method can be used on flat and arbitrarily-shaped objects, does not require expensive electronics or computation, and is relatively non-intrusive. It does, however, require intentional selection of object surfaces and attention to the process of documenting the traces through photography, which can have implications for the design of experimental procedures.

In Rogowitz et al. we captured touches on art objects as an early demonstration of touch tracer approaches [ 35 ]. In this paper, we provide a comprehensive description of this method and systematic tests of its application. First, we describe the selection of test objects and tracer substances, photographic and image processing methods for analyzing touch traces, and guidelines for capturing additional qualitative information. A user's manual describing best practices is included in the supplement material. The second part presents three user studies that demonstrate this technique and examine (1) the qualities of the generated traces, (2) the touch gestures used by participants, and (3) the application in the evaluation of data physicalizations. Although these controlled studies were not designed to test specific hypotheses about touch behavior, we were careful to select test situations that would allow researchers to imagine how this approach could be used in their domains. The results demonstrate the strengths and limitations of this method for capturing and analyzing touch behavior on real-world objects.

The first study shows that this method can capture point and stroke touches on a variety of 2D and 3D stimuli that represent various visualization and physicalization areas. The second study asks participants to touch an object shaped by Gaussian functions and answer questions about its features. This study produces a broader variety of real-world touch behaviors and also sets the stage for research aimed at understanding how touch patterns depend on a given task. In the third study, we measure undirected touch behaviors as participants interpret a physicalization of COVID-19 data and show how touch traces indicate areas of interest. The paper chronicles the development of this method, highlights design choices, and provides examples for researchers and designers interested in capturing touch behavior.

4 TRACER AND STIMULUS SELECTION

As a first step, we evaluated different tracer substances and their performance on different surfaces. Some of the tracers we evaluated were developed for simulating contamination through surface contact in medical and food safety settings [ 8 , 25 , 31 ]. We tested these tracers on a variety of materials—including glass, epoxy, and a variety of plastics—and identified several that provided the necessary visual contrast (i.e. were not UV fluorescent themselves) and were easy to clean. We achieved the best results with smooth, hard surfaces since the tracer is difficult to remove from textiles and objects with rough and textured surfaces.

  • Gaussian Surfaces (columns 2 and 3): Two objects composed of several 3D Gaussian distributions and milled from MDF and coated with a white UV-resistant epoxy resin. These were designed as ambiguous representations that could stand for multiple objects.
  • 3D Bar Chart (column 4). A re-creation of the physicalization object used in Jansen et al. [ 15 ] laser cut from acrylic.
  • Wine Glasses (column 5): A wine glass with ergonomic-inspired indentations for fingers.
  • Spirals and Troughs (columns 6 and 7): Two physicalizations of excess mortality p-scores during the years 2020 and 2021. 1 The objects were milled from solid PVC. 4.2 Selection of Tracer Substances

The evaluation criteria for fluorescent tracers sought substances that were easy to apply, transferred easily between surfaces to capture light touches, and fluoresced to produce high-contrast and legible traces under UV light. We compared insoluble fluorescent pigments, UV ink used for event access control, and different variations of commercial GloGerm tracer products, which are mostly used for hand-washing training and contagion studies. 2 GloGerm is based on microscopic plastic particles with a UV fluorescent coating. To allow for the fine spatial deposition and uniform application, we suspended the tracer substances in various media (e.g. rubbing alcohol, water, hand lotion, coconut oil, hair gel) and used various applicators (e.g. rubber brayer, make-up brush, airbrush). Figure 3 shows microscopy images of three candidate tracers under white and UV light.

We tested each substance under two conditions: subtractive (test object is covered with the fluorescent material and removed through touch) and additive mode (tracer is applied to the hands and transferred to the surface through touch). In both cases, touches followed a prescribed pattern (the 2D Test Pattern, seen in column 1 of Figure 2 ) and the traces were photographed under UV light. We measured the resulting physical and perceptual characteristics from the images, which we used to select the best tracers for our method.

4.3 Physical Luminance and Contrast

Fluorescent markers are typically evaluated based on the luminance re-radiated in the visible spectrum [ 18 ]. To approximate this measure, we measured the brightest color value of the image in L*a*b* space. The luminance of the substrate material is also taken into account using Maxwellian Contrast, (Lmax-Lmin)/(Lmax+ Lmin), a standard measure used in image legibility metrics [ 2 ]. The contrast measurement also allowed us to account for small variations in the photographs. These luminance and contrast measurements are shown in the first three columns of Figure 4 . Many of the materials produced high luminance traces, however only four also had sufficiently low background luminance levels to provide high contrast images.

4.4 Judged Image Quality

To account for the different qualities exhibited by the traces from different tracers and on different subtrates, we complemented the Maxwellian contrast measure with a number of measures based on subjective judgement by the researchers. Five members of our research team judged 5 subjective measures across the 16 observations.

  • Global contrast : the overall impression of light-to-dark ratio of the touches relative to the background, across the whole image
  • Local contrast : a measure of the individual stroke or dot that has the highest contrast; this measure correlates well with the physical luminance measure, which is based on the maximum luminance in an image
  • Resolution : the degree to which the vertical lines in the bottom pattern can be resolved; this measure captures the degree to which the touch features are well-formed and distinct
  • Stroke uniformity : the degree to which the touches are fully formed and continuous, without faded or missing regions
  • Touch/retouch : the degree to which dots that were touched twice can be distinguished from the dots that were touched once, or in the free-form curve, whether the over-written areas can be distinguished

4.5 Results

Image quality ratings are shown in the rightmost columns of Figure 4 . The highest resolution and highest local and global contrast images were produced using the GloGerm powder, applied to the surface with an airbrush, and removed when touched (subtractive). This method also produced the highest stroke uniformity and retouch contrast. Using the powder additively produced images that had higher physical contrast, and nearly as much resolution and contrast. However, in the additive version, touch strokes were less uniform and less continuous, since the powder often ran out between touches and did not register second touches. Additive powder was also often visible on the objects under normal light. Many candidate materials produced high local contrast at specific regions in the image, but did not provide consistently high contrast across the image (global contrast).

The two most important variables for legibility are contrast and spatial resolution. These two dimensions are visualized as a scatterplot in Figure 5 which reveal the clear superiority of the GloGerm powder in additive and subtractive mode. Other factors influenced our selection. We observed, for example, that a number of candidates, such as the GloGerm gel and the GloGerm powder in oil, produced very expressive fingerpainting-like images that conveyed rich information about how the participant touched the test pattern, however, were weak in contrast, resolution, and uniformity. Moreover, some candidates had additional attributes that led to their elimination; for example, the UV Ink left a permanent mark on the users’ hands and the GloGerm oil left orange traces.

Based on these measurements and observations, we selected two methods: (1) GloGerm powder applied to the test object with an airbrush and removed by the participant's touch (subtractive), and (2) GloGerm powder applied to the participant's hands and deposited when the participant touched the test object (additive) (Figures 4 and 5 ). The subtractive method turned out to create more consistent results, as the intensity of the traces did not diminish over the course of the interaction. Its disadvantage is the potential difficulty of applying a uniform powder coating, depending on the shape and size of the object. While we mostly used the subtractive approach in the subsequent studies, the additive method may still be useful under specific circumstances.

5 EXPERIMENTAL METHODS ACROSS USER STUDIES

The next part of the paper provides three demonstrations of our method using the subtractive approach. The first measures touch traces on objects representing use cases from scientific data visualization, data physicalization, and product design. The second evaluates undirected touches in response to open-ended questions. The third examines how people touch 3D data physicalizations of COVID-19 time series data when asked to make analytical and comparative judgments.

For all three user studies, participants were recruited through university email lists, posters, and convenience sampling and had limited familiarity with the research program. Each session took 20-30 minutes and participants were given a $ 5 gift card for their participation. All studies received IRB approval.

All sessions were video recorded from a roughly 45-degree downward angle focused on the object to make the contact between the participant's hands and the model as visible as possible. During the study, researchers asked participants to both explain their answers to the study questions and reflect on their reasoning process for each question using a semi-structured interview process. Immediately after each experimental session, researchers gathered their early reflections and noted key observations on the participants’ comments and behaviors. All videos were transcribed.

All conditions were presented in a within-subjects design, where each subject served as their own control. The test objects were fully cleaned and prepared between conditions and between participants. For each condition, questions were printed on a card that rested in front of the test objects, which were placed on a table.

Photographing the traces under UV light created a durable record that could be further enhanced and made sense of through image processing techniques. After each condition in an experiment, the stimulus object was photographed in a darkroom using a lightbox designed to prevent shadows and reflections of the UV-LED sources on the surface of the object. Many models were captured from a top-down view to allow us to extract the touch traces independently from the object geometry and avoid occlusions. Other objects were documented from multiple oblique angles that best captured their shape. As many cameras have difficulties adjusting to the monochromatic UV fluorescence, we used manual settings for white balance, aperture, and shutter speed. To minimize distortions, we used a telephoto lens. The entire set of photographs for each user study is included in the supplemental material.

To isolate the touch traces in the top-down view required additional steps. To maximize the contrast of traces, we found it effective to extract the blue and cyan color components, which dominate the fluorescence of the tracer. For the subsequent interpretation of traces, we mapped the trace image back onto a 3D model to allow inspecting them from all sides. Photogrammetric corrections are straightforward for 2.5D surfaces such as the Gaussian models; for complex geometries image-based 3D scanning can be used. Mapping traces back onto the models also allowed the creation of composite trace images. Additional information on the image processing is included in the supplemental material.

6 USER STUDY 1: PHYSICALIZING TOUCH BEHAVIOR ON 2- AND 3D OBJECTS

In this study, users were asked to touch four models in specific, directed ways. The first was the test object used in the selection of tracer materials. The three additional touch objects represented experimental stimuli used in scientific visualization, data physicalization, and design. The goal is to test the applicability and limits of our analog technique for applications that involve real-world objects and 3D data representations. This study allowed us to measure touch physicalizations on different objects created through a range of fabrication methods.

6.1 Experimental Methods

Participants followed explicit directions for touching four test objects: a 2D acrylic sheet, a Gaussian model (Big Gauss), a 3D bar chart, and a wine glass. Questions were designed to elicit different types of touches and test how well the method captures these touches on different objects. The objects and questions are described in Table 1 ; images of the objects are included in Figure 2 .

6.2 Results

Figure 6 shows examples of recorded traces, each row showing the traces corresponding to each of the three questions. While traces are generally better visible in situ, the photos clearly reveal touches and strokes: touches on the peaks of the Gaussian mountains, a swipe along the side of the 3D bar chart, and fingerprints where the glass was grasped. Overall, the surfaces produced crisp and continuous traces.

6.2.1 Quantitative Measurements.

We coded the traces with the same image quality metrics used earlier to evaluate the tracer candidates. Perceived contrast, one of the measures, followed the classic compressive function of physical luminance [ 37 ], which supported the reliability of our coding practice. A graph of perceived contrast as a function of physical contrast is included in the supplemental material. All touch gestures were visible on the acrylic test target, the epoxy Gaussian, and on the wine glass, but only about 70% of the touches were visible on the the 3D bars, which can be explained by its raw, untreated surfaces produced by the laser-cutter. Point touches generally produced lower contrast traces than tracing a finger across the surface. The latter produced traces with high contrast and uniformity for all subjects on all test objects.

Figure 7 shows the distribution of Maxwellian contrast for all test objects. Again, the glass surface created the highest contrast. The 2D acrylic test model provided close to 30% contrast, which is still very legible, followed closely by the epoxy surface of the Gaussian stimulus. The acrylic 3D bar chart came out last due to its rough surfaces. Touch traces along the side of this model produced highly variable contrast ratings.

6.2.2 Qualitative Results. Touch gestures were nuanced and show interesting variations between participants. These variations may be a result of their individual touch styles, the amount of pressure they applied, or the moisture on their skin. In this early study, the uniform application of powder was still a challenge, introducing further unintended variation.

For stroke gestures, the accumulation of displaced tracer generated traces with bright edges, which offered additional information about the direction of the stroke and whether the stroke was interrupted, and—to some extent—also its speed. Figure 8 shows accumulation of powder at the end of three traces. Based on the corresponding videos, we believe that these features may correspond to the angle of the participant's finger and the pressure applied.

Participants executed these tasks differently. For the symmetry-judgement task of the peaks in Big Gauss, some participants touched the entire peak while others touched just the very top of the peak. In the interviews, all 11 participants commented on the tasks and the objects, but interestingly, very few mentioned the powder coating on the objects. This suggests that at least the subtractive method is quite unobtrusive, which was one of our design goals. The videos, however, showed that a few participants seemed to hold their fingers or hands intentionally away from their bodies, suggesting awareness of the powder.

6.3 Summary

The goal of this user study was to test the ability of our method to reliably capture touch traces on 2D and 3D objects. We found contrast differences based on the material and surface texture of the objects; the method worked best on glass and was least effective on rough surfaces. Careful cleaning and uniform application of the tracer are critical and require some practice. Furthermore, we found that the participants showed no hesitation interacting with the coated objects, their different touch styles all produced usable traces; no special training was required. Finally, we learned to observe aspects of the trace—such as accumulation of tracer powder—that could be indicators for qualities of the touch, such as direction, pressure, and angle. The study also revealed significant limitations: the subtractive method is inferior on rough surfaces, and a certain background fluorescence due to small scratches or the optical properties of the material can reduce contrast. Nevertheless, in almost all conditions, the method produced high contrast, high resolution, and uniform touch traces.

7 USER STUDY 2: PHYSICALIZING TOUCH TRACES FOR DIFFERENT TASKS

Study 2 examines traces created by participants exploring the stimuli based on more open-ended tasks. After evaluating the capacity of the tracer to capture touch interactions on a variety of surfaces and objects, the main objective of study 2 is examining the range and variety of touch interactions elicited by different prompts for the same object.

An interesting open research question beyond the scope of this paper is to what extent touch exploration is driven by object characteristics (bottom-up processing), or by task characteristics (top-down processing). Yarbus [ 45 ] showed that eye movements when examining a painting depended on the task the participant was performing, not the features of the viewed image, per se. It is reasonable to expect that the task also shapes the patterns of tactile exploration. Study 2 examines the usefulness of our method to study this question. Since this study has a small number of conditions and participants, it is a proof of concept, not a fully-formed experiment.

7.1 Experimental Methods

In this study, eight participants answered two different questions about each of the Gaussian Models—"Big Gauss" and "Little Gauss." In one condition, we told the participants that the model represented physical terrain (either a mountainous landscape or a volcanic region); in the other, we told them that the model was a representation of scientific data (either a bacteria culture or a temperature map). Participants were asked to solve a simple task and describe their solution to the researcher. Table 2 shows the questions asked for each model. As described in Section 5 , the traces were photographed, digitized, mapped onto a digital model, and aggregated across observations; the participants’ interactions were again recorded on video and accompanied by semi-structured interviews.

Of particular interest in this study was the role of narrative framing. When framed as representing different phenomena, would participants touch the models differently? In this case, we would expect the traces to emphasize elements and relationships described in the task. Conversely, if the touch behavior were driven by the stimulus, we would expect the same prominent features to be touched in each question regardless of the task.

7.2 Results

All participants touched the models for all four questions, and in all cases, clear, high-contrast traces were obtained.

The touch traces collected not only showed a wide range of forms, but were also strongly dependent on the task. Figure 9 shows remapped 3D images of one participant's touch behavior in response to the four questions. With the caveat of small sample size, we observed that participants responding to the same task produced similar traces. When the Big Gauss model was framed as a visualization of bacterial growth, participants touched all the peaks to determine which sample had the highest magnitude and no areas between the peaks were touched. As a result of judging symmetry, touch strokes extended down along the slopes of the peaks. When the task performed on the same model was framed as a wayfinding problem on physical terrain, the participants tended to draw continuous traces from one mountaintop to another to "avoid the flood." On the Little Gauss model, the temperature question sometimes generated traces that resemble contour lines, corresponding to the task of identifying temperature differences. Finally, the question about estimating the direction of lava flow after the eruption of a volcano generated large wide traces running from the largest peak into the adjacent valleys and mimicking the flow of lava.

7.2.1 Combining Data from Multiple Participants. To visualize which model features attracted touch across our whole population of participants, we used a technique common in saliency research, where data are accumulated to provide a population map, highlighting the regions-of-interest salient to the judgment [ 7 ].

Figure 10 shows the cumulative touch patterns created by overlaying touch outcomes across all participants for each question. In this user study, a clear motif emerged for each question, showing common characteristics of the task-dependent touch traces. For judging peak height on Big Gauss, the composite touch pattern was discrete and concentrated on the peaks; there was little ambiguity about which features the participants found to be relevant for answering the question. When asked how to avoid a flood, a network of touch traces emerged connecting between the tops of the adjacent peaks. While participants chose different paths to answer this question, their patterns were relatively consistent. For Little Gauss, the question about judging temperature was challenging, and many of the participants had difficulty providing a meaningful answer. The composite image was the most diffuse for this question, covering large portions of the model while also highlighting five main features; the contour lines occasionally visible in individual images disappeared. The question framing the model as a terrain again produced a legible composite pattern. Traces in response to the lava flow question concentrated on the highest peak and surrounding valleys and covered large continuous areas, mirroring the scenario described in the question. Participants’ touch rates associated with each task and model also vary, as demonstrated in Figure 11 (left) which shows the percent of the pixels touched for each model and question across all subjects.

7.2.2 Individual differences. Figure 11 (right) shows the percent of pixels touched by each participant across models. This shows large differences between participants; overall, touches covered about 25% of the image, but participant P03’s touches covered up to almost 80% of one image, while participant P08’s touches always covered less than 10%, often leaving only a few points or traces on the model. This variation may indicate different approaches to reasoning through or communicating the results of tasks; further research is required to understand behaviors across a larger sample of participants and interpret the meaning and implications of these differences.

7.2.3 Observation and video. We observed that participants used different parts of their hand(s) when interacting with the model in response to complex tasks. This included single finger, multiple finger, whole hand, and two-handed touch strategies (Figure 12 ). There was additional variation within each of these approaches—for example, participants might complete a single finger touch with their index finger or their ring finger, or use their hand in different shapes during a whole hand touch.

Second, as noted in previous data physicalization studies [ 15 ], we saw that participants took advantage of the physical nature of the model by manipulating it or moving intentionally in relation to it when considering the questions. This included rotating, tilting, and lifting the model, as well as moving in relation to the model. At times, participants would also combine these actions with touch—for example, lifting the model to eye level and then touching the surface. This suggests the potential complexity of touch gestures. Does a single finger touch create the same kind of trace on the model when the model is on the table versus when it is held in the hand? The process of lifting the model can also create touch patterns on the surface.

7.3 Summary

The consistent results obtained demonstrate the power of this method for addressing cognitive questions related to touch exploration. We found that the pattern of touches varied depending on the question asked. Although not a large, or statistically validated study, it echoes the classic result by Yarbus [ 44 ] showing that touch gestures, like eye movements, can be driven by the task. We think this method can be a valuable contribution to our set of tools for studying the interplay of bottom-up and top-down processing of data physicalizations.

The video further demonstrated the complexity of touch in response to open-ended questions. A library of touch traces and videos of corresponding interactions could serve as a valuable resource to inform the interpretation of future results captured with our method, and dovetail with other efforts to categorize types of touch (e.g. [ 16 , 46 ]).

8 USER STUDY 3: USING TOUCH TRACES TO EVALUATE PHYSICALIZATIONS OF TIME-SERIES DATA

The goal of this study is to demonstrate the ability of our method to capture touch behavior in a real-life data physicalization scenario and to explore how measuring touch behavior might inform our understanding of how touch supports reasoning about data. We created data physicalizations of COVID-19 mortality data, over time, for several countries, and observed how participants touched these models in response to data analysis questions. One model rendered the time series data for one country as a spiral; the other rendered times series data for five countries as a set of linear troughs. In both physicalizations, the greater the excess death, relative to statistical average, the deeper the spiral or linear trough.

8.1 Experimental Methods

We began the study with a short explanation of the concept of excess mortality and how it is represented in the spiral and linear physicalizations. We also provided a summary sheet that participants could refer to throughout the study. Participants (n=12) answered three questions based on the models (Table 3 ). For question 1, both the spiral- and linear- trough models are presented together, and the participant is asked to identify the linear data representation that shows the same data as shown in the spiral representation. This question was designed to create space for a "lay-of-the-land exploration" which researchers have observed when participants encounter new data physicalizations [ 9 ]. The other questions ask them to analyze the data depicted in the spiral and linear physicalizations, separately.

8.2 Results

The first question asked the participants to explore both models, and select which linear trough depicted the data shown in the spiral. 9/12 correctly selected the linear trough 5. 11/12 participants ran their fingers completely around the spiral, touching all areas, extending their touches along the curve of the spiral toward the center. Most (8/12) also ran their fingers along each of the linear troughs, while four touched more selectively. Although all participants touched the correct trough completely, three went on to name an incorrect match.

For the second question, the participants were asked to examine the spiral and identify the region of lowest excess mortality. 10/12 participants provided a good estimate, even though the physicalization did not include any calibration information. For this question, the participants made more touch strokes that extended into the center of the spiral, which contained no data, perhaps thinking that these regions were in fact shallow, low-mortality data. While individual touches were rare in the first question, most of the participants (9/12) produced localized touches when seeking regions in the spiral with lowest excess death. For 7/12, these were additive traces; after touching the whole spiral, they evidently circled back to touch specific regions, leaving additive traces superimposed on the subtractive ones (Figure 13 ). These areas are clearly visible on the model, as they are brighter than the untouched coating, and in stark contrast to the darker areas cleared from powder.

In the third question, the participants were asked to focus on linear troughs for countries 2 and 5. All the participants touched these two troughs, although three touched other country's data, and one participant, who answered incorrectly, touched all 5 troughs. Identifying the regions of highest excess death (deepest troughs) was easier for the second country (10/11) than for the fifth (7/11), but all were able to identify which country had the higher overall excess death (11/11). Making an overall magnitude judgment, thus, seemed easier for our participants than identifying specific occurrences along the time line.

Figure 14 shows touch results for one participant (A11) across all questions. Observing these traces and their nuances provides preliminary insight into how the participant touched the model and how that might contribute to their judgements about the data. For Q2, we see that some regions of the spiral are darker than the others, indicating more frequent touches. Some touches also extend before the beginning of the spiral, as if the participant were trying to figure out exactly where the data started. To answer the question about the two linear troughs (Q3), we see that touches were drawn to the two countries specified in the question, with no extraneous touches to other countries. In Q1, where the task was to select the linear trough that contained the same data as the spiral, all the powder has been rubbed off all locations in the spiral, suggesting that the participant ran their finger back and forth while making the comparison. Unlike their response to Q3, to make the comparison they touched all the linear troughs. The widest impression was for the fifth row, which was the correct answer.

8.2.1 Connecting touch traces and reasoning. From the images, it is evident that participants touch different parts of the model in response to different reasoning tasks. For example, 11/12 participants touched the full spiral in Q1 (identifying the country present on both models) while only about half of participants touched the full spiral in Q2 (identifying the moment of lowest excess mortality). Participants also touched more of the country lines for question Q1 (identifying the country present in both models) than they did in Q3 (identifying the highest excess mortality in two countries).

The traces collected for Q3 closely correspond with the reasoning task—identifying the highest excess mortality in the second and fifth countries and comparing them—and show how participants go about solving it. 8/11 participants exclusively touched areas of data from countries two and five. Of the three remaining participants, A04 touched all the countries, A14 touched the flat, non-data parts of the model extensively, and A08 touched country four as well as countries two and five. (N.B. one participant's (A09) data was lost for this question due to researcher error.) 7/11 participants showed particular focus around the three deepest valleys in country five. This was manifest in a number of ways: some participants did not touch the other areas of line 5 (e.g. A06), other participants showed additive touches on the valleys of line 5 (e.g. A10), and a final set of participants had wider touch paths around the valleys of line 5 (e.g. A07)(Figure 15 ).

8.2.2 Informing physicalization design. Touch patterns documented on the data physicalizations illuminate aspects of the model that could inform future design iterations. That is, it provides information to researchers about how their representations are not understood as intended.

In Q2, some participants reported difficulties finding the beginning of the spiral, which was underscored by their touch traces showing a distinct emphasis on the same area. We found a similar pattern in the traces of participants who did not comment on this issue but may have experienced similar difficulties. Specifically, for Q2 we saw that 11/12 participants had considerable touch around the start of the spiral, including point touches and tracing explorations that extended the spiral inwards beyond its starting point (Figure 16 ). These findings have design implications for the physicalization; for example, researchers might choose to mark the start of the data if they wanted to avoid this ambiguity in the model.

On the linear model, the touch patterns revealed areas that were difficult for some participants to touch. Specifically, for Q1 we see that some participants touched the entire line for the fifth country but couldn't "reach" the deepest part of the grooves (Figure 17 ). This could inform future designs of the model—if participants are intended to fully sense the model through touch, the aspect ratio of the grooves may need to be altered to accommodate that. Or, designers might consider this a positive feature of the model—perhaps participants pay more attention to areas that they aren't able to touch and develop strategies to reach them.

8.3 Summary

The evaluation of visualizations often involves measuring how well participants perform a task under different conditions. In this user study, we demonstrate how such responses can be collected and compared with touch behaviors. Our goal was not to draw definitive connections between performance and touch behavior, but to illustrate the richness of touch behavior while performing data physicalization tasks. Where data objects were touched, which features were not touched, whether the object was touched continuously, or with individual fingertip points, are all valuable data about how these data physicalizations are perceived and interpreted.

9 DISCUSSION

The main goal of this work was to develop a method for capturing persistent traces of human touch interactions on the surfaces of arbitrary objects. In this paper, we identified a fluorescent material and method that produces reliable, high-contrast traces traces, and conducted three user studies that examine relevant aspects of the method. The first user study focused on the phenomenology of the captured traces: how well can the method capture traces of touch from participants, and what are the characteristic features of the resulting traces? After gaining insight on the kind of traces the method can produce, the second user study examined the range of touch gestures in response to different tasks. Since the participants were no longer directly instructed to touch but rather to solve tasks that involve touching, we were able to capture a range of touch gestures that we did not anticipate in our earlier tests. We tried to maximize the range of captured touch gestures by framing the same ambiguous models to represent different things: bacteria, landscapes, temperature gradients. The captured touches ranged from precise tracings to touch gestures that involve the full hand and covered much of the model. With the caveat of the small number of participants, the captured traces varied by question but were consistent across participants, especially for the wayfinding and geophysics questions. The third user study, finally, examined touch behaviors in a realistic evaluation scenario for a concrete data physicalization of COVID-19 data. In this study, the tasks focused on reasoning and data interpretation, instead of encouraging participants to touch the objects. All participants nevertheless touched the stimulus objects to varying degrees. In this evaluation scenario, we found that the traces offer insights into how participants make sense of the data representation, in particular highlighting areas where they experience uncertainty or confusion. At the same time, the traces resulting from the exploration revealed additional clues—whether an area has been touched multiple times and where touch gestures were interrupted. To support and improve our interpretation of traces, we recorded all interactions on video. This, however, should become less necessary as the traces produced by the method are better understood.

9.1 Characteristics and Limitations

The method excels at collecting touch traces that reveal areas of interest, of uncertainty, or the amount of touch interactions the task and the object provoke. A particular strength is that it works on a wide range of objects with different shapes (with the limitation that their surfaces should be relatively smooth) something not easily possible with digital sensors. Although not tested in this paper, the method could also be applied in real-world contexts, for example on maps and models of buildings to support wayfinding tasks. Likewise, it can be applied to very large objects with complex surfaces. What the method does not show is the sequence of touch interactions—something almost trivial for digital sensing or computer vision approaches. If both sequence and aggregate are needed, one can consider combining the methods since they complement each other's weaknesses.

While the tracer method is inexpensive and straight-forward, it also requires a considerable amount of preparation to capture a useful trace. This includes the spraying of the objects which requires skill as well as the photographic documentation of traces. However, one is rewarded with traces that are vibrant and rich in detail, while digital methods rarely offer more than estimates of touch location and pressure. The advantage of an analog approach is that one can always find additional clues by examining the traces to overcome the methods’ inherent limitation. The observation and interpretation of re-touch artifacts is such an example.

The ideal way to interpret the captured traces is to examine the object under UV light. Every photographic representation is inferior to this experience, even though photographic traces can be enhanced in many ways. We somewhat overcame this limitation by mapping the photographs back onto 3D models of the objects, which allowed for better interpretation. The challenge remains that even the most accurate trace does not always allow one to infer the interaction that caused it. This familiar problem of physical traces can be addressed through triangulation with other data sources. The use of photographed touch traces, video, and qualitative interviews may provide additional grounds for understanding traces.

Among the range of possible applications, the method is especially suited for evaluating static data physicalizations. The touch traces of study 3 revealed a weakness we did not consider when designing the stimulus objects: the ambiguity of where on the object the encoded data begins. This method allows designers to experiment with a range of materials such as plastic, glass, and metal.

Designers can work with the method's limitation of producing aggregate traces rather than the sequence of touch interactions over time—after all, eye-tracking data is also frequently evaluated in aggregate form to identify regions of interest. A possibility to take advantage of this characteristic is to use tracers with different degrees of "stickiness"—for example, the UV ink that we eliminated because it was not sensitive enough to touches might be used to document aggregate touch interactions over a longer time period. Within the additive method, we could also examine the touch interaction between multiple participants by using tracers of different color.

9.2 Future Opportunities

A method that can reliably capture touch behavior can be an important tool for research designed to understand how touch behavior contributes to making judgments about properties of physical space, and how specific touch patterns contribute to memory, aesthetic judgments, and spatial context. It could also play an important role in research geared at multimodal sensing, that is, the study of how information from the different senses combine. Measurements of where people touch can be a valuable tool for research in data visualization and public communication of data. Recent research in data physicalization has demonstrated that physical objects representing data can provide advantages in data understanding. Our method provides an opportunity to document how people use touch, which could extend this research. For example, measuring touch behavior explicitly could be added to a recent study of interpretation of network physicalizations [ 5 ] to provide more detail on what was touched and how it was touched. Touch tracking using UV tracers could also provide an additional data stream for documenting and interpreting reconfiguration strategies used by people interacting with composite data physicalizations [ 36 ].

An important thread in visualization research is to understand how people decode meaning from different representations of data. Now that we have a reliable method for capturing touch behaviors, we can also plan experiments that will allow us to learn more about the semantics of these touches. The famous Yarbus experiment showed that eye movement patterns depend not only on the low-level syntactic features of an image, but on the high-level meaning the observer wants to extract. Future research could use this method to record touch to help elucidate the relationship between intent and action, in the same way monitoring eye movements to carefully-controlled visual stimuli has helped to decode the semantics of visual attention. Touch patterns provide a direct interaction measure, which could be very valuable in understanding how features of different representations are explored, encoded, and compared.

This approach could be of value across a wide range of other domains, where it is important to document, interpret, and make sense of touch. Study of individual differences has had a resurgence in psychological research [ 28 ] and having a reliable method to capture individual and group touch behaviors could be a valuable tool to support this research. Human-Robot Interaction is particularly interested in social touch and includes work categorizing types of social touch [ 16 ]. Our method could be very valuable in evaluating the "tactiles" created for people who are blind or visually impaired, by providing data on how the components of the assistive display are touched. It could also be a valuable tool for product design, where differences in how objects are touched could guide innovation. There are also many medical applications where practitioners are trained to demonstrate knowledge of a physical object or to make specific touches on a training model. Having a reliable, durable method to evaluate the pattern of touches could provide better feedback to the instructor, and thereby reduce training time.

10 CONCLUSION

This paper introduces an accurate and accessible method for capturing and recording data about touch behavior on a variety of 3D objects and 2D surfaces. Our approach does not require electronic or optical instrumentation and can be used on a range of materials. Touching either deposits fluorescent powder onto the object (additive, accretion) or removes fluorescent material that previously been applied to the object (subtractive, erosion). The result is a high-contrast physicalization of the touch behavior, which can be captured photographically and image-processed to highlight characteristics of the touch pattern. The clarity of the results makes this method a good candidate for documenting how people touch 2D and 3D objects, data physicalizations, and real-world objects, making it a potentially valuable tool for scientific and artistic domains where touch behavior plays an important role. Having a reliable method for capturing touch behavior is the first step to understanding the semantics of touch, tactile interactions, and the fundamentals of how we learn about and interact with objects through touch.
