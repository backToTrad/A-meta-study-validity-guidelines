“Any bit of help, helps”: Understanding how older caregivers use carework platforms for caregiving support

Vitica Arnold , Department of Informatics, University of California Irvine, United States, viticaarnold@gmail.com

CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Older adults are increasingly acting as caregivers, given population aging and pervasive caregiver shortages. Meanwhile, gig-economy-based carework platforms have also become popular. As older adults are one of the fastest-growing groups of informal caregivers and technology users in the past decade, we conducted an online survey among older adults residing in the US (N = 193) about how they use these platforms to manage their caregiving tasks. We identified factors related to their frequency of using caregiving help and their intention to continue using caregiving help via carework platforms. We also reported how and why older adults use these platforms, their main concerns and needs related to these platforms, and their most significant positive and negative experiences. Our findings contribute to a foundational understanding of how older caregivers use carework platforms and how such platforms could be better designed to suit the needs and wants of older caregivers.

1 INTRODUCTION

Since the beginning of the 21 st century, there has been an unprecedented deficit in the number of caregivers in the United States [ 31 , 38 , 92 ]. This is partially due to an increase in the number of older adults needing care and the lack of adults available to provide care [ 38 ]. Societal trends such as the emergence of the nuclear family, increased geographic mobility, and high employment rates among women have contributed to the decreased number of available caregivers that resulted in the ‘care crisis’ [ 38 ]. The COVID-19 pandemic has also exacerbated the current care crisis in the United States [ 92 ]. The expected decrease in family caregiver support brings challenges about who will care for the growing number of older adults hoping to age in place [ 62 ]. Meanwhile, more and more older adults are becoming caregivers to fill the care gap as the population ages. In 2015, about 34% of informal caregivers are 65 and older [ 32 ], an increase from 13% in 2004 [ 59 ]. Unpaid caregiving is also increasing in prevalence, from 16.6 percent of caregivers being unpaid or informal caregivers in 2015 to 19.2 percent in 2020 [ 60 ]. There are increasing physical demands on caregivers, given that care recipients often have complicated conditions with multiple comorbidities such as long-term physical illnesses, emotional or mental health issues, and cognitive impairment from Alzheimer's Disease or other forms of dementia [ 60 ]. Caregivers also experience a significant financial burden, reporting that they spent over a quarter of their personal income on caregiving expenses [ 2 ]. Informal caregiving can have a substantial negative impact on caregivers' physical and mental health [ 57 , 80 ], which can be exacerbated for older caregivers who are experiencing their own age-related changes in health and well-being [ 9 ].

In response to the care crisis and ongoing burden experienced by informal caregivers, various carework platforms have emerged over the last two decades as part of the trend toward the gig economy. The gig economy is broadly defined as “labor markets that are characterized by independent contracting that happens through, via, and on digital platforms” [ 94 ]. Work obtained through gig economy platforms tends to be temporary, with little commitment from the worker and the employer [ 34 ]. Carework platforms are a sector of the gig economy that focuses on “service work relating to the care of human bodies and the maintenance of physical spaces” [ 34 ] and function as online marketplaces for childcare, senior care, disability-related care, and pet care. They act as intermediaries to algorithmically match workers who can work as caregivers with consumers looking to purchase their services [ 80 ]. By borrowing sociotechnical features from online marketplaces and social media platforms, care workers can sometimes create profiles with personal information and receive feedback and ratings [ 80 ]. Like Uber and Lyft, these carework platforms treat care workers as independent contractors rather than employees [ 34 ]. While carework platforms are a fast-growing and unique sector of the gig economy [ 80 ], prior scholarship has predominantly focused on ridesharing or freelancing platforms such as Uber, Lyft, or Upwork [ 19 , 43 , 69 , 83 ]. Similarly, extensive work outside of gig economy scholarship has examined caregivers’ routines and practices [ 21 , 73 , 82 ] and how technologies can facilitate caregiving support, coordination, and communication [ 10 , 51 , 68 , 78 , 81 ].

There is a great variety in the features of carework platforms depending on the specific business models of the company, and features within the same platform can also differ based on membership status. Care.com is one of the biggest players in the industry [ 74 ]. In a typical use case of Care.com, users are first presented with a series of questions about the kind of caregiving help they are looking for (e.g., household tasks, personal care, companionship, one-time vs. recurring), followed by prompts to create an account on the site and more questions on their preferred schedules of, compensation for, and qualities in a caregiver. Users can post a job ad for their caregiving needs directly or search for caregivers on the site near a specific geographic location. After a search, the website displays a list of candidates to the user and provides the option to book a certain caregiver. For each caregiver, the website shows a short self-introduction, along with a profile picture and relevant professional information, including years of experience, price, availability, services provided, qualifications, skills, and available reviews from other employers. The platform algorithm also recommends similar caregivers nearby. The basic (i.e., free) membership does not have the same feature as a premium membership, which allows the user to message caregivers to schedule interviews, request background checks, reply to applications, and communicate with caregivers. Depending on the plan, the premium membership of Care.com costs from $ 12.95 to $ 38.92 per month [ 17 ]. In contrast, while providing similar services of matching caregivers with families, platforms like HomeCare.com or Visiting Angels do not list caregivers’ information on the website nor specify their pricing. Instead, they ask the user to leave their personal information by filling out online forms and promise to respond to the user's request within a certain period [ 42 , 90 ].

Currently, we know little about how informal caregivers, particularly older caregivers, use carework platforms to supplement and offload some of the burdens of caregiving work. Given that older adults are more regularly serving as caregivers and carework platforms have the potential to play a role in their broader systems of care, we conducted an online survey to identify the current practices and challenges associated with older caregivers’ usage of carework platforms. This paper reports results from an online survey with 193 older caregivers (age 65-94) who have used a carework platform and have served as informal caregivers within the last year. The specific research questions we have sought to answer through our quantitative and qualitative analyses include:

RQ1: What carework platforms do older caregivers use for caregiving help? What tasks do older caregivers use them for? What factors are related to older caregivers’ frequency of using caregiving help via carework platforms? What factors are related to older caregivers’ intention to continue using caregiving help via carework platforms?

RQ2: How and why are older caregivers using caregiving help via carework platforms?

RQ3: What are the main concerns and needs of older caregivers seeking caregiving help via carework platforms?

We found that older caregivers in our sample are using a wide range of carework platforms to support a diversity of tasks, from getting support with feeding and bathing to grocery shopping and housework to help manage finances. Those who used carework platforms more frequently tended to use the services to support a greater number of tasks, perceived the caregiving help to be of higher quality, and had a higher level of internet skills. Our analysis also revealed that older adults use these platforms to help maintain balance in their lives, provide respite, and even augment their knowledge and ability to care for others. While the platforms play an important role in finding caregivers, older adults’ experiences largely depend on interactions with individual caregivers, which vary from highly positive (e.g., being well-trained professionals, showing genuine care) to extremely negative (e.g., theft, leaving people unattended).

As older adults are increasingly taking up the role of caregivers, this paper contributes to the HCI literature a foundational understanding of how older caregivers use carework platforms to address the care deficit in the following ways. First, it contributes to a more nuanced understanding of how and why older caregivers use carework platforms and empirical factors related to their usage of caregiving help via carework platforms. Second, by highlighting older adults as active users of carework platforms and their concerns and needs, we point to the urgency of designing more age-friendly carework platforms and provide practical recommendations for improving the user experience. Particularly, carework platforms could consider positioning some care workers as educators or consultants in helping families care for complex conditions, impose stricter background checks on care workers, screen for care workers’ ability to provide nurturant care, and allow fixed arrangements with specific care workers routinely. Third, we propose that to improve the quality of care and increase their user base, carework platforms could also engage in more cooperation with other stakeholders in the home care sector, including governments and insurance companies, to advocate for care workers’ better pay and benefits and transform the stereotypical image of care work as being unskilled and undemanding. In sum, our results offer a timely and practical contribution towards understanding how carework platforms could be designed better to support the needs and priorities of older caregivers and help relieve the care burden for older adults who may need it the most.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Older Adults and Online Technology Use

The rate of Internet-related technology adoption among older adults, including home broadband subscriptions, tablet and smartphone ownership, and social media usage, has grown significantly since 2012 [ 33 , 65 , 66 ]. The gap between the oldest and youngest adults has narrowed regarding adopting key technologies [ 33 ]. Notably, technology use among older adults skyrocketed during the pandemic, as older adults spent more on technology and more frequently used video chats/texting/emailing to connect with families and friends [ 1 ]. Older adults also learned new ways to use the Internet: purchasing groceries, visiting doctors, ordering prescriptions, making appointments, streaming videos, or doing online banking [ 1 ]. The pandemic has served as an accelerator in prompting significant changes in how older adults adopt technology for daily life and solve issues they have not relied on technology to solve pre-pandemic [ 76 ]. Yet even before the pandemic, HCI scholarship has focused on a variety of topics related to older adults and technology use (e.g., [ 14 , 16 , 29 , 52 , 54 ]). For example, scholars have examined how older adults use social media and online blogging to satisfy their social needs [ 7 , 13 , 52 ]. Other common topics involve designing and deploying systems to promote older adults’ social support and connectedness [ 70 , 87 ] and evaluating the acceptance, feasibility, and effectiveness of technology-based interventions [ 8 , 37 , 93 ].

2.2 Older Adults as Caregivers and Caregiving Technology

Extensive prior work has investigated issues related to caregivers’ roles and ways of supporting caregivers while identifying design opportunities for assisting caregivers [ 3 , 21 , 23 , 24 , 78 , 96 ]. For example, one study focusing on older informal caregivers identified the multiple and demanding challenges older caregivers face, such as critical needs for support and coordination with other stakeholders, including family members and care professionals [ 73 ]. Another study also provided an in-depth understanding of the effective social support that older informal caregivers benefited from during caregiving and discussed how these understandings could inform system design for strengthening older adults’ social support online and offline [ 82 ]. Yet another study described how informal caregivers served various roles during a hospital stay for patients, including a companion to provide emotional support, an assistant to provide instrumental support, a representative to advocate for the patient, and a navigator to help the patient understand the current situation and condition, as well as a planner to handle critical moments such as hospital discharge [ 57 ].

Prior work has also contributed design efforts to support caregivers’ needs for assistance with various tasks, interactions, and communication [ 24 , 35 , 51 , 96 ]. Related projects have introduced systems to improve dementia care through a prototype that collects and shares care-related messages among transient volunteers with high turnover rates [ 35 ]; facilitate communication between stakeholders (primary family caregivers, respite caregivers) [ 24 ]; and help family caregivers record and track their caregiving activities and patients’ conditions [ 96 ]. The tracking system, for example, was effective in helping family caregivers understand the illness and cope with depressed family members, as well as improved family communication [ 96 ]. In another design study aimed at supporting informal caregivers by encouraging self-disclosure, a social robot (i.e., Robot Pepper) was deployed as an intervention tool to lead conversations with caregivers and elicit self-disclosure in a longitudinal study [ 51 ]. Results indicated that caregivers’ self-disclosure to the robot increased over time, which could help relieve their stress. This study offers evidence of the potential positive impact of building social robots for providing care for caregivers [ 51 ].

On the one hand, previous studies provide comprehensive and theoretical insights into the nature of work as informal caregivers, including the challenges and demands caregivers face, their needs for support, and their multiple roles and tasks during caregiving, as well as the positive and negative impacts of caregiving [ 21 , 23 , 24 , 78 ]. On the other hand, design-oriented studies also showcased the efficacy of relieving specific aspects of the caregiving burden, such as coordination and communication, through new tools and applications designed and tested in prior studies (e.g., web applications for sharing care-related messages among transient volunteers, or social robots for supporting caregivers) [ 35 , 51 , 96 ]. Arguably, previous literature tackles issues directly related to the process of caregiving and caregivers without taking into account the broader context of the ongoing care crisis, i.e., the shortage of caregivers in a rapidly aging society, and the potential for existing scalable solutions to address the challenges of caregiving.

2.3 Carework Platforms as a Sector of the Gig Economy

Given the diversity and variety of gig economy platforms, scholars have also categorized them based on different criteria [ 72 , 86 ], such as along the dimensions of the spatial dispersion of workers (local vs. global) and skill and complexity of the work (low vs. high) [ 86 ]. Prior work on gig economy platforms focuses on workers’ experiences, practices, and strategies in connection with the platforms’ sociotechnical designs [ 19 , 36 , 43 , 69 ] and issues related to platform control [ 47 , 56 , 71 ]. One systematic literature review about the sharing economy in computing identified the most salient themes in studying sharing economy in HCI venues, which include the sociotechnical design of platforms (e.g., features/designs), understanding individuals’ motivations to participate, and the social relationships/sense of community (how groups of people interact with the platform and form or communities) [ 28 ]. Researchers also presented a case study of the digital nomad community and their work and life practices in navigating issues related to geographic spaces, organizational boundaries, and community building in the gig economy era [ 77 ]. Another study revealed the values and struggles of freelancers, whose work is more knowledge-based, and their experiences with gig economy platforms such as Upwork and Fiverr [ 19 ]. Raval and Dourish argued how the immaterial labor provided by Uber and Lyft drivers is central to what makes the experiences of Uber and Lyft social, efficient, effective, and pleasurable. Through monetizing immaterial labor, the sharing economy subjugates everyday social engagements into economic life [ 69 ].

Carework platforms have been overlooked in the literature on gig economy platforms in prior HCI scholarship (and other fields) [ 34 , 79 , 80 ], with a few exceptions [ 79 , 80 ]. Ticona and Mateescu provided one of the first studies to understand carework platforms as differentiated from ridesharing platforms. They identified how carework platforms are attempting to formalize employment relationships in a traditionally informal industry by facilitating the visibility and perceptions of trustworthiness of care workers and illustrated how such “formalization” reaffirms the interests of platforms and clients while failing workers [ 80 ]. In a most recent study, Ticona examined how care workers navigate scams in three major carework platforms by developing theories of the problem of scams and seeking help from online groups [ 79 ].

In sum, literature on carework platforms has been underrepresented in gig economy-related work [ 34 , 79 , 80 ], and we know little about how older adults taking on caregiving responsibilities use gig economy platforms or carework platforms. Considering how adults aged 50 and older are not only one of the fastest-growing groups of informal caregivers but also a rapidly growing group of online technology users [ 33 , 59 , 60 ], including gig-economy platforms and services [ 1 ], it is important to understand older adults’ experience with sharing economy services, particularly carework platforms. The current study answers questions on why older caregivers use carework platforms, factors related to their frequency of using caregiving help via carework platforms, and their concerns and needs while seeking help via carework platforms through quantitative and qualitative data to fill gaps in the literature.

3 METHOD

We designed an online survey to learn more about older adults’ experiences with carework platforms and deployed the survey through a participant recruitment company Cint. Like the Qualtrics Panel, participants’ recruitment and compensation are mediated through Cint. Cint contacted potentially qualifying older adults in their panels in the United States with an e-mail containing a survey link inviting them to complete an online survey. The Cint platform compensates its participants based on a point system. The number of points gained from taking a survey is determined by the length of the survey, and the points can be redeemed as either cash, credits to use in various participating businesses, or donations to charities once reaching a certain threshold [ 4 ]. Cint charged us $ 8 per complete response based on the estimated length of the survey (20-30 minutes) and the difficulty in reaching the target population. Cint panels have been used in previous HCI studies [ 12 , 97 ]. We deployed the survey in two rounds, starting with a small-scale (n = 30) pilot test in April 2022, followed by a full-scale launch in May 2022. The purpose of the pilot test was to ensure clarity in wording and that we gathered the information we intended to collect. After the pilot test, we added two open-ended questions to the survey. We asked Cint to recruit participants who were 65 or older and who lived in the United States. The survey took about 20 minutes to complete, and we paid $ 8 per complete response. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the university where the study was conducted. Responses from participants who passed less than half of the attention checks and self-reported their data as unusable were discarded. The selection criteria for the final sample ( N = 193) included age (>=65 years old), country of residence (United States), experience using carework platforms online, and experience being an informal caregiver for the past 12 months. The final sample consisted of 193 participants. The average age of the sample was 70 ( SD = 4.23). Fifty-five percent of the sample identified as female. Eighty-six percent of the sample was White, 10% was Black or African American, 0.5% was Asian, 0.5% was American Indian or Alaska Native, 0.5% was Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, 1% identified as mixed-race, and 2% identified as “Other”. 1

3.1 Defining Carework Platforms

We defined the concept of carework platforms at the beginning of the survey to screen participants and to ensure that participants have a clear and shared understanding of the concept before answering questions about it. We used the following definition when asking participants about their experiences with carework platforms,

“We are interested in your experience using online services, websites, or mobile apps that connect you with informal, contractual, or volunteer workers that can provide one-time, short-term, or long-term caregiving help. These services include Care.com, CareLinx, Papa Pal, the Helper bees, Honor, Senior Care Online, Senior Helpers, HomeCare.com, agingcare.com, etc. Through these services, you can hire workers who can help with your daily activities, ranging from providing transportation, medication reminders, companionship, to bathing or feeding. Sometimes you can see the profile of the workers online (e.g., pictures, certifications). In these online services, websites, or mobile apps, you may have only created an account without hiring anyone, you may have hired one or multiple workers, or you may have used the same worker once or many times. We are interested in all these scenarios and more.”

To make sure participants understand the nature of help hired through these carework platforms, we also used the term “caregiving help” often in this survey. After defining carework platforms, we also defined “caregiving help” as “ By caregiving help, we mean not only help from someone who works as a caregiver, but also occasional or regular task-based assistance from someone that's not a formal caregiver to ease the demands of caregiving .”

After participants passed all screeners, we asked participants to identify the specific carework platforms they have used in the past. We curated a list of common carework platforms based on extensive online searches and results from the pilot survey. The list included Care.com, CareLinx, Papa Pal, HomeCare.com, The Helper Bees, Honor, Senior Care Online, Senior Helpers, Agingcare.com, Comfort Keepers, Home Instead, Visiting Angels, Seniors Helping Seniors, Social media (please specify), and Other (please specify). We reported the percentages of participants who mentioned using these different platforms in our Supplementary Materials.

3.2 Measures

The frequency of using caregiving help via carework platforms was measured by asking participants their frequency of using caregiving help from the caregiver(s) or worker(s) they found online in the past 12 months. Participants indicated frequency on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “Never,” 5= “Daily or Almost Daily”) ( M = 3.10, SD = 1.33). Intention to continue using caregiving help via carework platforms was measured by adapting a validated instrument that assesses individuals’ behavior intention for technology use [ 88 ]. We asked participants to indicate their agreement with three statements on a 5-point Likert scale. Sample items included “I intend to continue using an online service, website, or mobile app to find caregiving help,” and “I plan to continue to use an online service, website, or mobile app to find caregiving help” (1 = “Strongly disagree,” 5= “Strongly agree”) ( M = 3.57, SD = 1.02).

To measure participants’ perceived quality of caregiving help via carework platforms, we asked participants to identify their level of agreement with three statements, including “The caregiver(s) lacked knowledge of my needs, such as medicine, exercise, diet,” “The caregiver(s) was unreliable in providing me with care and assistance,” and “The caregiver(s) failed to support me when I needed it” on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “Strongly disagree,” 5= “Strongly agree”). We reverse-coded the three items so that the higher score indicates a better experience. The Cronbach's alpha was .91 ( M = 3.81, SD = .85).

To measure the quantity of the activities of daily living (ADLs) participants received help with via carework platforms, we asked participants to identify ADLs they have used help with. The curated list of ADLs was adapted 2 from prior literature on caring for older adults [ 46 , 67 ] and consists of 14 common functional and instrumental activities of daily living, such as getting in and out of beds and chairs, getting dressed, arranging outside services (e.g., cleaning services, doctor's appointment, meal delivery). We summed the activities to form an index of the total number of activities participants used help with ( M = 6.43, SD = 3.86).

To measure participants’ privacy concerns related to the platforms, we asked participants to rate their level of agreement with three statements related to privacy as adapted from a previously validated scale on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “Strongly disagree,” 5= “Strongly agree”) [ 95 ]. Sample statements included, “I worry that the online service/website/app may disclose my personal information to others.” The Cronbach's alpha was .94 ( M = 6.43, SD = 3.86).

We also measured participants’ internet skills using an 18-item scale adapted from the Internet Skills Scale [ 27 ]. We adopted the operational, information navigation, creative, and mobile skills subscales for measuring participants’ digital skills. Sample items included “I find the way in which many websites are designed easy to navigate,” “I find it easy to decide what the best keywords are to use for online searches,” and “I know how to download apps to my mobile device.” Participants rated each item on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “not at all true of me,” 5= “very true of me”). Scores across all items were averaged to create a measure of Internet skills. The Cronbach alpha was .93 ( M = 3.73, SD = .75).

We measured participants’ health using self-rated general health status. Participants were asked to rate their health on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “Poor,” 5= “Excellent”) ( M = 3.10, SD = 1.01).

Lastly, we measured demographic variables, including age, gender, race, education, and family income. Age was measured by asking participants about their year of birth ( M = 70.91, SD = 4.23). We asked participants to self-identify their race(s), including “White,” “Black or African American,” “American Indian or Alaska Native,” “Asian,” “Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander,” and “Other.” Because a majority (87%) of the participants identified as White, we recoded the race variable as 1 = White, 0 = non-White. We measured participants’ level of education by asking them about the highest level of school they have completed or the highest degree they have received (1 = “Less than high school degree,” 8 = “Professional degree - JD, MD”). Family income was measured by asking participants about their entire household income in (the previous year) before taxes (1 = “Less than $ 10,000,” 12 = “ $ 150,000 or more,” M = 6.50, SD = 3.14). Gender was recorded by asking participants to self-identify their gender. The answer categories included “Male,” “Female,” “Non-binary,” “Prefer to self-describe,” and “Prefer not to disclose.” Fifty-five percent of the participants identified as female, and 44% identified as male.

In addition to the quantitative data, we also collected qualitative responses in a set of open-ended questions. After reminding participants of the name(s) of the carework platforms they reported using in the past, we asked participants to describe their reasons for using the services and the ways in which these services complement their existing resources for fulfilling caregiving tasks and responsibilities. We also asked participants about their concerns when using the services and what would the ideal online services for finding caregiving help look like for them. Additionally, drawing on the critical incident technique [ 15 , 75 ], we also asked participants to describe their most significant positive experience, including particularly meaningful or memorable interaction(s) they had with the website(s)/app(s), the caregiver(s), or worker(s), as well as their most significant negative experience, including particularly bad or unpleasant interaction(s) they had with the website(s)/app(s), the caregiver(s), or worker(s). 3

3.3 Analysis

To answer the first research question, we analyzed the descriptive data regarding the name of the carework platforms, and factors related to older adults’ frequency of using caregiving help and intention to continue using caregiving help via carework platforms. We analyzed two OLS regression models with the frequency of using help ( n = 189) and intention to continue using help ( n = 188) via carework platforms as dependent variables. Listwise deletion was used to handle the missing data, given the minimal amount of missing data (with age missing one observation and intention to continue missing one observation). There were no multicollinearity issues, and all analyses were conducted in Stata 14. Results are shown in section 4.1.

To answer the second research question on how and why older caregivers use carework platforms, we used reflexive thematic analysis [ 11 ] to analyze participants’ written responses to two open-ended questions, including reasons for using the services and how these services complement their existing resources. 4 Two researchers started the process by independently reading all responses for familiarization. Both researchers noted salient ideas or patterns across the responses during the process. We then engaged in an iterative process of coding the data by grouping similar responses under initial open codes, which were used to create themes in the final analysis. The themes were continuously revised and refined throughout the analysis process as two researchers met regularly to identify (in)consistencies in the themes and resolved disagreements through rigorous discussion. The final themes are described under RQ2 in sections 4.2 and 4.3.

To answer the third research question, we used quantitative content analysis to analyze responses to the questions asking about their concerns when using the carework platforms and what would ideal carework platforms look like for them to find caregiving help. We analyzed these two questions together because the responses mirrored each other. Two researchers came up with and refined the codebook after iteratively coding two subsets of the data (20% of the data). After finalizing the codebook, two coders each coded all entries for the two questions and resolved any disagreements through discussion. We report the frequency of each type of response for concerns and ideal carework platforms in section 4.4.

In addition, we also analyzed participants’ significant positive and negative incidents when using carework platforms by engaging in the same process of thematic analysis [ 11 ] as RQ2. The themes identified are used to illustrate the specific concerns and needs participants may have for these carework platforms. The themes are reported in section 4.5.

4 RESULTS

4.1 Overall Platform Usage

Of all the carework platforms mentioned in the survey, respondents most frequently used HomeCare.com (35.75%), followed by Care.com (34.20%), Visiting Angels (31.09%), Seniors Helpers (20.73%), Seniors Helping Seniors (16.06%), Senior Care Online (15.54%), and Home Instead (14.51%). Some of the “Other” platforms they mentioned included A Place for Mom, Partners in Care, Senior Resource Alliance, and regional services, such as Aspire Medical Care. The frequency of all carework platforms mentioned by participants is presented in the Supplementary materials. We also asked participants about their frequency of using caregiving help via carework platforms in the past 12 months. A third of participants reported using caregiving help weekly, 23.32% of participants used it less than once per month, and 16.06% used it daily. Additionally, participants answered questions about the types of caregiving help they used from carework platforms. Help with housework (63.21%) was the most frequently mentioned, followed by bathing or showering (60.42%), giving medication (50.78%), and arranging outside services (49.22%) ( Figure 1 ).

Lastly, we analyzed factors related to the frequency of using caregiving help and the intention to continue using caregiving help via carework platforms. For factors related to the frequency of using caregiving help (via carework platforms), we found that the quantity of ADLs ( b = .41, p < .001), perceived quality of caregiving help ( b = .14, p < .05), and internet skills ( b = .16, p < .05) were significantly and positively related to frequency of using caregiving help (via carework platforms). For factors related to the intention to continue using caregiving help (via carework platforms), we found that frequency of using caregiving help (via carework platforms) ( b = .20, p < .05), perceived quality of caregiving help ( b = .22, p < .01), and internet skills (b = .32, p < .001) were significantly and positively related to intention to continue using caregiving help. These results suggest that when participants used the caregiving help via carework platforms more frequently, when they perceived the caregiving help to be of higher quality, and when they had better internet skills, they reported a stronger intention to continue using caregiving help via these platforms.

4.2 Motivation for Carework Platform Use

We asked open-ended questions to understand older adults’ motivations for using these platforms. Participants’ reasons for using caregiving help via carework platforms fell into four main themes: (1) finding caregiving help for a loved one, (2) finding care for specific conditions or tasks, (3) desirable features of the carework platform or care workers, and (4) recommendations by family members, doctors, and friends.

Find caregiving help for a loved one. The most common theme in responses to this question is for help caring for a loved one, such as children, siblings, in-laws, and parents. Participants sometimes cited a specific condition or mentioned the particular relationship to the person who needs care, but usually without going into details. For example, participants wrote, “I needed service for my special needs son,” “I no longer could provide all the care my wife needed, so I went looking for external help,” or “helping my elderly neighbor with her everyday needs.”

Provide care for specific conditions and tasks. In contrast to participants who answered the question on reasons for using caregiving help via carework platforms generally as hiring help, some participants went into specific details about the conditions or tasks they needed caregiving help for, such as dementia, post-surgery recovery, broken hip, or catheter care. Particularly, participants cited, “Caring for mother with vascular dementia,” “my mother had transient ischemic attacks and worsening afib-she deteriorated due to afib and overall health issues. needed help.” Participants also mentioned specific tasks they needed care for, such as “I needed occasional housekeeping support for my very sick friend.”

Desirable features of the carework platform or care workers. Another primary reason participants cited for use involves the desirable characteristics of the platform, such as an easy-to-use website, reasonable rates, convenient services, quality and variety of available caregivers, and caregivers being available locally. Participants mentioned, “They have a large pool of vetted caregivers. I can always find someone to help me,” “Easy to use and handy to have for my situation,” “Gave reasonable rates for the services they offered,” and “I find them useful in finding local support and resources.” Some participants also cited the favorable traits of caregivers as reasons for using the carework platforms, including “Compassionate, professional and caring staff,” “qualified person for care of cancer patient,” and “they are compassionate and sympathetic to my brother's needs.”

Recommendations by family members, doctors, and friends. The fourth reason participants mentioned was the positive word-of-mouth recommendation by social networks, online reviews, and medical professionals. They noted these services are “recommended to me by health care workers,” “recommended by friends having same or similar situations,” or “recommendations from family members for these sites.” Participants also said those who recommended the services mentioned them as “responsible,” “honest and caring,” and provide “compassionate and understanding helpers.”

4.3 How Carework Platforms Complement Existing Care Support

In response to the open-ended question, “Can you describe the ways in which these services complement your existing resources for fulfilling your caregiving tasks and responsibilities?” participants overwhelmingly expressed that these carework platforms provide caregiving help that allows respite in caregiving. Respondents characterized the support as a “care partner” to support a better balance of caregiving and caregivers’ personal life, work, and rest. Participants indicated that these services help balance the intense demands of being a caregiver with the pace of personal life, including working, spending time with their family, and resting. Participants also mentioned these services help with giving them a break, lightening the burden, not feeling overwhelmed, and allowing them to concentrate on other tasks such as working or handling finances. For example, participants wrote, “It allows family to live a somewhat normal life, knowing a loved one is being cared for while they are away,” “In order for me to take care of other concerns, I cannot be with my mother 24/7,” and “Better to have someone experienced with the care of loved one than taking it on alone because it is overwhelming at times.” These statements reflected both the toll of being a caregiver and the vital importance of having other people fill in when the primary caregiver cannot provide care for personal or professional reasons. In a sense, it reflects the challenges of sustaining the family caregiving system that relies on the unpaid labor of informal caregivers without outside resources.

In addition to responses that mentioned how caregiving help via carework platforms supported balancing caregiving duties and personal life in a general sense, other participants characterized the support in three specific ways: (1) an essential element of one's caregiving system, (2) an extra layer of care and backup, and (3) filling gaps in specific expertise, experience, or tasks.

An essential element of one's caregiving system. A few participants described the carework platforms as an essential element of their caregiving system. They stated, “They are essential in getting my husband moved,” “We couldn't have made it on our own,” and “Without the assistance of the two organizations, I don't think we would have survived but they have done an outstanding job.”

An extra layer of care and backup. Participants also characterized these services as an external layer of care and emergency backup. Participants referred to these services as adding another helping hand and “that extra layer of care,” because “sometimes it takes a team.” These services are a “backup to current assistant,” “reliable as a backup,” and “They fill in the time when it's hard for family members to help.” Adding these services provided additional coverage, usually on top of primary caregivers, family members, nurses, and health aides. One participant also mentioned these services as being helpful for a caregiver who is caring for someone remotely, as “I like having someone just touching base with my mother a few times a week, I live an hour away and was hoping to have someone regularly come in and just keep her company a few hours a week.”

Filling gaps in specific expertise, experience, or tasks. Most frequently, participants referred to these services as helping fill gaps with specific tasks the caregiver(s) cannot complete, such as bathing or showering, food, administering injections, transportation, and household chores. Participants wrote, “they handle chores I cannot manage alone,” “They come in & help with morning duties. light housework. Getting him up & dressed & showered,” or “They fill in with issues I have problems with myself, cleaning, transportation, etc.” Additionally, these services are also valuable to the caregivers for the care worker's experiences, advice, and knowledge in caring for someone, as “They are far more experienced in these matters than I,” and “I try to stay knowledgeable regarding my partners needs but sometimes need additional resources.”

4.4 Concerns and Needs Related to Carework Platforms

To understand older caregivers’ concerns and needs related to using carework platforms, we asked two separate open-ended questions after reminding participants of the names of the services they have used before (“Can you describe your concerns when using the services mentioned above?” and “Can you describe what the ideal online services for finding caregiving help would be like for you?”). By nature of the multiple processes in using caregiving help via carework platforms, older caregivers’ concerns and needs are multifaceted, involving both the platform and the quality of support provided by care workers. We categorized the responses by their reference to platform design, care worker on the platform, or both. We analyzed responses to these two questions together, allowing each to inform our development of a codebook (see Table 2 )that captures participants’ priorities. We describe findings regarding participants’ concerns and needs in conjunction with each other as they reflect two sides of the same issue.

The group of concerns related to care workers stood out as the most salient group. The most frequently identified concern involves the care worker's professional skills (33%). Participants raised concerns about care workers’ professional skills involving their training, experience, certifications, etc., such as “if the person is qualified to do the jobs,” “lack of knowledge regarding the medication,” and “won't do the job correctly.” These concerns reveal participants’ uncertainties about the quality of care and level of professionalism they can consistently expect from care workers hired via carework platforms. On the flip side, more than one-quarter of participants (26%) described ideal services that featured issues related to the care worker's professional skills. For example, participants wanted care workers “who are qualified to handle my needs and wants” or “someone with experience and familiarity with the problem or concerns that I am having.”

As part of the concerns about care workers on the platform, nearly one-fifth of respondents (18%) also mentioned concerns about care workers’ personal & interpersonal traits, referencing perceptions of care workers’ character traits and ability to engage in positive social interactions and build relationships with care recipients. For example, participants expressed varied concerns about not knowing whether the caregiver is “respectful,” “honest and caring,” “kind and compassionate,” “a devoted person to the patient,” and “would not abuse their authority or the person they are caring for.” When it comes to ideal services, participants (12%) mentioned finding care workers who are “caring and helpful,” “passionate and consistent,” and “reliable and capable help.”

The second most pervasive group of concerns raised by participants (27% of responses) mentioned the design of carework platforms. Participants described concerns about the overall quality of services, website usability, process of hiring help, the privacy of personal information, online reviews, cost, unfamiliarity with the service, the limited scope of services provided, etc. For example, “information being sold,” “cost,” and “I would get scammed instead of helped,” were common concerns related to the platform itself. Notably, participants mentioned privacy as concerning, albeit we did not find statistical significance in our quantitative analysis regarding its relationship with the frequency of and intention to use caregiving help, a point we revisit in our Limitation section.

Nevertheless, when it comes to ideal carework platforms, the issues related to platform design constituted the most frequently mentioned group. With the diversity and variety of available platforms, participants’ responses painted a relatively uniform view of desired platform features, functions, and user experiences. Half of the participants (50%) described an ideal service that touched on one or more aspects related to carework platforms as a whole, including “cost fits my budget, have people who are qualified to handle my needs and wants,” “prescreening and background check already done,” “one the insurance will completely pay for,” and “a quick and easy site to navigate.” Their answers on platform design cover all aspects of searching, interviewing, hiring, and paying for a care worker. Participants wanted the site to be easy to navigate, with screened and credible listings of professional care workers, provide transparent information on pricing, and be reasonably priced. Currently, sites like HomeCare.com and Visiting Angels do not list available caregivers on their sites, and Care.com does not rigorously screen care workers’ listings for their authenticity [ 40 ]. Thus, more work must be done on the platforms to align with respondents’ visions of an ideal service, such as their usability and privacy as well as how they handle screening and billing.

As part of the concerns about platform design, a smaller number of participants (7%) mentioned concerns about access to care workers, particularly stable access to consistent care workers or access to care workers outside of regular hours, despite the temporary and one-time nature of service common to carework platforms. For example, “we got different people than the one that had come before, so too many different people coming in and out.” The lack of consistency sometimes determines whether or not to use the service. As one mentioned, “we did not end up using these services, they could not provide the same person that we needed.” More participants (14%) expressed their desires for stable and accessible care workers when being asked about ideal services, such as “available for consultation night and day,” “caring for the patient 24/7 meeting their needs,” or “one that could provide 24-hour care at a reasonable price.” While carework platforms provide the flexibility of hiring any care worker at any time, our data show that a small pool of users would also appreciate platforms allowing relatively fixed arrangements.

Issues related to trust and trustworthiness concern also came up repeatedly as a critical category (19% of responses mentioned trust directly or indirectly). For example, participants stated, “it is hard to trust someone to come into your house,” “I don't trust a whole lot of people in my home,” and “I need time to build relationship so I can fully trust them.” While trust was an important concern for nearly one-fifth of respondents, fewer responses (7%) mentioned trust or trustworthiness when describing an ideal carework platform. When asked about the ideal carework platform, participants wrote, “Finding the right person you can trust for the job.” At the same time, another mentioned, “I don't know if it is possible, but it would be nice to be able to trust the caregiver,” suggesting the participants’ desire to have trustworthy caregivers is overshadowed by their reservations about finding someone they can trust, potentially due to negative past experiences. These responses again reflected participants’ desire for carework platforms to provide credible and trustworthy care workers who are already rigorously screened and can be trusted.

4.5 Significant Positive and Negative Experiences

To understand the more detailed experiences older adults had with carework platforms, we also asked participants questions about their most significant positive and negative experiences. Participants almost exclusively recalled examples that are about care workers and the way they have interacted with the care recipients instead of examples related to the carework platforms themselves. We identified two key themes based on their answers to their most positive experiences.

Care workers are experienced and professional in meeting patient needs and showing genuine care for patients. Participants cited care workers’ competence and effectiveness in providing care that met their expectations as significantly positive experiences. Some participants mentioned incidents where the care worker was effective in handling high-stake situations, “In one instance, the caregiver probably prevented at least a bad medical experience if not a tragedy,” and “My mother had a bad reaction to medication one day and caregiver was quick to call 911 for help.” Other participants mentioned how care workers were helpful when they served as educators or consultants in providing the best care. For example, participants stated, “Home Care sent someone who was extremely caring and helped me with things I didn't even know I needed,” and “They explained how to offer the assistance necessary, with a minimum of embarrassment and discomfort.” Separate from the competence of care workers, some participants mentioned how care workers are genuinely committed to improving the care recipients’ quality of life. For example, “She was friendly and showed she cared about how the situation was going for us see our loved one decline,” “Not only did they care for them they interacted with them. They were actually giving care to their patients,” and “they seem genuinely concerned for my need.”

Care workers go above and beyond expectations. In addition to care workers demonstrating competence and genuine care, another theme involves how care workers go above and beyond to provide exceptional emotional support and build and maintain rapport with patients. Participants mentioned how care workers built intimate emotional bonds with care recipients, creating positive emotions that impacted the patient and the family. One example reads, “We had the most wonderful young lady helping and caring for our mom during her last months. She was like one of the family. Mom laughed a lot and smiled most always.” Another participant stated, “There is 1 particular care giver who goes beyond, she calls him, and they talk and talk, she shows him pictures of her children and discuss parenting, she listens to his ‘when I was a boy’ stories and they laugh.” Similarly, another mentioned that “The most positive thing that stands out in my mind is when we first met the young lady from Visiting Angels. When she arrives at our home and my relative were so afraid to interact with her, and he were trying to dress himself and he wouldn't allow her. The way she took control and ease his fear it was amazing, and she went over and beyond for my relative that after then he was so relax and couldn't wait for her next visit.” Common to all these examples, the care worker engaged in intense emotional labor, which makes the care feels authentic and genuine as if it comes from a member of the family [ 41 ].

Relatedly, in responding to our question about their most negative experience, we found that participants also exclusively focused on how the care workers behaved toward care recipients. We identified the following themes common to negative experiences.

Care workers are incompetent and show a lack of care for patients. One theme that stood out is how participants described the care workers as incompetent, lazy, tardy, unresponsive, lacking experience or knowledge, or sometimes refusing to provide service. One participant wrote, “Some of the online assistants were simply lazy and did the least amount they could get away with,” “The caregiver lacked the knowledge of the specific medicine in question,” “A caregiver let my husband fall during a transfer,” or “One caregiver had lots of things she wouldn't do and spent much of her time ‘resting’ and talking on the phone.” There are multiple mentions of care workers leaving the patient unattended, which could lead to devastating consequences. Participants gave examples: “Caregiver left early, and my mother was all alone for 3 1/2 hours and could not get to a phone.” Similarly, another participant mentioned, “When I left to run an errand the last one hired left my parents alone and my mom almost started a fire. I got home just in time.”

Care workers exhibit questionable character traits or behaviors when providing care. Under this theme, participants reported care workers being dishonest and disrespectful of patients’ personal space or belongings or infantilizing older care recipients. For example, one person mentioned that “One caregiver was a thief,” and two others mentioned examples of care worker's lack of boundaries, “One gal kept packing up mom's personal items and it made mom feel like she wasn't in her own space,” and “her angel (care worker) had no boundaries and had asked inappropriate things and even for money.” Lastly, one participant mentioned how care workers could lose patience with older care recipients, “Caregivers will treat the elders like a child and at times become frustrated on how a person of this age acts like one. They then take their frustrations out on their patient.”

In summary, at their best, care workers are playing a role that's similar to someone's family members or friends, and the care being provided is an act born out of emotional bonds, trust, and reciprocity [ 84 ]. At their worst, care workers are potentially threatening the health and safety of the care recipients by being unprofessional, neglectful, or dishonest. Therefore, the decision to hire a care worker and which person to hire are decisions the caregivers and families cannot afford to take lightly due to the high stakes involved. Our findings further pointed to the vital and sometimes unfulfilled role platforms can play in helping older caregivers find the right care worker for their needs. Considering how these critical incidents sway participants’ perceptions of carework platforms as a whole, we argue that the main implication for platform design is to optimize the platforms’ process of matching users with appropriate care workers.

5 DISCUSSION

The emergence of carework platforms in the 21 st century, which act as a broker to monetize the process of giving and receiving care, is not a new phenomenon. Individuals and families with the means to hire paid care have been doing so for centuries through various domestic service or employment agencies. Following the ethos of the movement of the marketization of care [ 85 ] and the casualization of work [ 22 , 30 ], carework platforms prioritize efficiency, choice, and the fungibility of care workers [ 34 ]. Given that older adults are both increasingly active online [ 1 , 65 ] and regularly serving as informal caregivers [ 60 ], understanding their use of carework platforms is an essential and timely area of study.

In contrast to pervasive narratives of older adults as late technology adopters and feeling anxious with technologies [ 58 , 65 , 91 ], our data show that older adults are becoming quickly engaged in using gig-economy-based services for caregiving support and integrating such services into their broader systems of care. Our analysis provides foundational evidence of how older caregivers use and perceive support provided through a variety of carework platforms. We find that participants with higher levels of internet skills, used a larger quantity of ADLs help, and perceived the caregiving help to be of higher quality, also used caregiving help via carework platforms more frequently and had a stronger intention to continue using these platforms. That is, older adults who are already having success using these platforms are likely to continue their use to find caregiving support. In sum, the quantitative findings confirmed that the quantity and quality of care caregivers receive matter regarding their current and future usage of the platform, as do the caregiver's digital skills. Our qualitative findings detail older caregivers’ perspectives on carework platforms and the workers themselves, including their reasons for using these services, concerns and needs, and what they perceive as salient positive and negative experiences. We build on our findings below as we discuss how gig workers supplement existing care work, the importance of nurturant and nonnurturant care, and how future research and design on carework platforms can reach other groups of older adults.

5.1 Supplementing Care with Gig Workers via Carework Platforms

Carework platforms are sociotechnical in nature as they combine technology with human care services. Not only do they provide interfaces to search for and contact care workers, which must be usable and accessible, but they also play a role in the success of human caregiving work through their ability to screen workers and match care recipients with caregivers. We outline three major design implications for carework platforms connected to our findings below, reflecting the various sociotechnical processes associated with using these platforms. By answering the question of how and why older caregivers use caregiving help via carework platforms, we found that caregivers in our study reported being attracted to carework platforms for a variety of reasons, including filling unmet needs for caregiving help (either generally or for specific conditions), favorable features of the carework platforms or care workers (either the usability of the platform or quality of its workers), and word-of-mouth recommendations from physicians and social contacts. Once caregivers employ care workers, they view them as playing a range of roles in their broader system of care. Most older caregivers in our sample view care workers as care partner that shares the tasks and responsibilities. This care partnership allows older caregivers to better balance caregiving and care for their own needs. Specifically, in serving as care partners, care workers can be essential to one's caregiving system and/or provide an extra layer of care and backup. For others, care workers provided complementary support related to specific expertise, experience, or tasks, filling critical gaps or needs for older caregivers.

In light of the varied role care worker plays, we first propose that carework platforms could explore the possibility of care workers acting as educators or consultants in helping families care for specific conditions. Aging in place means that many medically necessary and highly specialized tasks are now being performed at home by family members rather than trained nurses or medical staff [ 38 ]. Care workers can function as educational resources and help keep family members updated with the best care practices for certain commonly occurring conditions, such as surgery recovery, physical therapy, or dementia. The variety of ways caregivers use these carework platforms show the flexibility and freedom caregivers enjoy by combining on-demand paid caregiving help with their efforts as unpaid, informal caregivers, thus easing their overall burden of care.

Secondly, based on answers to the concerns and needs of older caregivers (sections 4.4 & 4.5), we also propose that platforms could play a bigger role in screening care workers by imposing more stringent background checks on care workers, and only listing screened, professional, and trustworthy care workers. Considering the stakes involved in hiring the ‘right’ care worker for a person's needs, respondents suggested a need for more stringent background checks on the care worker and potentially a face-to-face or virtual video interview before any hiring decisions are facilitated via carework platforms. The burden of judging the authenticity of a listing or the legitimacy of a worker on Care.com often falls on the already over-burdened families or caregivers, contradicting expectations of paying for a premium membership to access workers [ 40 ]. There have been instances when Care.com did not screen listings for their authenticity, leading to devastating consequences for families [ 40 ]. Nevertheless, for caregivers who only use the care workers occasionally for emergencies or specific tasks as they arise, the current gig-economy-based platform that allows flexibility in scheduling and freedom in choosing whoever is available seems to be working well, filling a critical need.

Third, we propose that carework platforms should allow fixed and recurring arrangements with specific care workers. While the flexibility and freedom of these services are helpful in many situations, challenges arise when caregivers want more regular access to consistent, known caregivers. Once a caregiver found a care worker who was a good fit for their needs, they wanted to be able to access this person more regularly, which may or may not be possible through the platform 5 . For caregivers who use carework platforms as a core part of their caregiving system, carework platforms could consider allowing caregivers to book the same care workers routinely at a fixed time, ensuring the consistency of access to specific care workers who are a good fit for the family or individual. As scholars have argued how carework platforms differ from ridesharing platforms in their efforts to formalize employment relationships through ratings and reviews that increase worker visibility and trustworthiness, adding these features that indicate formal arrangements may be another way to increase the worker credibility on the platform [ 80 ].

5.2 Seeking Nurturant and Nonnurturant Care

One challenge with the on-demand nature of carework platforms is that caregivers may not have time to form relationships with care recipients or their families. However, the critical difference between carework platforms and ridesharing or freelancing platforms may be the extreme importance of the interpersonal nature of home-based service work [ 34 ]. Gig-economy platforms have relied upon the interchangeability of workers in providing cheap labor with a high turnover rate [ 94 ]. However, such impersonality can be problematic in the context of care work. In a largely informal and underregulated industry, the success and quality of care work have long been tied to the identity and character traits of the caregivers themselves [ 6 , 34 ]. Scholars proposed three ‘C’’s to characterize the quality of care work, including care (caring for others in a vulnerable state), concern (which sustains intimacy and trust), and connection (affectional bonds even when a transaction is involved) [ 48 ]. Duffy has also distinguished between nurturant and nonnurturant care work, with nurturant care referring to intimate, relational labor that often takes place in a face-to-face setting. In contrast, nonnurturant care refers to labor that undergirds nurturant care but may not involve any relational aspects, such as the work of janitorial staff or housekeepers [ 31 ]. Nonnurturant care is often physical or does not involve direct relationships with care recipients. Arguably, the on-demand and flexible nature of the gig-economy-based gig work seems well-suited for providing nonnurturant care in many instances, primarily due to the lack of duration and consistency required for deeper relationship building between care recipients and care workers for nurturant care [ 31 ].

Yet, our findings indicate that older caregivers value both nurturant and nonnurturant care, as they highlight care workers’ personal and interpersonal traits and professional skills as top priorities. Nurturant care requires the care worker to build personal relationships with the care recipient and be seen in the context of a caring relationship as a close proxy of someone's family member or close friend. When caregivers reported their most significant positive experiences with these services, they frequently reported personal experiences with the care workers, not about the carework platforms. Caregivers recalled positive interactions with care workers who were not only professional and experienced but also went above and beyond in providing emotional support for the care recipient – echoing the dual importance of nurturant and nonnurturant care. Negative experiences often referenced care workers who were incompetent and showed a lack of care for patients, particularly workers who showed questionable character traits or behaviors. Caregivers have the most positive experience when care workers provide both types of care well. Relating to previous work on ridesharing platforms and how drivers often provide immaterial labor to build relationships with riders to make the experience efficient and pleasant [ 69 ], we conclude that the current platforms are gaining value and reputation in spite of the undervalued and unrewarded nurturant care carried out by particular care workers. In a way, while platforms advertise their ability to provide quality care, care workers who go above and beyond are being failed by the platforms as they are not rewarded for the emotional labor that is key to quality care.

It is critical for carework platforms to recognize that the quality of care work is a combination of “human relationships, skilled intervention, and plain hard work [ 31 ]”, and properly selecting care workers who adequately reflect these traits are crucial to determining how potential users perceive the value in carework platforms’ services. That is, screening mechanisms and matching algorithms could better support caregivers’ needs for workers to provide both nurturant and nonnurturant care. However, carework platforms in their current forms are not designed to reward care workers’ provision of nurturant care and are not intended to select care workers who are particularly suitable for providing nurturant care to care recipients who may need or desire it. Relating to caregivers’ wishes for platforms to impose more stringent background checks in section 5.1, finding quality care workers also demands moving beyond the standard practice of basic background checks [ 18 ] and potentially assessing personality or interpersonal skills that affect one's success in care work. Platform companies could improve their efficiency in screening and matching the right care workers with families by incorporating the screening of candidates’ ability to provide nurturant care. Platforms could consider administering psychometrically valid scales related to one's ability to be a caregiver, such as the Preparedness for Caregiving Scale [ 5 ], the Caregiver Competence Scale [ 64 ], and Caregiver Communication Competence Scale [ 20 ]. More holistic screening and matching procedures beyond basic criminal or motor vehicle records might help counteract the challenges associated with worker fungibility and short-term work agreements that are core to gig economy platforms and services.

5.3 Older Adults as Active Users of Carework Platforms

Care work is the foundation of every society and economy and can be defined as “the maintenance of people, environments, and communities” [ 45 ]. HCI and CSCW scholarship has focused on the labor of care and how to support caregiving processes, particularly within health contexts [ 21 , 23 , 35 ]. Older adults, however, are often positioned as recipients of care in technology-related research rather than the ones shouldering the burden of being a caregiver [ 87 , 89 ]. Brewer and colleagues have recently argued that older adults serve as active caregivers on social media through non-engagement (e.g., not clicking or not commenting) for relationship maintenance and conflict prevention [ 13 ]. More work along these lines has started to challenge ingrained assumptions about older adults, including the outdated narrative around older adults as Luddites, the frailty and vulnerability of the older adult population as a monolith, and older adults being passive users than active participants online [ 13 , 29 , 52 , 76 , 89 ]. Part of this shift requires viewing older adults’ as active and engaged online participants and recognizing the growing trend of older adults serving as caregivers, which is at the core of our analysis.

Older adults reported many concerns and needs related to the platform itself and the processes and transactions involved in hiring a care worker. For example, participants noted the usability of the platform, the accuracy associated with platforms’ efforts in matching care workers with clients, the procedures platforms adopted for screening care workers, the processes platforms follow in facilitating the hiring of the care workers, pricing, whether or not to accept specific insurance as payment, and the privacy and safety of personal information. In sum, older caregivers wish for the platforms to be easier to use, automatically match clients with care workers with precision, allow in-person meetings between workers and clients for some hiring decisions, cost less, or work with insurance to pay for their services. Older caregivers are also worried about being scammed, similar to care workers who reported such concerns in a previous study [ 79 ]. Notably, a small percentage of older caregivers expressed no concerns about the current platform services or found it ideal, indicating that some carework platforms have met a subset of older caregivers’ needs in their current form.

While the present study provides a new focus on how older caregivers use a novel online technology, it is only a first step. Our sample is predominantly white, relatively affluent, active online, and most likely able-bodied, neglecting other older caregivers who may not use carework platforms due to lower internet skills, accessibility barriers, cost structures, or even lack of awareness. For the carework platform to fulfill its promises and become an integral element in the system of care among more older adults, more cooperation among platform companies, federal and local governments, insurance companies, and individual users are essential. Carework platforms could make their products easier to use by incorporating accessibility guidelines [ 44 ], which have the potential to reach older caregivers with disabilities, as well as provide more clarity for all users on processes of screening, pricing, and caregiver availability. As older adults frequently mentioned concerns about costs, government policies that cover costs incurred on platforms or simplify the process of insurance claims for such costs could be especially beneficial for lower-income older adults.

Lastly, the ongoing care crisis cannot be ignored or singlehandedly fixed by carework platforms. As carework platforms compete for labor force with other service industry sectors, they also have an opportunity to advocate for care workers and play a significant role in revamping how home care work has long been viewed, with low pay and stereotypes of being “unskilled and undemanding” [ 31 ]. Such revamping could increase the quality of care and its user base by reducing the shortage of care workers and encouraging more people to choose home care as a viable career.

Future work could consider employing focus groups and interviews among a more diverse set of caregivers to understand better caregivers’ attitudes towards and use of carework platforms. Additionally, we limited our sample to older adults who self-identified as informal caregivers, leaving open questions about how older adults may use these same services to care for themselves or even earn income as care workers on these platforms.

5.4 Limitations

The current study has several limitations. First, while surveys allow us to reach a broader sample of older adults, the nature of the survey method limits its ability to elicit lengthier, in-depth, contextual, and reflective comments that participants may have about carework platforms for the open-ended questions. While our mixed-method survey is informative as an exploratory effort, future work could complement our findings with more qualitative methods, such as interviews and contextual inquiries, to generate a deeper understanding of how older adults as caregivers are using the carework platforms.

Second, while privacy concern was not a statistically significant factor for predicting the frequency of and intention to use caregiving help in our quantitative analysis, it has appeared in our qualitative data to be a concern for older caregivers. Since privacy concerns have shown to be a major factor in older adults’ usage of various online services and devices [ 59 , 87 ], our quantitative finding could reflect the relatively small sample size as the study may be underpowered. Alternatively, we also noticed that privacy concerns are twofold in the context of hiring help via carework platforms in our data, including concerns about platforms’ protection of personal information and care workers’ discretion in protecting care recipient's home and body privacy, considering the sometimes intimate and sensitive nature of their work. Our quantitative measurement only reflected the first type of privacy concern; however, the latter concern was more frequently mentioned in our qualitative data. Future work could better discriminate the two types of privacy concerns when surveying older caregivers about carework platforms.

Third, considering that we conducted an online survey with a convenience sample, we reached older adults who are more likely to have higher literacy for using the Internet for information seeking and services. Their attitudes toward carework platforms may be more favorable than peers who lack similar literacy and skills. As we mentioned in our Discussion, future work in this area could reach older adults who may not be able to access these online services as efficiently and examine their needs and priorities.

Lastly, our survey was designed and deployed in early 2022, after a period of dramatic changes in people's needs for and access to medical care, home care, and digital technologies brought about by the global COVID-19 pandemic [ 1 , 92 ]. As the older population suffered significantly in mortality due to their vulnerability against the virus [ 25 , 26 ], as well as broad-scaled news reporting on how nursing homes mishandled the virus [ 39 , 63 ], it is crucial to recognize that older caregivers’ adoption of the carework platforms may reflect the impacts of this historical event. Future work could explore whether and how older caregivers’ perceptions of and attitudes towards carework platforms shift as we head into the post-pandemic era.

6 CONCLUSION

As the care crisis continues to develop alongside a rapidly aging population in the United States and other developed nations [ 49 , 50 , 53 ], there is an urgent question about who can and will provide this care. Care has traditionally been viewed as a part of the gift economy with the expectation that care will be provided by family members, at least in part, as a “gift,” underlying policies that limit public benefits to support paid caregiving for older adults [ 84 ]. The COVID-19 pandemic has revealed the pitfalls of providing long-term care in nursing homes and prompted more people to choose to age in place. More is demanded of families for providing such care, and caregiving at home for some is simply infeasible without external support [ 92 ]. Carework platforms have also emerged in other countries, such as Germany and Australia, in light of similar shortages of caregivers. While there are inherent social, cultural, and economic differences across different societies, our study could provide an indirect understanding of caregivers’ reasons, concerns, needs, and priorities when using such platforms in other countries [ 55 , 61 ].

Carework platforms provide a potentially promising venue for addressing some of the care deficits. Our study demonstrates that paid caregiving has, in part, become necessary, as older caregivers reported unmet care needs, and some reported using it as an indispensable part of their caregiving system. However, our findings also revealed that while carework platforms may be adequate for nonnurturant care work, they are constrained by the short-term and transactional nature of gig work that makes them ill-equipped to provide nurturant care. Currently, there is an inherent and unresolved tension between the long-term relationship-building needed for high-quality care work and the flexible and one-off nature of gig work. Through understanding factors related to older caregivers’ usage of caregiving help via carework platforms, how and why older caregivers are using these platforms, and their concerns and needs, we proposed ways to design carework platforms to better support older caregivers through improving platforms’ screening mechanisms and matching algorithms and making the platform more accessible to older people who may not be tech-savvy or able-bodied.
