There is a trend for handcrafting bespoke prostheses that embody their wearers’ aesthetic tastes and identities. We explore how this might be extended by enabling users to co-design with algorithms. We report a design-led exploration (Figure 1 ) in which professional disabled dancers danced with a generative design algorithm to create personalised designs called aesthetic seeds. Further algorithms applied these to prosthetic greaves, rendering them in various materials before optimising for additive manufacture. Interviews with our dancers revealed that the aesthetics of prosthetics reach beyond visual decoration to encompass form, function, bodily experience, body image, and identity; that interactions with generative design algorithms can harness people's expressive and aesthetic skills; and that we must redesign supporting technologies for diverse bodies. We generalise our findings into a process for how people may co-design 3D printable products with algorithms.

1 INTRODUCTION

Traditionally, prostheses were treated as medical devices that were prescribed to help individuals overcome what were seen as disabilities. They were primarily designed for function and comfort and were expensive and long-lived, remaining with their wearers even as their bodies and needs changed [ 48 , 49 , 72 ]. Aesthetics were a secondary concern at best and when they were considered, the focus was typically on trying to recreate the appearance of stereotypical human limbs to hide away any perceived disability.

Much has changed over recent years. The aesthetics of prostheses have come to the fore and broadened, with some wearers choosing to reveal or celebrate them, sometimes in ways that make powerful statements about the nature of diverse bodies [ 18 , 39 , 78 ]. In turn, new manufacturing technologies, notably additive manufacturing, establish the possibility of cheaply and rapidly producing prostheses on demand, suggesting a future in which they might become fashion items that could be created and worn to suit particular activities or moods.

While promising, these developments raise the challenge of how best to personalise prostheses. How might their wearers shape and decorate them with designs that express their personalities, tastes and identities? Do we expect prosthetic wearers to become skilled graphic and product designers, using CAD packages to create designs that can drive today's additive manufacturing process, or might there be more expressive and creative ways of approaching personalisation?

We report on a design-led exploration of enabling people to aesthetically personalise their prostheses by interacting with algorithms. We began by creating an interactive generative design algorithm for sculpting aesthetic forms. Early testing inspired us to engage professional disabled dancers to create personal designs by dancing. We developed further algorithms to apply the resulting ‘aesthetic seeds’ to the form of a lower-limb prosthetic greave (a prosthetic cover), and then to optimise the result for efficient additive manufacturing. Discussions with the dancers revealed how the aesthetics of prosthetics runs more than skin deep, reaching beyond decorative appearance to include form, function, impact on embodied experience, and statements about body image and identity. They also revealed how enabling technologies such as motion capture embody normative assumptions about human bodies. Our overarching contribution is to generalise a co-design process through which people can use their expressive skills to aesthetically personalise 3D printed products by interacting with algorithms.

2 RELATED WORK

We weave together multiple strands of research spanning prosthetics, generative design, aesthetics, and designing for diverse bodies.

2.1 Prosthetics

According to [ 27 ], prosthetic limbs must meet three criteria: an operational need to support the body's functions, a social need to enable social activities, and a visible need related to the appearance of embodied wholeness. Millstein et al. suggest that for a prosthesis to be accepted by users it must be comfortable, functional, and attractive [ 46 ]. A person's acceptance of a prosthesis may be influenced by its look, while aesthetically appealing designs may increase psychological well-being [ 10 , 13 , 52 ]. More generally, visual aesthetics have been shown to positively impact user acceptance in other areas of assistive device design including hearing aids [ 54 ] and scoliosis braces [ 40 ].

But what is an appropriate aesthetic? There are two broad schools of thought. One maintains that prostheses should appear as natural as possible [ 20 ]. Murray [ 49 ] and Sousa et al.[ 72 ] found that a realistic-looking passive limb pleased female participants more than a less realistic but functional prosthetic limb. They argued that prostheses that give the appearance of a healthy physique improve aesthetic appeal as the individual believes they conform to the socially acceptable body image [ 72 ]. The other perspective argues that prostheses need not simulate realistic limbs, and that alternative designs can assist people in promoting their identities [ 30 , 39 , 79 , 86 ]. Hilhorst describes designing personalised prosthetic hands for children to enhance their sense of identity [ 31 ]. Hall & Orzada argue that the wearers of distinctive non-realistic prostheses are reclaiming and reframing the disability identity by fostering pride and positivity in diverse bodies [ 27 ]. Elite athletes and the paralympic movement have done much to promote a positive image of disability and of a stylish, sporty, and clearly non-realistic aesthetic [ 82 ].

There is an evolving relationship between prostheses and fashion. Pullin argued that medical engineering should draw on fashion design [ 55 ] while Vainshtein claimed that fashion design successfully generates new visual languages that can break barriers of invisibility traditionally associated with disabled bodies [ 84 ]. Drawing on the disability and fashion studies literature, Hall & Orzada argue that expressive prosthetic limbs that reflect the wearer's identity can reduce stigmatisation and increase self-confidence [ 27 ]. Vlachaki at al. report that expressive prostheses, customised to highlight users’ identities, increase self-confidence [ 86 ]. Pioneering design companies have been responding to such ideas through the creation of bespoke designer prostheses and covers [ 30 , 39 , 79 , 88 ].

It can be beneficial to directly involve wearers in the design of these fashionable prostheses. Blom explored the role of co-design in establishing artisanship in prosthetic aesthetics, focusing on the co-design of a prosthetic greave, a traditional item worn on the shin that in part fulfils a decorative function [ 23 ]. Insights from wearers revealed that being involved in the co-design process led to a deeper, richer expression of individual identity [ 11 ]. We apply our approach to the co-design of greaves below.

Finally, there has been interest in applying additive manufacturing to replace the traditional plaster moulding of prostheses which is widely seen as being laborious and time-consuming [ 4 ]. Wang et al.’s review of additive manufacturing of prostheses and orthotics highlights the twin challenges of designing for comfort and function and the lack of a systematic framework for integrating the technologies involved [ 87 ]. Their proposed framework generates finite element models of the target human limb (through scanning) and the prosthesis that extends this, using a biometric simulation tool to assess joint contact pressure, stress in tissues, loading transfer mechanism and pressure on contact interfaces. In what follows, we also introduce a framework for the systematic digital design of prosthesis, but in our case focusing on aesthetic factors and working with generative design algorithms.

Even this brief summary of the literature reveals that prostheses are increasingly personalised to express their wearers’ identities, may assume diverse appearances with varying degrees of realism, and might potentially become fashion items as well as medical devices. This motivates us to explore how digital technologies can enable people to personalise different aspects of their prostheses in a way that accommodates a variety of tastes and styles, both realistic and non-realistic, and that can also effectively drive 3D printing processes.

2.2 Generative design [ 44 ]

While there is growing interest in personalised prostheses, the cost of engaging an artisan designer lies beyond the means of many. A potential solution may be found in generative design algorithms that can quickly create design variants, often by imitating nature's evolutionary process [ 45 ]. Generative design has been explored for architecture [ 15 , 26 , 58 , 66 , 71 ], graphic layout [ 16 ], consumer electronics [ 41 ], jewellery [ 37 ], interface design [ 83 ], everyday products [ 2 ], and interactive decorative patterns [ 53 ]. Generative tools have been used for some time in the early stages of designing various products, services, systems and facilities [ 61 , 62 , 63 , 85 ]. Evolutionary generative algorithms have been applied to product design [ 9 , 25 , 34 ], for example for lamp holders [ 42 ], mobile phones [ 80 ], wine glasses [ 77 ] and vases [ 67 ]. The proponents of generative design identify potential benefits as being improved performance, reduced development time, increased creativity, increased efficiency, and customised product development which is our primary interest here [ 45 ].

However, generative design also raises difficult challenges. One of these concerns is the relationship between algorithms and human designers. Stones & Cassidy focus on computers enabling human designers to explore solutions that go beyond what they might normally imagine [ 76 ]. Mitchell referred to this as ‘digitally mediated design’; the computer is not a replacement for the designer, but neither is it equivalent to a traditional passive tool [ 47 ]. A second challenge concerns how to involve the consumer. Sanders considers generative tools/toolkits as a form of participatory design language that can be used by non-designers early in a design project [ 60 ]. However, appropriate tools must be provided if the consumer is to become a member of the design team as an ‘expert of his/her experiences’ [ 85 ]. Responding to this latter challenge, our focus is on creating a suite of tools that enable consumers to become co-designers of their own products. This involves finding ways in which they can meaningfully interact with generative algorithms and then embedding this within a wider co-design process.

2.3 Aesthetics

While the study of aesthetics often focuses on appearance, visual style and beauty, other factors come into play when making aesthetic judgements. Consideration of aesthetics within HCI has included the aesthetics of interaction as extending beyond the appearance of objects to include the sense of experience and expression that arises from interacting with them [ 51 ]. Baljko & Tenhaaf present the aesthetics of emergence as a theoretical perspective on the aesthetics of interaction, focusing on how co-constructed interactions occur when there is shared agency between user and system [ 6 ]. Dalsgaard & Hansen propose considering the perspectives of operators, performers and spectators as part of the aesthetics of staging interactions [ 17 ]. Redström introduces ‘tangled interaction’ to express the relationship between appearance and functionality in the aesthetics of interaction [ 57 ]. Hallnäs & Redström introduce the concept of the ‘expressional’ as an aesthetic foundation for how computational things can be the bearers of certain expressions, just as appliances are the bearers of functionality [ 28 ]. Wright et al. focus on feelings and emotions as aesthetically important facets of interaction, highlighting the importance of sense making [ 89 ].

Our interest in prosthetics and dance brings into focus the aesthetics of bodily experience. While historical accounts have often treated aesthetic experience as being something of the mind that stands apart from the everyday world of the body, contemporary scholars have argued for the inherently embodied nature of aesthetic experience, often drawing on the pragmatic aesthetics of Dewey [ 19 ]. Xenakis & Arnellos argue for aesthetic experience as being fundamentally bodily and emotional, involving sense-making through our bodily interactions with our environments [ 90 ]. Brinck argues that aesthetic experience is enacted and skillful, involving sense-making of movement [ 12 ]. Shusterman's practical philosophy of somaesthetics promotes a holistic mind-body approach to training one's aesthetic appreciation of bodily experience [ 69 ] and has inspired the approach of soma design for embodied interactions [ 32 ].

With specific reference to dancing, our approach mirrors Hsueh et al.’s exploration of the dynamic relations between the moving body and interactive technology during the creative process of movement ideation [ 33 ]. Building on the idea “kinaesthetic creativity” that describes the body's ability to enact alternate future possibilities via movement [ 59 ], they classified dancers’ interactions with a dynamic system as being directed, co-creative, following, negotiating, control-based or expressive.

Finally, returning to prostheses, Tamari reflects on how the visibility of disabled athletes and their prostheses at the London 2012 Paralympic Games brought to the fore the relationship between elite Paralympians and advanced prosthetic technology, arguing that the modern discourse of prostheses has shifted from the made-up and camouflaged body to the empowered and exhibited body, leading to the idea of prosthetic aesthetics as negotiating a tension between the two “polarized sensitivities" of attractiveness/coolness and abjection/the uncanny [ 82 ]. In defining the concept of disability aesthetics , Siebers argues that disability provides a critical framework for questioning aesthetics, is worthy of representation in its own right, and that “good art incorporates disability” [ 70 ]. We build on these notions of prosthetic aesthetics and disability aesthetics, and also HCI's wider interest in the aesthetics of interaction, by introducing an approach whereby disabled dancers are empowered to decorate and reveal their prostheses in a way that reflects their identities.

2.4 Designing for diverse bodies

There is a growing discourse in HCI concerning designing interactive technologies for diverse bodies. Spiel's feminist content analysis of selected papers from the Tangible, Embedded and Embodied Interaction conference concluded that ‘artefacts are implicitly oriented on unmarked bodily norms, while technologies designed for diverse bodies treat those as deviant and in need of correction’ [ 73 ]. Analysis of fitness trackers revealed how they “re-affirm the fitness of already fit people and can have an adversarial effect on others” [ 74 ] and how they require additional effort to be used by wheelchair users [ 14 ]. Even laudable attempts to recognise the diversity of bodies when designing technologies for women's health may miss out on discussions of race, class, sexuality and cultural contexts [ 36 ].

Research into assistive technologies has considered the question of to what extent they are made visible and how this relates to stigma. Faucett et al. undertook a meta-analysis of research examining the visibility of disability, highlighting visibility as a key tension for the design of assistive technologies [ 21 ]. Shinohara and Wobbrock's diary study of the experience of assistive devices in daily life concluded that social accessibility, which in part relates to visibility, may be as important as functional accessibility and proposed Design for Social Accessibility as a design stance “focused on balancing an AT [Assistive Technology] user's sociotechnical identity with functional requirements.” [ 68 ]. Looking beyond HCI, dance researchers exploring the artistic use of medical biosensors reported an example in which a motion capture technology effectively rendered a user with Parkinson's disease invisible because he was unable to stand still for the system to properly calibrate itself [ 50 ]. Our stance is to enable people to aesthetically tailor different aspects of their prostheses to be more or less visible according to their tastes and so negotiate these complex issues for themselves.

3 METHOD

We followed a research-through-design approach in which generalisable knowledge emerges from a practice-led process of designing, making and reflecting on various kinds of products [ 24 , 91 ]. We engaged dancers as co-designers, involving them in a process of ‘inquiry and imagination’ [ 75 ]. While co-design emphasises users’ input it does not entirely put the user in the position of designer [ 43 , 61 ], but rather requires that the designer analyse and translate acquired knowledge into design-relevant information [ 61 ]. Our process involved moving back and forth between design-led technical innovations and dancer-led responses to these. This required us to balance our own technical ideas with facilitating, listening to, and observing our co-designers [ 65 ].

Participants. We recruited two professional disabled dancers and two dance researchers. The dancers, referred to as T and W, were both female with many years of training and professional experience in expressing themselves through improvised bodily movements. Both had high amputations on one leg. They were compensated using industry daily rates recommended by their professional body. The two dance researchers, S and K, were also experienced dance practitioners. They recruited the dancers and contributed insights into dance and disability from their research. K is also a disabled artist researcher with lived experience of disability. The relatively small number of external participants was due to the scarcity of and demand for professional disabled dancers, combined with the effects of the global COVID pandemic which halted our plans for hosting a larger event to gather feedback from the wider dance community. However, engaging even just a few professionals in our design process proved highly illuminating, revealing unanticipated insights as we report below.

Design process. Our research unfolded over an eighteen-month period, beginning with a period of technical exploration that led to the creation of an interactive generative algorithm called Mogrow. Early experimentation with Mogrow inspired the idea of interacting by dancing, leading us to recruit the dance researchers who in turn recruited the professional dancers. Our first joint workshop occurred in person and explored creative interactions with Mogrow, delivering a set of designs that we came to refer to as aesthetic seeds. A second period of technical development then established further algorithms to apply these seeds to the form of prosthetic greave and to optimise the results for 3D printing, delivering a portfolio of personalised prostheses designs. COVID intervened at this point, introducing some delay while the dancers were unavailable and driving us to collaborate online. Our second joint workshop involved the entire team discussing the final designs and reflecting on the wider process. This was preceded by a planning session with the dance researchers. In the following, Workshop 2.a refers to this planning session while Workshop 2.b refers to the final workshop involving the whole team. As the process evolved and a tight-knit team emerged, so our roles shifted. The HCI researchers moved from initially being technology designers to becoming co-design facilitators. In turn, both dance researchers and professionals moved from being ’users’ to being co-designers, and ultimately co-researchers (with all four appearing as authors of the paper).

Data capture and analysis. We audio recorded and transcribed the discussions from the three workshops. We followed an inductive approach to coding that proceed in five iterations: (i) the HCI researchers analysed the transcript of the first workshop, generating an initial mindmap of tentative themes, quotes and example interactions; (ii) this was shared and discussed with the two dance researchers in workshop 2.a; (iii) refined themes and examples were then discussed with the two professional dancers in workshop 2.b and the ensuing conversation was transcribed; (iv) the HCI researchers analysed this second transcript, further refining and thickening the themes; (v) the findings and themes were written up in the paper which was read and approved by all parties. Themes concerning visual aesthetics and designing for disabled bodies were evident from the initial workshop, though were greatly expanded throughout subsequent discussions. The insights that aesthetics might extend to other matters such as form, materials and even the optimisation of 3D printing emerged from the later workshops, as did our generalised account of the co-design process.

3.1 Co-designing aesthetic seeds by dancing with Mogrow

3.1.1 The Mogrow generative design algorithm. Our design process began with a period of creative technical exploration during which the HCI researchers developed and experimented with an initial generative algorithm. One idea that emerged early on was that the wearers of prostheses would directly interact with our algorithm to sculpt designs. This led us away from the evolutionary approach that has been popular in the literature as discussed earlier towards an algorithm that could be more readily and transparently controlled in real-time. For this, we took inspiration from forms that have been sculpted by nature and that exhibit a rich and organic feeling of visual complexity, for example, rock formations have been weathered into complex forms by wind and water.

Our technical response was Mogrow, an implementation of a particle-spring system, a widely-used technique for creating physical simulations [ 38 ]. Particle-spring systems are based on lumped masses, called particles, which are connected by linear elastic springs, each of which is assigned a constant axial stiffness, an initial length, and a damping coefficient. Springs generate a force when displaced from their rest length. Besides the internal force from springs, particles can also be influenced by external forces, such as a vertical downward force (simulating gravity), a horizontal force (simulating wind) or attractive and repulsive forces (simulating the impacts of external objects). When these external forces are continually changing, the system becomes dynamic, exhibiting a daedal behaviour that produces intricate and complex designs.

Mogrow works with closed chains of springs as shown in Figure 2 . Multiple particles in the chain can be designated as nozzles, points at which new particles can be injected which leads to greater wrinkling. The opposite process is shrinking in which particles are removed which has the effect of smoothing out wrinkles. Varying the rates of injecting and shrinking via multiple nozzles creates a dynamic, complex and unpredictable wrinkling effect that defines Mogrow's distinct aesthetic style.

A further parameter of Mogrow is friction, which further enriches the behaviour of the particle-spring system, bringing more flexibility to interact with Mogrow. New particles are injected onto the chain with an initial velocity that decreases to zero at a rate that is determined by friction. A high value of friction causes particles to accumulate close to nozzles, resulting in more compact wrinkling. Dynamically varying friction radically shapes wrinkling behaviour over time as shown in Figure 3 .

The second aspect of Mogrow involves stacking. This periodically freezes the state of the closed chain, vertically stacking the results as a series of slices to gradually grow a 3D form from the ground up. The effect is that the user is able to sculpt a slowly growing wrinkly 3D form. Figure 4 shows a vase-like form (rendered in a neutral material here for ease of visualisation-see later for a discussion of materials), that was created by varying rates of injection and shrinking throughout the freezing and stacking process. At the base is an initial circular chain. Early on, injection increases the diameter and produces more and more wrinkles. Later, near the “neck”, shrinking narrows the chain and reduces wrinkling. Finally, at the top, increasing injection once again opens the top and causes greater wrinkling.

3.1.2 Interacting with Mogrow. Our next challenge was how best to dynamically manipulate injection, shrinking and friction to sculpt organic-looking forms. We initially created a conventional direct manipulation interface comprising buttons and sliders to experiment with Mogrow during its development. However, this felt overly complex and technical as an interface for prostheses wearers, requiring them to understand and focus on Mogrow's technical details rather than on the creative acts of aesthetic personalisation. At this point, our experimentation with Mogrow inspired the idea of interacting by dancing, which we felt might be a suitably expressive act. This led us to focus on disabled dancers as specific users, raising the further challenge of how to capture their dancing motions as an input to Mogrow,

We used a ROKOKO Smart suit [ 1 ], a full-body motion capture suit widely employed to animate characters in computer games, as this was readily available and reliable. The suit employs inertial tracking and provides 17 tracking points distributed over the body as shown in Figure 5 , each of which can capture the kinematic data of relative position, velocity, acceleration, rotation and torque. However, fitting the ROKOKO suit proved to be challenging as it had been designed with a stereotypical view of the human body. As there was no easy way to disconnect the superfluous segment of the suit, our dancers faced the prospect of somehow tying it up to their body or dancing with it flapping around. The latter led to some awkwardness, but also some opportunities for playful improvisation as they danced with an unusual flapping physical costume and corresponding flapping virtual limb. This issue had not been anticipated beforehand and so emerged as an important lesson for future engagements.

We experimented with two different mappings between dancers’ movements and Mogrow's key parameters of injection, shrinking and friction as shown in Table 1 .

Table 1: Two modes of interaction with Mogrow
Mogrow parameter Functional mode Dancing mode
Injection Shake left hand to increase Shift bodily alignment right
Shrinking Shake right hand to increase Shift bodily alignment left
Friction Move body faster to reduce Move body faster to reduce

In the functional mode , the movements of specific body parts were directly mapped to key parameters. Moving the left hand (tracked by point 5 on the suit) increased the rate of particle injection. Moving the right hand (point 11) faster increased the rate of shrinking. The velocity of the remaining 15 points on the suit was averaged to derive a general rate of bodily movement that was mapped onto friction so that moving one's body faster-reduced friction which led to less compact wrinkling. The functional mode can therefore be summarised as: the dancer shakes their left hand to increase wrinkles, their right hand to decrease them, and moves their whole body faster to produce a smoother form.

Our second mode of interaction, dancing mode , was inspired by dance theory. The two dance researchers on the team guided us to a conceptual framework that articulates ten underlying principles of expressive dance movement [ 56 ] summarised in Table 2 .

Table 2: Underlying principles of expressive dance movement [ 56 ]
Symmetry Ability to perform with both right and left side of the body, arm or leg the exact same movement, simultaneously or sequentially, both in position and in motion.
Directionality Awareness of body orientation in space.
Balance Ability to stand and move in balance, but also out of balance, depending on whether the line of gravity falls within the line of your supporting limb(s).
Alignment Awareness of the geometry of the body (e.g., the sagittal, horizontal, vertical axes) and how the relation of body parts create “lines” in the body shape.
Weight bearing vs gesturing Capacity to distinguish movements implying bearing weights (e.g., weight transference, stepping).
Gross vs fine articulation Ability to distinguish small movements executed by specific body parts (e.g., hand, hip, shoulder) without moving the rest of the body.
Coordination Ability to synchronise parts of the body to move in the same or separate tempos.
Motion through space Capacity of progressing through space in particular directions versus dancing on the spot. Also, the use of body as a moving point or changing moving volume.
Rhythm and phrasing Ability to move in predefined or improvised rhythms which may be related to the rhythmic aspects of music.
Stillness While movement seems to be the essence of dance, a dancer needs to improve her/his ability to remain still, either as part of choreography or interpretation of rhythmical pauses, or an exercise for balance and isolation of body parts.

With this source of structure and inspiration, we faced a bewildering array of potential mappings between dancing and Mogrow. After experimentation, we elected to focus on the principle of alignment as our starting point as we believed this to be both expressive and relatively easy to detect using the ROKOKO suit. Other concepts such as rhythm and phrasing, coordination, weight-bearing etc., were felt to be more challenging to detect and hence not so suitable for a first exploration, but remain interesting for the future. We analysed the movements of two axes running across the body, the first running from the left shoulder (motion capture point 3) to the right shoulder (point 9), and the second from the left hip bone (point 2) to the right hip bone (point 8) (Figure 6 ). Having the right shoulder higher than the left triggers injection while having the left higher than the right triggers shrinking, with the rates of each increasing as the angle between the two axes increases. Simultaneously, increasing the overall velocity of all four points decreases friction. The net result is for the dancer to be able to shift their overall bodily alignment to switch between injection and shrinking, while speeding or slowing their general movement to control friction.

3.1.3 Generating aesthetic seeds. In our first workshop we set up a projected view of Mogrow in a dance studio so that the dancers could see their designs grow as they danced as shown in Figures 7 and 8 . W chose to dance without her prostheses or any other assistive device, while T danced using her crutches. The dancers observed, supported and commented on each others’ explorations throughout.

The session began with our dancers getting familiar with controlling Mogrow using the ROKOKO suit. W and T first tried out Mogrow's functional mode, repetitively rehearsing the hand gestures and overall body movements to control injection, shrinking and friction while watching the visualisation on the screen. They then moved onto using Mogrow's dancing mode to improvise dance movements. For this, they drew on their extensive professional dance experience, which is primarily contemporary dance, though not confined to a single ‘style’, to improvise dance movements in response to Mogrow. In keeping with their usual practice, they used music as a general stimulus and to provide a mood ground for improvisation work, but did not try to dance to (i.e., closely follow) the music. The result was a dialogic process in which they improvised with the system, though we note that the usual fluidity of improvised dance was interrupted somewhat by questions and needing to make adjustments to the technology. Individual dance sessions tended to last for around twenty minutes, finishing when each dancer felt they had concluded. This was influenced both by their relationship with the growing visualisation (a sense of seeing that enough sculpting had appeared on screen) and/or their own sense of their improvised dance having being completed.

We saved the various designs that Mogrow generated from each dance session, referring to these unique records of a particular moment of dance as ‘aesthetic seeds’ reflecting the idea that they embodied the seeds of visual designs that might then be applied to various products including prostheses. Figure 9 shows a selection of aesthetic seeds that were generated by both dancers during the workshop.

3.2 Making prosthetic covers

Our second workshop focused on applying aesthetic seeds to the design of prosthetic covers. A typical transtibial prosthesis comprises: a socket which provides the interface with the wearer; a titanium, stainless steel or aluminium pylon that replicates the structural function of bones in human limbs; a foot that may vary in form and material according to intended activity; and a cover that gives the prosthetic a particular visual appearance. Given that this research is oriented towards the appearance of the prosthetic and does not have such stringent structural requirements, we choose the cover as our target for design, following previous research into the aesthetics of prosthetics [ 11 , 30 , 79 , 86 ].

We created a second algorithm to apply an aesthetic seed generated by Mogrow to the physical form of a lower-limb prosthetic cover or greave (Figure 10 ). Our aim was to satisfy the morphological and functional requirements of the greave without losing the unique appearance of the aesthetic seed. The first challenge was to design the physical form. Our initial idea was to scan each dancer's leg and mirror and segment the result to produce a mirror-image form that might be a good match. The aesthetic seed would then be applied to this somewhat like a tattoo. However, this was not possible due to COVID restrictions which banned physical contact with dancers. We, therefore, used an existing 3D reference model of a leg, segmenting this at appropriate points that matched the desired length of the greave. The 3D model of the aesthetic seed was then scaled and contoured to produce a greave design that fitted the segment. The final stage was to integrate this with a 3D structure that represents a sculpted socket cover to yield the final design.

To widen the discussion of aesthetics, we simulated how the resulting design might appear if printed using various materials that reveal the range of colours, textures and transparencies that can be achievable with 3D printing. These included: Polyactic Acid (PLA) and Polyethylene Terephthalate Glycol (PETG) with varying colours and transparency; wood powder-filled PLA simulating wood texture; and a pioneering glass material from [1]. Figure 11 shows various designs produced from the aesthetic seeds generated by W and T respectively.

In order to be able to 3D print the resulting designs, we needed to solve one further challenge. In additive manufacturing, printing additional structures to support overhanging parts takes considerable time and wastes materials. We, therefore, developed a final algorithm to improve efficiency and sustainability by reducing overhanging structures, hopefully without compromising the aesthetic. Without going into detail in this paper, our algorithm iteratively traverses the 3D model in both top-down and bottom-up directions, incrementally adjusting overhanging contours to be less than a target 55-degree threshold while correcting for various anomalies that might arise that might be visually noticeable. Figure 12 illustrates three examples of the kinds of changes made by this algorithm.

Small as these changes might appear, they can have a significant impact on the additive manufacturing process. Figure 13 compares unoptimised and optimised designs when printed. The printed version of the optimised design, Model B, requires 63.5% of the printing time and 67.6% materials of the original, Model A. To give an indication of the timescales involved Model A took 9 hours and 21 minutes to print on the 3D printer - Flashforge Creator 3 [ 22 ] while Model B took 5 hours and 54 minutes.

4 FINDINGS: CONVERSATIONS ABOUT AESTHETIC PROSTHETICS

We now report the findings from our conversations with the dancers and dance researchers. We report quotes from the two disabled dancers, T and W, as well as from the two dance researchers, K and S, that prompted and helped interpret these as part of an unfolding conversation.

4.1 Mogrow's visual aesthetic

Our dancers appreciated the organic visual aesthetic of Mogrow's designs:

W: “I love the wrinkles because I do think they're really organic and human.” (Workshop 2.b)

However, they acknowledged this as being a matter of personal taste, setting their comment in the wider context of a shift in fashion towards the ‘bare metal leg’ as a contrast to the traditional aesthetic of emulating a human-like limb:

W: “ I don't particularly really like the metal leg. It's really what I have said lots of people are loving at the moment. It's not the fact that I don't want my leg to be seen. I particularly don't like the look of them, a lot of them, since they're all very shiny. And I like organic.” (Workshop 2.b)

As early as the first workshop, they made a specific connection between the wrinkly nature of Mogrow's designs and their own identities as disabled people:

T: “Because I don't want something sleek, that's also not what I am. That's what I think disabled people are not.”

W: “Yeah, we're wrinkling …"

T: “Yeah, we are. We're so wrinkles.” (Workshop 1)

Finally, on being directly asked whether they noticed the differences between the initial designs of the prosthetic covers and the optimised versions without overhangs, both dancers indicated that these were generally not noticeable to them.

4.2 The aesthetic seed

They appreciated the idea of the aesthetic seed as an abstract representation of their dancing, noting how this ‘design by dance’ approach transformed an individual's personal and ephemeral dance into a concrete personalised design. They observed that making the ephemeral concrete in this way invites viewers to make an interpretation and read a narrative into the design:

K: “As observers of W and T, I can look at these beautiful designs and I can think I really remember the way they were moving and even if I am making it up for myself, I can kind of create a narrative around ’Oh they were doing this kind of movement and I think I am seeing that in this thing.” (Workshop 2.a)

W: “I love the word - the aesthetic seeds that you sort of seed something through your own movement. So there is that organic kind of feel to it.” (Workshop 2.b)

Participants noted a stronger sense of personalised ownership of the abstract seeds compared to the prosthetic covers to which they were ultimately applied:

W: “The ones before, when they were on the computer [aesthetic seeds], draw back memories. Looking at the designs [prosthetic covers] doesn't.” (Workshop 2.b)

T: "I can't recognize which of the seeds has been taken [to produce a particular prosthetic cover design]. For example, if I know that was the aesthetic seed number one of T in that prosthetic leg and then I can make that connection again probably.” (Workshop 2.b)

This might be in part because the seed represents dance in a purer form, one that is not distorted by then applying it to another shape, which perhaps makes it easier to read. However, they also commented that seeing the aesthetic seed growing in front of their eyes while interacting helped establish a memorable connection to it; one that they were able to draw on over a year later:

W: “We could see what we were doing and it was on the screen. So there's the feels more of a connection there.” (Workshop 2.b)

In contrast, they had not witnessed the process of applying their aesthetic seeds to the forms of the leg covers (Figure 10 ) as this had been done in their absence, with the results only being presented to them later on.

4.3 Generating designs by dancing

In general, the dancers preferred the mode of interaction based on the dancing mode using the principle of alignment, finding it less clumsy and more immediate than the functional mode, and that it reinforced their sense of agency in generating the design:

T: “I did very much feel that [agency] in the second round [dancing principle mode] with the axis because also it directed quicker. So, I could see the result quicker on the screen. When I spiral, it creates. I can see it spiralling. Or when I go up faster, I can see it, you know, growing the points and there, I felt very much ownership on what I create.” (Workshop 1)

They also reported that, with the dancing mode, it felt more like the system was responding to their dancing rather than them having to respond to the system by trying to learn how to control it:

K: “Is it about responding to the way we move versus we're moving to control what we see.” (Workshop 1).

We also observed that they attended to the screen early on when in functional mode as they figured out the effects of particular gestures, while then appeared to dance more freely in front of (but not necessarily attending to) the screen later on when in dancing mode. Of course, we need to be careful in interpreting these comments and observations as they could easily reflect a growing familiarity with the system rather than a preference for a mode of interaction. It might also be that functional mode is useful for gaining some appreciation of how Mogrow works before one then switches over to the more expressive dancing mode.

4.4 The challenges of the mocap suit

As noted earlier, the use of the Mocap suit proved to be challenging as it had clearly not been designed with diverse bodies in mind. As soon as our dancers tried to put on the suit, it became apparent that one of its legs was surplus to requirements. This was evident physically, with the leg of the costume dangling from their bodies, but also virtually, as the digital representation of the body portrayed by the software still showed the phantom limb, albeit now moving in unusual ways, further calling attention to the mismatch. There was no immediate way of reconfiguring the suit for their bodies, for example, by unzipping segments that were not required and/or adapting the visual representation, which led our dancers to improvise solutions such as ‘tucking it up’ or trying to dance while it flapped around behind them. Understandably, donning a suit that was so evidently not designed for diverse bodies impacted how our dancers felt:

T: “Seeing these four limbs on screen, where I have three. It's like, what is this?” (Workshop 1).

There was a sense in which donning the suit was like putting on someone else, potentially even becoming them:

S: “Putting on a suit feels like putting on a not you. I think that's true with any of these motion capture suits. So, you sort of put on somebody else you put on this kind of suit, you become something else.” (Workshop 1).

However, as creative practitioners, this evident mismatch between the suit and their bodies also generated opportunities for playing with the technology, provoking new experimental movements:

T: “It's an interesting aspect maybe to even play with... you know, when I swing my left side, where I don't have my left leg, but the point still works,... I could create a speed that I couldn't with a leg.” (Workshop 1).

Dancers suggested that motion capture could also be applied to various assistive objects that feature in their dancing and that are, in a sense, extensions of their bodies, including crutches (see Figure 8 for an example of T dancing with her crutches) and wheelchairs:

T: “Does it need a suit? Is it more like the sense you can add to the joints or to the wheels, or to the crutches?” (Workshop 1).

W: “Or in the chair? Because I was thinking about that as well in the chair. It'd be interesting to be in the chair and then maybe incorporate the chair within it.” (Workshop 1).

4.5 Having greater agency in prosthetic design

The dancers commented that visually personalising their prostheses in this way could deepen personal connections:

T: “I think to have this option of putting a layer on it, makes it [prosthesis] even more interesting. It's a bit more of my taste, just like that part is connected to the experience where I had the laughter.” (Workshop 2.b)

They expanded at length on how such an approach might increase their sense of autonomy, introducing a degree of control over their prostheses (and hence their own bodies), rather than having them handed over by others:

K: “Something you just said about people not liking their assistive tools or having a negative feeling towards them was super interesting. And actually, that autonomy in the designs seems to make a really big difference to how you relate to them. It's not medical model of disability and it's not something that's being handed to you. It's kind of you're in control of it. You can change it.” (Workshop 2.b)

W: “I think back ten or twenty years, there's no way we would have been able to get some crutches or prostheses that we would be able to design ourselves. I think that's really sad because I think for all the last generations, they've had to have things that they've been told” (Workshop 2.b)

They contrasted this sense of agency with their frustrations at dealing with the conventional approach to making prostheses which involves multiple trips back and forth to a specialised centre throughout the design and manufacturing process:

W: “Recently, the limb centre has been giving me parts of my leg, taking home or showing me how to do stuff” … “for 28 years, I haven't been allowed to do that, allowed to touch or play with them or do anything because you've got a sticker that says this is property of (others).” (Workshop 1)

The discussion turned to the question of whether the rapid design and manufacture of aesthetic prostheses might even enable their production as costumes for specific shows and if so, who might then have agency and ownership? The dancers wanted a dialogue with costume designers rather than being told what to wear as was traditionally the case in the industry:

W: "I think wearing a costume really can completely transform how you feel on stage or how you feel on film or how you feel in a photograph. And that's changed as well, because back in the day, quite often you'd do a show and you just wear whatever they tell you to wear. It might make you feel awful.” (Workshop 2.b)

K: “I wonder is that about how as dancers and particularly disabled dancers, how our voices are included in that?” … “it's so much better to be in conversation with a designer or a costume maker, particularly if you occupy a disabled body. It's really great to have those conversations about how a particular costume works for your body, versus someone just layering stuff on.” (Workshop 2.b)

4.6 Materials

The dancers were intrigued by the various materials that were shown. They expressed a preference for organic materials that resonated with the visual aesthetic noted previously. They also observed how layering organic materials over the underlying metal structure could heighten the sense of the prosthesis being connected to their bodies:

W: "For me personally, it will be organic ones. I love the silk, the wood, the stone, all of those kinds of ones.” … “It's a funny one as well. Because underneath all the [prosthetic] legs, there is metal in there. That doesn't feel connected, but I feel connected by organic material outside of it.” (Workshop 2.b)

The transparent material proved especially interesting, both for its visual aesthetic in terms of the way in which it distorted the metal bar that remained partially visible underneath, but also because of the subtle statement that this made about the visibility of the prostheses. The idea of making invisible supports such as transparent crutches was raised as a potentially exciting performance aesthetic.

T: “I was very intrigued by the see-through or the glass one because it reshapes even the metal within. What the leg looks like is the various sleek lines breaking it up again, which makes it even more fluid and moving” (Workshop 2.b)

T: “It seems to be moving differently in itself. Then I can imagine when the light breaks in, you know, you can play some much with that, especially in a performance.” (Workshop 2.b)

The discussion turned to the possibility of embedding colour-changing lights into the greave which received a mixed response:

W: “And I do like the one that changes colour, because I think that's quite nice, in the sense that you can have two different [prostheses]. It can give a different life. If you're going to go out clubbing, then you can go [with the luminous one].” (Workshop 2.b)

K: “I'm aware of something in me that slightly avoids anything that might appear comedic. Comedic is not quite the right word. I'm saying I do not want to go against things and stand out.” (Workshop 2.b)

4.7 Form and function

The discussion of materiality extended to the physical form of the prostheses. Dancers reflected on issues with comfort and fit, speculating whether 3D printing might help here:

T: "I think 3D printing enables customisation. We all have different bodies. I had a prosthetic once. But it was also quite based on a normative body and my body was so not normative. So, I decided not to use a prosthetic because it didn't work with my body”. (Workshop 1)

K: "That's the same for me. I can't wear a prosthetic because I've got too much arm. There's not enough room to fit the mechanisms in.”

However, as creative dancers, they also recognised that unusual, constraining, and potentially even uncomfortable, physical forms might stimulate new expressive movements during dance performance. There were discussions of dancing on spikes and springs, and even of deliberately dragging around heavy limbs:

K: “I was thinking about a performance context and thinking I'd love one, which may be not like a hand, but something else, maybe a non-functional prosthesis. In dance, you could have a wooden leg that's just for dancing. But your dance is informed by the fact that you're wearing something really heavy... and then you use movement and dance to adapt around that thing” (Workshop 2.b)

They also noted how unusual forms can make political statements by drawing attention to disability:

K: “There seems to be something about the political. I was imagining, if I'd build one of your things, there's something that could attach to my left hand and be massive, like really long and curl around my body. I could push it across the stage. It takes up space and there's something fundamentally political about that” … "I love this idea of just decorating my left hand in a way that invites looks.” (Workshop 2.b)

Finally, our dancers raised the potential of personalising other products beyond prostheses, including assistive devices such as crutches, braces, and wheelchairs, but also more generally jewellery, costumes and other body ornamentation:

K: “But there's something about identity and about creating something, particularly about a disabled identity. So recently, I just bought a solid silver hand that I sometimes wear on a necklace, and actually, it's got so much meaning for me.” (Workshop 1)

W: “I feel like jewellery and dancing really go well. Creating jewellery with your body would be amazing.” (Workshop 1)

5 DISCUSSION

Our overarching contribution is to generalise our approach into a process for humans and algorithms to co-design personalised products. We present this as progressing through seven stages and engaging various algorithms along the way. In what follows, we draw on this generalised process to articulate two further contributions: a discussion of how (i) aesthetic considerations and (ii) the need to consider diverse bodies run throughout. Figure 14 summarises these contributions as an integrated diagrammatic representation of the process, example designs and interactions, and the themes that emerged when discussing these with the dancers.

5.1 Considering aesthetics throughout the co-design process

While everyday notions of aesthetics often focus on visual ‘beauty’, our review of related work revealed a far wider set of considerations. Ideas originating in the philosophy of aesthetics that have been brought to HCI reveal how wider aesthetic considerations, including sense-making and emotional engagement, shape interactive user experiences [ 35 , 89 , 90 ]. In turn, the prosthetics literature reveals a diversity of tastes for prostheses, from more traditional realistic looking designs to contemporary fashion-inspired ones that reveal themselves as part of celebrating the identities of disabled people. Our approach enables people to express their particular personal tastes by making aesthetic choices at multiple points along the co-design process.

First, it is necessary to choose a generative design algorithm with which to co-design. It should be feasible to create a suite of algorithms, each which its own distinctive design style ranging from the more realistic to the more abstract. There are, for example, algorithms that have been applied to produce 3D designs of installations and architecture with various recognised visual styles [ 3 , 29 , 64 ]. Choosing an algorithm then becomes like choosing to engage a particular designer whose style one appreciates.

Deciding how to interact expressively with the chosen algorithm is a key aesthetic choice in our approach. Our dancers appreciated being able to interact through a highly tuned and hard-earned skill that enabled them to express themselves. In response, we developed an interaction technique that was attuned to this skill, in our case by drawing on deeper knowledge of dance principles. Future work is required to evaluate whether this mechanism is actually preferable to other (perhaps more functional) possibilities, so we raise it here as an example of a novel approach to be explored in further work. Briefly returning to Hsueh et al.’s taxonomy for kinaesthetic creativity reviewed above, our approach falls under their category of ‘control’ where dancing is used to control a system, though arguably also includes aspects of ‘co-creating’, though in this case between human and algorithm, rather than between two humans (mediated by an algorithm) as they discuss [ 33 ].

However, the broader implication here is to seek out equivalent personally expressive skills in other situations. This might be a recognised artistic skill such as music, dance, painting, or sculpting, or perhaps some other somaesthetic skill that involves an aesthetic bodily interaction (various sporting skills spring to mind). In turn, this requires developing interaction techniques that embody a deep knowledge of the chosen skill (as we were able to do by drawing on dancing principles from dance research).

A key idea to emerge during our process was that of capturing moments of expressive interaction with the design algorithm as abstract representations called aesthetic seeds. Our dancers appreciated being able to revisit these seeds even after some considerable time had passed, re-interpreting them in terms of their movements. Indeed, this may have been easier with the abstract form of the seeds rather than in cases where they had been applied to the form of a prosthetic cover. We propose that choosing an aesthetic seed likely extends beyond visual beauty to encompass memory, reflection and personal connection, mirroring Wright et al.’s emphasis on sense making as being key to the aesthetics of interaction [ 89 ]. With this in mind, we envisage potentially feeding offline versions of algorithms to generate personally meaningful designs from recordings of personally significant and memorable interactions.

Our dancers highlighted aesthetic concerns arising from choosing the physical form of the product , in our case the shape of the prosthetic cover onto which the aesthetic seeds were mapped. Shape, size and weight all potentially affected the aesthetics of their bodily movement with the product, both in terms of constraining movement, but also establishing new possibilities. Unconventional ideas such as wearing an unusually shaped prosthetic such as a spike to enable a new kind of expressive dance movement become feasible when such products can be rapidly co-designed and manufactured.

Choosing a material for the product involved aesthetic concerns for its look and feel, in our case with our dancers preferring organic materials, and further highlighted the matter of visibility through transparent materials that both partially cover and partially reveal the inner workings of the product. In our research process, due to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, these choices were independently made by the researchers before being reviewed by the dancers and we recognise that this distance from the process may have contributed to our dancers not being able to do so easily identify their aesthetic seeds on the decorated leg covers. We propose that human design partners should be deeply engaged throughout the entire process so that they can make sense of the final designs.

Being present throughout the design process even extends to final manufacturing, in our case additive manufacturing. In order to become feasible at scale, the cost, efficiency and sustainability of manufacturing need to be acknowledged as also raising aesthetic concerns. There is sometimes a tricky negotiation between designers and manufacturers over aesthetics and efficiency: designers may pursue rich and complex aesthetics, while manufacturers may pursue efficiency. This is a widespread phenomenon that can be found in many domains, for example, 3D graphics for games and virtual worlds in which the visual complexity of world design needs to be balanced with the processing power of graphics hardware. We expect this tension to be especially intense in designs with a primary concern for aesthetics, requiring designers, manufacturers and algorithms to seek out appropriate compromises. For example, designers need to optimise the design to the requirements of the chosen manufacturing technique (in our case by reducing overhanging elements to optimise 3D printing). Such changes might significantly affect the resulting design and so should be open to scrutiny and control by human co-designers. Users may also be concerned about the environmental impacts of manufacture, wanting a say in design choices that concern the sustainable use of materials, for example, trading off the fine details of appearance and form with the need for wasteful supporting materials.

The process that we followed embodied a particular set of aesthetic choices, some of which were made by our technology researchers (the design of Mogrow, using a realistic looking limb, available materials, and optimisation), while others were largely made by the dancers and dance researchers (how dancing controlled Mogrow, how they danced, choosing aesthetic seeds). Interestingly, in terms of prosthetics, the resulting design combines aspects of non-realism (Mogrow's wrinkly forms) and realism (applying these to a realistic limb). These choices were made by our team in a somewhat improvised manner as part of an exploratory process and other choices clearly could have been possible. For example, feedback from our dancers suggests that we might have explored a wider range of physical forms than a realistic looming limb.

Stepping back from the details of the process, this discussion of aesthetic considerations throughout the co-design process mirrors previous discussions of aesthetics within HCI as: arising from shared agency between user and system [ 6 ]; entangling appearance and functionality [ 57 ]; arising from making sense of experiences [ 89 ]; being embodied [ 32 , 90 ]; and potentially being staged [ 17 ].

Our process also speaks to wider discussions on the relationship between customisation and personalisation. Customisation has been seen as involving consumers in making explicit choices when tailoring products, whereas personalisation is algorithmic and data-driven [ 5 , 81 ]. Customisation is often applied to tailor physical products, even if delivered through digital means such as websites, whereas algorithmic personalisation underpins the world of digitally native products such as search engines and social media. Our research shows how consumers may engage in both customisation (e.g, explicitly choosing algorithms and forms) and personalisation (e.g., interacting with algorithms) to deeply tailor products as part of an extended design process.

Finally, we observe that being able to rapidly manufacture products in this way will likely change people's relationship with them. A prosthetic, for example, may shift from not only being a medical device that is prescribed and designed by medical experts, or from being an expensive handmade bespoke piece of art that is made by artists, to being a more consumable product that can be made afresh for specific individuals, purposes or even occasions. Our discussions with dancers raised the question of whether 3D-printed personalised prostheses should be considered as extensions of their bodies, props, or even as costumes that are created and worn for a given show. This is not to say that medical input is not still required. Medical, functional and safety concerns remain important, but are now extended by aesthetic ones. This may require us to take a more nuanced view of which stakeholders need to be involved in co-designing which elements of a prosthesis. Previous HCI research has introduced the idea of customisation maps to describe how the different stakeholders in a product (e.g., manufacturers, distributers, consumers and prosumers) may customise both its physical and digital aspects [ 8 ]. Along similar lines, we reflect that other stakeholders (e.g., costume and set designers, fashion designers, and also medical professionals) might potentially engage with our extended prosthetic design process, potentially by providing further algorithms (e.g., to help design the biometric aspects of prostheses as described in [ 87 ]).

5.2 Considering diverse bodies throughout the co-design process

A second theme that emerged from our design process, and one that also runs as a thread throughout the entire design process, is designing for and with diverse bodies.

Our dancers appeared to appreciate Mogrow's distinctive wrinkly style which they felt reflected the organic and fluid nature of their movement as disabled dancers. While employing motion capture to interact with Mogrow by dancing was generally well received, we encountered challenges arising from the assumptions inherent in the ROKOKO suit that users have four limbs and move in conventional ways, assumptions which permeate both the physical design of the suit and its underlying software model and virtual representation of the moving body. This suggests choosing/developing modular versions of such suits that can be better tailored to individual bodies (e.g., through removable and reshapable segments) and extended to assistive devices such as crutches and wheelchairs.

Turning to form and materiality, both our literature review and discussions highlighted a tension between realism and non-realism in the design of prostheses. This speaks to the wider question of visibility and stigma with regard to assistive technologies as previously considered by the HCI community, notably Shinohara and Wobbrock's argument that “social accessibility” may be as important as “functional accessibility” [ 68 ] and Faucett et al's meta-analysis of stigma and the visibility or otherwise of both disabilities and assistive technologies [ 21 , 68 ]. Our dancers discussed how highly unusual physical forms might be consciously employed to make political statements about disability, directing attention to the physical and social awkwardness of prostheses, a tactic that reflects previous discussions of uncomfortable interactions in HCI in which interfaces are deliberately made viscerally or culturally uncomfortable in pursuit of entertainment, enlightenment or sociality [ 7 ]. In this case, extreme and uncomfortable prostheses, for example, ones that are heavy and so require dragging around, or are extended and so collide with the world, may help enlighten people as to how diverse bodies experience the world. Such approaches mirror Siebers’ idea of disability aesthetics described above that asserts that ”disability intercedes to make the difference between good art and bad art” [ 70 ].

Finally, the idea of transforming prostheses from being specialised lifelong medical devices into more transient everyday products, especially the idea of them potentially becoming like costumes, raises further questions about the politics of the body. Our dancers commented on tensions and changing attitudes within the industry as to whether dancers should have to wear whatever costume designers choose to create. To what extent is a prosthesis an extension of the body or a costume or even a prop in a show, and which stakeholders should have a say in how it is designed? Our co-design process opens up the possibility for the form, material and appearance of prostheses to potentially be shaped by various stakeholders – dancers, choreographers, costume designers, and even algorithm designers – each of whom might make input. Overall then a dance prosthesis appears to be a site for complex negotiations between wearers and various potential external designers – medical, costume, fashion, and perhaps now algorithms too – all of whom may need to be factored into the design process, while recognising the vital importance of agency to the wearer.

6 CONCLUSIONS

Attitudes towards prostheses have been shifting over recent years, especially regarding their aesthetic qualities. Questions of whether and how to reveal prostheses are now very much matters of personal taste, even fashion, and there is an emerging market for personalising bespoke prostheses. In this context, the combination of two emerging technologies–generative design and additive manufacturing–promises to radically transform how prostheses can be aesthetically personalised.

We have contributed a process through which people can co-design personalised prostheses by interacting with algorithms that (i) generate unique personal visual designs called aesthetic seeds from moments of meaningful and expressive interaction; (ii) apply these to a chosen form of prostheses; and (iii) optimise the final design for efficient additive manufacturing. Reflection on this process revealed that the aesthetic choices involved run far more than skin deep, reaching beyond visual decoration to encompass form, function, fashion, meaning and identity, and even the manufacturing process. We also saw how the diversity of bodies needs to be accounted for throughout the design process, including in the design of enabling technologies.

We highlight some limitations of our approach that suggest directions for future research. First, we have engaged only a small number of professional disabled dancers. This raises the question of whether our process can generalise to wider populations and other kinds of product. In particular, will non-professionals possess the aesthetic and performative skills to successfully drive such a process? Future work might explore harnessing more everyday creative skills, for example the talents of many millions of amateurs who participate in diverse forms of aesthetic expression such as dancing, singing, playing instruments, painting, sculpting, sports, and computer games among others. Second, we recognise that the exploratory nature of our approach led us in some unanticipated and improvised directions that we might have approached differently with hindsight. Might we have engaged our dancers in the initial design or choice of the generative algorithm, or better anticipated the challenges posed by the motion capture suit? Future research could now apply the process that emerged from our initial explorations more systematically from the outset, engaging users in making more of the choices and therefore deeper co-design throughout. A third limitation concerns iteration and circularity. We have only undertaken one initial iteration of a design process so far, whereas in practice, design is often more iterative and circular, potentially even incorporating feedback from ongoing product use. Future work might explore more circular versions of our process in which there are feedback loops between both stages and iterations.

Given these caveats, we hope that our approach might eventually enable people with disabilities to use their expressive skills to personalise a range of assistive devices and products, from mobility aids to adapted tableware, cutlery and perhaps even furniture. Beyond this, we hope that our approach might apply even more widely to help people meaningfully personalise all manner of everyday products for the widest diversity of bodies.
