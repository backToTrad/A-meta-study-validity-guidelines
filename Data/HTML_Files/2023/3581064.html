Lihi Zelnik-Manor , Electrical and Computer Engineering, Technion - Israel Institute of Technology, Israel, lihi@technion.ac.il

CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Many of our activities rely on tactile feedback perceived through mechanoreceptors in our skin. While visual and auditory devices provide immersive experiences, cutaneous feedback devices are typically limited in the range of sensations they provide and are hence usually used and tested on relatively simple synthetic surfaces. We present a device designed in a human-centered process, triggering the mechanoreceptors sensitive to pressure, low-frequency vibrations, and high-frequency vibrations, enabling one to experience touch of complex real-world surfaces. The device is based on a parallel manipulator and a pin-array, that operate simultaneously at 200Hz and emulate coarse and fine geometrical features, respectively. The decomposition into coarse and fine features, alongside the high operation frequency, enable simulation of virtual surfaces. This was corroborated via experiments on complex real-world surfaces via both a quantitative recognition test and a usability questionnaire. We believe that this design can be incorporated in numerous applications.

1 INTRODUCTION

While touch is involved in almost all activities that we do in the real world, in the virtual world touch is not yet sufficiently available. Being able to touch objects virtually could be useful in numerous domains. For example, in e-commerce touch would enable customers to feel the products they are buying, which could provide a better and more enjoyable shopping experience as well as reduce returns of unsatisfactory items. Such a device will need to convey the sensation of fine textures and by doing so, feel and choose different materials. A virtual touch device can also be easily integrated in the vision of the metaverse to generate a more immersive experience involving multiple senses. Such a device should enable users to interact with objects as if they were real. To do so it will need to provide multiple types of sensations, including shear, normal forces, macro and micro roughness and warmness.

Human tactile perception is based on four types of mechanoreceptors lying in different depths relative to the skin surface. The combination of these four types of mechanoreceptors creates the known dimensionalities of mechanical perception: hardness, friction, macro roughness and fine roughness [ 7 , 17 ]. Previously proposed tactile feedback devices were typically aimed at solving one aspect of the human haptic perception. For example, a device that generates normal forces at different orientations to emulate surface hardness [ 2 ], normal and shear forces that simulate friction [ 16 ], or a tactile pin-array that simulates fine roughness vibrations [ 26 ]. While these devices perform well in their intended setting, they are not capable of creating immersive experiences since they do not integrate multiple modalities. Recent studies have discussed and suggested multi-modal devices capable of conveying two or more aspects of the tactile perception [ 24 , 27 , 28 ]. While these multi-modal devices offer a significant leap towards an immersive haptic interface, many focused only on material properties without attention to geometric structure.

The main contribution of this paper is HUGO, a High-resolution UX for Geometrical Objects (Figure 1 ). HUGO can emulate real-world surfaces, i.e., the surfaces can have complex geometry such as surfaces used for computer games or reconstructed from real images of objects or products. The key idea behind HUGO's design is to stimulate multiple types of mechanoreceptors, and through that achieve the ability to emulate multiple tactile modalities, including hardness, macro roughness and fine roughness.

Reviewing previous studies, we observe that most of them perform empirical evaluations on relatively simple synthetic surfaces, such as surfaces showing lines in different orientations and thicknesses. This is not surprising given the single modality nature of these devices. Others perform more extensive evaluations of material identification, and some worked on higher resolution synthetic surfaces such as digits and letters. We wish to push forward the baseline of tactile device evaluation, towards complex real-world surfaces. Therefore, a secondary contribution of this paper is a benchmark that will be made public, that consists of digitized real-world surfaces, such as wood, ground, fabric, and more. This challenging benchmark shows the broadness of surfaces that can be emulated by our device.

HUGO is based on a Three Revolute-Revolute-Spherical (3RRS) parallel manipulator for displaying surface normal – macro geometry, and a high-speed solenoid array for displaying micro geometry and fine texture information. The solenoid array is assembled on the manipulator, and together they are mounted on a computer mouse. The device is based on simple and scalable technology and was designed according to the sensing capabilities of the mechanoreceptors in the human skin so that it can emulate real-world, high-resolution surfaces. HUGOs’ performance was evaluated in an experiment (IRB approved) where 20 subjects were challenged in two scenarios, (1) a set of synthesized surfaces divided into pairs to evaluate resolution and feedback capabilities using controllable parameters. Users were asked to state if the two surfaces in each pair are identical or not; (2) a set of 21 complex surfaces divided into seven categories which are taken from an online PBR library to test surface recognition on real-world surfaces. Users were asked to match the three virtual surfaces in each category to images of those surfaces. In addition, the users were asked to fill out the USE questionnaire [ 12 ] to evaluate usability, satisfaction and ease of use. The average success rate for the synthesized surfaces is 83.41% and for the real-world surfaces is 86.25%. These experiments demonstrate and quantify the capabilities of the device to emulate high-resolution (temporal and spatial) real-world surfaces.

In summary, this paper presents HUGO, a device capable of mechanically emulating multiple mechanoreceptors and provide immersive tactile feedback of complex real-world surfaces. HUGO can be used in a plethora of applications ranging from e-commerce and gaming to sensory rehabilitation. Moreover, due to its high-resolution, the device can be used as a research tool for research on tactile sensation and feedback.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Mechanoreceptors and human touch

This paper focuses on the human tactile sensation, i.e., local pressure, shear, and vibrations that are sensed by a combination of four types of mechanoreceptors lying in different depths relative to the skin surface [ 3 ]. Since the density of the mechanoreceptors is significantly higher in the inner parts of the hand we focus on devices for tactile feedback to the hand. Each one of the four cells can convert different types of mechanical sensations such as normal and shear forces at varying frequencies to electrical information [ 5 , 7 , 19 ]. The superposition of these four cells and thermoreceptors in the hand results in the five known modalities that compose our tactile perception: (1) hardness, (2) friction, (3) warmness, (4) macro (coarse) roughness and (5) micro (fine) roughness [ 17 ]. When people explore an object, hand movements are used to sample the surface in continuous areas or in windows to create a representation in memory. These finger explorations are necessary for tactile sensing of objects [ 10 ].

2.2 Haptic interfaces

In many cases touchable surfaces are used to represent geometric surfaces and can be based on large scale pin arrays [ 11 ], pneumatic cells with changeable stiffness [ 22 ], and other actuation techniques combined with different materials to create a continuous surface [ 23 ]. These interfaces have the advantage of allowing the users to explore them freely and by doing so reduce the requirements of the generated sensations, e.g., shear forces are generated by the movement of the finger with respect to the device and do not need to be emulated directly, in addition, they allow for multi finger interaction. However, due to the need to create a large surface these devices often provide low-resolution.

Tactile interfaces have many forms, such as graspable, mouse-based devices. Different actuation techniques were implemented in mouse devices, for example, a pin array [ 8 ], pneumatic actuator [ 4 ], and a multimodal device incorporating multiple actuation techniques [ 24 ]. Marquardt et al. developed a device similar to a computer mouse based on a single pin and a brake capable of conveying the sensation of height, malleability and friction, the device was evaluated in a variety of interactions [ 13 ]. By mounting these interfaces on a computer mouse, the use of the interface become quite natural for most users and very accurate tracking can be achieved easily. Wearable tactile devices are similar to the mouse-based devices in the sense that the finger is tracked and according to its location the device needs to emulate only the small “window” where the finger is “touching” the virtual surface. Existing wearable tactile interfaces are in most cases aimed at providing a specific sensation, e.g., grasping an object [ 9 ], or a specific aspect of the human tactile perception, e.g., normal forces using a parallel manipulator [ 2 ]. Ujitoko et al. developed a high-resolution pin array for small features like shapes and letters [ 26 ]. Benko et al. developed two hand-held devices one based on a parallel manipulator designed to emulate surface normals and the other based on a pin array to emulate surface texture however at a lower spatial and temporal resolution than discussed in this paper [ 1 ]. Pacchierotti et al. published a review of wearable haptic devices and describe many other solutions [ 18 ]. While those devices perform well in specific situations, they are limited to the sensation they are designed for, and the activation of mechanoreceptors is limited to those relevant sensations.

In recent years, multimodal haptic interfaces have also been proposed. Multimodal interfaces can provide multiple tactile sensations that are sometimes combined with kinesthetic sensations [ 27 ]. Young et al. proposed a wearable fingertip device with six Degrees of Freedom (DoF) capable of creating forces in any direction meaning it can emulate both shear and normal forces [ 30 ]. A similar concept based on embedded soft actuation with four DoF was suggested in [ 31 ]. Murakami et al. presented a device based on a belt connected to two DC motors and a Peltier element. By turning the motors in and out of sync, the device can generate, normal and shear force and vibrations as well [ 15 ]. While these devices generate multi-axes forces, they are based on one active element and thus have lower spatial resolutions. Yem et al. suggested FinGAR, a device capable of providing multiple haptic sensations using a DC motor for vibrations and shear forces, an electrotactile array for pressure and low frequency vibrations and a Peltier element for heat transfer [ 28 , 29 ]. This design offers a significant leap in its multimodality abilities, yet it cannot convey complex geometric surfaces and is limited to lower resolutions. In addition, it has not been tested on real-world surfaces. Similarly, Strese et al. [ 24 ]. Suggested a mouse-based device incorporating a servo, electromagnets, voice coil and Peltier element for conveying all five tactile modalities. The device design and experimental setup was more focused on surface material and is not designed to covey geometric information. Moreover, due the use of a single voice coil the spatial resolution is relatively low.

3 THE HUGO DEVICE = HIGH-RESOLUTION UX FOR GEOMETRIC OBJECTS

HUGO was designed with the goal of enabling sensing of any geometric object, or in other words, allowing simulation of real-world surfaces. The design is based on three main key ideas that facilitate this ambitious goal. The first key idea is to rely on reverse engineering of the human somatosensory system. Specifically, the device needs to generate multiple tactile modalities out of those that can be sensed by our cutaneous mechanoreceptors. The presented design of HUGO can generate forces and vibrations at multiple frequencies, up to 200Hz. The second key idea is to decompose the surface geometry into two scales, coarse and fine, in line with the human ability to sense both macro and micro roughness. Therefore, HUGO's design is composed of two main components, a manipulator that simulates coarse geometry, and a pin array that simulates fine geometry. Lastly, to allow simulation of surfaces at very high resolutions, HUGO incorporates hand motion tracking, thus allowing continuous scanning of the surface geometry by moving the finger along the object shown on a screen. The scanning motion, joint with high-frequency activation of the device, enables HUGO to overcome the spatial resolution limitation of its mechanical design, and provide a very high-resolution experience to the user. We next explain these ideas and their implementation in further detail.

3.1 Device Implementation

As mentioned before, HUGO is composed of two main components; (1) the macro geometry emulator is based on a 3RRS parallel manipulator with three DoF – two rotations and one translation and by doing so can emulate the macro surface geometry of the object, and (2) the micro geometry emulator which is based on a 25 DoF high speed solenoid array with pins. The 25 DoF manipulator is mounted as the platform of the 3RRS manipulator such that the pin array changes height and orientation. HUGO is mounted on a computer mouse and provides sensations of surfaces in 2.5D. We have also developed a wearable version that is attached to the user's finger and provides sensations in 3D; however, we leave it outside the scope of this paper. Both devices are based on the same actuation methods and techniques and are shown in Figure 1 . Since HUGO is mounted on a computer mouse, the finger scanning motion is tracked using the mouse optical tracking mechanism and allows the user to explore virtual surfaces displayed on the screen. A schematic diagram of the system is shown in Figure 2 . We will next provide further details on the implementation.

3.1.1 3RRS coarse geometry manipulator. The surface normal sensation is reconstructed using a 3RRS manipulator similar to the device offered by Chinello et al. [ 2 ]. An illustration of the CAD model and a picture of the actual device are presented in Figure 1 . The manipulator is capable of three DoF, its output is orientation about two axes (x and y, parallel to the bottom plate) and the distance between the bottom and top plates. The output is denoted by $\vec{\zeta } = ( {{P}_z,\psi ,\theta } )$ and is shown in Figure 1c . The manipulator orientation recreates the sensation of the object surface normal, and the manipulator translation motion simulates the surface height. In addition, it provides the sense of touch by applying normal reaction forces to the finger. The manipulator is actuated using three digital servo motors (Hitech HS-5035HD). While undesired artifacts from such motors could degrade the quality of feedback, using high-end motors would significantly impact the size, weight, and cost of the device. Moreover, previous studies based on similar architectures used similar servo motors (or identical ones) show empirically that such motors could be used in haptic devices [ 1 , 2 ]. 3.1.2 Pin-array micro geometry and fine texture emulator. The purpose of the pin-array is to recreate the sensation of micro geometry and fine texture. The design goals were therefore, to provide a wide range of frequencies including high frequency forces and vibrations, while maintaining low weight and power consumption.

The 25 DoF pin-array is comprised of 25 high speed solenoid actuators, the structure of the actuator is shown in Figure 3 . Each solenoid is composed of a copper coil (1) in which a pin (6) having a permanent magnet (3) attached to its distal part is inserted. The static magnet (2) attached to the base of the coil creates a constant magnetic field that repels the pin and the magnet. By passing electric current in opposite directions through the coil a magnetic field is generated (Lenz's law) and either counteract the static magnetic field and pull the magnet and pin in or enhance the static magnetic field to push the floating magnet and pin out by force. The actuator is covered with a 3D printed piece (4) that allows for a 3mm stroke. Additional technical information regarding the solenoids is given in Table 1 and Figure 3 . The described design and coils allow the device to reach frequencies of up to 200Hz with a push force of up to 1N, this is while maintaining low weight and low power consumption. In addition, the technology is simple and scalable enabling to increase the number of pins relatively easily. The magnets and coil were chosen to achieve the above-mentioned values which are suitable for sensation by the various mechanoreceptors in the human skin [ 7 ].

The design of the pin-array layout is based on previous studies on human ability to discriminate between two distinct points, that show mixed results but range around 1-2mm [ 6 , 25 ]. Most previous pin arrays had larger pin pitch (distance), however, a recent device proposed by Ujitoko et al. presents a significant improvement with a 1.4mm pitch [ 26 ]. Nonetheless, guided by our design constraints we chose a pin pitch of 2mm, with 1mm diameter brass pins. While this layout resolution is lower than [ 26 ] the final simulation resolution is much higher since HUGO also incorporates the concept of super-resolution by integrating the scanning motion of the finger. The idea is inspired by the super-resolution task in images, where multiple images are taken consequently with small misalignments and are then combined to create a single higher resolution image. Similarly, HUGO allows the user to sample the digital surface at small increments as they move their finger along the surface and by doing so collect additional information and construct an object representation in memory with a higher resolution. This concept is similar to the “window” concept in finger exploration for object recognition presented in [ 10 ]. The high accuracy tracking capabilities of the mouse and the high temporal frequency of communication (motor commands are updated at 110Hz) are critical factors that enable this super-resolution effect. The actuators are laid out in a 5X5 grid resulting in an 10X10mm tactile area. The entire pin array was covered by a 0.6mm thick silicone rubber (Shore 20A) to increase comfort for the user and to smooth out the sensation of the distinct pins.

3.1.3 Communications and control. The interface was designed in Unity 3D game engine and is operated at a frame rate of 110Hz. The entire system is controlled through a custom-made PCB, the circuit is based on three microcontrollers (Microchip dsPIC33F) communicating with the Unity app running on the PC through serial communication at 110Hz. At every frame, updated motor commands are sent to the motors from the app through the circuit. The control signals for the three digital servos of the 3RRS manipulator are three PWM signals running in a frequency of 280Hz. The 25 solenoids need to change commands at higher frequencies and therefore the control signals for them are 25 PWM signals with a frequency of 28KHz that are passed through motor drivers (TI DRV8833). This allows for each of the motors to be controlled individually and apply a different force through each of the pins. A diagram of the process is shown in Figure 2 . The solenoid array operates in an open loop, by applying different voltages through the PWM signals on the solenoids, the current through the coil changes such that we can manipulate the force the solenoid exerts as described above. These forces are counteracted by the silicone-rubber membrane and the fingertip which act as a return spring.

3.2 Simulating Geometric Objects with HUGO

Touching a virtual geometric object with HUGO requires actively tuning the 3RRS manipulator and the pin-array such that they represent the surface at the point of touch. This boils down to setting the height and orientation of the 3RRS manipulator, and the height (solenoid position) and vibrations of each of the 25 pins. The user sees an avatar of their finger as shown in Figure 1d and controls the avatar's location by moving their finger in a scanning motion along the object surface. An overview of the mapping from a 3D surface to a sensation given to the user is shown in Figure 4 . Next, we explain each component.

3.2.1 Tuning manipulator height. The height of the manipulator with respect to its base position is determined by the surface height at the specific location of the avatar on the object surface with respect to the average height of the surface, i.e., it is assumed that the finger is laid directly on the surface. The only exception is with small crevices and holes in which the finger cannot fit and, in those cases, the finger rests on the highest areas of the surface it fits in. The base position for the 3RRS manipulator is in the middle of the manipulators’ workspace i.e., when all motor angles are zero and the first linkage in each arm is parallel to the ground as seen in Figure 3c .

3.2.2 Tuning manipulator orientation. To determine the orientation of the 3RRS manipulator we take the finger position on the surface and ray-cast a 5X5 grid, with similar dimensions to the pin array, to obtain the 25 corresponding surface normal vectors. The vectors are smoothed and averaged to generate a single normal vector that represents the macro geometry of the surface and determines the commands to the 3RRS manipulator. The averaging implies that the manipulator simulates geometry with a spatial resolution of approximately 10X10mm.

3.2.3 Tuning pin-array to simulate micro geometry. To determine the height setting of each pin we ray-cast a 5X5 grid again but with the local finger orientation set according to the computed orientation of the manipulator, i.e., the vector representing the local macro geometry. The nearest collision of each ray with the surface determines the height of the corresponding pin. The height of the pins generates the sensation of the local micro geometry, via a 5X5 sample with 2mm spacing. To simulate higher surface resolution, and provide a sensation of texture, we further consider the finger motion. As the finger moves along the surface, we update the pins height to vibrate at a frequency of up to 200Hz, that is determined by the speed of motion. The pins vibrate with some small amplitude around the position determined according to the surface geometry. The amplitude is proportional to the surface roughness which is ideally determined by a known roughness map of the specific surface being emulated. Alternatively, for PBR materials, the roughness image is used to determine the amplitude using the same ray-cast technique described above. While the pins’ location is not directly controlled which could theoretically present challenges in conveying the sensations of geometry, the concept that HUGO is based on is that of object exploration. i.e., we do not aim to create an accurate height map at each time point. For that reason, when the finger is not moving, the sensation of geometry conveyed by the device is very limited. However, when moving the finger and exploring an object the pins are constantly in motion due to changes in micro geometry and by doing so the user can sense the varying forces caused by different geometries quite easily.

3.2.4 Personalization. As haptic sensation varies between users and is considered very individual, personalization is critical for properly displaying haptic information [ 20 , 21 ]. To address this, our GUI allows the user to adjust three parameters: (1) Hand movement speed – the user can increase/decrease the avatar motion speed to provide easier control of the device's location, (2) Surface orientation gain – the orientation vector controlling the 3RRS manipulator was multiplied by this parameter to exaggerate/play-down the macro geometry, (3) Pin solenoid gain – allowed the user to exaggerate/play-down the micro geometry. For all three parameters the default value is 1, and users could adjust it in the range $[ {0.5,1.5} ]$ .

4 SYSTEM EVALUATION

4.1 Subjects

An experiment was carried out to evaluate HUGO's performance. The research sample consists of 20 subjects who volunteered to participate in the study. All subjects were right-handed. Six of the subjects were females and 14 were males (average age of 26). None of the participants had a diagnosed orthopedic or neurological disorder of their dominant hand and none of them suffered from sensory sensitivity. Written informed consent was obtained from each of the subjects and IRB approval was obtained before study commencement.

4.2 Experimental setup

The experimental setup is shown in Figure 5 . Subjects were asked to sit at a table. Their right-hand was placed on the device such that their index finger was situated on the tactile area of the device. The device was placed in a box to obscure any visual information and subjects wore protective earmuffs to obscure auditory information from the motors and pins. The subject had two screens visible, the first screen displayed the user interface of the device and the second displayed instructions for the current step of the experiment.

4.3 Experiment steps

4.3.1 Calibration. The subject was given a set of three surfaces to familiarize themselves with the system. The surfaces are recreated from a PBR materials library and are based on 3D scans of real materials. The subjects were presented with three displays of the three surfaces: (1) displayed on the screen, (2) 3D prints, and (3) images printed on paper of 2D renderings of the surfaces from multiple views to help the subject understand the 3D structure of the surface. Subjects were first given three minutes to get acquainted with the device. Next, subjects were asked to compare the sensation of the printed surfaces vs the sensation provided by the device while we modify the personalization parameters described above until a set of values suitable for the subject is found, i.e., the sensation of the 3D prints and the feedback from the device are closest. A picture of a subject conducting the calibration step is shown in Figure 5 .

4.3.2 Training. While the device is designed to provide as natural as possible mechanical stimulation it still requires some training to master it. Each step in the training section is comprised of six surfaces divided into two groups. For each group, two surfaces were visible, and one was hidden. First, subjects felt the two visible surfaces using the system and learned the provided sensation. Next, they progressed to the third hidden surface. The subjects were requested to identify the shape and texture of the hidden surface and answer questions comparing it to the visible surfaces. There were six training steps, and each step was limited to eight minutes. An example of a training step is shown in Figure 6a .

4.3.3 Parametric surfaces - Same/not same test. One goal of our experimental setup is to obtain quantitative assessment of recognition abilities with HUGO. Hence, we designed an experiment based on 2.5D surfaces, with shape parameters that are meaningful and easy to control.

Each step in this section was comprised of pairs of surfaces that are either identical or very similar except for one distinct feature, e.g., an elliptic cylinder vs a circular cylinder as shown in Figure 6b . One of the surfaces was shown on screen while the other was hidden. The subjects were asked to determine if the two surfaces are identical or not. Each pair of surfaces appeared once to avoid training effects. The pairs of surfaces were divided into four groups of similar features. The order within the pair was constant, the order of groups was constant but the order of steps within each group was randomized. The list of steps and division to groups is shown in Table 2 . The hand avatar did not change heights on the hidden surface to avoid visual information.

4.3.4 Complex surface recognition. To showcase and evaluate the representation capabilities of HUGO we further test recognition accuracy of digitized versions of real-world surfaces. The experimental data consisted of 10 groups of three surfaces created from PBR materials taken from textures.com . The surfaces are based on 3D scans of areas with a size of around 2X2m. For ease of use, the surfaces were scaled down to 100X100mm with a resolution of 512X512 pixels. The surfaces are shown in Figure 7 . In every step the task is to match printed images of surfaces with either their simulation with HUGO (device setup) or with their corresponding 3D prints (control setup). Out of the 10 groups, one was used for calibration, one for training on the device, and one for training on the 3D prints. The subjects had up to five minutes to complete this task. Pictures taken during the experiment in both experimental setups, and a picture of the printed surfaces and the rendering of the cobblestone category are shown in Figure 8 .

This section starts with a training stage where all surfaces are visible as done in the calibration and two practice steps (one for each setup). Next, we move on to the actual test, where the order of surfaces within each group was randomized, the order of groups was randomized, and the testing setup was also chosen randomly. We measure the recognition success rate as well as the time it takes to complete each task.

4.3.5 Questionnaire. At the end of the experiment, subjects were asked to complete the USE questionnaire [ 12 ] to measure Usefulness, Ease of use, Ease of learning and Satisfaction. The subjects ranked multiple statements on a Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). In case subjects felt that a question is not applicable they could leave it empty. Some items from the questionnaire were not used as they were less applicable to our setup, most of which were items that were considered less significant by the authors of [ 12 ]. In the usefulness section of the questionnaire, subjects were shown several potential futuristic applications for the device and were asked to consider performing these tasks with or without the device. The applications shown were, e-commerce, gaming, and remote social communication (patting a dog).

In addition to the USE questionnaire, subjects were also asked to answer a few additional questions directly concerning the different sensations generated by the device. The ranking ranged from 1 (not clear at all) to 7 (very clearly), and in case subjects felt that a question is not applicable they could leave it empty. 5 RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

5.1 Same/not same

A description of all steps of this experiment together with results can be found in Table 2 . The average success rate of all steps is 83.41% which is very significantly better than random guessing for which the expected average success rate is 50% with standard error of 2.38% (based on the 440 “guesses” made by 20 subjects on 22 steps). The average completion time is 39.07 sec. Based on a binomial test for the success rate of each step being better than 50% (guessing), all steps except for 2, 5 and 19 were found to have success rates significantly better than guessing ( $p < 0.05$ ). A logistic regression model was used to test the effect of partition into groups (A-D as shown in Table 2 ), and the effect of each subject on the success rate in the Same/Not Same phase. There were no subjects that were significantly better or worse than others. Pair-wise comparison between the groups shows that success rates for groups A, C and D are not significantly different (78.75%, 81.67%, and 81.67% respectively), while group B (94%) had significantly higher success rates compared to all other groups with a difference of over 12.33% $(p < 0.017)$ . Similarly, a linear mixed model was used for modelling the effect of the partition into groups on the completion time, the steps themselves within each group, and the random effect of each subject on the time that was taken to complete each step. The random subject effect (the variance between times for different subjects) is significant ( $p < 0.005$ ). Group A and C had similar average completion times with 41.56 sec and 40.13 sec respectively with no significant difference, group B had significantly shorter completion times with an average of 28.65 sec ( $\Delta t = 11.48$ sec, $p < 0.0001$ ), and group D had significantly longer completion times with an average of 47.65 sec ( $\Delta t = 6.09$ sec, $p < 0.009$ ). As can be seen in the list of steps, groups A and B were aimed at testing the identification of macro features, i.e., the geometric structure emulated by the 3RRS manipulator. Groups C and D on the other hand were mainly aimed at testing the micro features, i.e., the fine geometric information emulated by the pin array. To compare different groups of steps, first the difference in average success rates between the two groups of steps was computed for each subject. These 20 differences were then tested for having a mean or median different from zero using either a t-test, or a signed rank test, respectively. The average success rate for groups A and B is 84.62% with an average time of 36.59 sec, vs 81.67% and 42.64 sec for groups C and D. While these results suggest a superior performance of the 3RRS manipulator and correspond to results obtained in other parts of the experiment the differences are not statistically significant in both the t-test and the signed-rank test.

Steps 1-3 in group A were designed to estimate curvature differentiation using cylinders and elliptic cylinders. Step one consisted of two identical cylinders while steps two and three consisted of a cylinder and an elliptic cylinder with a 20% and 40% difference between the major and minor radii, respectively. Steps one and three had similar success rates of 85% and 90%, respectively, while step two was harder to identify with a 65% success rate corresponding to the smaller difference between the curvature of the two surfaces. As mentioned above, the success rate for step two was not significantly better than random guess. This result provides an estimation for the recognition capability of curvature using the device; however, this should be compared in future studies to the recognition capability of subjects sensing actual surfaces, similar to the control setup in the real-world surfaces experiment described above. Completion times were compared using the linear-mixed model and all pair-wise comparisons between steps 1-3 were not significantly different. In steps 4-6 very similar shapes were tested, an elliptical cylinder and a slotted cylinder (a cylinder with a slot as a cross section – two quarter circles and a straight line between them as shown in Figure 6c ). The proportion between the minor and major radii was constant in these three steps. The average success rate of these steps is lower (73.33%), however, the difference between these three steps and the rest of the steps in the group is not significant in both the t-test and the signed-rank test. As mentioned above, while step five which had a Not-same pair was not significantly better than random guess, steps four and six which that were comprised of a Same pair were significantly better than random guess. These results suggest that these shapes are very hard to differentiate with the device and all simply feel somewhat the same. In steps seven and eight we tested the ability to differentiate angles using rectangular prisms with different draft angles for the top surface. Subjects were able to successfully identify identical angles ( $\theta = 8^\circ $ ) as well as identify the difference between $\theta = 2^\circ $ and $\theta = 15^\circ $ at 85% success rate.

Group B was used to test repetitions of shapes. The two patterns used were a cylindrical wave (multiple half cylinders adjacent to one another) and a sawtooth wave with different number of repetitions. The wave patterns were identified relatively easily, both changes in number of repetitions and shape changes. As mentioned, both success rates and completion times for group B (94% and 28.65 sec) are significantly better than those of all other groups. Success rates and times for steps 10 and 11 (85% and 35.47 sec) with higher number of repetitions are significantly worse than for steps 9, 12 and 13 (100% and 24.10 Sec) with the 15% difference in success rates being significant for both the t-test and the signed-rank test ( $p < 0.03$ ). The analysis of completion times used a linear mixed model for assessing the difference between two specific groups of steps, taking into account the different steps within each group, and the random effect of each subject on the time that was taken to complete each step. On average steps 10 and 11 took 11.375 sec longer than steps 9, 12 and 13 ( $p < 0.0001$ ). These results are not surprising, since all surfaces had the same size, increasing the number of repetitions means smaller features and requires attention to higher resolutions.

Group C was used to test synthesized objects emulated by the pin array. The surfaces include lines at different orientations and outlines of circles with different layouts and different number of repetitions. Different alignments seem to be easier to identify than number of repetitions, success rate of step 15 (grid lines with different number of repetitions) vs steps 14 and 16-18 (different alignments) is smaller with a difference of 18.75%, however, the difference is not significant ( $p = 0.086$ ) in both tests. Nevertheless, the difference in times is significant, step 15 took 11.563 sec longer than steps 14, 16, 17 and 18 ( $p = 0.0003$ ), as found by the linear mixed model mentioned above. In step 19 which had outlines of circles with different diameters, the success rate is 40% which is lower than random guessing. This indicates that identifying small differences in absolute size using the device is very hard. This result corresponds to the results of Kim et al. in which much of the confusion was between line textures with the same number of repetitions but different widths [ 8 ].

The last group was based on surfaces with fine textures (submillimeter resolution). These fine textures took additional time to explore with an average of 47.65 sec which is significantly higher than other groups as mentioned above. The success rate however did not change significantly. Similarly, step 22 which included both fine textures and larger geometrical features was the step with the highest average completion time, this result makes sense as there is more information to explore but the pair-wise evaluation with the linear-mixed model found that the difference is not significant with respect to steps 20 and 21.

5.2 Complex surface recognition

The next section of the experiment was identifying complex, 2.5D real-world surfaces. The success rates and completion times are shown in Table 4 . The key takeaway from these results is a success rate of 86.25% in identifying surfaces using the device vs 97.64% in the control trials. Using a mixed model which considers the fixed effects of groups and setup (device or control) as well as the random differences between subjects, the comparison between device and control setups was analyzed. The results show a significant difference in success rates, (11.39% average in favor of the control surfaces with $p = 0.0007$ ) and a significant difference in average times to complete (52.36 sec longer with the device, $p < 0.0001$ ).

The success rate over all surfaces with the device (86.25%) is very significantly better than random guessing which has a mean success rate of 33% and a standard deviation of 4.01% (based on the 69 groups of surfaces that were evaluated using the device). While there is a significant difference between the two setups, the results and analysis show that the device is capable of emulating complex real-world surfaces. The difference in task completion times indicates that using the device might not be as natural as touching a real 3D printed surface, however, as the result and analysis show, the information could be picked up by the user quite accurately. Moreover, it is important to point out that the average completion times of the control trials is over one minute indicating that even when the subjects feel the geometric structure of the surfaces with their own hand, the task is not trivial and takes time to complete.

Independent sample t-tests were used to compare the accuracy of device and control setups, separately for each surface group. In general, the accuracy was lower for the device, but the difference was only significant for the Asphalt group with a difference of 28.57% ( $p = 0.008$ ) and the Metal group with a difference of 24.24% $(p < 0.045)$ , suggesting that the performance for the rest of the groups is comparable. A z-test was carried for each surface group to test whether the device accuracy was better than guessing (for which the success rate is 1/3 with a standard deviation of 1/3). For all surfaces the device success rate was significantly better than that achieved by guessing. This suggests that even for the Asphalt and metal groups using the device offers a significant benefit (recognition rates are over 70%). Independent sample t-tests were used to compare the completion times with the device and the control setup, separately for each surface group. In general, the completion times were lower for the printed surfaces, but the difference was only significant for Asphalt ( $\Delta t = 78.74$ sec, $p < 0.03$ ), Concrete $(\Delta t = 84.69$ sec, $p < 0.017)$ and Metal ( $\Delta t = 84.64$ sec, $p < 0.004$ ) groups, suggesting that the completion times for device and printed surfaces for the rest of the groups are comparable. A generalized linear model, modelling the effect of the surface groups on the matching success scores when using the device was used to evaluate the difficulty in recognition for each of the groups. The Fabric group was found to be significantly different from the Asphalt, Ground and Metal groups with a difference in success rate of over 24.24% ( $p < 0.05$ ). Relaxing the significance level to ( $p < 0.1$ ) yields a significant difference for the Wood group compared to the Asphalt and Ground groups with a difference in success rate of over 23.11%. This, combined with the fact that the Ground and Asphalt groups had the lowest success rates, suggests that these were the most difficult groups in the task. These groups exhibit mostly variations in micro features. These features are also ranked lower in the questionnaire as discussed below. Overall, the surfaces in the Ground group are very difficult to identify, and as mentioned above, the time it took to complete the task in the two setups is comparable but relatively long (over two minutes).

Figure 9 includes confusion matrices for the cobblestone and ground groups as these groups had errors in both experimental conditions. While there were a limited number of mistakes in the control trials of the ground group, yet it seems that the types of errors in the ground group are comparable between the setups. Subjects were mainly confused between the first and second surfaces in both conditions. The similarity in the errors between the setups indicates that the feedback provided by the device corresponds to the actual geometric structure. For the cobblestone group, while the type of error is different, the overall success rate is comparable as discussed above.

5.3 Questionnaire

All the questionnaire items, means and standard deviations are presented in Table 3 . In addition, box plots for all items are shown in Figure 10 . The notches in the box plot indicate a 5% significance level around the median and these were used to determine whether a difference between items was significant. All 20 subjects answered all items in the questionnaire. The medians for all categories of the USE questionnaire are five or higher which indicates users feel positive with respect to the device. Users are satisfied with the device; they can use it and learn to use it easily and they find it useful. The results for the items in the Usefulness category are not very high, however, it is important to note that these questions were asked with respect to specific applications shown to the user and the device was not designed specifically for those applications. Moreover, the users did not get a chance to try out those applications. This also relates to item 4.6, the need to have it, which is discussed below. The median for item 1.3 which is much more generic, is much higher than the other three and significantly different from items 1.2 and 1.4 with a difference of one and two in medians respectively. Regarding the Ease of use items, using the device does not seem to be as effortless as hoped (item 2.4) with a median of five, however, the difference with other ease of use items is not significant. Users found that there are some inconsistencies when using the device (item 2.6, median is four) as this item is significantly lower than some items in the category. The results for these two items could be related to one another and potentially caused by a bug we had with some of the subjects in which communication errors required a reboot of the microcontrollers. In contrast to item 2.4, users found the device quite simple to use (item 2.2, median is six) the difference between the two values is not statistically significant. The medians in the Ease of learning category are even higher (medians are all over six), which means that the users find the device easy to learn which could be related to the thorough training phase, however, we also believe it relates to the design of the device which was planned to provide as natural a sensation as possible by mechanically stimulating the mechanoreceptors. The results are high for most items of the satisfaction category, but users do not feel they “need to have it” (item 4.6, median is three) the difference between this item to other satisfaction items is significant. This item could relate to the Usefulness category and indicate that users did not find the suggested use cases important. The highest-ranking item in the entire questionnaire is item 4.3 “it is fun to use” with a median of 6.5 and a mean of 6.51. The difference with other satisfaction items is significant, which suggests that users enjoy using the device implying that the device might be more suited for gaming applications.

In the last items regarding the devices’ performance, the median of the macro features item is much higher than that of the micro features item with a difference in medians of 2.5. The items are significantly different. This corresponds to the asphalt and ground categories in the surface identification section of the experiment and potentially also to the same\not same section. The lower result for the micro features could also be related to the inconsistencies and bug discussed above, the higher variation of this item ( $\sigma = 1.7$ ) also supports this option. Another explanation for the difference could potentially be the phrasing of the micro and macro items. The definition of the micro features is ambiguous, and users might have answered with respect to features that are simply beyond the capabilities of the device. The average of the friction item is 4.26 and the median is 4.0, which is even higher than the micro features item, the difference in medians is 0.5 but these items are not significantly different. This is surprising as the current design does not emulate shear forces directly and was not designed to emulate friction. It is also important to point out the higher variation of this item ( $\sigma = 2$ ). This could potentially mean that the question was a too ambiguous and different people define friction differently. Nevertheless, the users that did “feel” friction probably meant the feeling generated by the high-speed movement of the pins in response to the movement of the finger. A similar mix-up between micro\macro roughness and friction was also observed in previous studies [ 17 , 24 ]. The median of the differentiation item is relatively high (median is 5.5) and corresponds to the quantitative results of the surface identification experiment. This supports the capabilities of the device to emulate complex real-world surfaces and shows that the users were confident in their responses (the users did not know how they performed during the experiment).

6 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

The high recognition rates obtained with HUGO on complex real-world surfaces are very encouraging, yet further improvements are required to fully utilize its potential.

Ergonomics: the experiments we conducted took about two hours per subject. The subjects held the mouse-mounted version of HUGO during most of the experiment. While this is a long continuous period, some subjects complained about discomfort. We believe this is caused by the fact that the device is symmetric to fit both right- and left-handed users. Users needed to place their index finger on the middle of the device which caused discomfort. A new asymmetric design should be tested (like the common designs of computer mouse).

Additional feedback modalities: the presented version of HUGO can generate a broad range of sensations corresponding to the sensing capabilities of the human hand, yet shear forces were not emulated. An updated version of the device, that includes the shear modality, was designed and shown in Figure 11 . Shear sensation was implemented by connecting the silicone membrane covering the pins to three servo motors. The motors move the membrane along the x and y axes, resulting in five DoF (in addition to the 25 pins) denoted by $\vec{\zeta } = ( {{P}_x,{P}_y,{P}_z,\psi ,\theta } )$ . The coordinate system and output variables are shown in Figure 11 . This means we can apply shear forces on the user's finger without comprising the currently validated feedback. The updated design works with both the wearable version and the mouse-based version.

Experiments: while the experiments conducted are substantial, additional experiments should be conducted on both versions of the device. Additional experiments with the mouse-based version should be conducted on a larger set of synthesized surfaces to expand the understanding of the device resolution (mainly for the 3RRS manipulator). The results for the set of surfaces should be compared to printed surfaces sensed by users directly using their hand, similar to what we did in real-world surface differentiation experiments. Moreover, the wearable version was not validated in the current set of experiments. While the actuation mechanism is the same, a different tracking setup might affect the results. Also, some variation might be caused by the fact that the wearable version allows for full 3D interactions, i.e., change finger orientation and height. Lastly, recognition tasks could potentially be resolved by providing the user specific cues (e.g., edges), however, our rendering and control algorithms are not designed to provide such information but rather to emulate the full surface including all information. While the current study provides a valuable benchmark for the performance of the device, and recognition tasks are widely used in haptic device evaluation as cited in the paper, future studies should validate realism as well to provide a more complete understanding for the device's performance.

Additional sensing areas: HUGO is currently designed as a single finger device. While the wearable version can be multiplied and mounted on multiple fingers, the mouse-based version does not have this option. A new prototype with additional feedback areas should be considered to potentially provide an even more immersive experience. The layout and density of these feedback areas should be correlated with the corresponding mechanoreceptors as discussed in [ 5 ].

7 CONCLUSIONS

In this paper we present HUGO, a device capable of conveying the sensations of complex real-world surfaces. The device is based on a simple and scalable technology and was designed according to the mechanoreceptors in the human skin. The device was validated by a set of experiments, (1) synthetic surfaces to gain understanding about the technical capabilities of the device, (2) differentiation between real-world surfaces and (3) a usability questionnaire. The experimental protocol will be made public and offers a new benchmark for tactile feedback of real-world surfaces. In all the experimental steps, users were able to achieve satisfactory results, with recognition accuracy comparable to previous studies that were conducted on sets of simple synthetic surfaces or synthesized 2D outlines.

We believe that HUGO presents a significant leap forward by, to the best of our knowledge, allowing sensing of digital, complex, real-world surfaces for the first time. Users were excited about working with the device and highly enjoyed the interaction. Future generations of the device can be used in a plethora of applications such as e-commerce by conveying the sensation of varied materials and structures of assorted products, it can also be used as a controller with high-resolution haptics for gaming and as a tool for sensory rehabilitation. The device has been shown to experts in those fields who were highly enthusiastic about its capabilities, mostly the ability to emulate a wide range of surfaces without limiting the amount of information provided to the user and without focusing on a specific tactile sensation making it very versatile. Moreover, the fact that the device was designed to mechanically stimulate the mechanoreceptors made it very appealing to physicians and physical therapists working on sensory trauma evaluation and rehabilitation. Research collaborations have been initiated in that field in which the device will be used to render a wide range of surfaces to the patient and by doing so improve the rehabilitation process. The physician will be able to control different parameters such as gains during the process and will also use the device to evaluate the patient's progress. Lastly, due to the wide range of sensations that could be given to the user, the device can be used as a research tool for studying tactile sensing and perception.
