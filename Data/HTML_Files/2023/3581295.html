TexonMask: Facial Expression Recognition Using Textile Electrodes on Commodity Facemasks

Zengrong Guo , Eindhoven University of Technology, Netherlands, z.guo@tue.nl
Rong-Hao Liang , Eindhoven University of Technology, Netherlands, r.liang@tue.nl

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581295
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

This paper presents TexonMask, a facial expression recognition system using lightweight electrode-augmented commodity facemasks. With a matrix of textile electrodes carefully deployed on a commodity mask, our edge computing system recognizes the wearer's facial expressions with machine learning based on the capacitive sensor readings, provides a wearable affective display and communicates with external devices using low bandwidth. Results from user studies show that the system is effective and efficient at recognizing five or ten facial expressions with an accuracy of around $90\%$ , using a personalized classifier trained with only six data points per expression. The system's performance is stable across the use sessions and further improves when more data points are collected. We further developed two LiveEmoji applications for facilitating online and face-to-face communication of facemask wearers, demonstrated them in user interviews, and obtained positive participant feedback. Based on the results and findings of the study, we discuss implications and future research directions for facilitating emotional communication between facemask wearers and others.

1 INTRODUCTION

As people witnessed in the time of pandemics, wearing a facemask effectively reduces the chance of infection based on aerosol transmission [ 22 , 24 ]. However, the facemask also impedes people from observing each other's facial expressions [ 8 ], which are essential for emotional communication.

Since people's first impression is related to an individual's affective display, the social information loss introduced by facemask occlusion may reduce people's trustworthiness and empathy when interacting with a facemask wearer [ 39 ]. Wearing a facemask also makes it harder for the users to speak because it blocks the transmission of sounds with frequencies higher than 1000Hz, making it difficult for the consonants in human language to be effectively conveyed [ 42 , 60 ]. Facemask occlusion also reduces people's empathy in actions that compromise facial mimicry and behavioral synchrony [ 39 ], which is crucial for preschoolers to learn social interactions. People with cognitive impairments such as high-function autism, Asperger syndrome [ 4 ], and anxiety are more difficult to recognize emotions with facemasks [ 45 , 50 ]. The loss of social information is even more significant for people with hearing deficits because a facemask disables their lip-reading [ 39 , 47 ].

Researchers have proposed computer-mediated solutions for unmasking the users’ facial expressions when they are wearing a commodity facemask during their daily activities. For instance, Chen et al. [ 11 ] proposed a wearable facial expression recognition method based on a system with two miniature cameras mounted on the user's ears to track the facemask wearer's facial expressions by observing their face contour. Nonetheless, these cameras usually are sensitive to environmental lighting conditions and may introduce privacy issues to the users themselves and their surroundings [ 59 , 64 ]. Previous researchers also developed systems of wearable sensors [ 33 , 41 , 53 , 62 , 65 ] for recognizing facial expressions in real-time, but these systems required either obtrusive (e.g., on-body) instrumentation on the users or introduced noticeable mechanisms, making them challenging to be adopted into more inclusive and unobtrusive forms for everyday applications.

In this paper, we present TexonMask (Figure 1 ), a non-obtrusive facial expression recognition method using the tex tile electrodes augmented on commodity face mask as a user-friendly wearable facial expression recognition system. Inspired by the guidelines of embedded interaction design [ 28 ], we strive for a minimal augmentation that keeps the original functions, looks, and feels of a commodity facemask. We intentionally keep the facemask a fully passive component without adding any active electronics. With a matrix of lightweight textile electrodes carefully deployed on the surface of a commodity facemask as touch sensors, our system captures the capacitive touch sensor readings as a proxy for detecting the users’ facial muscle movement [ 25 ]. Our system then uses capacitive sensor readings to recognize facial expressions with a simple and lightweight machine learning (ML) classifier that can be used with limited computing resources, such as microcontrollers. This edge ML classifier enables the system to provide a wearable affective display (Figure 2 a) and communicate with external devices using low bandwidth (Figure 2 b).

Figure 3 shows two application examples with an additional display: a computer screen for online conversation (Figure 3 a) or wearable LED matrix for face-to-face conversation (Figure 3 b), the system provides a new channel of real-time facial expression feedback for the facemask wearers to better communication their feelings with others. A LiveEmoji provides an abstract version of facial expression for people who are used to online chat, allowing the users to disambiguate their verbal expressions without taking extra actions (e.g., explicitly saying what they are feeling) and further reduces their dependency on verbal languages [ 71 ]. The emoji display was placed next to the facemask wearer's face so that it does not occlude the user's face or facemask. TexonMask also reliably recognizes a few additional facial expressions (e.g., bulging) that can be used as commands for controlling a mobile device hands-freely.

Our study first explored various materials and fabrication methods to make sure such a sensor augmentation does not compromise the functions, looks, and feels of a facemask, and then parametrically explored several signal processing and machine learning methods to ensure an expressive set of capacitive touch sensor readings can be reliably captured for discrete facial expression recognition. With these methods and obtained parameters, a wearable facial expression recognition system has been carefully implemented and tested according to the design objectives.

Studies have been conducted to assess the system's performance and the application's usefulness when used by human subjects. The first user study shows that the proposed method effectively and efficiently recognized five facial expressions (four emotion-related expressions and one neutral expression) with a mean accuracy of around $90\%$ in real-time across multiple sessions of use and facemask re-wearing, using a personalized classifier trained on only 6 data points per expression. The results also showed that the system could reliably handle three mouth expressions, such as bulging, without affecting the recognition performance of the emotion-related expressions. When more data points were used for training, the mean recognition accuracy further improved to around 95 $\%$ , indicating that the system can continue to improve over time. In the second user study, users also agreed on the motivation of this work and reported positive feedback on the usefulness of the applications in a semi-structured interview. The comfort of wearing was also supported by a series of objective measurements.

The main contribution of this paper is the development of a method for recognizing discrete facial expressions using a commercially available facemask that has been enhanced with textile sensing electrodes. This method enables the facemask to retain its usual look while still being able to identify and respond to the wearer's occluded facial expressions. The paper also describes an edge computing system that enables the facemask to be used for human-computer interaction (HCI) applications.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Related work is discussed in Section 2. Designing and implementation are presented in Section 3. User studies are presented in Section 4. Other applications, limitations, and future work are discussed in Section 5. The conclusion is drawn in Section 6.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Facial Expression vs. Mask Wearing

Facial expression recognition is the basis of emotional understanding that plays an essential role in communication [ 3 ]. Ekman defined six basic facial expressions: happy, angry, surprise, fear, disgust, and sad [ 16 ] that can express emotion, but facemasks that occlude the lower portion of the face may significantly impair their readability [ 5 ], especially for happiness [ 6 ], sadness, and anger [ 17 , 29 ]. Therefore, individuals preferred having a visualization of the wearer's mouth outside the facemask to mitigate facial expression occlusion [ 18 ]. Marini et al. [ 39 ] demonstrated that transparent facemasks can significantly boost the ability to detect emotional expressions, except for neutral ones; however, these masks are still not a commonly available product. Face shields could provide better visibility of facial expressions [ 46 ], but they are less effective than cloth masks in the prevention of aerosol exposure and source control [ 67 ].

2.2 Facial Expression Recognition for Facilitating Interpersonal Communication

Most computer-mediated facial expression recognition solutions are camera-based methods. Researchers address the challenge of facial expression recognition under facemask occlusion by adding an attention mechanism in their model training process [ 12 , 35 ] or using a new local feature descriptor  [ 13 ]. For mobile applications, researchers proposed mounting two miniature cameras mounted on the user's ears [ 11 ] or a neckband [ 10 ] to track the facemask wearer's facial expressions by observing part of their face so they don't have to put a camera in front of their face. Still, these camera-based methods may introduce privacy issues to the users themselves and their surroundings [ 20 ]. Researchers also applied sensor-based methods for camera-free facial expression recognition using Electromyography (EMG) [ 19 ] with sticky electrodes, head-mounted devices based on capacitive sensing [ 53 ] or acoustic interferometry [ 23 ], piezoelectric sensing [ 57 ] or strain sensing [ 34 ]. Less obtrusive forms of wearables such as eyewear [ 52 , 54 ], and (in-ear) earphones [ 40 , 66 ] were also considered, but they are still considered extra wearables that burden the user adaptation.

2.3 Facemask Augmentation for Facilitating Interpersonal Communication

Researchers appropriate sensor-embedded facemasks for facial expression recognition by incorporating capacitive sensing [ 62 ], camera  [ 41 , 65 ], photo-reflective sensing [ 30 , 33 ], triboelectric sensing [ 38 ], accelerometry and IMU  [ 31 , 41 ] inside the facemask, and put a visual display directly outside the facemask, as summarized in Table 1 . However, their looks and feels deviate from a commodity facemask as they are no longer lightweight, soft, stretchable, and washable. Their rigid component and structure not only make them uncomfortable to wear but also may restrict facial movements. Furthermore, having some electronic components exposed outside of the facemask may reduce their social acceptability [ 27 ].

3 DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION

3.1 Design Considerations

According to the guidelines for designing for embedded interaction [ 28 ], we formulate three design considerations for the TexonMask, a specialized input device in a daily wearable form for sensing facial expression, and discuss the requirements to fulfill. These requirements are:

  • (C1) A TexonMask should keep a facemask's original functions, looks, and feels. The device will be worn and used in both personal and social contexts, so all the technological elements should be invisibly, comfortably, and seamlessly integrated into the facemask to support the wearer in keeping their existing personal and social activities. The facemask should also stay effective in its filtration capability.
  • (C2) A TexonMask should support both short-term and long-term life cycles of use. The device has to be easy to use and maintain. Therefore, the facemask should be lightweight to put on and take off. The material chosen should be low-cost and sustainable enough to keep the facemask disposable and washable.
  • (C3) A TexonMask should support on-demand uses. The device should leave the users to decide when to use it. Therefore, it should provide a lightweight and accessible activation mechanism with clear visual feedback, which should be located close to the face yet without occluding it. On the other hand, it is supposed to behave like a normal facemask when it is not in use. Last but not least, the TexonMask should be reliable enough during the operation so the users want to activate and use it.

Based on these considerations, we argue that a system that decouples the form of an electrode-augmented facemask from the computing components is a more practical approach, as shown in Figure 1 . Unlike a fully integrated facemask design like FaceBit [ 14 ], our approach keeps the original looks and feels ( C1 ) as it does not require mounting all the necessary yet bulky components, such as a battery or energy harvester, a signal aggregator, and/or an optional information display directly on the facemask itself. With these design considerations as requirements, we conducted two iterations of design, implementation, and evaluation.

3.2 Iteration 1: Concept Proofing

The first iteration aims at concept proofing. We first referred to Suzuki et al. [ 62 ] and prototyped a simple capacitive sensing facemask with vinyl-cut copper-foil electrodes because they can be invisibly integrated into a facemask ( C1 ) and lightweight enough to keep the facemask disposable ( C2 ).

The positions of the electrodes on the facemask are crucial because the facial expressions are driven by the facial muscles. We first identify the muscles of a greater influence on expressions based on the current studies on the analysis of facial expressions and muscle movements [ 25 , 70 ] and summarized facial muscles involved in the four basic emotion-related expressions: happy, sad, surprised, and angry, that is frequently used in social media (e.g., Facebook) yet have been reported to be difficult to read while one is wearing a facemask [ 6 , 17 , 29 ], as shown in Figure 4 a 1 . An electrode pattern (Figure 4 b) is therefore designed and fabricated using a Brother CM300 vinyl cutter, and deployed on the surface of a commodity facemask, as shown in Figure 4 c. Paper tape was used to insulate the contact between the electrodes and the user's face for comfort.

A Teensy 3.2 microcontroller was used for data collection. Each of the 12 built-in touch-sensing pins is connected to one of the electrodes deployed on the facemask. The capacitive sensor readings were formed into a 12-dimensional feature vector and sent to a PC through a serial connection for further analysis.

Evaluation . The first author wore the facemask and performed each of the five facial expressions (happy, sad, surprised, angry, and neutral). Every session of 5 (expression) × 5 (repetition) × 10 (sample) = 250 data points of data was collected in a 5 × 5 Latin-Square order. The facemask was also taken off and put on after every data collection session to simulate multiple uses. A total of 8 (session) × 250 = 2000 data points were collected and made a uniform 50-50 train-test split for evaluation.

Results show that a linear support vector machine (SVM) classifier ( C = 1) model with 100% training accuracy achieved 88.4% accuracy on the test set. The confusion matrix (Figure 4 d) shows that the classifier correctly recognized most of the happy, sad, and angry cases. False recognition mostly occurs between neutral and surprised expressions. In surprised cases, we observed that the nose lifts the facemask so the user's mouth movements were not always clearly captured by the facemask. Some neutral cases were also recognized as sad because the difference between neutral and sad faces is more subtle than other combinations.

Discussion . Although the results show the feasibility of recognizing facial expressions using this proof-of-concept prototype, the sensitivity is limited for some expressions due to the limited amount and density of the electrodes, which failed to meet our design requirement C3 . Also, the copper-foil electrodes were not comfortable to wear after an extended period of data collection, which also failed to meet our design requirements C1 and C2 .

3.3 Iteration 2: Improving Reliability and Wearability

The second iteration aims at 1) improving the reliability of facial expression recognition by increasing the amount and density of electrodes and 2) improving the facemask's wearability by considering alternative materials and fabrication methods in order to fulfill the design requirements.

Material . Regarding the materials used for fabrication, conductive threads were chosen over other options, such as stretchable printed circuits [ 43 ] or conductive fabrics [ 7 ], for several reasons. First, it is possible to directly solder the conductive threads to the interface of the microcontroller without adding rigid and bulky connectors, so it is easier to keep the look and feel of the facemask. Second, compared to highly conductive fabrics that also support soldering and washability, such as the Zelt Fabrics used in [ 7 ], conductive yarn is lightweight and more stretchable.

The conductive yarn we chose for fabricating the sensing electrodes is DataStretch 2 , which is a low-ohmic (4Ω/ m ) stretchable and semi-insulated conductive thread. This yarn has a diameter of 0.2mm and consists of 4-strand enameled wires insulated by a combination of Elasthane and Polyacrylamide. It is able to stretch up to $30\%$ without altering its resistance.

Electrode Design and Implementation . To effectively and efficiently increase the amount and density of electrodes, we applied an electrode matrix design that decoupled the transmitters and receivers from the rows and columns. We initially considered the classical two-layered diamond pattern [ 15 ] as it provides a higher signal-to-noise ratio and can be fabricated using conductive yarn as well [ 1 ]. However, the diamond pattern design also compromises the comfort of wearing as it makes the facemask less flexible and less stretchable. Therefore, we opt for a warped two-dimensional matrix layout to fit the curved area of a commodity facemask.

Fabrication Methods . With a commodity cloth facemask that has two layers of fabrics, inner and outer layers, three fabrication methods were applied for fabrication, as shown in Figure 5 :

  1. Hand-sewn Surface Wiring : With manual sewing, the textile electrodes were floating on the surface of the inner layer of the facemask. The textile electrodes were fixed by tiny pieces of facemask fabric provoked by a needle without punching through the outer layer. This fabrication method provides the lowest entry threshold because only a needle is needed.
  2. Hand-sewn Internal Wiring : With manual sewing, the textile electrodes were sandwiched between the inner and the outer layers of the facemask without punching through the outer layer. This method maximizes the comfort of wearing as there is no direct contact between the textile electrodes and the user's face.
  3. Machine-sewn Surface Wiring : With machine sewing, the textile electrodes were floating on the surface of the inner layer of the facemask. Cotton threads and conductive yarn were used as upper and bobbin threads, respectively. This method provides the highest speed and repeatability with a digital embroidery machine.

Figure 5 also shows comparable signals collected from the three samples fabricated in different methods yet made in the same size and used by the same user. Regarding fabrication speed, machine-sewing is the fastest, and surface wiring is faster than internal wiring. However, machine-sewing compromised the function of the facemask ( C1 ) as it may reduce the facemask's filtration effectiveness with the upper thread punched through both layers of fabric. Hence, we mainly focus on the hand-sewn surface wiring method in our later evaluation.

Overall, all three methods keep the original looks and feels of a commodity facemask ( C1 ). The textile electrodes keep the facemask flexible, stretchable, and comfortable to wear. With an 8 × 15 textile electrode matrix hand-sewn on the surface of a commodity facemask, as the sample shown in Figure 5 a, the weight of the facemask only slightly increased from 8g to 9g after modification.

Capacitive Sensing Hardware . We initially implemented the projected multi-touch toolkit provided by Pourjafarian et al. [ 49 ], but we did not get a clear signal because of the electrode pattern we used. Therefore, we used the FocalTech FT5316DME capacitive multitouch controller with the MuCa 3 breakout and the drivers developed by Teyssier et al. [ 63 ]. An Arduino-compatible Adafruit Feather M0 WiFi w/ATWINC1500 board was used for data collection. With a 500mAh LiPo battery, this WiFi solution supports up to 2 hours of mobile use with a smartphone serving as an access point. The signal processing unit supports up to 21 TX lines and 12 RX lines and returns clear signals of mutual capacitive multi-touch sensing in real-time (> 15 Hz ). Finally, we make a necklace closure to contain all the electronic components and support wireless use to make it easy to use and maintain ( C2 ), as shown in Figure 1 .

Signal Processing . The signal processing pipeline is depicted in Figure 2 . The readings from the capacitive sensors on the facemask are arranged into an N r × N c -dimensional vector V = [ v r , c ], where 1 ≤ r ≤ N r and 1 ≤ c ≤ N c . The background is obtained by averaging the readings from the sensors when the user is wearing the facemask with a neutral facial expression, resulting in a reference frame $B = \overline{V_k}$ , where 1 ≤ k ≤ K . The background-subtracted feature vector X = V − B is then sent to a supervised machine learning classifier f . The classifier has been trained on a collection of labelled data D i = [ X i , y i ], where y i is a facial expression type associated with the feature vector X i . For prediction, every value x r , c in the feature vector X was first min-max scaled to $x^{\prime }_{r,c}$ using the range of the training data, min r , c and max r , c , and then the scaled vector X ′ is passed to the classifier f to obtain the classification result $\hat{y} = f(X^{\prime })$ . This result is then used to determine the display pattern or sent to external devices through a wired or wireless connection.

Model Training and Deployment . Due to the limited computing and memory capabilities of microcontrollers, it is more practical to train and deploy machine learning models using an external computer. To facilitate this, a model training and deployment workflow has been developed, involving the microcontroller streaming sensor data to the model training software running on a computer, collecting facial expression data, training the model to a format readable by the microcontroller, and uploading an Arduino sketch with the necessary code to the microcontroller.

3.4 Applications Examples: Live Emoji

To support on-demand uses ( C3 ) in the user's personal and social contexts, we implement two Live emoji applications to exemplify how TexonMask can mediate interpersonal communication in online and offline conversion scenarios.

Live Emoji for Online Conversation . When a user is wearing a facemask during video conferencing, the live emoji is showing as a call-out bubble in proximity to the user's face to enhance the facemask wearer's facial expression (Figure 3 a). The type of emoji can be customized according to the user's gender, skin color, and facial features as the Apple Memoji 4 . The user can enable or disable the emoji display using an on-screen switch in the GUI.

Live Emoji for Face-to-Face Conversation . When a user is having a face-to-face conversation with others, an always-on live emoji is showing on the necklace mounted with an LED matrix to enhance the facemask wearer's facial expression during the conversation (Figure 3 b). In our example implementation, a low-resolution (8 × 8) yet flexible WS2812B LED matrix is connected to the signal aggregation module to provide a live emoji for face-to-face communication. A higher-resolution emoji can be displayed by using an OLED module. The user can enable or disable the emoji display using a switch on the necklace.

3.5 Technical Evaluation

Before we evaluated the facemask design with users, we conducted a series of technical evaluations to identify the study parameters and know whether the implementation fulfilled our requirements.

Electrode Resolution . According to the facemask's aspect ratio, we fabricated three facemasks with an electrode density of 6 × 11, 8 × 15, 10 × 19 using the hand-sewn surface wiring method and used them with the aforementioned hardware as the experimental apparatus, but all the sensor data were streamed to a PC for analysis. The first author wore each of the three facemasks and performed a set of five emotion-related facial expressions (Figure 6 a), including the neutral and four emotion-related facial expressions (happy, sad, surprised, and angry). Additionally, the first author also chose another set of five additional facial expressions (Figure 6 b) that are straightforward to perform, including three mouth expressions (bulging, left bulging, right bulging) and two eye expressions (left winking, right winking) related to cheek movements.

Every session consists of 2 (set) × 5 (expression) × 5 (repetition) × 50 (sample) = 2500 data points, where the collected in a Latin-square order, where 50 samples were taken for each condition during the normal breathing cycles (around 8 seconds) to let the algorithm learn the signal variation during breathing. The subject also took off and put on the facemask after each data session to simulate multiple uses. A total of 3 (resolution) × 4 (session) × 2500 = 30000 data points were collected and made a uniform 80-20 train-test split.

Results show a linear SVM classifier ( C =1) with a 100% training accuracy achieved a test accuracy of 100%, 100%, and 99.9% on the 6 × 11, 8 × 15, 10 × 19 samples, respectively. The visualization shown in Figure 7 portrays a clear improvement from the prototype used in iteration 1 as the subtle features were better captured by the increasing amount and density of the electrode matrix.

Signal Stability and Sensitivity . Signal stability was first tested by measuring the quiescent noise level. A facemask with hand-sewn surface wiring 8 × 15 electrodes was used for testing. We connected the facemask to the PC through a signal aggregation device, laid the facemask on the table, and then collected 10 minutes, 3500 frames of raw data at an average of ∼ 5.8Hz. After the collection, we calculated the standard deviation SD ( v r , c ) of every electrode (1 ≤ r ≤ 8, 1 ≤ c ≤ 15). The mean of all the SD ( v r , c ) is $M_{noise}=1.42~(SD_{noise} = 0.228)$ .

Then, signal sensitivity was tested by a female participant (21 years old) to perform a round of ten facial expressions (Figure 6 ) in the Latin square order described in the Electrode Resolution study, which also took about 10 minutes. After the collection, we retrieved maximum max r , c and minimum min r , c values of every electrode (1 ≤ r ≤ 8, 1 ≤ c ≤ 15), and found the maximum difference of all d r , c = max r , c − min r , c is Max ( d r , c ) = 528, which is about 372 times of M noise . These results suggest promising sensitivity and stability of this sample over 10 minutes time span.

Breathability of Modified Facemasks . To support short-term use ( C2 ), we tested whether facemasks are comfortable to wear after the modification. Breathability is the measure that refers to the resistance that a facemask wearer experiences during inhalation and exhalation, which is characterized as the difference in pressure across the facemask and is typically reported in Pascals (Pa) [ 44 ]. To understand whether adding textile electrodes affected the breathability of the facemask, we did a brief lab measurement using an experimental apparatus made of a NaCl pump, a Plexiglas chamber, and a barometric sensor. We followed a standard procedure by attaching the facemask to the tube, filling up the compressor, opening the valve, and then recording the differential barometric data in the first two minutes. Two types of commodity facemasks were used in the test. Four conditions, including the unmodified and the three fabrication methods (Figure 5 ), were tested. A total of 2 (type) × 4 (conditions) × 3 = 48 measurements were performed. Results show that the modifications increased the breathability of both types of original facemasks. The mean breathability of the two types of facemasks increased from 7.52 Pa / cm 2 (unmodified) to 9.386 pa / cm 2 for hand-sewn surface wiring, 10.42 Pa / cm 2 for hand-sewn internal wiring, and 10.71 Pa / cm 2 (machine-sewn surface wiring. These results are all lower than the World Health Organization's recommendation: < 40 Pa / cm 2 of maximum differential pressure for an adult using medical facemasks [ 44 ], showing that it is safe and comfortable to be worn for user testing.

Washability . To support long-term use ( C2 ), we conducted a preliminary washability test [ 55 ] with a facemask made by hand-sewn surface wiring. In every cycle, the facemask was put in a water basin, soaked into water, rubbed with hands about ten times, and then twisted and laid flat until it was dry. We found that the sample functioned properly without any broken electrodes after six rounds of tests.

4 USER STUDIES

Two user studies were conducted to evaluate the proposed system. The first study is a technical evaluation to understand the system's performance in recognizing the users’ facial expressions with our implementation. The second study is a semi-structured interview for gathering feedback on the proposed LiveEmoji applications.

4.1 Study 1: Facial Expression Recognition

4.1.1 Methods.

Participants and Apparatus . Eight subjects (3 males and 5 females), ranging in age from 21 to 33 years old ( M = 25.88, SD = 4.08) were recruited in the study. With hygiene and health considerations, each sensing facemask was only used by one subject during the study. We fabricated one facemask for each participant according to their face dimensions, as the experimental apparatus shown in Figure 8 . Six (out of 8) participants (3 males and 3 females) used a customized facemask made of the hand-sewn surface wiring method as they are the easiest to fabricate, while another two participants used a facemask made of different methods. The 8 × 15 setting of electrodes was chosen to ensure a participant with a larger or smaller face gets sufficient electrode density for facial recognition. During the experiment, each facemask was connected to the same data aggregator and the same software for data collection. All participants were recruited from the same region by snowball sampling and considered healthy if they had no physical or mental impairments that limited their participation. Every participant has consented to the study procedures and the data collection protocols, which were also approved by the local Ethical Review Board, and have complied with the national and regional pandemic policy.

Procedures . Similar to the previous technical evaluation, each participant was asked to wear their facemask and collect two sets of five facial expressions. One set includes emotion-related facial expressions (Figure 6 a): neutral, happy, sad, surprised, and angry. Another set includes three mouth expressions (bulging, left bulging, right bulging) and two eye expressions (left winking, right winking) related to cheek movements (Figure 6 b).

The study involved collecting 1250 data points for each of the 5 sessions, with 5 repetitions each, for a total of 6250 data points per expression. This was repeated for 5 expressions, resulting in a dataset of 5 × 6250 = 31250 data points. A second data set was also collected with another 5 expressions and then merged into the first one, resulting in a total of 10 × 6250 = 62500 data points. To simulate practical use, participants were asked to remove and wear the face mask between each session. Each expression was collected in a 5 × 5 Latin-square order in each session and 50 samples were taken for each condition during the normal breathing cycles (around 8 seconds) to feature the signal variation during breathing. A total of 8 (subject) × 12500 = 100000 data points were collected. Each subject's data collection took approximately one hour, followed by a brief interview to gain insight into their experience.

4.1.2 Personalized Classifier Performance across Sessions of Use. The train-test split was first determined by leaving out a specific number of sessions for testing and using the remaining sessions for training. This was done for all possible combinations of N sessions being left out, and the method is called leave-N-session-out (LNSO) in this paper. This approach accounts for the ordering and learning effects of the users and ensures that no samples from the same repetition are included in both the test and training sets.

The results of the leave- N -session-out cross-validation are presented in Table 2 . Among all values of N from 1 to 4, the most representative for across-session use are N = 2 and N = 3, as both of these cases include face mask removals in both the training and test datasets. The leave-2-session-out (L2SO) cross-validation consists of $\binom{4}{2} = 10$ rounds, each of which involves a unique combination of 3 and 2 sessions for training and testing, respectively. The results show a linear SVM classifier ( C = 1, default parameter) achieved a mean accuracy of 94.52% (SD= 5.53%) and 94.72% (SD= 4.32%) for 5 and 10 expressions, respectively. No single expression had a mean F1 score of less than 0.90 for either the 5 or 10 expression conditions. Welch's t-tests also showed no significant differences in accuracy between the L2SO and the L3SO cross-validation methods in both 5-expressions ( t (13.5) = 0.53, p = 0.60, Cohen's d = 0.26) and 10-expressions conditions ( t (13) = 1, p = 0.34, Cohen's d = 0.50). Therefore, we chose L2SO cross-validation as the baseline performance for further analysis.

To reduce the burden of per-user training, we simulate shorter training sessions [ r , s ] by only using the first r repetitions (out of 5) from each session and the first s samples (out of 50) from each repetition to train the classifiers. These trained classifiers are then tested on the full test set, which consists of 2 × 5 × 50 = 500 data points per expression, as in the previous case. Figure 10 presents the key findings from the L2SO cross-validation with 5 and 10 expressions. Welch's t-tests showed no significant effects ( p > 0.05) in the accuracy between every [2 ≤ r ≤ 5, 1 ≤ s ≤ 50] condition and their baseline [ r = 5, s = 50] condition, which used 750 datapoints per expression for training the classifier. The most economical training set, [ r = 2, s = 1], requires only 3 sessions × 2 repetitions × 1 sample = 6 data points per expression for training a linear SVM classifier (C=1) that achieved a mean accuracy of 89.57% (SD= 9.68%) and 90.25% (SD= 6.16%) for 5 and 10 expressions, respectively. Collecting more samples per repetition, as the [ r = 2, s = 50] condition highlighted in Figure 10 , also increases accuracy without requiring the users to perform more repetitions.

Figure 11 shows the confusion matrices of the classifiers discussed above. In the 5-expression condition (Figure 11 a), a [ r = 2, s = 1] linear SVM classifier (C=1) reliably classified both neutral and happy with a mean accuracy of $96\%$ . About $12\%$ of sad expressions, $8\%$ of surprise expressions, and $9\%$ of angry expressions were misclassified as neutral, which may not cause any issues in social situations. The rest of the false classifications were all less than $4\%$ . In the 10-expression condition (Figure 11 d), a [ r = 2, s = 1] linear SVM classifier (C=1) correctly classified neutral and happy with a mean accuracy of $95\%$ . In the dataset, $11\%$ of sad expressions, $7\%$ of angry expressions, and $7\%$ of angry expressions were misclassified as neutral. This is unlikely to be an issue in social settings. Regarding the five additional mouth and eye expressions, the three mouth expressions (bulging, left bulging, and right bulging) were all reliably classified with less than $2\%$ confusion with the emotion-related expressions. In contrast, the eye expressions (left winking and right winking) are confused more with the emotion-related expressions. The rest of the false classifications were all less than $4\%$ . Increasing the number of samples, as in the case where [ r = 2, s = 50] in Figure 11 b, or adding repetitions, as in the case where [ r = 5, s = 50] in Figures 11 c and 11 e, can improve the recall for every facial expression.

4.1.3 Cross-User Model Performance. To determine whether the system allows a user to be excluded from the training process, we applied leave-one-subject-out cross-validation to test the feasibility of using a cross-user classifier.

We first tested the data collected from six subjects (S[2-7]) who used facemasks fabricated using the same method (hand-sewn surface wiring) by training a classifier on the combined data from the other five subjects and testing it on each of the six subjects’ data, which consisted of 5 sessions, 5 repetitions, 50 samples, and 5 or 10 expressions. However, we found the results unsatisfactory. As shown in Table 4 , a kernel SVM classifier (RBF, C = 1, γ = 0.1) recognized five expressions with a mean accuracy of $M = 69.39\%$ ( $SD = 21.11\%$ ) and a mean F1-score of M = 0.652 ( SD = 0.121). The kernel SVM classifier (RBF, C = 1, γ = 0.1) also recognized five expressions with a mean accuracy of $M = 68.77\%$ ( $SD = 21.18\%$ ) and a mean F1-score of M = 0.668 ( SD = 0.148). The linear SVM classifier did not perform as well as the kernel SVM classifier, but it was much faster to train and make predictions.

Table 4 also shows that the recognition was unstable among the users, as indicated by the large standard deviation in performance. For example, in the 5-expression case, user S4 achieved an accuracy of 99.97%, but user S2 only achieved 46.50%; in the 10-expression case, S4 still achieved an accuracy of 93.63%, but S2 only achieved 37.22%. The confusion matrices shown in Figure 12 further indicate that no single expression stood out in the classification. In addition to the false negatives that misclassified emotions as neutral, a significant amount of confusion was observed between emotions such as happy and angry, which is less appropriate in social contexts. Mouth expressions, such as bulging, were more reliable in recall, but their sensitivity was still unsatisfactory.

For completeness, we also tested the performance of our classifiers on two subjects (S1 and S8) who used facemasks made using different methods than the other six subjects (S[2-7]) who used facemasks made using the hand-sewn surface wiring method. We trained a classifier on the combined data from S[2-7] and test it with the data from S1 or S8. Unfortunately, none of these cross-fabrication-method validations achieved a mean accuracy higher than $50\%$ or an F1 score higher than 0.5, indicating that the trained classifiers cannot be generalized to facemasks made using different methods at this time.

4.1.4 User Experience. Seven (out of 8) subjects reported no abnormalities after completing the one-hour data collection. S5 reported her face was a little itchy after being rubbed by the facemask a few times. S1 indicated that the Hand-sewn Internal Wiring sample was very comfortable.

4.1.5 Summary. Our facial recognition method is reliable ( C1 ) in our test cases if users are willing to train a personal classifier themselves. This is shown by the consistently high accuracy of personalized classifiers, despite the limited subject size (8 users). The personalized linear SVM classifier achieved a mean accuracy of around $90\%$ with only 6 data points per expression, and is lightweight enough to be used on a micro-controller for real-time prediction. As the number of data points used for training increases, the mean accuracy of the system rises to approximately $95\%$ , suggesting that it can become more accurate with further training.

The average accuracy of cross-user classifiers was roughly $70\%$ , with significant differences between users, making it difficult to determine if a trained model can accurately identify the facial expressions of new users. Previous research [ 66 ] has suggested that this may be due to individual preferences in performing facial expressions, as well as factors such as gender, region, and obesity that affect face dimensions [ 37 , 69 ]. However, this hypothesis needs further investigation.

4.2 Study 2: LiveEmoji

Remote interviews were conducted to gain insight into people's opinions on design of the two LiveEmoji applications.

4.2.1 Methods. Participants . 12 participants (6 males and 6 females), ranging in age from 23 to 31 years old ( M = 25.75, SD = 2.99) were recruited in the study. Everyone has experienced wearing facemasks during the last few years. Every participant has consented to the study procedures and the data collection protocols which were also approved by the local Ethical Review Board and have complied with the national and regional pandemic policy.

Procedures . The interview is conducted remotely using Microsoft Teams with both the researcher's and every participant's webcam open. The participants were first asked to fill in a pre-questionnaire, which collected their previous experiences of wearing a facemask during interpersonal conversations. Then, the researcher introduced the TexonMask system, how to use the system, and the two LiveEmoji applications to the users. Then, the participants were asked to fill in a short version of the user experience questionnaire (UEQ-S) [ 58 ], which provides a brief yet meaningful measure of the user's perception on a product's pragmatic and hedonic qualities. It consists of 8 items and each item consists of a pair of terms with opposite meanings, and participants rated each item on a 7-point Likert scale. Then, we conducted a semi-structured interview with the participants to know what they liked or disliked about the LiveEmoji applications, their reasons, and suggestions.

4.2.2 Results. Pre-questionnaire . The results show that the participants generally agreed on communication problems when they wore facemasks. Participants agreed that facial expressions have great effects on their communication ( M = 4.33, SD = 0.49). The facial occlusion affected their recognition of others’ facial expressions ( M = 4.08, SD = 0.51) and sometimes they couldn't clearly recognize their facial expressions ( M = 3.67, SD = 0.89). They also felt they might misunderstand their facial expressions ( M = 3.42, SD = 1.16) and hoped to quickly distinguish the facial expressions covered by facemasks ( M = 3.92, SD = 0.90). They also agreed that they felt their kind expressions (such as smiles) were hard to be recognized by people during face-to-face communication ( M = 3.75, SD = 0.75). They also felt that wearing facemasks makes people become less enthusiastic than before, and social distance has increased ( M = 3.75, SD = 0.75). Last but not least, participants agreed that real-time feedback on their facial expressions can improve their communication effectiveness ( M = 4.25, SD = 0.62) and would like to give it a try when there is a solution ( M = 4.12, SD = 0.58).

User Experience Questionnaire - Short Version . In the UEQ-S quesionnaire, participants rate each item on a 7-point Likert scale. The answers are scaled from -3 (fully agree with the negative term) to +3 (fully agree with the positive term). Results of 12 participants’ UEQ-S and the terms of each item are listed in Figure 13 a. The participants found this product clear ( M = 2.1), inventive ( M = 2.2), and interesting ( M = 2.4). Figure 13 b shows the scale means of each participant. The mean values of these participants’ UEQ-S on the pragmatic quality scale ( M = 1.250), hedonic quality scale ( M = 2.000), and overall are positive ( M = 1.625).

Interview . Regarding the LiveEmoji for online conversation, P1 stated that “I think this system will be very useful for the people who need to have an online meeting in a public place like a lab and have to wear facemasks” . Participants like this intuitive way of expressing their facial expression, for example, “The thing I like is the expression is very direct (P2),” “I like it, and it can show the direct expression on the screen about feelings (P6).” P3 and P10 stated that it could also make the atmosphere of online meetings more relaxed and fun. Nonetheless, P7 stated it might be more suitable for casual chatting, and P11 also mentioned that “What I dislike is, if this is a very formal meeting, maybe it is not appropriate to use it” , which resonates with P11’s neutral rating on the pragmatic quality. P6 mentioned that he likes the switch set on the interface for users to decide whether or not to use the emoji during the meeting. Participants further suggested the future version to develop the displayed elements for more formal situations (P7), set the emoji in the position of the facemask (P8), express her own face pictures instead of the emoji (P2), add a function to only show positive emotions (P10), and detect more subtle expressions (P11).

Regarding the LiveEmoji for face-to-face conversation, the participants liked the clear feedback and the form of decoration, for example, “I like this design because it shows our emotions directly and increases the interaction of people's communication (P5),” “I like that small emoji matrix is very cute as a necklace (P2).” P11 mentioned it could be helpful for the special group who have problems with emotion recognition or expression. Nonetheless, some participants worried about the potential misinterpretation issue (P8), and it may amplify the expression of moods (P12) or distract the counterparts’ attention (P1). P2 disliked the expression feedback is always in red, and that's why P2 rated neutral on the pragmatic quality scale of UEQ-S. Participants also mentioned the design of the feedback part using the LED matrix is not aesthetic enough (P3, P6, P12). Participants also suggested the future version combine the color with the emotions for feedback (P2, P3, P5, P6), develop a non-obvious feedback method (P1, P6), and further design the feedback part into miniature and fancy ornaments (P2, P4, P7) or as a sticker to patch on users’ clothes (P9).

In addition to these two applications, the users also wanted to use the systems for online or offline presentations (P12), parties (P12), real-time feedback for lectures (P1), game playing (P5), virtual reality (P7), controlling IoT devices (P8), and healthcare (P12). Overall, the participants perceived the system to be valuable and beneficial in everyday situations.

5 DISCUSSION

In this session, we discuss more potential applications of TexonMask as well as the current limitations and future work.

More Applications and Implications for Embedded Interaction . TexonMask is a wearable device that reliably captures facial expressions, which can be used as an accessory for controlling mobile devices (e.g., phone, head-mount display) in a hands-free manner. For instance, in instant messaging, a TexonMask user can type an emoji with their face so that they can stay on their current keyboard and keep typing; meanwhile, they can control their music playlist by bulging their cheeks without sending a wrong emoji. Nonetheless, the way we trained and used the system only ensures a TexonMask reliably recognizes the explicit, intended actions (e.g., a user intended to send an emoji) rather than the implicit emotions represented by a facial expression that a user posed unintentionally or a system never seen before. In future work, allowing the end users to customize multiple ways to express an emoji (i.e., over-provisioning [ 28 ]) may increase the fluency and richness of expression. Recognizing human emotion behind these unintentional facial movements can provide useful context for an environment deployed ambient intelligence, such as moderating the room light according to the user's mood.

Beyond Discrete Facial Expressions . Multiple emotions are expressed at once through a face when one has mixed feelings. This feature can be straightforwardly implemented by showing the top K recalls of the classification results, but presenting the results in a social context is more challenging than showing an emoji. Another potential direction for further research is to continuously reconstruct facial expressions and present the results in a similar manner to Apple Memojis, which could provide more expressive affective information than static emojis. This could be achieved by obtaining rich and reliable ground truth data for training the machine learning model, such as using a camera to capture facial landmarks [ 51 ] during data collection. The facial landmark features could enable the model to accurately reconstruct facial expressions in real-time.

Machine Learning vs. Deep Learning . The signal processing and machine learning methods used in TexonMask are effective, but not the most advanced. Our intention was to show the usefulness and effectiveness of the sensors in the context of wearable and edge computing [ 56 ] rather than relying on complex and costly techniques that would have required more data and computing power. The TexonMask system is kept simple and lightweight so it can be used with limited computing resources, such as microcontrollers and microprocessors, to make it easier to deploy and optimize in the future. On the other hand, we did evaluate our data collection with a convolutional neural network (CNN) classifier based on MobileNet [ 21 ], which is lightweight and efficient for mobile applications, but it did take a much longer time for training. We also evaluated our datasets collected from user study 1 with personalized MobileNet V1 classifiers (30 epochs) and found the mean accuracy ( $M=89.39\%, SD=8.31\%$ ) lower than the SVM classifier, even after a few iterations of multi-hour training and hyperparameter tuning, which took a few days in total. We still believe that a CNN can better handle spatial features in the capacitive image than traditional learning algorithms [ 32 ], and we have open-sourced our experiment data set to support further research on deep learning techniques.

Personalized Training vs. Cross-User Model . Since a facemask is a personal device, we believe and argue a one-time personalized training, such as the FaceID for unlocking a smartphone, is not a serious bottleneck if the model provides promising reliability and stability. Personalized training is also necessary for individuals with facial disorders, such as Bell's palsy, which can cause unusual facial expressions that cannot be accurately classified by a general model. Our study results indicate that it is feasible to train a reliable model using a short training session. The results also suggest that unusual and imbalanced facial expressions, such as left bulging and right bulging, can be reliably recognized. The model can be easily personalized with minimal training, allowing it to classify a broader range of facial expressions. Still, our present workflow of model training and deployment depends on an external computer, which can impede its access to persistently train the model to adjust to emerging situations or facial expressions. MCUNet [ 36 ], which supports on-device training on microcontrollers, could be a viable option for further developing our results while eliminating the dependency on an external computer.

Training a cross-user model for recognizing the facial recognition of unseen users is still desirable because it neither requires an end user to train their system. However, it is still challenging for our low-resolution textile electrode array. Let alone the individual preference of performing different facial expressions, the individual differences in the user's gender, region, and obesity significantly affect their face dimensions [ 37 , 69 ] are still significant. Involve a diverse set of users in the training process may also enable the training of a more inclusive classifier that further improves the robustness of a cross-user classifier.

Conditions that Influence Sensing . Although capacitive sensing has low hysteresis [ 2 ] and is stable over time as shown in pilot study 2, the user's skin and the environmental condition may not be. Even for the same user, the signal quality may also vary across different skin conditions (e.g., sweating), body grounding conditions (e.g., leaning on a metal plate), climate conditions (e.g., air humidity), and so on. These conditions were not featured in our studies but they are common in real life. We suggest that future work should implement a manual training button on the data aggregation module to facilitate the adaptation of the current classifier to new circumstances using transfer learning.

The influence of breathing on facial expression recognition was considered in our user study. The results suggest that featuring the facial expression over a full breathing cycle (i.e., s=50) can improve recognition accuracy. However, intensive body movements or breathing can affect the stability of the recognition system, particularly when the user is wearing a tight mask. Our experimental data was collected while the subjects were in a sedentary state (i.e., sitting). Future work could include data from subjects in different body postures and movements to ensure that the recognition system remains stable during daily activities.

Fabrication of TaxonMask . Providing rapid prototyping tools also enable end-users to create their applications for the embedded interactions [ 28 ]. The gridded textile electrodes were sewn on a curved mask in our study because the main purpose of making them was to quickly customize the mask for individuals for evaluation. Sewing is a low-threshold prototyping method useful for researchers who want to replicate our results.

For greater scalability and repeatability, we recommend that future work integrate textile electrodes by weaving the conductive yarn into the mask fabric during the fabricating process. This would keep the fabric layers intact while enabling the use of textile electrodes. Another plausible method for speeding up the fabrication is to do machine sewing on the inner layer first and then apply the electrode-embedded inner layer to the outer layer, which is convenient when making a TexonMask from raw material rather than modifying an existing facemask. For a larger-scale study, industrial weaving is a more scalable fabrication method that supports mass production, as demonstrated in Project Jacquard [ 48 ] and ZebraSense [ 68 ]. Still, interfacing the sewn or woven textile electrodes to the signal processing units requires either extensive manual labor (e.g., soldering, clamping) or bulky mechanisms (e.g., cloth buttons, flexible PCB connectors). Therefore, an electrical connection that is user-friendly and does not affect the appearance or tactile sensation of a facemask is still required.

More Types of Facemasks and Forms . Our approach is not yet universal because there are many types of facemasks available on the market. Some of them apply an origami pattern that may complicate the textile electrode deployment. A more universal additive manufacturing approach, such as attaching a thin mesh of fiber electrodes, could be investigated in future work without compromising the breathability of facemasks, as opposed to sewing the electrodes onto the fabrics.

The physical form for facial recognition also does not limit to a facemask. The textile electrodes can also be embedded in other head wears that consist of textile elements such as a headphone, a knitted beanie, or a woven neck cover as long as the form design allows the skin movement related to these facial expressions can be captured. Making textile electrodes aesthetically appealing like WovenSkin [ 61 ] that can be attached directly to the skin as a temporal epidermal device [ 26 ] (e.g., tattoo sticker) can detect more subtle facial expressions such as blinking with higher sensitivity.

6 CONCLUSION

People are recommended to wear facemasks in public places to protect each other from aerosol transmissions during social interactions, but the facemask occlusion also negatively affects the wearers’ emotional communication. This study proposes a subtle facemask augmentation for supporting online and offline emotional communications between wearers of commodity facemasks. With design considerations formulated based on embedded interaction design guidelines, we carefully design the facemask to keep TexonMask's original functions, looks, and feels that warrant their short-term and long-term life cycles of on-demand uses. With a matrix of textile electrodes seamlessly embedded on the surface of the commodity facemask, the edge computing system recognizes the wearer's emotion-related facial expressions and a few additional expressions, which can be further exploited for controlling a device in a hands-free manner. Users also reported positive feedback on the usefulness of its applications in a semi-structured interview. With the design implications and future research directions discussed by the end of this paper, we sincerely hope this work will support future expeditions on both supporting emotional communications of the facemask wearers and the goals of embedding human-computer interactions seamlessly into people's everyday life.
