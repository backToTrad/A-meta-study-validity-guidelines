Homelessness presents a long-standing problem worldwide. Like other welfare services, homeless services have gained increased traction in Machine Learning (ML) research. Unhoused persons are vulnerable and using their data in the ML pipeline raises serious concerns about the unintended harms and consequences of prioritizing different ML values . To address this, we conducted a critical analysis of 40 research papers identified through a systematic literature review in ML homelessness service provision research. We found that the values of novelty , performance , and identifying limitations were uplifted in these papers, whereas (in) efficiency , (low/high) cost , fast , (violated) privacy , and (homeless condition) reproducibility values collapse . Consequently , unhoused persons were lost (i.e., humans were deprioritized) at multi-level ML abstraction of predictors , categories , and algorithms . Our findings illuminate potential pathways forward at the intersection of data science, HCI and STS by situating humans at the center to support this vulnerable community.

1 INTRODUCTION

Homelessness 1 is a persistent and challenging social problem worldwide. More than 580,000 people lack stable housing in the US [ 89 ]. Simultaneously co-existing problems of job loss, family issues, substance abuse, mental health problems, and lack of affordable housing, among others, [ 28 ] often cause homelessness [ 42 ]. Similar to other public welfare service domains (e.g., Child Welfare Systems (CWS) [ 100 , 101 ], automated hiring decisions [ 42 , 91 ]), homelessness service provision has gained increasing interest from Machine Learning (ML) and algorithmic interventions. Despite cautionary tales against deploying algorithms in the welfare service domain, it is also not uncommon to deploy new automated algorithms (minted in the lab [ 76 , 122 , 123 ]) to automate workers’ processes where algorithms are currently not used in practice [ 4 , 7 ].

Homelessness service provision caseworkers and volunteers rely on multiple tools (e.g., Next Step Tool (NST) for youth by OrgCode [ 55 ]), including internal tools for shelters to predict re-entry [ 22 ]), and tools that are operated manually using a simple rubric; these tools are often data-driven (e.g., Homeless Management Information System (HMIS) database [ 89 ]), and do not rely on traditional ML algorithms per se [ 76 ]. Nonetheless, we observed an increasing trend toward developing cost-effective (e.g., reducing the number of re-entries), fast, efficient algorithm productions in homelessness research [ 28 , 33 , 48 , 57 ] – to enable efficient ways of resource allocation and vulnerability assessment. Almost all these works often focus on optimization and creating novel algorithmic tools. They largely ignore the issues of societal implications and engage little in harm and ethical discourses, with the notable exception of reparative algorithms that seek to repair harms caused to historically marginalized communities [ 34 , 108 , 111 ]. Reparative algorithms and those that employ similar approaches are moving us towards – establishing social justice, eradicating biases [ 47 ] , bringing equality, and repairing harm to those who were historically excluded from wealth accumulation, i.e., who faced social and economic barriers [ 79 , 99 ].

Persons experiencing homelessness or unhoused persons represent vulnerable communities, and using their data in the ML pipeline inherently raises concerns around unintended harm and values that are perpetuated, hence requiring critical review. In this work, we were interested to discover how ML values are upheld in this non-trivial high-stake algorithmic decision-making context; in other words, what values we as a research field are producing in this context, what needs further research attention, missing and bridging aspects, and where and how humans are situated in these works to guide the potential path forward. In this paper, we aim not to criticize the important work that has enabled us to discuss algorithmic homelessness service provision. Instead, we aim to reduce the gap between works from distinct domains, i.e., between CS/HCI and STS) that would remain disconnected otherwise, thereby missing the benefits that could be availed in the context of ML for homelessness.

In an attempt to deeply understand and critically assess these aspects, inspired and informed by building off several related works [ 10 , 24 , 100 , 110 ], and using the homelessness service domain as a case study, we present a critical analysis of 40 research papers identified through a systematic literature review in the ML homelessness service provision research. The following research questions (RQs) guided our inquiry:

  • RQ1: What specific values are encoded and uplifted in ML or algorithmic approaches in homelessness research?
  • RQ1a: How have some traditional ML values translated in this context?
  • RQ2: How are homeless populations (persons) positioned in data and algorithms in this context?

Our findings suggest, that the values of novelty , performance , and identifying limitations were uplifted in these papers. In contrast, ML values (in) efficiency , (low/high) cost , fast , (violated) privacy , and (homeless condition) reproducibility collapsed in homelessness details. Here, we define values collapse pertaining to – any ML values requiring re-definition as they break down or no longer uphold their true definition (as we know it in ML) in the homelessness study context. We found that unhoused persons were lost at different levels of ML abstraction: predictors, categories, and algorithms. In summary, this work makes the following novel contributions to HCI.

  • Informed by Birhane et al. [ 10 ], our work identifies and validates traditional ML values (e.g., novelty, performance ), in addition to re-defining several values (e.g., (high/low) cost, (violated) privacy ) to further contextualize and critically analyze them in the homelessness service domain. In this way, this work expands upon prior work in [ 10 ] and provides an exemplar case study for others to replicate in other scarce-resourced high-stake public service domains.
  • We show how persons experiencing homelessness are nullified, i.e., under-represented, almost lost in the ambiguity of data pre-processing, ML feature engineering, and ML performance-driven metrics – in many cases, facing double stigmatization by algorithms and risk factors identification. We would argue, that homelessness is driven by facets beyond what dependent/independent variables can represent, and much research work still needs to be done to look past – “what new predictors?” .
  • We provide an extensive systematic literature review of research encompassing CS/HCI and STS in the homelessness domain to discover trends, gaps, and understudied avenues, to provide guidance towards potential future interdisciplinary work in this space [ 5 , 24 , 60 , 67 , 105 ].
  • Lastly, “Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) does not count precariously housed or doubled-up families, only those living on the street or in shelters” [ 1 ]. That is, even the legal definitions do not account for the social reality of many families. Here, HCI has a role to play in uncovering these systemic issues (through qualitative and mixed-methods studies) that are abstracted out of ML systems.

2 RELATED WORK

Our RQs focus on ML-related research papers in the homelessness context; however, in order to provide the reader with a holistic understanding of other relevant research (in CS/HCI and STS), this background section will (1) first, briefly introduce the processes in which persons experiencing homelessness obtain public housing services, (2) followed by related work in (a) HCI research that addressed important problems in this area, and then (b) data science and ML-driven research in the studied domain.

2.1 Tools and Processes Used in Practice

We define homelessness using the same definition as U.S. Code § 11302. In brief, an individual experiencing homelessness or houselessness is an individual who lacks a fixed, regular, and adequate nighttime residence [ 59 ]. A number of social (e.g., employment status [ 70 ]), demographic (e.g., race [ 54 ]), and mental/health factors (e.g., use of mental health services [ 20 ]) often drive a person to homelessness. At the individual level, low educational attainment, job loss, poverty, substance abuse, separation from family, and mental health conditions often cause homelessness [ 25 , 45 ]. In the US, persons experiencing homelessness are large, e.g., 553,772 persons experience homelessness on a given night [ 28 ]. A lack of stable income further exacerbates their access to education, jobs, and healthcare to lead a better life.

To support the large homeless population in the US, various support services are available , e.g., emergency shelters, day shelters, and transitional housing services . In the US, most local governments use the Homeless Management Information System (HMIS) managed under the Continuum of Care (CoC) and funded by the Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) [ 48 ], e.g., HMIS for the Capital Region of New York State. HMIS is a local repository of homeless client information (e.g., personal identifiers, demographics, risks for housing problems, and all welfare service interactions) for those seeking federal housing assistance [ 28 ]. Local agencies, e.g., not-for-profit organizations partnering with CoC provide basic housing needs services. A homeless person can be in three kinds of homeless stays: transitional (enters the system only once, stays briefly), episodic (a number of days to a few months), and chronic (spend extended periods of time) [ 33 , 57 ]. Homeless service programs are generally categorized as veterans, youth, single adults (without children), and families (with children). People usually discover publicly available service facilities or access points through word of mouth, meal services, outreach programs, and public awareness campaigns among others [ 52 ].

Additionally, to ensure fair and equal housing, prioritization, and accurate assessment of each individual to provide them with the support they need, Coordinated Entry System (CES) was introduced – to end chronic (high-risk clients) homelessness (also addressed using the Housing First service program [ 79 ]). CES and HMIS provide a centralized database where clients’ data can be viewed by multiple providers (e.g., healthcare [ 7 ]). To enter into the CES, a client is evaluated utilizing the Vulnerability-Index and Service Prioritization Decision Assistance Tool (VI-SPDAT [ 55 ]) (also used in Canada and UK [ 121 ]) developed by OrgCode. VI-SPDAT is a (client) self-reported questionnaire filled out by the provider (org/nonprofits that provide homeless services). A client can obtain the highest vulnerability (aka risk score) score of 17; scores in the 0-3 range are considered least vulnerable and score 4-7 recommended for Rapid Re-Housing program (RRH), and scores >=8 is recommended for Permanent Supportive Housing (PSH) or alike. Different versions of VI-SPDAT are available e.g., single, youth, and families [ 22 ]. A simplified diagram of a homeless person's transition through the CES is shown in Figure 1 .

2.2 Related Work in Homelessness

2.2.1 Prior HCI Work in the Homelessness Domain. Prior research in HCI engaged persons experiencing homelessness in various studies with the objective of discovering their values [ 63 , 65 , 66 , 75 ], understanding their perception of education and needs [ 58 , 114 ], proposing specific services or interventions [ 74 , 90 , 95 ] and engaging them in participatory design (PD) activities [ 19 , 62 , 71 , 77 ]. Koepfler et al. [ 66 ] identified values (e.g., broad-mindedness, spirituality) of interest for design that are operating within homeless communities by studying their Twitter activities for designing ICT technologies. Social Networking Sites (SNSs), such as Twitter and Tumbler, are well-known among the HCI community for studying these hard-to-reach populations (e.g., quantitative work [ 58 ]). In another work, Strohmayer et al. [ 114 ] examined homeless youth's educational needs and learning barriers with the broader goal of educational reform attainable through digitization. Among interventional research, Ogbonnaya-Ogburu et al. [ 90 ] aimed at providing effective digital education for those who were previously incarcerated (returning citizens) and trying to merge into society experiencing homelessness [ 118 ]. Le Dantec et al. [ 74 ] designed and deployed a ubiquitous system to connect shelter users. Recent PD works also addressed important problems, such as Lanz et al. [ 71 ] who co-designed a working prototype “Charge n Chill” - a space for homeless persons to easily get online [ 25 ]. Most of these studies are qualitative (except for a few [ 58 ]) in nature , however, the data science approach (discussed next) to homelessness service also exists in abundance (especially in STS).

Studies that investigate human values in the homeless context are available in HCI (e.g., [ 65 , 66 , 75 ] ). Koepfler et al. [ 66 ] study indicated above discovered homeless stakeholders (e.g., homeless/formerly homeless, homeless advocate, non-profit) values for design; Strohmayer et al. [ 114 ] also reported homeless youth's core values (e.g., survival, curiosity, friendship) in the context of education. This being said, no prior studies aimed at discovering (1) values that are encoded in ML research for homelessness and emergent values collapse , and (2) showed the disconnect between these human values and ML approaches, we will discuss them in this paper.

2.2.2 Applying Data Science in Homeless Service Domain.

“Data science has the potential to be a powerful tool for helping to end homelessness.” [ 79 ].

Indeed, both Computer Science CS/HCI (e.g., ACM) and STS (e.g., Journal of Social Distress and the Homeless) literature have expounded on the potential of data-centric approaches in the homelessness context, e.g., to identify important homelessness risk factors [ 105 , 115 ]), see Table 1 . Although CS's take on a data science approach is quite recent [ 28 , 48 , 115 ], STS applied quantitative data-driven techniques (both statistical and ML) for quite some time as early as in the 1990s (e.g., [ 33 , 104 , 106 ]). In most cases, the data-driven quantitative research papers followed the general steps of the data science pipeline, i.e., data collection, curation, feature engineering, etc., for homeless data analysis [ 53 , 85 , 94 ]. Below, we will first clarify what data sources are used in these studies followed by quantitative methods that are applied to these studies.

Where Does Data Come From? Before doing any data analysis, homelessness service domain researchers collect data. In most cases, this data comes from partnering organizations that administer homeless client databases, i.e., HMIS/CES databases [ 48 , 52 , 68 , 116 ]. For instance, in a recent work, Chelmis et al. [ 28 ] collected HMIS data from CARES of NY, Inc., a not-for-profit organization in Albany, New York. Similarly, Chan et al. [ 22 ] collected a homeless youth HMIS dataset to propose improved AI and predictive tools. It is also not uncommon for researchers to collect data through large-scale surveys (questionnaires), and interviews [ 115 , 120 , 130 ], i.e., creating datasets; for example, Tabar et al. [ 115 ] collected 1400+ homeless youth Substance Use Disorder (SUD) data to discover indicators for SUD. Once the research team has access to the datasets, they go through rigorous data pre-processing (e.g., cleaning, replacing missing values [ 54 ], removing duplicates [ 48 ]) steps to make the data ready for quantitative analysis.

What ML Algorithms are Used in Homelessness Research? Interestingly, compared to classic quantitative work [ 33 ] in homelessness research, recent works both in CS/HCI and STS have applied descriptive statistical methods and ML algorithms to discover risk predictors, homelessness forecasting, cost optimization, targeting specific outcomes (e.g., PSH to end chronic homelessness) among others. Statistical modeling (e.g., t-test) was only used in STS in early 2000. We also observed a consistent use of predictive modeling (1998-2022) in both CS and STS (more details can be found in Figure 3 ) .

First, statistical approaches ranging from logistic regression [ 104 ], Chi-square, and t-test [ 116 ] to several variations of logistic regression (e.g., cox survival analysis [ 52 , 105 ], multivariate binary logistic regression, [ 7 , 20 ]) are applied in homelessness risk factor modeling; second, recent ML homelessness service provision approaches ranges from state-of-the-art ML algorithms such as K-means clustering [ 57 ], Random-forest [ 48 ], Gradient Boosting [ 99 ], Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), Support Vector Machine (SVM) [ 115 ] and decision-tree [ 117 ] classification, to modern classic deep neural networks [ 44 , 45 , 79 ] models e.g., Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) with Multi-layer Perceptron (MLP) [ 121 ], MLP with ReLU activation function [ 115 ] are applied to solve classification or prediction problems in homelessness context. Third, the purpose of applying the clustering method was to group cluster, or classify (e.g., transitional, episodic [ 57 ]) homeless population, as opposed to predicting significant risk factors (e.g., citizenship, age, medical conditions [ 57 ]) or shelter entry/no-entry or re-entry using (binary) classification [ 79 ]. Finally, explainable, fair, and interpretable ML methods have also gained momentum in ML Homelessness service provision research [ 6 , 99 , 121 , 130 ], especially in the CS . VanBerlo et al. [ 121 ] proposed post-hoc Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations interpretable model for neural nets (RNN), while others have claimed to use interpretable classifiers, e.g., decision trees in this context [ 2 , 22 ].

In this work, we are building off all the above-mentioned works from CS/HCI and STS through a systematic literature review (SLR) [ 24 , 38 , 100 ] to (1) identify and validate common ML values that are encoded, and (2) to demonstrate ML values collapse in homelessness ML algorithmic research and, (3) to discover how humans are represented in data and algorithms to guide the potential path forward.

3 METHODS

Below, we provide details about the systematic literature review (SLR) process, followed by the data analysis process and a brief description of the researchers’ positionality.

3.1 Data Collection and Analysis

Data analysis for this paper consists of two phases: first, we conducted a systematic literature review of papers that apply Machine Learning techniques (ranging from descriptive statistics to neural networks) within the homeless population context following guidance suggested in [ 38 , 100 , 128 ]; second, we conducted a thematic analysis [ 16 ] to discover themes to answer our RQs.

3.1.1 Systematic Literature Review and Dataset Description. The first researcher conducted a systematic review of the literature to find candidate papers and familiarize themselves with this studied domain. We used specific search keywords such as “homelessness”, “homeless”, “homeless algorithms”, “machine learning in homelessness”, “unhoused”, “homeless [female, youth, veteran, etc.]”, and “homelessness ACM”, including their combination. We did not look beyond the first 3-5 pages for these searches since the relevance started to diminish. We searched papers on ACM digital library, Google Scholar, IEEE explore, Springer, and Routledge. At the time when SLR (early 2022) was conducted, we found a relatively small count of papers at ACM venues, (e.g., for the “homelessness” keyword, past 2 years: 207 papers), in the homelessness domain in general, whereas Google Scholar had a higher number of search results, most of which were from STS venues (e.g., past 2 years: 20,300 papers). That is why we did not limit ourselves to any specific ML venues (e.g., ICML, NeurIPS [ 10 ]) since quantitative (e.g., descriptive statistics, ML) homelessness service provision papers can span across diverse disciplines, e.g., AAI [ 68 ], Journal of Social Distress and the Homeless [ 7 , 54 ], Journal of Policy Analysis and Management [ 33 ], Psychiatric Services [ 104 ]. Paper inclusion criteria for SLR are as follows:

  1. Search results were narrowed based on the initial reading of the paper title and abstract, i.e., if a paper title/abstract had “risk factor”, “predictor”, “machine learning”, or “algorithm” and was related to homelessness domain then those papers were included. Some papers did not have “homelessness” related terms in their title (e.g., [ 2 , 44 ]), in such cases we had to skim the abstract and other parts of the paper to determine its relevance.
  2. Any published research/government report on homelessness tools or published in CS/HCI/STS venues, or descriptive statistics and ML intervention work were considered in this study.
  3. If a paper satisfied step 1 OR step 2, the paper was included; otherwise, it was excluded.
  4. We also exclude systematic reviews/meta-reviews during our iterative search.

While adding a paper to our SLR running list, the researcher also summarized each of the paper's key findings, objective/goal, RQs, data/datasets used, and (qualitative/quantitative) methods/algorithms applied in each paper by line-by-line reading during this step. Additionally, the author carefully reviewed each paper's reference sections (cross-reference). In total, we had 88 candidate papers encompassing CS/HCI and STS literature, among which around 50 papers were quantitative (e.g., Twitter content analysis [ 58 ] ) in nature. However, focusing on our RQs , we selected 40 papers that were exclusively related to ML/algorithm service provisions research in the homelessness context (also see PRISMA in the Appendix, Figure 4 ). These papers are situated in US/Canada. The latter part of this analysis was conducted on this 40 set of papers. Among 40 papers, 24 $(60\%)$ of the papers were from STS, and 16 $(40\%)$ of the papers were published in CS venues (see Table 1 ) . The list of papers can be found in the metadata with funding details. For the remainder of this paper, we will refer to this set of papers as corpora and each paper as a text or a document .

3.1.2 Thematic Analysis to Discover Values and Human Positioning in Algorithms. During the second part of the analysis, the author applied thematic analysis (TA) [ 16 ] on the corpus of 40 papers to –

  1. Identify deductive values informed by 67 values reported in Birhane et al. [ 10 ] paper. We followed a similar coding strategy described in [ 10 ] to code for each deductive value utilizing open-source materials provided on the paper's GitHub page [ 11 ]. We used a values cluster of performance, building on past work, generalization, and efficiency values for result discussion, although our results vary in significant ways.
  2. Summarize novel or surprising inductive themes [ 84 ] that a) emerged from the corpus, and b) to further contextualize this work and answer specific RQs of this study.
  3. Following Birhane et al. [ 10 ], the authors carefully line-by-line read each paper's abstract, introduction, discussion, and conclusion sections for values coding. Additionally, they also very closely read the methods and experiment results section to identify important themes regarding (1) data pre/post-processing strategies, predictors (independent variables), and algorithmic outcomes (dependent variables), and (2) most importantly, clearly identify how humans were represented in data and ML algorithms . One of our objectives was also to examine how human circumstances were nullified or lost in high-level algorithmic abstractions, and how numerical prioritizations were made among equally or competing negative human conditions in this homelessness context.
  4. Finally, creating high-level themes emerged from prior steps to answer specific RQ.

Qualitative analysis was chosen primarily due to the nature of RQs which required careful and thorough reading and analysis of the prior literature that would not have been possible with ML/NLP or automated tools. 31 papers $(77.5\%)$ were coded by the first author, and 9 papers $(22.5\%)$ were coded by other researchers. Congruence of themes was achieved through collaborative group discussion of individual themes, and themes were reported only after reaching group consensus. Additionally, we conducted cross-coding analysis or cross-tabs across themes to further deepen our understanding of how different values inadvertently impact other values and dominant themes. To do so, we counted occurrences of i) dominant values with values collapse, and ii) dominant values with themes across each paper. The iterative nature of the analysis, i.e., comparing data (text documents) with codes, codes with data (text documents), data (text documents) with data (text documents), and codes with codes provides a richer set of themes and findings [ 26 ].

4 FINDINGS

4.1 Common and Re-contextualized Collapsed Values in ML Homelessness Research

In this section, we first provide a deep analysis of commonly described values in ML homelessness service provision research, followed by ML values that required re-definition as they broke down in this context, we will refer to this as values collapse . Finally, we present how humans were positioned in our corpora; see Figure 2 for major theme interactions.

4.1.1 Common ML Values Contextualized: Our analysis showed that novelty , performance , and identifying limitations were the most prominent values in our corpora (Table 2 ). The entire deductive values count and CS/STS breakdown can be found in the Appendix (see Figure 5 and Figure 6 ) . Novelty: One of the most common recurring values prominent in our corpora was a focus on novelty $(62.5\%)$ , i.e., approaches that are original, new, and unfamiliar. The concept of novelty manifested in the choice of algorithms, dataset use, homeless population studied, experimental site selection , including novel predictor or risk factor identification . Interestingly, the authors justified their position on novelty not only in the abstract, introduction, discussion, or conclusion but also in the related work section when they compared novelty of their work with others or used prior work $(27.5\%)$ as the foundation of their own.

While it was common for many classic works in STS ( $75\%$ ) to apply Logistic Regression (e.g., [ 33 , 104 ]), several recent studies especially in the CS applied more novel and complex ML algorithms (e.g., Decision Trees, Random Forest [ 22 , 48 ]) . In one text, Chelmis et al. [ 28 ] described the novelty of K-nearest neighbor for classifying homeless services stay in the related work section, “To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has explored hierarchical classification for homeless service provision” . CS ( $75\%$ ) and STS ( $25\%$ ) authors went as far as using deep neural networks (e.g., [ 45 ]). Similarly, CS tends to focus on interpretable models, and novel optimizations (2020-2022), see Figure 3 . Surprisingly, recent (2018 - 2022) CS ( $15\%$ ) papers (e.g., decision trees [ 6 ]) promoted fairness more than STS ( $7.5\%$ ) papers (e.g., logistic regression [ 118 ]).

An emphasis on using large novel datasets was also common. These datasets often consist of small samples, however, authors still referred to them as large datasets, since collecting these datasets was challenging. Byrne et al. [ 20 ] used nationwide data/samples [ 20 , 21 ] as a way to demonstrate the novelty of their work. Furthermore, novelty appeared when authors studied specific homeless populations, such as homeless youth, runaway females, and veterans, among others [ 82 , 83 , 116 ]. Tabar et al. [ 115 ] used ML to identify novel predictors for SUD among homeless youth. “[...] there has been no prior work on building and understanding models for predicting SUD among homeless youth” . Another set of documents focused on specific sites (e.g., NY, CA) and developed predictive models for them [ 117 ]. Novelty was equally prioritized across CS ( $48\%$ ) and STS ( $52.5\%$ ) papers from a technical lens (e.g., dataset, predictor) rather than a human-focused lens despite the homelessness context.

Performance: We observed a propensity towards improved performance, i.e., numbers or quantities that tell you whether a model is progressing. The second most commonly occurring value in our corpora was an emphasis on performance $(52.5\%)$ , accuracy $(47.5\%)$ , and state-of-the-art $(10\%)$ .

Such descriptions were revealed when authors justified their proposed methods (among many ML models) based on numerical scores and experimental results. In most documents, we found authors use the metrics of Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC), Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC), or AUROC for performance evaluation [ 22 , 117 ]. Byrne et al. [ 20 ] used AUC to estimate salient predictors of homelessness among youth exiting foster care to assist them, “Applying the parameters of the classification model estimated on the downsampled development sample to the validation sample yielded an AUC of 0.94, which is in the excellent range by conventional guidelines” . Some even tried various metrics for model evaluation, recall, precision, F1-score, AUC, and weighted F1 loss to predict chronic homelessness [ 121 ]. Authors selected the trained model that achieved a high F1 score through parameter tuning/cross-validation. We also noticed a rising tension among different research teams’ considerations between the choice of lowering false-negative or false-positive. While both represent vulnerable individuals (at least for HCI researchers), research teams justified their position in terms of monetary future costs [ 121 ]. 20 ( $50\%$ ) of the papers used variations of logistic regression and evaluate model performance using AUC (see Table 6 ). A higher AUC score implies better and more accurate prediction ability. AUC and accuracy were the most commonly used performance metric across all ML methods (Table 6 ). Additionally, choosing methods that work better or even improved compared to state-of-the-art models was preferred [ 2 ]. Text documents focusing on fair ML also prioritized novelty and performance [ 2 , 6 ]. Cross-tabs showed that $52.3\%$ CS papers and $47.6\%$ STS papers discussed performance (Figure 6 ). Similar to novelty, performance decisions are often based upon algorithmic feasibility, i.e., accuracy measures, rather than considerations for humans or the consequence of ignoring human circumstances in them.

Identifying Limitations: Nearly all text documents included limitation sections identifying problems in a proposed ML approach. These discussions often excluded human considerations in favor of technical limitations (e.g., dataset or sample limitations, limited predictor variable usage, and non-generality).

One of the most common limitations stated in text documents was related to sample bias and self-reported data. For instance, Thompson et al. [ 116 ] study investigated risk factors predicting pregnancy among runaway/homeless female adolescents limited to shelter service users, “The sample does not include “street youth” who do not use shelter services” . Similar sentiments were also echoed in [ 48 , 115 ] study, among others. Several studies [ 7 , 104 , 115 ] further wrote about the limitations of their study findings that come from self-report ed datasets , citing that clients needed to rely on memory for information [ 104 ], and sometimes they are dis honest. Byrne et al. [ 21 ], on the other hand, discussed limitations that occur in the absence of some predictor variables, such as education level and adverse childhood experiences for individuals/families’ homelessness after exiting homelessness. All of these concerns often lead to a larger generalizability issue. Likewise, most documents often used these concerns as a gateway to discuss the lack of generality of their ML interventions. Cross-tabs further suggest that STS ( $66.6\%$ ) papers tend to include study limitations more often than CS ( $33.3\%$ ) papers.

Summary: In the above paragraphs, we discussed the three of the most dominant, i.e., frequently occuring values in our corpora. While CS-focused papers tend to emphasize performance and novelty, STS papers prioritized discussing study limitations and used several variations of logistic regression models. We found that CS authors were keen to propose novel ML (e.g., fair and interpretable decision trees) models than discussing their practical or societal ramifications, whereas STS authors were focused on (novel) risk-factor identification. This impulse raises the question of how researchers are approaching ML homelessness service provision research that often placed humans (and the loss of context) as secondary. Consequently, several ML values have broken apart in the homelessness context (shown in Figure 2 ) – triggering the event of ML values collapse .

4.1.2 Re-contextualized Collapsed ML Values: Despite using the same words, the “traditional” meaning of the ML values efficiency, cost, fast, privacy, and reproducibility have significantly altered in the homelessness context (see themes summary in Table 2 ). To understand this phenomenon, we conducted cross-coding (Table 8 ), i.e., how often dominant and collapsed values occurred together. By traditional ML values, we refer to the values of novelty, performance, efficiency, and accuracy that are often prioritized when justifying ML models in various applications [ 10 ]. Although we included these four values in our cross-tabs, we will only discuss the influence of novelty and performance on collapsed values due to their prominence in our corpora.

(in) Efficiency: We observed that the algorithmically charged value efficiency $(30\%)$ no longer conveyed a similar meaning as we know it in ML. It was no longer about ML or algorithms prescribing to label efficiency $(0\%)$ , energy efficiency $(0\%)$ , or reduced training time $(0\%)$ as they never appeared in our corpora. While data efficiency $(30\%)$ and memory efficiency $(2.5\%)$ occured in some texts, their descriptions were scant, and the values of ML efficiency , (low/high) cost , and fast are also almost fragmented in the homelessness context.

Efficiency, in our corpora, often describes the processes and practices in which to efficiently and effectively house unhoused persons to improve the homeless system (e.g., PSH for highest risk client); efficiency was also used to decide “who is the right homeless client?” . In one text, Greer et al. [ 52 ] described , “Efficiency—getting services to the right people—may be the harder problem” . Although this study used ML to identify risk factors to inform efficient housing decisions, the value efficiency was not about algorithmic efficiency. It was geared toward optimal assignment to reach those with the greatest housing need. Similarly, in several texts, we observed a tension between prioritizing staff workers versus ML models for efficient housing allocation. While one document urged increasing staff workers in the public housing pipeline, others, at times, went as far as claiming ML models making better decisions than staff workers when justifying research [ 52 ]. Yet, some recognized the importance of staff workers ability to override ML decisions when necessary and based on evidence ; for excerpts, see Table 3 . A lack of strategic resource management and human resource constraints can cause (in) efficient homeless systems [ 130 ]. 4.2.1 Human Lost in Predictors and Categorization: We found numerous documents showed a predilection with the discovery and identification of risk factors. We found seven $(17.5\%)$ texts predicting risk factors for families and three $(7.5\%)$ texts predicting risk factors for youth homelessness. In total, 20 $(50\%)$ text documents focused on risk factors directly. As this happens , humans were lost in ML abstractions in the following ways. Predicting the Right ‘Who’ and Categorization : We found several ML homelessness research and its focus on efficiently classifying the right people, i.e., people who are at the highest risk or who will benefit the most from public housing service assistance [ 52 , 105 ]. We found these objectives to be problematic as follows . First , in most texts, algorithms often used incorrect assumptions about identity when predicting high-risk clients. In several texts, race and gender were the most common demographic predictor of homelessness [ 83 ]. We repetitively found papers from early as in the ‘90s to the recent 2020’s studies where the ‘gender’ identity marker was considered as a “time-invariant” variable [ 28 , 33 , 48 ] and/or only considered a binary and trans-exclusive. This use of incorrect identity markers to predict the right ‘who’ could lend itself to producing not only biased outcomes but also sometimes outcomes that are impractical and insensitive. Second , the impact of predicting current and future clients can be extremely dangerous. Public housing services are limited resources; as a result, staff workers still need to make difficult, yet, high-stake decisions. Likewise, in one text, authors justified such decisions by identifying the future high-cost individuals, such that the saved resources can be used to support others who need them [ 117 ]. Predictions went further, where ML predictions were used to decide , not only those who will cost more in the future but also “who will remain stuck in homelessness” [ 118 ]. This means, according to the algorithm, if a homeless client will not fall under these categories (e.g., future high-cost, remain stuck), they will not receive a service. Third, categorical abstraction for housing outcome prediction further exacerbated this situation , i.e., a categorical range is used to make housing decisions (e.g., PSH/RRH). In the current SPDAT system, an individual scoring more than 7 is considered for housing services, and anyone having a risk score less than 7 receives an information packet [ 97 ].

All these inputs, outputs and categorical encoding is often useful for staff workers and mostly amenable to ML application i.e., for achieving high performance (AUC, Accuracy in Table 6 ) but is at the expense of loss of human context [ 24 , 42 ]. In that way, ML values efficiency and cost collapsed. For populations like youth (e.g., with opioid addiction [ 130 ]) facing homelessness, justifying the right ‘who’ algorithmically can be difficult (e.g., a false negative can be an equally good candidate for housing) [ 40 , 51 ] using historical data , enabling reproducibility value collapse . Lastly, how will the decision be made when multiple clients are at the same risk level? How those conflicts will be resolved in practice? [ 93 ]. First come first serve algorithms, i.e., ”first found, first assigned” [ 72 ] might not encapsulate historical injustices. These complex scenarios are often absent for our corpora that hailed the ML value of “Identifying Limitations” .

5 DISCUSSION AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

In the U.S., a large number of people experience homelessness every day. In January 2020, 91,271 persons experienced homelessness in NY state for at least one day 2 . To optimize and scale high-stake decision-making (e.g., prediction, allocation) for this large vulnerable population, including dataset availability (e.g., HMIS) has inspired data science research practitioners to utilize the efficiency of ML in this socially-sensitive context . While we cannot deny the potential of ML and data science's application in homeless service provision undertakings, our analysis demonstrates how humans were nullified and lost at multi-level of ML abstractions (e.g., predictors, algorithms) when novelty and performance values are uplifted. Below, we provide a detailed description of how interdisciplinary collaborative efforts [ 22 ] from CS/HCI and ML (e.g., Human-centered Data Science (HCDS) [ 27 , 67 ]) can reposition humans (e.g, unhoused persons , staff workers) and their lost context at the center.

5.1 Human-centered Approaches for Homelessness Service Provision Research

This paper was inspired and informed by human-centered HCI works in [ 10 , 24 , 100 ]. With this research, we intervene in the current ML/data science approaches to homelessness research. In that note, our work closely aligns with the work of Chancellor et al. [ 24 ] who focuses on Human-centered Machine Learning (HCML) for mental health. However , our work looked beyond dehumanization or increased stigma caused by data/dataset usage . Instead, our work is a response to “ what could be the consequences of this loss of context? ” [ 24 , p.17] by illustrating how humans were lost in ML abstractions when performance and novelty values were uplifted . In line with Saxena et al. [ 100 ] work, we found researchers often focused on easily measurable and novel predictors to produce the desired accuracy. Our work simultaneously builds off and also expands these works [ 24 , 100 ] , slightly leaning towards critical HCDS line of work [ 5 , 67 , 109 ], guiding potential path forward at their intersection by re-centering humans.

Consistent with Birhane et al. [ 10 ] work that looked at values encoded in ML research in general, our work also discovered a great emphasis put forth on the (deductive) values of performance and novelty in ML homelessness research. In contrast, we found identifying limitations as one of the core values in our corpora. We also observed small occurrences of values of beneficence , respect for persons , and respect for law and policy , perhaps due to the specific (vulnerable) population and research domain involved in our corpora. We also identified two novel ML (inductive) values of consistency and reliability not mentioned in [ 10 ] (these values are not detailed in this paper because of their low occurrences and to focus on RQs). As noted earlier, prioritizing performance and novelty nullified the homeless person's representation in data and algorithms. Humans and their circumstances often became predictors. When novelty (e.g., novel predictors [ 115 ]) was prioritized, adding/removing predictors to see their influence on the final algorithmic outcome or accuracy score (e.g., predictors influencing regression coefficients and inferences [ 54 ]) was more important than paying attention to ’what’ about humans were getting lost. Essentially, prioritizing dominant ML values violated homeless individuals’ privacy, and sometimes reproduced their circumstances algorithmically (e.g., unhoused persons without Medicaid receipt will remain unhoused [ 20 ]), and at worst, these ML methods created a vulnerability ranking mechanism among unhoused clients (e.g., who to prioritize among high-risk clients [ 72 , 118 ]). External factors (e.g., political, social, and institutional) are already pushing these people away from their deserved resources [ 42 ], and our critical review further confirmed that ML approaches cannot serve this vulnerable community any better. This finding directs us towards using other methods inspired by HCI (e.g., photo-elicitation methods, survey vignettes [ 69 , 75 ]). Several recent works provide inspiration [ 69 , 109 , 111 , 119 ]. For example, Kube et al. [ 69 ] used survey instruments to identify users’ decision-making preferences between high-risk clients or high-cost future clients without ML intervention. They showed that decision-makers’ decisions varied when additional context (e.g., household description) was provided in combination with algorithmic prediction. This work shows an exemplary methodological contribution for conducting research involving vulnerable populations without using their real data (e.g., HMIS [ 7 , 97 ]) and prevents causing double stigmatization through values collapse. Below, we provide further directions for HCI and beyond.

5.2 What can HCI Bring to Homelessness Services Work?

Following HCI's long history of empowering vulnerable communities [ 32 , 63 , 90 , 98 , 100 ], we provide concrete directions in which HCI can support unhoused persons through 1) centering human values in service design, and 2) supporting their practical needs.

5.2.1 Prioritize Human Values in ML Service Tool Design. Prior HCI work has developed a deep understanding of homeless populations’ values [ 65 , 66 , 73 , 75 ]. Le Dantec et al. [ 75 ] discovered six values of staying connected, identity control, and tensions with autonomy at the center of homelessness and technology. Staying connected was identified as the top value for most homeless persons; however, the social connection often boiled down to technology and information access. Le Dantec et al. [ 75 ] also showed that technology (e.g., public transportation system) violated homeless individuals’ values of autonomy, e.g., the inability to plan might cause service denial or face humiliation. In a similar vein, our findings illustrated important values such as privacy and autonomy were violated (e.g., asking intimate questions [ 42 ]). This basic human values (e.g., autonomy [ 46 ]) are disconnected from values promoted (e.g., novelty, performance) in ML research for homelessness.

Perhaps the challenge underlies when embedding homeless individuals’ values in algorithmic ML interventions through design while there exists values tension among them (e.g., uplifting performance causing privacy value collapse, see Table 8 ) . One potential way workaround towards this emergent values tension is to leverage value-sensitive design (VSD) and value-sensitive algorithm design in the design process [ 3 , 46 , 107 , 132 ]. VSD can help shift the design lens from technically charged ML values to account for human values in design (e.g., avoiding preexisting, technical, emergent biases in design [ 47 ]), whereas value-sensitive algorithm design can support researchers to take into consideration multiple stakeholders’ tacit knowledge, insights, needs, the social and organizational context for algorithm creation — to understand algorithms acceptability and impact. In that way, the design will be responsive to relevant direct and indirect stakeholders’ needs, vulnerabilities, and the designs would uphold human values not only at the interface feature level but also at the underlying algorithmic working level. Currently, ML homelessness algorithms use various datasets and utilize performance metrics (e.g., ROC, AUC) to determine significant risk factors. At times, those risk factors (e.g., age, race, gender, pregnancy, SUD [ 104 , 115 , 116 ] ) replicate stigmatization (e.g., dehumanization [ 24 ]). Engaging VSD early in the algorithm design process can alleviate some of this stigma and reduce the focus on ML technical values of performance (e.g., AUC, Accuracy) and novelty (from Table 8 ) . For example, vulnerable populations’ autonomy can be protected by allowing them to decide what information (e.g., socio-demographic, health/mental) about them (independent variables) should be or should not be used? which ones should be weighted less or more and by how much? how their un-quantified histories and untold stories can (and when) and should work as a proxy to the outcome they receive? Thereby reducing researchers’ attention around novel predictors and shifting research praxis toward supporting human autonomy and values-focused design.

Similarly, ML values cost , reproducibility , and efficiency collapse also hint to us that humans should not be the object of (performance) optimization (e.g., minimize future cost [ 117 ]) or ranking (e.g., high-risk clients [ 52 , 125 ]), and victim of past history they have no control over (e.g., incarcerated parents/partner [ 42 ]). We want to design algorithms that use optimization to reduce causing further harm, algorithms that are social context-aware and human values sensitive [ 56 , 132 ] . Current systems (e.g., logistic regression) are good at punishing through historic replication. However, service decision tools (e.g., VI-SPDAT [ 7 ]) should move beyond that, instead, they should allow relevant stakeholders the ability to bend the rules when required e.g., negotiating human intuition/counter-intuition with low/high accuracy when necessary [ 93 ]. Recent reparative algorithms and affirmative actions to circumvent housing discrimination are a good starting place to repair historical harms through transformative action [ 111 ]. However, intentional housing reallocation can forebode friction [ 34 ]. Similarly , texts often lacked emphasis on vulnerable population engagement in algorithm design. Future work might benefit from utilizing trauma-informed computing framework [ 30 ] to ensure that ML homeless systems are not developed in isolation of those who are directly impacted and are co-created with them. ML modelers, homeless staff workers, researchers, policymakers, including those who are affected have an integral role to bring about practical change in the social, policy, and technical aspects of homelessness [ 56 , 78 , 98 , 109 ] .

5.2.2 HCI Supporting Practical Needs. At least $17.5\%$ of papers described practical problems existing in various homeless population groups. These problems can benefit from HCI research and design initiatives .

Homeless populations often suffer from simultaneously existing problems. Studies show that seriously mentally ill patients (e.g., older and Black people [ 33 , 54 ], young youth [ 104 , 115 ]) experience homelessness due to no income and substance abuse [ 72 , 115 , 130 ] . A lack of (stable) income further exacerbates their access to healthy food and healthcare needs. There is a need for improved employment stability [ 90 ] , efforts toward increasing job eligibility, and targeted intervention to provide adequate mental health treatment [ 54 ] . Some interventions could focus on supporting specific homeless groups (e.g., families [ 48 , 105 , 106 ], women [ 116 , 126 ]) . For example, homeless youth, with a history of social disconnection, incarceration, and SUD often face barriers to finding jobs, e.g., due to not completing education [ 83 , 118 ]. Youth could be supported via behavioral health training and education. Similarly, homeless female youth are at the highest risk of becoming pregnant (e.g., when they are on the street) [ 106 ] . They are often drop out of school and live in an endless poverty cycle (e.g., kids born often end up in foster care [ 116 ] ). These examples illustrate that homelessness is interlinked with individual, social, and institutional facets that require interventions at each of these levels [ 41 , 72 , 115 , 118 , 130 ]. Therefore, HCI should contribute in substantial research initiatives to support homeless persons by making education [ 90 , 114 ], training (e.g., reduce shelter stays program [ 33 ]) , internet services [ 71 ] , and technologies (e.g., mobile phones [ 129 ]) accessible. Without access to these resources, unhoused persons are stuck in locating help services (e.g., low-wage workers after losing jobs [ 118 ]). Thus, reducing barriers to “access” and “information” could be an essential starting step.

HCI design can further extend its support through design interventions. Participatory design (PD) activities [ 87 , 113 ] can support learning important technical (e.g., how to use advanced spreadsheets, surfing the internet), non-technical (creative) skills (e.g., playing an instrument, cooking, creating videos), and help raise awareness (e.g., preventing HIV spread, self-care [ 127 ] ) among homeless individuals. PD can also facilitate moving away from solutionist strategies, understanding their (other) needs, and imagining creative ways to support these vulnerable communities [ 12 ]. PD design activities can bring small joy in their lives in constructive ways and can aim to repair and heal mental or emotional harm through reflection [ 103 ]. Such positive-focused (as opposed to existing deficit-focused methods [ 56 , 106 , 115 ]) PD approaches can instill belief in them that growth is possible despite current circumstances. HCI have already started doing so on a smaller scale (e.g., speculative work [ 39 ]), and we should do it more. Lastly, we should also take into consideration the possibility that sometimes the output of a PD process is the implication to not design because systemic injustices may require non-technical answers in terms of reformed policies, support for vulnerable communities, and collective action [ 8 , 9 , 64 , 96 , 100 , 102 ] . As our HCI research and design responsibilities in the homelessness context evolve, so must our existing ways of engaging with data and ML.

5.3 Data and Algorithms: What to do Next in the Homelessness Context?

Below, we provide concrete directions for centering relevant stakeholders in the homelessness context through 1) improved ML replicability to enable algorithmic accountability, and 2) preventing inadvertent biases (e.g., researcher biases) regarding dataset (its usage) in the ML homelessness research .

5.3.1 Make ML Models, Anonymized Data Accessible and Uplift Simplicity . Our findings revealed a lack of ML reproducibility concern in the homelessness ML research context. Similarly, no efforts were made to make data or models/code accessible, although at times authors do confirm how their findings are consistent with previous work. No algorithm setup is perfect since most studies collected administrative datasets (from organizations) that were not created for research purposes [ 54 ]. These datasets are often shared with research teams without providing much-contextualized details. The absence of one or more feature set(s), the use of specific imputation methods (e.g., MissForest off-the-shelf algorithm [ 115 ]), and the unavailability of other datasets (e.g., healthcare, justice systems, employment) could render any proposed tool/software or method non-reusable. This automatically questions the reproducibility [ 43 ] aspects of (almost) any proposed interventions (e.g., homeless prevention [ 37 , 118 ]) in our corpus. Moreover, when ML authors are focusing on performance (metrics e.g., AUC, ROC) and accuracy values, a lot of the time ignoring mathematically insignificant variables (e.g., variables with small feature importance value) can have serious negative practical consequences, e.g., sexual assault feature had a low score in SUD prediction in Tabar et al. [ 115 ] study . Put differently, according to the algorithmic outcome, sexual assault can receive less attention compared to other significant variables for SUD. Similarly, a lack of consideration for intersectional identities makes proposed interventions impractical [ 34 , 99 , 111 ]. To be practically useful, these methods should be widely tested in the field and or should be made accessible (e.g., via GitHub) to the wider research community to enhance transparency, accountability, and reuse [ 15 , 17 , 24 , 76 , 123 ]. Model descriptors such as utilizing model cards can provide essential context (e.g., stakeholder information, intended use) for ML model reuse [ 81 ]. We understand that making sensitive research datasets and algorithms publicly available could have unintended consequences (e.g., gaming), however, a lack of thereof also limits the benefits that can be availed if made accessible. Making these methods accessible will enable empirical evaluation, and facilitate understanding their impact beyond quantitative performance or accuracy measures [ 131 ]. If the real dataset is difficult to share, authors can resort to publicly sharing synthetic or an anonymized version of datasets so that the targeted audience can still repurpose them.

We found simplicity $(7.5\%)$ as one of the least occurring values in our dataset. Similarly, most papers focused on novel or complex ML models (e.g., NN [ 45 , 121 ]) which makes achieving replicability a harder challenge. Research teams partnering organizations are often non-profit [ 48 ], consequently, workers often operate in resource-constrained environments [ 13 , 119 , 124 , 130 ], making deployment of ML techniques infeasible or even impractical [ 79 ]. Utilizing ML techniques requires the integration of IT infrastructure (database and secure cloud services) that non-profit organizations may not be able to afford in terms of human and technology resources. Future research should discover ways to assist these agencies without causing further resource burdens using simpler methods (spreadsheets [ 79 ]) that can be easily adopted and disseminated . Simpler methods might also enable research teams to share proposed methods easily, thereby somewhat alleviating code replicability challenges. Replicability challenges often arise due to not only code unavailability but also challenges associated with datasets.

5.3.2 Attend to Quality of Data Discovery and Curation. As briefly discussed in the results section, several limitations of our corpora emerged from dataset problems alone. Datasets were imperfect, incomplete [ 33 , 92 ], had missing values [ 37 , 54 , 85 ], and sometimes substituting with (e.g., 0) numeric values generated by imputation methods were amenable to numerical analysis [ 91 ], however, in reality, it is a context loss [ 24 ] , and to the extreme, it's also human loss, when each row presents an individual in the dataset.

Datasets were often noisy as they are generated by humans informed by human behavior and are inherently complex for inferring causal predictions [ 48 ]. These datasets were also often small (although bigger data is not always better [ 14 ]), and imbalanced (e.g., emergency service data $38\%$ , PSH data $4\%$ ) for rigorous ML analysis (e.g., 6k+ samples in [ 48 ], 17k+ samples in [ 37 ], 40k sample in [ 60 ]), given these were hard-to-reach population dataset and data collection are hard. As a result, most research papers did not put much emphasis on reducing the computational running time as opposed to conventional ML studies.

These datasets are often collected in the association of shelters, and service providers or agencies serving the homeless population, thus, samples may only represent a specific subgroup of homeless individuals [ 104 ]. They are collected from organizations situated in major cities of New York NY, San Diego CA, Los Angeles county [ 125 ], Massachusetts, Philadelphia PA in the US, and Canada, thus existing research is limited to the choice of the sampling site. Existing research also shows that all these cities use the SPDAT tool to determine vulnerability [ 55 ]. Vulnerability/risk assessment methods similarly were predicted based on what data were available to the researchers (e.g., clients unable to fill out the form, although in most need [ 7 ]).

A lot of the factors that determined homelessness (e.g., in youth [ 104 ]) were based on self-reported data requiring participants to retrospectively reflect on situations (e.g., interview in [ 72 , 106 , 116 ]) that happened in the past (e.g., childhood), consequently limits study findings. Sometimes, homeless persons were reluctant to respond when they were asked very intimate questions (e.g., a survey in [ 115 ] for young youth SUD; clients’ lack of knowledge, or survey skip patterns [ 37 ]).

Most ML/algorithmic studies utilized datasets collected from organizations (e.g., CARES NY [ 28 ]) responsible for housing homeless persons. Some authors collected data through large-scale longitudinal survey/interview methods to apply ML, this is exceptionally new in CS/HCI studies (e.g., [ 115 ]). Thus, future work should discover novel methods to engage these hard-to-reach populations, and pay further heed to the quality of data acquisition methods [ 109 ]; discovery, capture, curation steps in the data science pipeline [ 85 ]. The research team should seek expert support (professional in data curation) to further ensure that they are sensitive to marginalized communities’ intersectional identities [ 31 ], historical, and nuanced socially-dependent contexts [ 32 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 67 ].

Lastly, datasets given to the research team were often associated with personal identifiers (e.g., SSN, birthdate), (ML can still work without these sensitive identifiers), therefore, partnering organizations and research teams should exhibit caution when accessing private information. When doing so both parties should ensure that data is properly anonymized, consented , and client privacy is properly taken care of, to ensure ethical data usage. Furthermore, the research team should also commit to transparent data work practices [ 5 ] using well-known data documentation practices, e.g., datasheets for datasets [ 15 , 50 ], to further contextualized (e.g., motivation, collection process) and enable accountable data usage. This will also ensure that the data work practices or steps (e.g., feature engineering, creation [ 85 ]) are free from various unforeseen biases, e.g., biases that emerge from the social context of use [ 47 ]. Without efficient documentation practices, it is also easier to forget important data work steps for both equally harmful and beneficial intentions [ 86 ]. We understand the challenges of deploying new tools even as simple as documentation practice within the homeless system when the data is produced [ 80 ], however, it will take little to no effort but slight team re-organization to incorporate documentation practices in team ML work pipeline [ 131 ].

6 STUDY LIMITATIONS

Like any empirical study, our study has several limitations. First, our empirical finding is limited to the subset of papers (n = 40) identified through SLR, where we deeply analyzed papers that took a data science and/or ML approach to homelessness service provision research. Put differently , we analyzed papers that explicitly applied ML or other quantitative analysis techniques (e.g., descriptive statistics) as the primary means of data analysis and used homeless population dataset(s). We found that, there were a couple of values such as theoretical guarantees , label/energy efficiency , and reduced training time were absent in our analysis. An explanation for this would be, most papers in our corpora used small dataset(s) that were not computationally demanding compared to ML models that are often applied to solve very large-scale problems (e.g., > 1 Billion parameters for large language models [ 18 ]). Similarly, requires fewer resources (such as GPU, memory) ML value was mentioned only once in a paper. Future work can further expand, investigate these missing values, and discover other alternative values in this context [ 49 ]. Second, our analysis revealed how ML/algorithms are currently being used in the homelessness context in general, however, we also discovered an open line of future inquiry that can potentially focus on analyzing a specific homeless population group, e.g., homeless youth, females, and veterans to identify their specific needs. Third, our cross-coding analysis was based upon unequal sample size across 16 CS and 24 STS papers, thus, those analyses should be read with caution. Finally, the set of papers examined in this work was primed for research that focuses on aspects of homelessness and ML/algorithms in the western US/Canada, therefore, future work could expand on our work through extensive analysis of papers/empirical work that focuses on other non-dominant geographical regions (e.g., Global south).

7 CONCLUSION

Our goal was to critically examine current ML techniques as they are applied in the homelessness service provision research by identifying (1) the most common ML values stated in research papers, (2) re-contextualized ML values as they collapse in this context, and (3) discover the ways homeless persons were positioned in – predictors, categories, and algorithmic inference. We showed that novelty and performance values are often uplifted in most CS and STS papers causing several important values collapse , consequently, humans were nullified or lost, i.e., they were deprioritized under multi-level of ML abstractions.

The current state-of-the-art ML/algorithm homelessness provision research has been limited to a specific service intervention, discovering (novel) risk factors, efficient and effective targeting of an outcome (e.g., homeless prevention, decreasing homelessness cost), or proposing novel optimization techniques [ 23 ]. While all this is important, there is a lack of research on recommending additional, care and support services; instead, we discovered that existing works tend to focus on binary classification of – who gets housed and who doesn't? Who is the next homeless person and who isn't? Who will re-enter or exit? Current studies also show that when a homeless person does not receive housing (e.g., RRH, PSH), the housing navigator, sometimes the case manager, stops the process by providing some information. This is problematic, since a homeless person experience hardship in many ways, such as from deteriorating mental health, to substance abuse, domestic violence, and malnourishment, to name a few; this implies that if immediate housing need cannot be met, additional support services could still be beneficial to individuals’ experiencing homelessness . These practical needs require further attention and interrogation from HCI and the broader SIGCHI community.
