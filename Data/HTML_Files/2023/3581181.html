Online Proctoring: Privacy Invasion or Study Alleviation?
Discovering Acceptability Using Contextual Integrity

Detecting fraud during online exams using proctoring software comes with substantial privacy challenges. Previous work argues students experience heightened anxiety and have privacy concerns. However, little is known about which specific aspects of online proctoring cause these concerns. This study contributes such insights by using the Contextual Integrity (CI) framework to discover how students (N = 456) rate the acceptability of 1064 proctoring information flows with varying information types, recipients, and transmission principles. We find that the acceptability varies considerably depending on the context. Besides exposing obvious privacy violations, we find that, under certain conditions, students consider it acceptable to share data with teachers - despite their lack of involvement in proctoring. Also, the acceptability of sharing highly sensitive information - which should under no circumstances be shared - sometimes increases. We discuss the implications of these and other findings and provide concrete recommendations for educational institutions using online proctoring.

1 INTRODUCTION

During the COVID-19 pandemic, regulations enacted by governments worldwide imposed substantial limits on the number of people allowed to be physically present in the same space. Many students were confined to their own homes or dorms while education had to be received online, on personally owned devices. Although Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) had been experimenting with digital educational technology before [ 1 - 4 ], they were now forced to quickly move most of their study programs to online [ 5 ]. One particularly critical aspect of education came with substantial challenges: examination. Naturally, it is imperative that students do not commit fraud during exams, which some do [ 6 - 8 ]. In ‘normal’ circumstances (i.e., before the pandemic), exam fraud was prevented by having a trained proctor (also known as invigilate ) be physically present to supervise students and intervene, if necessary, while students were taking their exams on-site (referred to as pencil and paper testing, PPT ). The pandemic left no other possibility but to move towards online examination (referred to as computer-based testing, CBT ), meaning traditional methods for fraud prevention were no longer usable, as CBT opens up new and different ways of cheating [ 8 ].

Online proctoring software can be used to prevent and detect fraud during CBT [ 9 ]. Students typically install this software on their personal devices after which the student can be monitored while taking the exam. Due to the nature of what must be observed or recorded by online proctoring software, 1 it comes as no surprise that privacy issues have been raised numerous times [ 10 - 17 ]. For example, to demonstrate that students are alone while taking an exam and no forbidden material can be consulted during the exam, students are required at the start of the exam to provide a 360-degree view of their entire room. This exposes their private spaces which reveals a lot more information than simply the absence of books or cheat sheets. Most of the known privacy-related critique against online proctoring software reveals issues around three main topics: what data is collected, with whom is this data shared and under which conditions does the data collection and sharing take place. Regarding what data, the software uses the device's sensors such as the webcam and the microphone (to observe students and their surroundings during the exam) and operating system information (to observe whether the student performs any additional activities during the exam and to block certain features, such as visiting other websites). With whom the data is shared refers to who has access to the data recorded by the software. The proctor, the first in line to determine whether fraud has taken place, can see and hear everything. If fraud is suspected, a member of the exam committee also needs to see/hear the data. Moreover, in some cases an employee from the proctoring software vendor might access the data, for example to solve technical issues. It thus matters under which conditions online proctoring software is used. HEIs employ technical and organisational measures to reduce privacy risks, such as limiting the number of people with access to the data and reducing the storage duration of the data. Yet, no prior studies have investigated which specific factors fall under these topics and how they might affect privacy concerns and acceptability of online proctoring software.

Current and future applications of online proctoring therefore deserve extensive scrutiny from the field of Human Computer Interaction (HCI), exposing which specifics of online proctoring software increase or decrease its acceptability, as well as discovering insights into how to potentially mitigate those. The goal of the present study is to provide such detailed insights by unfolding the nature and application of online proctoring software, using the Contextual Integrity (CI) framework [ 18 ]. Within CI, an appropriate flow of information is defined according to five parameters: (1) the sender of the information, (2) the recipient of the information, (3) the type of information being transmitted, (4) the subject about whom the information is and (5) under which condition(s) or transmission principle(s) the information flows. Investigating this may inform us which specific aspects of online proctoring software cause the most privacy concerns, as well as under which conditions these concerns might be alleviated, for example by designing interfaces with more friction to help students reflect on and understand the software and potential privacy issues better [ 19 ]. In addition, by including actors and conditions which are not specific to online proctoring per se , the results of this study may expose in more general terms how acceptable sharing certain information with certain recipients under certain conditions may be. This may lead to insights about situations in which monitoring online behaviour is seen as acceptable or not. Building on the results of the present study, the HCI community can further investigate how online proctoring software could be improved to reduce privacy issues and test anxiety, and without deteriorating its ability to prevent and detect fraud.

2 ONLINE PROCTORING AND PRIVACY

Online proctoring software is used to prevent and detect two important aspects of exam fraud during online exams: (1) test- taker fraud and (2) test- taking fraud. The former refers to ensuring the person taking the exam is really the person who is supposed to take the exam, not someone else (authorship and identity verification [ 20 ]); the latter refers to ensuring the exam has been made according to certain rules, such as the prohibition of consulting books, cheat sheets or other information sources during the exam (cheating detection) [ 21 ].

To counter these two forms of fraud, online proctoring can be applied in three different ways: (1) traditional or live proctoring, (2) recorded proctoring and (3) automated proctoring [ 9 , 22 - 24 ]. With live proctoring, a proctor observes one or more students (commonly up to 7 to 10 students simultaneously [ 25 ]) for the entire duration of the online exam. Students share their webcam (video feed) and microphone (audio feed) with a proctor. In this case, standard video conferencing software can be used (e.g., for oral exams [ 26 ]). Test-taker fraud is prevented by requesting students to display their identity document or student ID in front of the webcam; test-taking fraud is prevented by watching and listening to whether students do not consult any prohibited sources. However, the proctor cannot see what students are doing on their devices themselves, which means that looking up information online or chatting with other students is still possible. To prevent such additional types of cheating, specialised proctoring software can be used, which allows for more extensive observations (such as observing the student's screen, browser logs, system logs, keystrokes, mouse gestures, etc.). In addition, further interventions on the student's device are possible (browser lockdown: blocking the student from visiting any websites during the exam [ 9 ]). With recorded proctoring, the specialised proctoring software records and analyses various inputs to allow proctors to determine post-hoc whether fraudulent behaviour has taken place. These recordings are stored for a certain period after completing the exam, allowing proctors to analyse the data at their own time and pace. This yields extra efficiency, as recordings can be played back more rapidly. Furthermore, proctors have certain aids at their disposal. Firstly, (most) proctoring software uses algorithms to detect and automatically flag suspicious behaviour [ 27 ], such that the proctor can quickly skip to certain potentially relevant time stamps. Secondly, HEIs can opt to review only a subset of all recordings, e.g., a random sample or only the top 20% with the most suspicious flags. Decisions about whether actual exam fraud have occurred are always left to a (real) person [ 4 ].

Prior research suggests that the application of online proctoring leads to less academic dishonesty [ 28 - 35 ], which is evidenced by higher grades and longer exam completion times in the non-proctored as opposed to the proctored condition. These results indicate that online proctoring software does a good job at preventing exam fraud. However, much less is known about the ability of online proctoring software to correctly detect actual misconduct during an online exam. Anecdotal evidence exists of online proctoring software incorrectly flagging student behaviour as fraudulent, oftentimes leading to major student backlash [ 36 , 37 ]. One study recruited Computer Science students who were asked to intentionally cheat during an online exam; none of the cheaters were caught by the automated analysis and only one was caught by a human agent [ 38 ]. These researchers conclude the use of online proctoring can be best compared to a placebo effect.

Despite the understudied efficacy of online proctoring software, students have on multiple occasions reported that the use of online proctoring leads to increased levels of exam anxiety and privacy concern. For example, one study found that online exams, particularly those with online proctoring, increased exam anxiety while there was no difference in the temptation to cheat [ 10 ]. Another study found that (medical) students worried most about ‘unjustified invalidation’ due to technical and/or privacy issues [ 39 ]. Whether exam performance is affected by proctoring anxiety is however not yet clear. Some found no consistent effect [ 10 , 40 ], others found a moderate effect [ 41 ], and yet others hinted at a correlation between trait anxiety and lower exam scores [ 42 ], but in a follow-up study, the authors conclude online proctoring does not cause but only strengthens this correlation [ 43 ]. When it comes to privacy, some studies find that both students and educational staff clearly express privacy concerns. Still, concerns are expressed in generic terms, thus not much is known about what specifics of proctoring are problematic [ 11 , 15 ]. Others, studying privacy concerns in more detail, however, have found significant differences in comfort between different monitoring methods (e.g., mouse tracking, microphone recording, eye tracking), clearly indicating some methods are considered less comfortable than others. In addition, concerns exist about the amount and nature of personal data that was being recorded and sharing such data with proctoring software companies [ 14 ].

Another privacy concern stems from the lack of clarity (amongst students) about who is allowed access to the data recorded by the online proctoring software [ 38 ]. Others have identified different actors relevant to the broader context of digital education, which may or may not have access to the data (these include students themselves; instructors and administrative staff; service providers and their third-party affiliates; and external adversaries [ 12 ]). In addition to issues around privacy and personal data, the use of proctoring software has led to cases of discrimination against people of colour, religious people, and people with disabilities [ 16 , 44 - 48 ]. Likely, the algorithms used by proctoring software, for example to perform eye tracking, face detection, and flag suspicious behaviour are at the root of these issues [ 49 ].

In popular media, several articles have been published on privacy concerns related to online proctoring. Issues raised here are about anxiety of being monitored constantly, concerns about too much invasion of private spaces, fear of data misuse and concerns about sharing personally identifiable information with proctoring software vendors [ 50 - 53 ]. It is often not clear to students who exactly is watching, why certain data is being collected, which behaviour is still acceptable during the online exam, if and why they are being suspected of cheating, who (else) has access to the data, etc. [ 54 - 56 ]. Moreover, recordings made by the software may include additional (non-relevant) personal information which according to for example the European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) can be categorised as sensitive data: e.g., government identifiers, information about medical conditions, political views, race and ethnicity, etc. [ 57 ]). Such data can only be processed under very strict conditions and if there a clear need.

3 PRIVACY AS CONTEXTUAL INTEGRITY

The Contextual Integrity (CI) framework [ 18 ] is a well-established framework that can be used to discover and describe privacy norms and expectations in more detail. CI states that what is considered appropriate information flowing from one actor to another is determined by context-specific norms, which originate from the social, cultural, and legal domain. Such norms can be explicit, for instance codified in laws and regulations, as well as implicit, such as social norms prescribing acceptable behaviour in certain social settings. Information flows consist of five parameters: (1) the sender ; the actor responsible for sending out the information, (2) the recipient ; the actor receiving the information, (3) the type of information ; the actual information being transmitted, (4) the subject ; the actor to whom the information relates and (5) the transmission principle ; under which conditions or constraints the information is allowed to flow. For example, an (acceptable) information flow might be a student (sender) sharing with their study advisor (recipient) a particular dislike about a certain teacher (information type, subject), if the study advisor guarantees to keep this information between them (transmission principle). Whenever one (or more) of these five parameters are not in line with a person's norm, a privacy violation occurs.

CI has previously been utilised to discover privacy norms in different contexts, such as Internet of Things (IoT) [ 58 - 60 ] and education [ 61 , 62 ], and provides a means to study the nuanced influence of context-specific information on the acceptability of information flows. Such insights are highly necessary, for example, to ‘solve’ the often-quoted privacy paradox , which states that although many value their privacy, most disclose significantly more personal information online than their stated intentions would predict [ 63 - 65 ]. As a matter of fact, the privacy paradox might be a ‘myth’, because in many studies, people are asked in general how they feel about privacy while the during the actual behavioural experiment which then follows, people make much more nuanced privacy decisions based on the five contextual factors described by the CI framework [ 18 , 66 ]. Thus, conceptualisations and assessments of privacy should incorporate more nuanced contextual factors as opposed to generally stated privacy preferences in order to better understand why individuals want to share data or not [ 67 ].

In the present study, we assess privacy in the context of online proctoring software using precisely those nuanced contextual factors. We use qualitative methods to discover which factors are relevant to online proctoring software and operationalise them using the CI framework. We then analyse the results of a survey in which participants jointly rate the acceptability of in total 1064 information flows. By using different analyses, we can discover general attitudes towards each of the individual contextual factors (information type, recipient, transmission principle) but also how they affect each other.

4 METHOD

The approach for this study is based on previous work using the CI framework in other contexts [ 59 , 60 ] and consists of the following steps: (1) defining the relevant CI parameters, (2) generating information flows based on these parameters and filtering out those that do not make sense, (3) running a survey and (4) describing and analysing the results. In this section, this approach is described in more detail.

4.1 Defining CI parameters relevant to online proctoring

The first step for this study was to find and select CI parameters (senders, recipients, subjects, attributes, transmission principles) relevant to the context of online proctoring. In order to get a complete list, we used a combination of methods: first, we conducted semi-structured interviews with three professionals at three different Dutch HEIs. 2 In their roles as assessment specialists, they support and train colleagues with setting up (online) exams and correct usage of online proctoring software. Through these interviews, we discovered which software they used, what types of data were collected, with whom and under which conditions data could be shared. 3 Second, we read previously published academic work [ 9 , 23 , 27 , 68 ] and analysed the privacy policies of several popular proctoring software solutions (Proctorio, Proctor Exam, ProctorU [ 23 ]) to find additional parameters not mentioned during the interviews, until no new information emerged. The authors then discussed whether and how to include each parameter mentioned or found in this study. Some parameters were reworded to improve clarity, others were combined into a single parameter to reduce the total number of parameters, and because some parameters are very similar - treating them separately would likely yield no additional insights (for example, the information type “hardware and software information (the websites the student visits, which applications are running, the contents of the student's clipboard (ctrl + C, ctrl + V), etc.)” was combined into a single parameter instead of three distinct parameters). This resulted in 1 sender, 1 subject, 7 recipients, 14 attributes and 14 transmission principles. See below for details. 4

4.1.1 Subject. During an exam, the proctoring software monitors and gathers information about the “test taker” (the student taking the exam). It might also be the case that others are accidentally included in the recordings - those who happen to be in the same room or within the range of the camera or microphone, such as the test taker's partner, housemates, family members - those could be then considered to be subjects as well. While it is important for detecting fraud such events are recorded, the intention is that the only subject visible/audible in the recording is the student itself. Therefore, within this study we used one subject: the student (formulated as “you”, the student taking the survey).

4.1.2 Sender. Regardless of which type of proctoring is used (live or recorded (with or without automation)), the sender always remains the same: the software installed on students’ devices which monitors them during the exam. One could argue the proctor who first watches the recordings becomes a sender if (s)he suspects fraud. The proctor is usually not the person who has the final say in whether fraud indeed took place and if yes, what the consequences are for the student. Usually, HEIs follow predefined escalation procedures where proctors forward the footage and their findings to a member of the exam commission. In rare cases, footage may even be forwarded even to others within and outside the HEI. 5 This means a proctor or exam commission become senders themselves. However, since the information types do not change in this flow and (a member of) the exam commission is already included as recipient (see below), adding additional senders would only lead to a significant increase is the number of flows to be rated by participants whilst unlikely leading to important (new) insights for this study. Thus, for this study we used only one sender parameter: online proctoring software.

4.1.3 Recipient. Regardless of which type of proctoring is used, the data captured by the proctoring software will be reviewed by a proctor. Generally, the proctor is an employee of the HEI itself; alternatively, a proctor can also be provided as a service by the proctoring software vendor. Most HEIs have well-defined and strict policies who is allowed to view the recordings (only the proctor and only in some cases, the exam commission). However, HEIs may have different policies, allowing, for example, teachers to view the recordings. Moreover, from the interviews we derived several other recipients who could potentially access the recorded data, even though regulations would currently not allow this (such as the student's future employer). To test whether this would signify a crossing into unacceptable information flows and to compare the acceptability of a range of potential recipients, we included these possibilities. We included the following list of potential recipients in this study (to improve readability, we use the short name for each information type when reporting the results):

  1. a proctor from the student's institution (proctor)
  2. the course teacher (teacher)
  3. a member of the exam committee (oversight board) from the student's institution (exam com)
  4. the proctoring software company (vendor)
  5. the student's parents/guardians (parents)
  6. the student's future employer (employer)
  7. law enforcement agencies (e.g., the police, government intelligence agencies) (law enforcement)

4.1.4 Information types. To determine fraud, the most obvious factors to observe are students themselves (while taking the exam) and the rooms or dorms students are taking the exam in. This way, a proctor can see whether students are alone and are not using any forbidden material such as books or notes. However, proctoring software can potentially monitor many other types of data that may indicate cheating, for example web browser use during the exam. Increasing the number of data sources also increases (the chance of) recording unrelated privacy-sensitive material. For example, prior to the exam test takers are (often) required to perform a room or desk scan: a video recording of the entire room (or desk) which they are working from (this would expose forbidden material physically present in the same room). However, it also shows the student's personal space (house / (bed) room) which often reveals additional information about the test taker which is sometimes classified as highly sensitive, such as religious or political views. 6 We included the following information types in this study:

  1. a video recording of the student (video)
  2. a video recording of the student's entire room (room scan)
  3. a video recording of the student's desk/table (desk scan)
  4. how often the student looks away from the screen by tracking the student's eye movements (gaze detection)
  5. an audio recording using the student's computer's microphone (audio)
  6. a recording of the student's (computer) screen (screen)
  7. all the student's keystrokes and mouse movements, clicks and scrolling motions (keystrokes)
  8. (a photo of) the student's passport / official ID card (passport)
  9. (a photo of) the student's student ID card (student id)
  10. the student's personal details (e.g., name, age, birthplace) (personal)
  11. information about the student's personal self (religious, political, medical) (political)
  12. the student's location (location)
  13. hardware and software information (the websites the student visits, which applications are running, the contents of the student's clipboard (ctrl + C, ctrl + V), etc.) (device info)
  14. the student's answers to the exam questions (answers)

4.1.5 Transmission principles. There are many ways of handling and managing the data recorded by proctoring software which may affect the acceptability of using proctoring. In addition to having the recordings viewed by a proctor (manually), most proctoring software solutions use algorithms to find and flag suspicious situations or behaviour. The interviews made clear that HEIs use this feature differently: some opt to use this feature as a guide to determine which recordings to look back at, by only fully watching the top 10% with most flagged situations (in addition to a random sample of the rest), whilst others watch recordings of every student but using the suspicious situations markers to skip to that point in the video and see what really happened. To reduce privacy risks, HEIs employ several technical and organisational measures, such as limiting the duration the data is being stored, allowing students to object to taking the exam online, asking students to show their student IDs instead of passports, etc. All these factors may influence the extent to which students find online proctoring acceptable. Within the context of this study, we translated such measures into transmission principles. In addition, there are several other possible circumstances in which information can be stored or used differently and furthermore, data from proctoring may result in ‘function creep’, a well-known phenomenon which states a system or technology is expanded beyond its original purpose [ 69 ], such as using the recorded data for advertising purposes or to monitor students' health. We included the following transmission principles in this study:

  1. if the data is viewed/analysed by a real person (manual)
  2. if the data is analysed using artificial intelligence (automated)
  3. if the data is removed after you received your final grade (stored temp)
  4. if it's unknown for how long the data is stored (stored always)
  5. if you've given explicit permission/consent (consent)
  6. if due to exceptional circumstances (such as a pandemic), your institution requires it (pandemic)
  7. if your institution assures you it's fully compliant with regulations (such as the GDPR) (compliant)
  8. to verify your identity (verify)
  9. if the data is used for profiling (i.e., to learn certain characteristics about students in order to make future predictions) (profiling)
  10. if the data is used for personalised advertisements (advertisements)
  11. if the data is used to monitor your health (wellbeing)
  12. if the data is used to catch criminals and terrorists (criminals)
  13. if the data includes data collected during a period of 24 hours before the exam (collected before)
  14. if the software has difficulty recognising people of colour or people with disabilities (discriminatory)

4.2 Survey deployment

By combining the different CI parameters, information flows can be generated. The second step of this study was thus to create an online survey in which participants could rate the acceptability of these proctoring information flows. In the following sections, we describe the details of this approach in more detail.

The survey was created in and administered through Qualtrics. 7 After having received approval from the authors’ institution's Ethical Review Board (ERB), we recruited participants through Prolific, an online service for researchers to distribute a survey quickly and easily amongst a large population. 8 We sought to recruit a total of 500 participants to ensure an average of 30 participants per set. Completing the survey on average took slightly under 14 minutes and participants received a small reward for their participation ( $ 2.30 US Dollar). 9 In order to partake in the study, participants needed to be a student actively enrolled in an undergraduate (BA/BSc/other) or graduate (MA/MSc/MPhil/other) program and have a 100% ‘Minimum Approval Rate’ of earlier studies they completed through Prolific. 10 Those who failed to meet those requirements could not proceed with the survey and received no reward.

4.3 Survey design

4.3.1 Introduction, consent, pre-screening, and proctoring explanation. In the first part of the survey, we asked participants to read through the consent form, which contained details about the study, the researcher and that the study had been approved by the ERB of the authors' institution. After giving consent, participants had to answer two pre-screening questions to ensure they were still (active as) a student: (1) “are you a student,” and (2): “which level of education are you currently in?” 11 Participants who failed to meet this requirement were not eligible to complete the study. 12 Next, an explanation was given about online proctoring software as we expected not all participants were familiar with it, or some of the terms we used throughout the survey.

4.3.2 Generating Contextual Integrity statements. The next part of the survey contained questions about the acceptability of proctoring information flows. To study the influence of varying CI parameters (information type, recipient, transmission principle) on the acceptability of these information flows, the first question every participant answered was a baseline question. For this question, a ‘default’ recipient was chosen (a proctor from the student's institution) and a transmission principle was omitted (see Figure 1 ). The participant then scored the acceptability of each of the of information types on a 5-point Likert scale from “(1) completely unacceptable” to “(5) completely acceptable”. Responses to this baseline question can later be compared with information flows containing specific contextual factors. By starting the survey with the baseline questions, we prevented priming effects of the other CI parameters.

With the parameters outlined in the previous section, a total of 1 * 1 * 7 * 14 * 14 = 1372 statements about possible proctoring information flows were generated in the following form: “During an online exam which you [subject] take from home on your own computer, online proctoring software [sender] sends [information type] to [recipient]. How acceptable is this for you when this transfer occurs under the following conditions [transmission principle]?”

Some combinations of parameters were filtered out because they did not make sense or were technically impossible. For example, it is highly unlikely that “the student's parents” (recipient) “analysed the data using artificial intelligence” (transmission principle), or that “a member of the exam committee (oversight board) from the student's institution” (recipient) uses the data “to catch criminals” (transmission principle). After filtering out these oddities, 1064 unique information flows remained. Rating each of the 1064 information flows would have been too much work for the participants. Therefore, to prevent fatigue effects, the complete set of information flows was further divided into smaller sets, grouped by information type. Since we have 14 information types, this resulted in 14 sets of (on average) 76 information flows. Each set contains, for that specific information type, every valid combination of recipient and transmission principle. We displayed each set to participants in a fixed order on 7 separate subsequent pages in the survey: every page contained an information flow with a different recipient. For each recipient, a matrix table was displayed containing all the (relevant) transmission principles.

Each participant was assigned randomly to and answered only one set (see Figure 2 for an example of one such set). For example, after answering the baseline question, a participant was randomly assigned to the information type “the student's personal details (e.g., name, age, birthplace)”. All questions about the acceptability of information flows which then followed contained this information type. For example, the first question from this set was “During an online exam which you take from home on your own computer, online proctoring software sends your personal details (e.g., name, age, birthplace) to a proctor from your institution . How acceptable is this for you when this transfer occurs under the following conditions?” (Emphasis was added on the CI parameters to improve readability throughout the survey.) In the next question, the recipient changed to “the course teacher”. Subsequent questions contained the other recipients. The order of recipients was set, but to prevent habituation bias, the order of the matrix table to rate the acceptability of transmission principles was randomised.

We aimed for a minimum of 30 participants per set, so at least 420 participants were required for the entire survey. By making sure each participant could only partake in the survey once and each participant only answered one of 14 sets, survey responses across different sets are independent.

4.3.3 Final open questions and demographics. The final part of the survey consisted of several partially optional open questions, followed by several questions about participants’ demographics. To gain an understanding of the participants, we asked how many online exams they had done where online proctoring was used; if offered the choice, how many exams they would take online with proctoring instead of in-person; if they preferred taking exams online, for what reasons; and if they had any objections against online proctoring, they could describe them in their own words. 13 The demographics questions asked participants about their age, completed years of study, gender and current country of residence.

4.4 Response Analysis

For the analysis, we were mainly interested in the influence of different CI parameters (information type, recipient, transmission principle) on the acceptability of proctoring information flows. First, we observed general attitudes towards the acceptability of each of the CI parameters individually, then we explored the pairwise effects (how each of the CI parameters influences each other). In the sections below, these steps are explained in further detail.

4.4.1 Filtering incomplete responses and failed attention checks. A total of 540 people started with the survey through Prolific. Of these people, 41 did not finish the study or did not meet the criteria for participation. Some participants (43) failed one or both attention checks we built into the survey, resulting in a final sample of 456 valid responses with an average of 32 responses per set.

4.4.2 Calculating average acceptability scores. To observe general trends, we first computed averages for each of the CI parameter we varied throughout the survey (information type, recipient, transmission principle). 14 For information type, these averages simply consist of the answers to the baseline question. For recipient, we computed averages for each of the seven recipients across all information types and transmission principles. For example, we calculated the mean of all information flows where recipient is “a proctor from the student's institution”, regardless of the information type and transmission principle. This provides a general overview of how acceptable each of the seven recipients is considered. Next, we computed averages for each of the fourteen transmission principles across all attributes and recipient. For example, we calculated the means of all information flows where transmission principle is “if your institution assures you it's fully compliant with regulations (such as the GDPR)”, regardless of the information type and recipient. This provides an overview of how acceptable each of the fourteen transmission principles is considered generally.

Then, to study the effect of different CI parameters each other more closely, we grouped two of the three parameters which we varied throughout this study together in different combinations. This resulted in three pairs : (1) an information type/recipient pair (calculated by computing means for each combination of information type/recipient across all transmission principles), (2) an information type/transmission principle pair (calculated by computing means of each combination information type/transmission principle across all recipients) and (3) a recipient/transmission principle pair (calculated by computing means for each combination of recipient/transmission principle across all information types). For example, the information type “a video recording of the student's entire room” and the recipient “the course teacher” were combined in a single pair – the average was calculated by computing the mean of all information flows with these two parameters and all transmission principles. Using these averages, we can study the pairwise effect of each of the CI parameters on the acceptability of proctoring information flows. For example, a specific recipient or transmission principle might have a positive or negative effect on a specific information type. If one of the parameters has a distinctively high or low average acceptability score across all pairs, it is likely this parameter is important to the acceptability of the proctoring information flow and thus the privacy norms students uphold.

4.4.3 Comparing with baseline: significance testing. Because the sample of responses is independent, we used the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to measure the difference between specific information flows containing all five CI parameters and the baseline condition (cf., the method used in [ 59 , 60 ]). For the recipient/transmission principle pair, we used “if the data is viewed/analysed by a real person” as a baseline condition. The combination of recipient “your parents/guardians” and transmission principle “if the data is viewed/analysed by a real person” was excluded from the survey to reduce cognitive load and because we believed it did not make sense to explicitly ask participants to rate this combination. This meant there is no baseline score for this recipient to use. We therefore excluded this recipient from further analysis.

For each pair, we conducted multiple tests: one for each combination of CI parameters per set. The information type/recipient set consisted of 14 (information types) * 7 (recipients) = 98 comparisons with the baseline condition; the attribute/transmission principle set of 14 (information types) * 14 (transmission principles) - 10 (exclusions) = 186 comparisons; and the recipient/transmission principle set of 7 (recipients) * 14 (transmission principles) - 17 (exclusions) = 81 comparisons. When performing multiple tests, Family Wise Error Rate (FWER) must be considered, to reduce the likelihood of finding type 1 error false positives. For this, we used the Holm-Bonferroni method [ 70 ], using the total number of comparisons per set to reduce the prespecified significance level ( $\alpha = .05$ ).

6 DISCUSSION

It is likely HEIs will continue to use online exams and online proctoring software, for instance for students who prefer not to travel towards the HEI [ 72 ], to support students with busy schedules (such as professional athletes) who can more easily integrate online tests into their agendas or for students with disabilities for whom travelling towards the institution might be (extra) challenging. However, student privacy concerns need to be addressed. Those responsible for applying, developing, and regulating online proctoring software (HEIs, proctoring software vendors, and regulators) can build the more nuanced results of this study to improve not only the product itself, but also to raise awareness, clarify instructions, and improve policies. In this section, the results are interpreted and discussed, and several recommendations are presented.

6.1 Context matters

Previous studies have raised several privacy issues concerning the use of online proctoring software [ 10 , 14 , 39 , 42 , 50 - 53 , 73 ]. Our study confirms those results, as in general, the acceptability of many information flows hardly ever rises above neutral. When looking at the ‘general’ results/trends (as outlined in section 5.2.1), few information types, recipients, and transmission principles are on average considered (somewhat) acceptable. However, this changes when contextual factors are made explicit. When studying the pairwise effects, some combinations of contextual factors lead to significantly decreased acceptability, while others cause significant increases in acceptability. Interestingly, the top 3 and bottom 2 information types in the baseline condition (see Table 1 ) are influenced the most by recipients and transmission principles. The acceptability of the top 3 information types significantly decreases for all recipients (except “the student's parents/guardians”) and most of the transmission principles. The other way around, the acceptability of the bottom 2 information types significantly increases when explicitly including recipients who are relevant to the context of digital education in the information flow. The acceptability remains slightly low (between somewhat unacceptable and neutral), but the significant differences hint that if these highly sensitive information types are not shared with recipients outside of the context of education, students’ objections are somewhat alleviated. The same holds for certain transmission principles in combination with both information types; these transmission principles are about choice or a force majeure (giving consent and exceptional circumstances) and compliancy (including purpose limitation (identify verification) and limiting storage duration). One of the information types (“information about your personal self (religious, political, medical)”) is significantly increased further by four additional transmission principles (see Figure 4 ). This confirms describing information flows with all five parameters makes one (re)consider the acceptability of the entire information flow, even for those information types which initially score highest or lowest.

6.2 Who, what or why?

While the acceptability of certain information types and transmission principles varies substantially, it is striking that individual differences between all recipients included in this study are quite small. Even though we intentionally included recipients which would clearly lead to a crossing of contextual borders, on average, all recipients (ignoring specific information types and transmission principles) have a mean average value of between 2 (somewhat unacceptable) and 3 (neutral). This indicates privacy issues within the context of online proctoring are not so much about who receives/has access to certain data as they are about what data is being recorded/transmitted under which conditions . It would be interesting to explore this seeming indifference about recipients in more detail and learn which reasons or motivations lie behind it.

6.3 Obvious privacy violations

The results confirm that crossing contextual boundaries indeed leads to a privacy violation. This is the strongest for the pairs involving transmission principles (see Figure 4 and Figure 5 ). For nearly all information types, using the data for personal advertisements purposes significantly reduces acceptability. Although to our knowledge, online proctoring software does not display advertisements nor actively sells (aggregated) data, fourth party relationships have been found for one proctoring software vendor [ 74 ], meaning parties are involved in the ecosystem with which the originating HEI has no (contractual) relationship.

For information types which without contextual information are considered (somewhat/completely) acceptable or neutral, using the data for profiling and/or monitoring the student's health clearly and significantly decreases acceptability (those where the difference is not significant already scored low in the baseline condition). In addition, there are some obvious conditions related to the software itself or its configuration which are considered (somewhat/completely) unacceptable. If it's unknown (to the student) for how long the data collected by the software is stored and if the software (its algorithms) has difficulties recognising people of colour or people with disabilities, acceptability of any information type is very low. The latter should be a clear indication for HEIs and online proctoring software vendors to reconsider the usage of automated analysis.

Another transmission principle which is consistently considered (somewhat/completely) unacceptable is “if the data includes data collected during a period of 24 hours before the exam”. To be able to be proctored online using specialised software, students must install software on their personal devices (usually a browser extension) which requires a vast set of system permissions. For a smooth start, students are advised to install this software well before the start of the exam. Moreover, once installed our assumption is that most students refrain from uninstalling or disabling the plugin once the exam is over. In theory, the software can thus abuse the given system permissions to gather data outside of the context of an exam. Perhaps additional controls can be designed to give students more control over when the software is activated or not, and if such controls already exist, the interface can be improved to make students aware of such controls.

6.4 Student awareness and making (bad) choices

Some of the results may also raise concerns. First, when observing pairs involving recipients (see Figure 3 and Figure 5 ) we notice that the recipient “the course teacher” has similar acceptability scores as the proctor and the exam committee. During the interviews we held with assessment experts, it was clearly mentioned that teachers have no role to play in the actual proctoring, which is strictly left to (trained) proctors. The respondents, however, seemed willing to share even highly sensitive data with teachers despite the lack of reasons to do so. Possibly, students have high trust in their teachers and expect their personal data to be safe with them. Or, as the assessment experts we interviewed were from Dutch HEIs, whereas respondents came from all over Europe, it might be that other European countries allow teachers to view the data recorded by the proctoring software. However, we do not believe this is highly likely because the same GDPR applies throughout Europe. Second, “information about the student's personal self (religious, political, medical)” is usually considered highly sensitive information and is therefore subjected to additional (stricter) regulations. It has no relevance to determining whether fraud has occurred during online exams and likely, HEIs are in violation of regulations if they process such information (unless they obtain explicit, freely given consent [ 75 ]). However, the results indicate that under certain conditions, the processing of such information is significantly (more) acceptable. To protect their privacy, students should possibly be made aware disclosing such information is highly undesirable or even prohibited. Third, we observe contextual factors diminish the initial difference in acceptability in the baseline condition between the student's passport and the student's student ID. Both information types can be used to verify the student's identity, but a passport typically leaks more personal information than a student ID (e.g., government identifiers). However, this distinction in sensitivity is not reflected in the results.

Another interesting result is the substantial positive effect that giving consent has on other contextual factors. The acceptability of nearly all information types and recipients is significantly increased, even when contextual boundaries are clearly crossed. This fits the long-held tradition of “privacy self-management”, however, several problems with consent have been identified before [ 76 ]: ‘structural’ problems, relating to the number of entities and complex nature of privacy involved in making privacy decisions; and ‘cognitive’ problems, relating to the fact that most privacy decisions are made intuitively, without properly reflecting on the consequences and risks [ 19 ]. Therefore, regulations such as the GDPR impose severe limitations on the use of consent: it must be freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous; moreover, it should be revokable (as easy as it was given) [ 77 ]. The first limitation (“freely given”) immediately causes problems due to the power imbalance between students and their HEIs: students are inclined to say yes to decisions put forward by their HEI, since getting their degree depends on it [ 78 ]. At the very least, this makes the use of consent within the context of education highly questionable [ 79 ]. To truly make a decision for online examination with proctoring “freely given”, a viable (offline) alternative must be offered to students [ 25 ]. In addition, careful consideration must be given towards ensuring students make well-informed decisions which they understand and can actively reflect on, to meet the other requirements about specific, informed and unambiguous consent. Although writing manuals and readable privacy policies with clear texts might help, interfaces can be designed in such a way as to trigger reflective thinking [ 19 ], which might be more effective.

Finally, we observe differences in acceptability scores between people with and without prior proctoring experience. Regarding recipients, all recipients outside the context of the HEI were considered significantly more acceptable by students with prior proctoring experience. For several transmission principles, a similar difference is observed. Especially those that would indicate a crossing into different contexts (“if the data is used for profiling (i.e., to learn certain characteristics about students in order to make future predictions)”, “if the data is used to monitor your health” and “if the data is used to catch criminals and terrorists”) are considered more acceptable by students with proctoring experience. Although we did not explicitly study the reasons for these differences, a possible explanation is that simply being familiar with technology reduces concerns about privacy.

6.5 Recommendations for technical and organisational measures

Based on the results, we provide the following recommendations to HEIs for the use of online proctoring software:

  • Stop using the privacy invasive entire room scan; instead, use the desk scan , to determine whether the surroundings the student is in during the exam qualifies. The room scan is considered as the least acceptable information type, and although some contextual factors significantly improve acceptance, it remains at the bottom. A desk scan allows the student to demonstrate no forbidden material lies within reach without exposing their entire private space.
  • Ask students for explicit permission before partaking in online exams with online proctoring, even though this might not adhere to the strict requirements of the GDPR and can therefore possibly not be used as legal grounds for the processing of personal data. The results of this study clearly indicate giving consent significantly increases acceptability of the information flows for nearly all other contextual factors. Presumably, this will lead to less privacy concerns.
  • Carefully configure which information types are used for each exam . Clearly, some information types are more problematic than others, regardless of the effect of other contextual factors. Most proctoring software can be configured to toggle some specific information types on or off per exam. When it is unknown whether an additional information type improves the chances of catching frauds, the results of this study suggest it is recommendable to not gather such information. Moreover, online proctoring software seems to do a much better job at preventing fraud than actually detecting it [ 38 ].
  • Maintain and communicate clearly about storage limitations . The results clearly indicate students to not accept the processing of their data if it is not known (to them) for how long the data is stored. On the other hand, if the data is stored until the final grade is known belongs to the top 3 most acceptable transmission principles. This indicates it is important for students that (and to know that) their information is removed once it is no longer needed.
  • Do not use online proctoring data for any other purposes than fraud detection . This might be self-evident, but worth mentioning nonetheless as the results of this study clearly confirm this recommendation. For example, using the data for profiling activities is considered (somewhat/completely) unacceptable. Though not explicitly mentioned, profiling could also refer to student/learning analytics [ 80 ], another aspect of digital education. Data from online exams can be used in this context [ 81 ], but according to the results of this study, should not.

6.6 Limitations

The results of the present study should be considered with the following limitations in mind.

6.6.1 Respondents. Participants were recruited through an online platform, Prolific. While we recruited a diverse set of students from across Europe, the results might be different in other populations, such as students from countries with less academic freedom or different views of privacy.

In addition, some statements were quite long and may have been difficult to understand for participants of which English was not their primary language. However, although we did not check English proficiency, we have only selected participants with a high trust rating. As most of the studies on prolific are in English, we can be fairly sure that students understood all statements. Furthermore, as participants answered on average 76 statements, fatigue effects may have occurred. However, we could not statistically verify this.

Lastly, participants using the Prolific platform might have above average knowledge and understanding of digital technology. Future studies could account for these uncertainties by recruiting participants through other means.

6.6.2 Attitudes vs. actual behaviour. The difference in reported privacy attitudes compared to actual behaviour often observed in studies about privacy choices led to the concept of the privacy paradox. This study did not measure actual behaviour, only privacy attitudes. Whether there is a difference between attitudes reported in this study and actual behaviour with online proctoring software should be studied in the future. However, we believe by using the CI framework, chances of encountering the privacy paradox in this context diminished substantially, because instead of asking participants to report more general attitudes to privacy (for example, by using the General Privacy Concern scale [ 82 ]), we took all contextual factors which jointly determine how acceptable information flows are and thus how people make privacy decisions into account.

Another limitation which might startle a potential difference between reported attitudes and actual behaviour is the lack of a ‘real’ context. Participating in an online survey is not the same as actually experiencing the stress of doing an (online) exam, hoping your device will not fail and the internet connection remains stable [ 39 ], not to mention the stress of not knowing whether one is prepared well enough. Such contextual factors might influence privacy attitudes as well.

6.6.3 CI parameters formulation interpretation. It is possible that respondents had different understanding of what certain contextual factors meant (to them). Because of the relatively small sample size for each set (32 on average), this might have influenced the results. For example, “if the data is analysed using artificial intelligence” might have different meanings to different people; we do not explain what artificial intelligence means nor what kind of algorithms online proctoring software uses.

Furthermore, recipients and transmission principles used in this study are at least in part based on how Dutch HEIs use online proctoring. In turn, this is based on Dutch laws and regulations, for example what procedures must be followed if students object to the verdict given by the proctor and/or the exam committee. Possibly, there are differences in how proctoring is used in other countries.

6.6.4 Data breaches. When discussing online privacy, one can hardly abstain from considering data breaches. As more and more of our daily lives (including education) moves towards the digital domain, the risk of a data breach greatly increases. Of course, proctoring software vendors are no exception: for one vendor, a vulnerability was found exposing the data of tens of thousands of students (but it is unknown whether the vulnerability has been abused) [ 83 ] while other vendors have suffered actual data breaches [ 84 , 85 ]. It can be expected data breaches and weak security influences students’ attitudes towards online proctoring software; future studies should investigate that in more detail.

7 CONCLUSION

This study both confirmed that students have privacy concerns about the usage of online proctoring during online exams, as well as provided new insights into the nature of such concerns. By operationalising the CI framework and surveying students (N = 456), contextual factors determining the acceptability of 1064 proctoring information flows (information type, recipient, and transmission principle) are exposed. Subsequently, detailed analyses revealed how certain factors influence each other. We provide recommendations how these results can be leveraged by HEIs, proctoring software vendors and regulators to alleviate privacy concerns and consequently, increase acceptability of online proctoring software.
