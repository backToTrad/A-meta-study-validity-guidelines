Flat Panel Haptics: Embedded Electroosmotic Pumps for Scalable Shape Displays

Craig Shultz , Human-Computer Interaction Institute, Carnegie Mellon University, United States, cshultz@cmu.edu
Chris Harrison , Human-Computer Interaction Institute, Carnegie Mellon University, United States, chris.harrison@cs.cmu.edu

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581547
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Flat touch interfaces, with or without screens, pervade the modern world. However, their haptic feedback is minimal, prompting much research into haptic and shape-changing display technologies which are self-contained, fast acting, and offer millimeters of displacement while only being only millimeters thick. We present a new, miniaturizable type of shape-changing display using embedded electroosmotic pumps (EEOPs). Our pumps, controlled and powered directly by applied voltage, are 1.5mm in thickness, and allow complete stackups under 5mm. Nonetheless, they can move their entire volume's worth of fluid in 1 second, and generate pressures of +/-50kPa, enough to create dynamic, millimeter-scale tactile features on a surface that can withstand typical interaction forces (<1N). These are the requisite technical ingredients to enable, for example, a pop-up keyboard on a flat smartphone. We experimentally quantify the mechanical and psychophysical performance of our displays and conclude with a set of example interfaces.

1 INTRODUCTION

Today's computing devices are more stimulating than ever. Screens are brighter, more colorful, and have finer resolution than can be perceived. Speakers are louder, more clear, and embedded in so many devices that it comes as no surprise when even the most basic computing devices can talk or play music. Audio and video feedback are so ingrained in our everyday lives that we hardly give these technologies a second thought. Haptic devices, on the other hand, have failed to reach this same level of capability and pervasiveness. Tactile feedback in devices typically comes in the form of mechanical mechanisms like buttons and switches, or in the form of vibration from oscillating mass transducers such as linear resonant actuators (LRAs). These devices offer useful tactile affordances and feedback to our daily computing experiences, allowing us to play more immersive video games, type on our keyboards, adjust the settings in our cars, or subtly notify us of incoming messages without needing much visual attention. However they come nowhere close to mimicking our everyday haptic experiences with physical objects.

The way we interact with the physical world is primarily through the use of active touch exploration [ 20 ]. Our skin is innervated by over 200,000 mechanical nerve afferents, with over 15,000 afferents in each hand [ 14 ]. We actively explore the world around us with these afferents, using a range of exploratory movements and procedures to come to an understanding of the shapes, physical properties, and functions of the objects around us [ 31 ]. Most modern computing interfaces, however, are unable to take advantage of this exploratory, active touch manner of interaction, as the interfaces themselves are incapable of meaningfully changing their shape or configuration during operation. Buttons and switches are discrete, unchanging components once they are implemented, and touchscreens, while offering dynamic reconfigurability of visual UI elements, offer little to no tactile feedback beyond vibration. To put it simply, there is a wide gulf between the types of things we can feel and the ability of our devices to render these same types of feelings.

This lack of tactility has been commented on for well over a decade within the haptics and HCI communities, and work on haptic and shape-changing displays has a rich and compelling history [ 13 , 15 , 23 , 44 , 50 ]. Despite sustained interest in imbuing computer interfaces with dynamic and complex haptic shape-changing ability, the lack of tactility still remains. This is, in part, because the nature of solving this problem is incredibly cross-disciplinary, pulling from diverse fields such as material science, electrical and mechanical engineering, human-machine interface design, psychological perception, and more. Indeed, only recently have these challenges begun to be fully articulated in the form of open grand challenges for researchers to solve [ 2 , 7 ].

In this work, we present a new approach and vision for miniaturizing haptic shape-changing displays. Our devices are slim and lightweight (<5mm in total thickness and <30g), produce mechanically useful ranges of forces (about 1N), respond quickly (<1s), can be shrunk from centimeter scale to millimeter scale. We also offer a path forward for creating compact, high-performance, modular haptic devices which could eventually scale up to hundreds or thousands of individual units, yet have an overall simple construction and function. This approach, which we call flat panel haptics (FPH), contributes to the technical miniaturization and resolution improvement of shape display rendering, directly addressing one of the twelve grand challenges of shape-changing interfaces in the HCI community [ 2 ].

Flat panel haptics takes its inspiration from LCD flat panel visual displays, which embed their actuator element directly in a thin, compact form factor, and allow control of a display element through an applied voltage. This scalable manufacturing technique allowed large arrays of picture elements (i.e., pixels) to be built in many form factors and at low cost, enabling a revolution in the proliferation and embedding of high-performance visual displays in our world. With a similar end in mind for flat panel haptic displays, we focused on materials and methods that are inexpensive, simple, scalable, and bootstrap existing supply chains and materials. All materials were easily procured, processed, and assembled in our lab. Our prototype devices are self-contained, and they are both controlled and powered solely through an electrical cable and set of control electronics. They are also functionally capable of exceptionally high deformations per unit volume, and produce enough mechanical work output to deform the skin and withstand typical interaction forces coming from the user.

We detail the construction and working principles of our FPH displays, focusing much attention on the development and physical evaluation of the actuation layer and embedded electroosmotic pumps (EEOPs), as we believe the development of this compact, scalable, modular method is a core contribution. We also share the results of a psychophysical judgment task we performed with a small set of users. Finally, we showcase three different example flat panel haptic interfaces: a general-purpose shape display, an audio control panel, and a deformable smartphone display (Fig. 1 ).

2 RELATED WORK

In our related work section, we focus on two primary areas: shape changing actuation technologies capable of haptic feedback, and electroosmotic micropumps. We discuss a broad array of actuator technologies, from solid actuators, to pneumatic and hydraulic techniques. Finally, we review the basics of electroosmotic pumps, the micropump technology we selected to serve as the basis for our flat panel haptic prototypes.

2.1 Haptic Shape Changing Technologies

Shape-changing interfaces come many different shapes, sizes, and configurations. A review of their design space identified at least 8 types of object qualities that these displays can achieve: orientation, form, volume, texture, viscosity, spatiality, adding/subtracting, and permeability [ 44 ]. Instead of reviewing all types of shape display, a vast space, we focus specifically on approaches that are tailored more towards delivering direct haptic feedback to users. Specifically, we focus on high strain deformation haptic feedback, intentionally avoiding discussion of other haptic feedback types such as vibration, electrotactile, or variable friction. Our actuator categories are taken from recent material and actuation review articles by Biswas and Visell [ 7 ], and Ankit et al. [ 3 ]. We include some representative interface systems, when applicable.

2.1.1 Solids. Solid actuators are made of either traditional mechanical parts and mechanisms, or bulk materials which can intrinsically create changes in strain. A large majority of shape-changing technologies have been made from electromagnetic motor-based devices, such as DC motors, AC motors, stepper motors, voice coil actuators, and solenoids. While these devices offer high forces and robustness, they are traditionally bulky and difficult to miniaturize and mass produce in large quantities, although recent work seeks to address these challenges [ 16 ]. Example interfaces using electromagnetic technologies include inFORM [ 17 ], BubbleWrap [ 5 ], Project FEELEX [ 25 ], miniature pin arrays [ 53 ], and more recently, a system that used small pot magnets [ 56 ].

Piezoceramic-based actuators are another robust technology with a long history of manufacturing and widespread implementation (commercial braille displays use arrays of piezoceramic elements for their actuation). However, all piezoceramic actuators suffer from very low strain rates due to their exceedingly high stiffness. This makes it impossible to make compact yet high-displacement piezoceramic actuators. Example interfaces include the STRESS display [ 39 ], which used long, comb-like actuators, and a haptic edge display by Jang et al. [ 26 ]. Piezoelectric polymers, such as PVDF and its co-polymers, attempt to mitigate the strain issues, but do not succeed to a significant enough degree; see [ 43 ] for more details. There is one possible exception: so-called electromechanical polymers (EMPs) produced by a company called Novasentis (acquired by Kemet), however little information is available about these materials 1 .

Shape memory alloy (SMAs) actuators are a separate class of bulk shape changing material which deforms with the application of high current. Coelho et al. demonstrated Surflex [ 12 ], a surface that was able to change its bending deformation with SMAs, while Nakatani et al. [ 37 ] was able to array them to create a basic 3D form display. A more compact flexible SMA skin array was demonstrated by Besse et al. [ 6 ], and Hamdan et al. explored SMA "Springlets" for skin stretch [ 22 ]. SMAs also suffer from low strain rates, have traditionally required large amounts of energy, and have been slow to respond, although recent developments have shown that they can be fast [ 38 ], and the company Cambridge Mechatronics 2 has brought to market compact, robust solutions for small displacement actuation.

In an effort to get away from the strain issues with high Young's modulus materials, much research has shifted to even softer materials. Electroactive polymers (EAPs) work on an electrostatic type actuation. The materials are promising as they can achieve very high strains of 100%-300% percent, but are typically too thin for even millimeter-scale deformation. Additionally, they are currently difficult to manufacture at scale and require somewhat exotic materials. They have primarily seen use in art installations, but there are arrays for tactile feedback [ 4 ]. See Franinović et al. [ 18 ] for more information.

Further research continues into even more exotic materials for solid based haptic displays, such as piezoelectrets [ 55 ], liquid crystal networks (LCNs), Ionic Polymer-Metal Composites (IPMCs), ionic polymers (wet EAPs), or thermal bimorph actuators. However, the fact remains that solid actuators will always be limited by small strain rates as they are, by their very definition of being solid, incapable of significant shape change. Because of this, an increasing amount of research has focused on soft encapsulating materials, which are actuated via a pressurized fluid.

2.1.2 Pneumatic. Pneumatic artificial muscles have been in development since the 1950s and have seen widespread use in soft robotics. While the shape-changing portion of pneumatic devices can be made lightweight and high performance, the difficulty comes in the generation and application of high air pressures needed to actuate the shape-changing structure. Long air supply lines and tethers to bulky solenoids, valves and air compressors are difficult to miniaturize. Example interfaces usually consist of elastomeric materials that are filled with pressurized air, such as Harrison and Hudson [ 23 ], or [ 52 ]. More recent work has focused on computationally determining structure form [ 54 ], creating new ways of compactly controlling air on a large scale, such as [ 45 , 46 ] and STOMA [ 21 ], or a combination of pneumatic with particle jamming [ 48 ]. There are commercial haptic gloves on the market which operate using pneumatic methods and offer high numbers of actuators; however they cost nearly $ 100k and must be pneumatically tethered to large, bulky base stations filled with compressors and control arrays, making them impractical for most use cases.

2.1.3 Hydraulic. We believe that a promising approach to compact haptic actuation lies in the realm of hydraulic actuation. Hydraulics offer high potential bandwidth and good power transmission due to the incompressible nature of fluids. The body itself is also made of mostly water, so fluid-based actuators offer good mechanical impedance matching. Despite efforts to commercialize hydraulic-based haptic displays a decade ago by a company called Tactus [ 11 ], little advancement has been made in hydraulic methods until very recently.

A few researchers have showcased actuated bags of fluid using a zipping type electrostatic actuation, also called electrohydraulic soft actuators (ESAs). In SurfaceFlow, Shao et al. [ 47 ] demonstrated large area shape display using compliant dielectric liquid actuators. A similar method was used in Electroflow [ 42 ]. In perhaps the most compact version of this technique, HAXELs [ 33 ] demonstrated moderate force and displacement in an impressive form factor. All of these methods, however, require 500V-10kV to operate and are difficult to scale to different sizes and configurations. Nonetheless, they do show the versatility of hydraulic methods.

We encountered one other reference of a microhydraulic pumping method being used for physical interfaces. LayerPump [ 19 ], and follow-up work Hydromod [ 36 ], use comb-type electrohydrodynamic (EHD) pumps to actuate structures and interface objects. Electrohydrodynamic pumps are closely related to electroosmotic pumps (both are types of electrokinetic pumps). However, we have initially found that electroosmotic pumps can offer a much more compact form factor and higher mechanical performance per applied voltage.

This can be seen in Fig. 2 , which directly compares the two electrokinetic pumps, Hydromod, and our EEOPs (along with many other prior methods). Our EEOP is approximately 100x smaller in volume, yet achieves an approximately 7x higher normalized volume flow rate for 20x less applied voltage (250V vs. 5kV). We used a conservative estimate of volume (1.5 × 5 × 0.5cm) and a reported flow rate of 50ml/min from [ 36 ] for Hydromod, in comparison to the volume ( π × 0.3 × 0.3 × 0.15mm) and flow rate of 4ml/min from our D=6mm EEOP. We include this comparison for the reader simply as a baseline, and wish to acknowledge that additional advancements could potentially be made to EHD "ion drag pumps" in the future.

2.2 Electroosmotic Pumps (EOPs)

We decided to utilize a hydraulic method for flat panel haptics and broadly investigated different micropump technologies. One technology that stood out was electroosmotic pumps (EOPs). This class of pump offers several key advantages: simple and miniaturizable construction (fundamentally operating on length scales of under 1 μ m), direct, reversible, pulse-free flow control via the application of a control voltage with no valves or moving parts, and the ability to be tailored to a wide range of pressure and flow rates. EOPs have found application in microfluidic systems ranging from liquid chromatography [ 41 ], to fuel cell water management [ 9 ], closed-loop microelectronic cooling [ 27 ], and even drug delivery [ 10 ]. Additionally, they are on par with more traditional micropumps, such as piezoelectric diaphram pumps, on a key metric of volume normalized flow rate (or so-called self-pumping frequency), see Fig. 2 . This plot shows how both the pump size (volume) and normalized flow rate compare across a wide range of sizes and pump types.

EOPs operate on the principle of electroosmotic flow (EOF), a type of electrokinetic flow. Electroosmosis is different from other types of electrokinetic phenomena, such as electrophoresis, electrohydrodynamic (EHD) flow, or electrowetting. We refer readers to a review article on micropumps which contains a full taxonomy of micropump technologies, including the main differences between electrokinetic pumps [ 24 ]. The unique aspect of EOF is that it takes advantage of an electrical double layer that forms within the working volume of the pump (illustration in Fig. 3 A). The double layer occurs at the nanometer-scale boundary between the fluid and pump membrane surface. A surface charge is created at this boundary as a result of the electrochemisty between the fluid and the solid surface. This surface charge is intrinsic to the surface/fluid interaction and does not require external power to generate or sustain. Its effect in the system is to create a net charge density in the fluid, which is actuated by applying a separate, external bulk electric field using a pair of electrodes on either side of the pump membrane. Charge in the fluid is dragged via Coloumbic forces, and this motion is transferred to the surrounding fluid via viscous interaction [ 1 ]. The closed-form classical equation that governs electroosmotic flow is the Helmholtz-Smoluchowski equation:

\begin{equation} u_{EO} = -\frac{\epsilon _0\epsilon _f\zeta }{\mu _f}E_{app} \end{equation}
(1)

where u EO is the electroosmotic flow velocity, ϵ 0 is the permittivity of free space, ϵ f is the permittivity of the fluid, μ f is the fluid viscosity, ζ is the double layer wall potential due to the surface charge density, and E app is the bulk applied electric field. Equation ( 1 ) is only valid when the double layer is sufficiently thin (a condition we do not believe we satisfy due to the low ion density property of our fluid, see [ 29 ]). However, the equation still describes the main relationships between the key parameters in the system. Importantly, since E app is just V app / t , where t is the thickness between electrodes, it shows how a high voltage over a small distance can be used to induce large electroosmotic flows.

As can be seen from a cross-section of our embedded EOPs (Fig. 3 C), we went to great lengths to maximize E app by minimizing the distance between the electrodes to just 0.25mm. Similar EO constructions use pumping membrane layers that are over 1mm thick [ 9 , 29 ]. We were also able to maintain a high voltage at these small distances by using a fluid with lower conductivity than traditional electroosmotic designs (which use high-conductivity fluids). We believe these two reasons, reduced electrode distance and low conductivity fluid, resulted in a substantial performance increase over previous electroosmotic pump devices. Equation (1) also implies that fluids with high dielectric constant and low viscosity will lead to high flow rates. The fluid we use has a very large dielectric constant (65) and a fluid viscosity on par with water.

When electroosmotic flow is used to move fluid against a back pressure, it becomes an electroosmotic pump. For our interface, we chose a planar porous membrane configuration of EOP. A full review of electroosmostic pumping configurations is outside the scope of this work. We refer the reader to several comprehensive review articles by Laser and Santiago [ 30 ], Iverson and Garimella [ 24 ], and Li et al. [ 34 ]. The basic requirements of an electroosmotic pump can be seen in Fig. 3 B. They include: control electrodes, a pumping membrane, and a pumping liquid that fills the pumping channel. The pumping membrane's dimensions and material determine much of the performance of the EOP.

For a given device's general construction, the pressure and flow of the device theoretically can be adjusted by changing the thickness and pump area. Increasing the thickness of the pumping membrane increases the output pressure (assuming a constant electric field), while increasing the area of the pump increases the flow rate in proportion. This offers flexibility to designers of EOPs to scale and tailor the pump layer to precisely fit their needs. In our case, we desired to always keep a 1:1 relation of pump area to the output layer area, as this maximizes the density of EOP arrays we can produce. This, however, was more of a conscious design decision we made instead of an inherent limitation of EOPs.

3 IMPLEMENTATION

This section describes the implementation details concerning our various flat panel haptic shape display prototypes. The materials and construction methods were prototyped over numerous iterations until sufficient performance was achieved. We note that while the devices presented in this work are already able to achieve a thin form factor, we believe there is still significant room for further miniaturization and integration in future iterations.

3.1 General Construction

Our prototypes are constructed out of multiple layers of processed sheet materials. A full stackup of an example display is shown in Fig 4 . There are 11 different layers that are laminated together, 7 functional layers, and 4 adhesive layers. This is including the printed circuit boards (PCBs) each as a single functional layer. In general, materials are processed separately and then brought together with adhesives during assembly. We used adhesive transfer tape sheets from 3M, 468MP and 478MP, to bond the pump and reservoir layers. Sheets were were bought online and laser processed. The only tools used to achieve the designed manufacturing tolerances are the photo-lithograph and drilling capabilities of the PCB vendor (down to 100 μ m trace/space on the PCB copper, and 200 μ m diameter hole drilling), and an in house commercial CO 2 laser (approximately 125 μ m kerf width laser processing). Our displays are designed and constructed in 3 major parts: the reservoir layer, the pumping layer, and the output layer (see Figs. 1 A and 4 ). We detail each layer below.

3.2 Embedded Electroosmotic Pumps (EEOPs)

The functional core of our displays is the compact pumping layer, which we call embedded electroosmotic pumps (EEOPs). This EEOP layer is fluidically connected to the reservoir and output layers through a series of plated through holes (vias) in the PCBs, see 3 C for a cross-section showing 2 PCBs with vias on either side of the pumping material. Similar to how an LCD pixel modulates light from a common backlight to the display output, an applied voltage coming from our EEOP controller can turn an individual electroosmotic pump on, inducing flow from the common reservoir and into the output layer. The pump can also operate in a reverse state, pulling liquid from the output layer and into the reservoir. See Fig. 3 C for a close-up cross section of this construction, showing the fluidic via connections. Note that the pumping layer only makes up a small part of the thickness of the pump, just 0.25mm (1/6th) of the total 1.5mm thickness.

The PCBs were designed in EAGLE CAD and purchased through a 3rd party manufacturer. They are made up of 4 layers. We use one outside layer as an EOP electrode layer, and the two inner layers for routing our signals to a 0.5mm pitch connector. The soldermask is removed from around the pumps in order to allow better adhesion to the output and reservoir layers. Vias were drilled between 0.2-0.25mm in diameter to form fluid connections across the PCBs. The size of the holes was chosen to be as small as possible and as close together as possible within the reasonable constraints of our manufacturer. This is inline with our inexpensive construction ethos. Typical via pitch is 1mm, center-to-center. Further work should be done to examine how hole size and pitch relate to flow rate, however we propose that the minimum hole size should be on the order of the spacing between electrodes. This would allow fringing electric fields from the edges of the hole to "fill in" the space of the missing conductor. If it is much larger, then the electric field will become non-uniform and reach a minimum in the center of the via. Standard 1oz copper plating was used, which was covered with an ENIG (electroless nickel immersion gold) coating. The PCBs are approximately 0.6mm in thickness.

Sandwiched in-between the two electrodes on the PCBs is an electroosmotic pump membrane material 3 B/C. We used borosilicate glass fiber filter (GFF) paper purchased from a scientific filter membrane distributor. We used Grade F filter paper, which has an equivalent filtration particle size of 0.7 μ m. Electroosmotic pumps can be made from many different materials, however, we found that glass fiber filter paper can be cleanly and easily cut with a laser, and offers surprising flexibility and robustness to chemical attack. Borosilicate glass is also known to achieve high chemical surface potentials, leading to strong electroosmotic flows. There is a separate piece of GFF for each individual pump in the display. To mechanically fill in the space between the pumps and prevent fluid connections between them, we use a spacer layer made of polyethylene terephthalate (PET). Holes are laser cut in the PET which allow for the insertion of the GFF during assembly, what we refer to as "pixel stuffing". We found PET to be a suitable material for this spacer, as it comes in precise thicknesses, can be easily laser processed, and has exceptional mechanical and chemical properties.

The assembly process for the pump layer is shown in Fig. 5 (A-F). A PCB is placed with electrodes facing up (A), and a precut adhesive sheet is aligned and brought into contact with the surface. Pressure is then applied to the tape uniformly using a handheld roller (B). The tape liner is removed and the transparent PET spacer layer is placed into contact with the exposed adhesive (C). The spacer is also pressed down using a hand roller. The holes of the PET spacer are slightly larger (0.1mm) than the holes in the adhesive. This forms a small lip of adhesive, which serves as a gasket for the GFF.

In step (D), individual pieces of GFF are picked up with tweezers and placed into the holes left by the PET spacer. This aligns them directly over the gold electrodes and light pressure is applied to the GFF so that it is held loosely into place by the underlying adhesive lip. Another sheet of adhesive is applied to a second PCB, similar to (A), and the second PCB is placed on top of the entire stack, with electrodes facing down, towards the GFF (E). Once everything is aligned, a bench vise was used to apply uniform clamping pressure to the pump. This ensure total contact between all adhesive layers, and a compression of the GFF between the PCB electrodes. A final inspection step (F) with a back light ensures that there are no voids in-between the layers. Interior trace routing can also be seen.

3.3 Output Layer

The primary function of the output layer is to contain the fluid coming from the pump layer, and serve as a soft, pressure coupling material between the pump and the user. The majority of our output layers are made of a soft, platinum-catalyzed silicone (Smooth-On Ecoflex 00-30). This material is simple and safe to use, and offers high durability. We used this material primarily for its exceptional elastic strain capability. A mold for the silicone was created by laser etching a piece of Delrin sheet. An example piece of silicone and the mold used to create it are shown in Fig. 5 G. An additional top plate was used as part of the mold to set the thickness of the top of our output cells. The thickness of the top part of the output cell is approximately 0.25mm.

The cast piece of silicone was cleaned and dried, then glued to the top of the pump layer using a rubber silicone epoxy glue (Smooth-On Sil-Poxy). Glue was first applied to the top layer of the pump using a syringe and 27 gauge dispensing needle, then the silicone layer was aligned and brought down into contact with the glue. Uniform pressure was applied to ensure good contact with the adhesive, and the device was set aside to cure. In one additional use case, discussed in Subsection 6.3 , multiple layers of PET were used instead of silicone for the output layer. In this case, a top layer of 50 μ m PET was adhered to a spacer layer of 500 μ m PET. This contains the fluid in the output layer, but limits the strain capability, due to much higher Young's modulus of PET.

3.4 Reservoir Layer

We opted for a relatively simple reservoir construction. All of the pumps are fluidically connected to a single large chamber on the bottom side of our displays. This chamber facilitates fluid moving back and fourth between pumps quickly and efficiently. The layer is made by a thick (1.5mm) Delrin spacer, which is then capped with a thin (0.1mm) PET backer (see Fig. 5 H). The Delrin/PET construction is then aligned and adhered to the pump layer. We used an open configuration for the majority of our experimental testing, and a closed configuration for our example interfaces. In closed configurations, fluid is injected into the device using a hypodermic needle and syringe through the PET backer. The hole in the back is then sealed with a small amount of silicone rubber epoxy. Air is evacuated from the device either by using a small vacuum chamber, or by simply pushing it out of the output chambers and removing it from the reservoir via syringe.

3.5 Pumping Fluid

The pumping fluid is a critical part of any electroosmotic pump. While much of the electroosmotic literature uses di-ionized water for the pumping fluid, we found this to be unacceptable for our application. This is due to the production of gas bubbles from electrolysis at high applied voltages. Instead, we use a non-aqueous solvent, propylene carbonate (PC). Non-aqueous solvents are not commonly used in electroosmosis, but have seen some interest due to their ability to produce high flow rates per applied power [ 29 ]. They have also been shown to be compatible with capillary electrophoresis [ 49 ]. This solvent has some additional properties which make it well suited for our use case. It has an exceptionally wide liquid phase temperature window (-48.8° to 242°C), high dielectric constant (65), relatively low viscosity, low DC conductivity when it is in a purified state, and is commonly available for purchase. However, one downside of using a solvent as the main pumping liquid, is that it places very high chemical compatibility constraints on the remaining materials of the pump. Propylene carbonate can break down many glues and polymers, and we had to select all of the materials (glass fiber, PCB, silicone, adhesive sheets, PET, and Delrin) to withstand this solvent.

3.6 Control Electronics

Each pump has two electrodes which are driven with moderately high voltages (250V) in order to operate the pump. We developed two sets of control electronics to drive the electrodes. For our physical and psychophysical evaluations, we developed a linear, closed-loop voltage amplifier based around a high common mode voltage differential amplifier (Analog Devices p/n AD8479) in a boot-strapped op-amp configuration. This allowed us to apply precise voltage waveforms. For our multi-pump prototypes, we developed a 64 channel high voltage driver based around 8 channel piezo driver chips (Microchip p/n HV513K7-G), which come in a 6 × 6 × 0.8mm SMD package. These were controlled via SPI using a Teensy 4.0 microcontroller. Our electronics were powered by either a 300V power supply module (EMCO p/n FS03-12) purchased from an electronics retailer or an adjustable 45-390V capacitor charging boost converter purchased online from Amazon. Photos of the of the 64 channel HV digital drive electronics are shown in Figure 6 . The custom linear, closed-loop voltage controller used in evaluation experiments is not shown, but is of similar size.

3.7 Safety

Our EEOP actuators require moderately high voltages and use a solvent for the pumping fluid. This raises the question of system safety due to shock and chemical exposure. We note here that we used high dielectric strength materials to insulate all high voltage conductors from our users, including at least 0.25mm of silicone on the output surface to insulate the active electrodes. Additionally, the solvent we use is considered non toxic, and is not considered a volatile organic compound (VOC), meaning there is little risk from inhalation. There is, however, a small risk of skin irritation from prolonged exposure.

3.8 Cost

We contend that our approach makes it easy to build large arrays of actuators for relatively low cost. The raw materials for the core pump are the glass fiber filter (0.02¢/mm 2 ), propylene carbonate (3.8¢/ml), sheet adhesive (0.001¢/mm 2 ), Delrin spacer (0.006¢/mm 2 ), silicone rubber (23¢/g) and rubber adhesive (51¢/g). The cost of PET is negligible. As a concrete example, we estimate that the 32 channel general shape display in Section 6.1 , Fig. 1 B, and Fig. 12 cost under $ 1.50 in raw materials. The largest costs come in the form of traditional electronic components, such as the PCBs, which cost between $ 100-200 for a quantity of 50 (almost entirely in NRE), the connectors ( $ 2.20/each) cables ( $ 2.33/each), high voltage drivers ( $ 2 per channel), and high voltage power supply ( $ 13). Many of these traditional electronics packaging costs can come down considerably in large quantities.

4 PHYSICAL EVALUATION

We characterized our pumps and actuators through a series of physical experiments. First, we examined how pressure and flow vary by size, verifying the scaling capabilities of embedded electroosmotic pumps. Next, we looked at the displacement capabilities of our actuators once the output layer is applied, and finally, we investigate how they react under mechanical load.

4.1 Flow and Pressure

4.1.1 Experimental Setup. For pressure and flow recordings, we replaced the top silicone output layer with a rigid layer made of Delrin. A photo of the test pump can be seen in Fig. 7 A. We tested 5 sizes of pump, ranging in diameter from 2mm to 10mm, allowing us to assess the scalability of pump constriction. The Delrin outlets were coupled to pressure and flow sensors, while the inlet was left open to atmosphere (seen Fig. 7 B). We measured pressure in a blocked configuration with an MEMs absolute pressure gauge (TE Connectivity p/n MS5837-30BA), seen in Fig. 7 C. This pressure sensor is temperature compensated and contains a calibrated vacuum reference. Flow rate was measured in a open configuration with a CMOS microflow gauge (Sensirion AG p/n SLF3S-0600F); Fig. 7 D. This sensor was calibrated in house by precisely measuring the total fluid mass passing through the sensor over a known time window.

Our blocked pressure and open flow measurements are similar to stall torque and no-load velocity measurements used in motor characterization. We used a 1Hz square wave as a test signal, with voltages ranging from ±25V to ±250V in 25V increments. Each test signal voltage was randomized within a given pump diameter, and run for 10 seconds, resulting in 10 positive half cycles and 10 negative half cycles, all run at the same voltage level but opposite polarity. Within each half cycle (positive or negative) we averaged the system response in time (to account for transients), resulting in 20 half cycles means. Finally, we then averaged half cycle means across the total 10 seconds. In total, each pump diameter had 10 voltage levels × 10 half cycles × 2 polarities, or 200 mean data points generated for pressure or flow measurements.

4.1.2 Results and Discussion. Results of our flow and pressure experiments are summarized and shown in Fig. 8 . Open flow was extremely linear with applied voltage, as evidenced by red linear fit lines (and as predicted from EOP theory). Flows ranged from ±0.5mL/min at 2mm diameter to ±7.5mL/min at 10mm diameter. When comparing this to other types of micropumps in a highly cited micropump review article [ 30 ], our pumps outperform all of them in one critical metric – the so-called "self-pumping frequency" – see Fig. 2 . The self-pumping frequency is not the frequency the pump is operated at, it is only a metric for size-normalized flow rate. It asks the question: how long does it take for the entire volume of the pump to pass through the pump itself? It is a non-dimensionalized number which is calculated by dividing the flow rate by the total volume of the pump. Our pumps showed self pumping frequencies on the order of 1-2 Hz (assuming 1.5mm thick pumps). This means they can pump their own volume's worth of fluid every 0.5-1.0 second. We believe this benchmark metric is extremely relevant for the future of flat panel haptic displays, as it speaks to the speed/size tradeoff of the pump. Therefore, we describe this metric to facilitate direct comparison with other micropumps in the future, and provide a baseline for our pump in comparison to other devices (Fig. 2 ).

Importantly, we show that not only can our pumps move incredible amounts of fluid for their size, we also empirically demonstrate that this flow scales linearly with area. As seen in Fig. 8 B, we plotted area versus voltage normalized flow rate ( μ L/min/V) from our linear fits in Fig. 8 A. This shows that, as the area of the pump increases, so does the flow rate of the pump, as predicted by theory.

Turning to blocked pressure recordings, Fig. 8 C shows average half cycle pressures across voltage and pump size. Pressure is not as linear as flow with voltage, but nonetheless, we see a continuing increase in pressure given an increase in applied voltage. Pressure is generally in the range of ±50kPa, which is about 2-3x the normal pressures coming from a user's fingertip during dynamic touch contact [ 40 ]. Notable here is the consistency of pressure magnitude with pump size. When normalized by the highest pump pressure and plotted versus area (Fig. 8 D), there is variation only of about ±10%, with our highest pressure pump also being the smallest (D=2mm). This also matches theory, which dictates that only pump thickness (t) should influence pressure output.

4.2 Displacement and Load Response

4.2.1 Experimental Setup. While our pumps showed good performance in open and blocked configurations, we wanted to test how they performed once the elastomeric output layer was included. We built another series of 5 pumps, and placed it under a custom displacement measurement device built around a low cost linear variable differential transformer (LVDT) sensor (TE Connectivity p/n 02560542-000). This sensor was calibrated using a mechanical micrometer. We coupled the magnetic core of the LVDT to a lightweight nylon rod, which pressed against the output layer. An example inflation cycle can be seen Fig. 9 A. We ran two displacement tests, each applying voltage pulses ranging from 50-250V (an example waveform is shown in Fig. 10 A). First, we applied only a light 5g pre-load, and looked at how displacement varied across pump size. Next, we took one pump size, D=10mm, and applied increasing weight loads, up to 140g (1.37N). For reference, this is similar in magnitude to the threshold of a "hard press" by a user on a surface [ 40 ].

4.2.2 Results and Discussion. Results of our displacement test are shown in Fig. 9 B. Displacement increases with applied voltage, but the relationship does not appear to be linear, except only at very low applied voltage. The deflation rate appears to be faster than the inflation rate, most likely due to the compliance of the membrane. At high voltage, the displacement seems to saturate, indicating a blocked flow condition. This means that peak pressure is probably applied at this point. In general, the volume normalized flow rates look to be somewhat less than what was measured in the open flow conditions (though still a similar order of magnitude), but this is to be expected due to the loading of the elastomer membrane. The peak displacement also scales with device size, and is approximately equal to the radius of the pump, except for D=2mm and D=4mm, where it is approximately 1.5x the radius.

The loading test results are shown in Fig. 10 B. In this case, only 250V pulses were applied. We can see clear effects of loading in the decrease of peak displacement with increasing load. At 80g, the displacement drops by about half, while at 140g, it has dropped by nearly 2/3rds. This indicates that our current displays would not achieve their desired shapes under significant loads from the user, but they are able to retain most of their shape change ability under moderate force.

5 PSYCHOPHYSICAL EVALUATION

After physically characterizing the performance of our actuators we wanted to evaluate the ability of participants to feel different tactile stimuli under real world conditions. We chose a task that we thought would be common in everyday use: a blocked configuration where participants already have the skin of their fingertip in contact with the pump output layer, under enough contact pressure to ensure their fingertip was fully engaged with the surface. This is generally known to be under 1N of force, or approximately 30kPa peak applied pressure [ 28 , 40 ]. We did not explicitly measure or prescribe contact force during experiments, as we wanted participants to find their own comfortable pressing force, which will vary from participant to participant. The main question we were investigating, therefore, was if our EEOPs could supply enough pressure to remain discernible, even under "light contact" conditions. We asked at what voltage amplitude participants could no longer detect a tactile change in the output layer.

5.1 Absolute Detection Threshold

5.1.1 Experimental Setup. We recruited 8 participants (mean age of 26, 3 identified as female, 5 as male), and ran a series of psychophysical trials to measure the absolute detection threshold for 6 different stimuli. Our stimuli included two sizes of pump (D=4mm and D=8mm) and 3 different frequencies (0.25Hz (quasi-static), 1Hz, and 10Hz). We chose 2 sizes to include a variation in size, but not overburden participants with too many tests, as participant fatigue can lead to unreliable results. Representative no load displacement measurements of these different stimuli can be seen in Fig. 11 B, as measured by our LVDT apparatus of Section 4.2 . It is notable that only in our 0.25Hz condition is the system allowed to reach near fully inflation, and the displacement is reduced at higher frequency. We have left further analysis of the system's frequency response to future work. Each stimuli was shown for a maximum of 4 seconds, and the peak voltage (Vpk) of the applied square wave was adjusted between trials, depending response of the participant.

We adopted a transformed 2-down-1-up double staircase experimental procedure, with alternating ascending and descending staircases. The ascending staircase started from 0V, while the descending staircase started from 250Vpk (or ±250V, similar to the waveform in Fig. 10 A). Each staircase was adjusted in steps of 25V. Participants were acquainted with the stimuli before each trial to ensure they could easily feel it. Stimuli order was randomized between trials. They were asked the question "Do you feel the stimuli?" and were allowed to answer yes or no.

Participants were instructed to place their finger on top of the actuator under test, and apply no more than light tactile pressure. See Fig. 11 A for the device used. Because our actuation method is silent, no headphones were used for the test. Users were allowed to rest their palms during the test, and were asked not to significantly change the configuration of their finger during a single trial. Each trial lasted approximately 4 minutes, with the overall experiment lasting between 30-45 minutes per participant. Participants were paid $ 10 USD. A single trial proceeded until 10 reversals were achieved on each staircase. The last 6 reversal intensities of each staircase were averaged to calculate the 70.7% detection response threshold on the psychometric curve.

5.1.2 Results and Discussion. All participants had quickly converging staircases. The results of the trials are shown in Fig. 11 C. The across participant means for 8mm were 71V, 92V, and 118V for 0.25Hz, 1Hz and 10Hz stimuli, respectively. Similarly, the means for 4mm were 105V, 121V, and 146V. Standard deviations are shown in Fig. 11 C. Detection thresholds were lower for the D=8mm case than the D=4mm case across 81% of trials between all participants, and, on average, thresholds monotonically increased with increasing frequency. These trends can possibly be explained by the displacement behavior of our EEOPs, which are capable of higher displacement at large sizes and lower frequencies.

Looking at our displacement data from Fig. 9 for D=4mm and D=8mm, this implies that participants were, on average, likely feeling displacements well under under 1mm at D=4mm, and well under 2mm at D=8mm. Looking at Fig. 11 B also implies that subjects were likely feeling 1Hz and 10Hz displacements possibly down to the 10 μ m level. These thresholds are not outside the realm of known tactile discrimination displacement thresholds using voice-coil indention actuators [ 8 ].

A direct comparison to these thresholds is difficult to make due to different loading conditions and stimuli sites, however, judging from the detection range and frequency, we propose that participants’ low amplitude perception was mostly mediated by fast adapting type I (FA1) afferents (Meissner Corpuscles). This aligns well with participants’ spontaneous descriptions of the stimuli tingling or pulsing, and reports of feeling subtle cues during stimuli onset and offset. No doubt, however, other tactile afferent channels contribute to tactile perception of shape at higher displacement amplitudes (i.e., SA1), or vibration at higher frequency (i.e., FAII). While further testing is needed to illuminate further perceptual dimensions of EEOP actuators (in particular JND measurements to discriminate judgments between voltage levels) these results provide evidence that participants are easily able to detect device stimuli under light contact pressure conditions at applied voltages within our available voltage limits.

6 EXAMPLE INTERFACES

Shape-changing displays have been well motivated in prior work, with applications including remote work [ 17 ], geospatial visualization [ 32 ], ambient information displays [ 35 ], and 3D manipulation [ 51 ], to name just a few areas. The advances we present in this paper can serve to make these existing demonstrations more practical, compact and economical. Nonetheless, we created three demo interfaces to help convey the expressivity and feasibility of our approach. Please also refer to our Video Figure. Note that we did not integrate touch sensing, though this could be achieved using several conventional technologies. Also note that, while the EEOPs used in previous sections were left open to the atmosphere, we sealed all hydraulic systems in this section.

6.1 General Interface

First and most general, we created a square shape-changing display with a grid of haptic pixels (6 × 6, with corner pixels missing). Each pixel utilized nearly the same construction as our D=8mm EEOPs from earlier sections. A thin layer of white silicone was applied to the top surface after construction to color the device. This device, measuring 64 × 72 × 5mm and weighing 32.5g, can be seen in Figures 1 B and 12 . An overview of its constituent layers is shown in Figure 12 K. A general-purpose display such as this can be used to transform from a flat surface (Fig. 1 B) into various output patterns (F), icons (A,G), emojis (J) and text (I), or it could act as a large widget, for instance, a dial (H) or power button (G). The surface can also host many small and simple widgets, such as plus and minus buttons and a slider (C-E). Patterns can also be played back-to-back to create animations over time. This example interface most closely matches our vision of generalized flat panel haptic devices, and we believe that large arrays of smaller EEOPs can also be constructed depending on the end use case of the surface.

6.2 Audio Control Interface

Our general shape display was application agnostic and used a grid of actuating square elements acting like pixels to form larger, more complex shapes. Application-specific interfaces, on the other hand, can "bake in" more complex geometries, allowing single actuators to express complex icons. As one illustrative example, we built an audio control keypad, shown Figure 13 and measuring 40 × 112 × 5mm while weighing 32g. In the off state, no power is applied, and the device surface is flat (A1). Starting in the top left and moving right in Figure 13 (A2), we have power, minus, pause, play, and plus buttons. Along the bottom, we have a row of small surface pixels in a line. All of these elements are flat in the off state (no voltage applied), and can be individually controlled to be popped out or pulled in (depending on the polarity of voltage applied).

Imagine the following interaction sequence: In the standby state, only a raised power button is visible (all other elements are flat and invisible). The user presses the power button (C1), which indents in response (C2). A play button rises (D1), along with the left five surface pixels (G5), representing volume level. Volume can be adjusted up and down by tapping on the plus and minus buttons (seen in A2). The user hits play (D1), which causes the play button to disappear and the pause button to morph out of the surface (E1). Music starts to play. After a few seconds, the surface pixels change from volume mode (G1-5) to progress bar mode (F1-4), indicating where the user is in the track. At any time, the user can hit plus or minus to adjust the volume, which temporally causes the interface to switch into volume mode.

We envision this type of shape changing audio interface being integrated into a car dashboard, living room wall, smartspeaker or other designs seeking a minimalist aesthetic with dynamic capability, where the device can remain flat (A1) when not in use. While we used a grey silicone as our top layer in this demo, other thin and elastic cover materials are possible, such as leather, vinyl, and textiles. Sufficiently elastic paints (e.g., latex) may also be applicable. Overall, these other cover materials offer designers a high degree of customization in their products.

6.3 Smartphone Display Interface

The flat, featureless nature of smartphone displays has meant keyboard text entry is slower and more error-prone than on mechanical counterparts (even when holding size constant). Ideally, we could maintain the graphical flexibility afforded by displays, but carry over at least some of the tactile benefits of a physical keyboard. Flat panel haptic displays have the potential to achieve this.

As a proof-of-concept implementation, we built a custom flat panel haptic display and used an off-the-shelf, 6" flexible AMOLED screen (2160x1080) as our top cover layer. This is almost identical to the type of screen used in recent folding smartphone models, which are rapidly dropping in cost and improving in physical robustness. A general view of the prototype can be seen in Fig. 14 .

To showcase different potential use cases, we statically defined geometry for a variety of interface elements, including application icons, keyboard, snooze button, camera buttons, volume control, notification, and settings panel widgets, some of which can be seen in Fig. 14 C-D and Fig. 15 . See our Video Figure for others. As in Section 6.2, these elements are pre-defined shapes, which can then be co-located with visuals when activated. We can inflate and deflate these surface elements from below the OLED display, as demonstrated in Fig. 15 . Since the OLED and surface layers are much less compliant than the silicone elastomers used previously, we also measured dynamic deformations of examples in Fig. 15 . These peak-to-peak deformations were 0.46mm on an app icon (A), 0.19mm on a single keyboard letter key (B), and 0.8mm on the snooze button (C). Additionally, each are capable of changing their state in a fraction of a second, and are easily perceived and located haptically. We note that, in our smartphone surface, the increased compliance of the screen means that the surface may be slightly deformed during the "OFF" state, and the presence of a low-stiffness area may be able to felt haptically by the user. This could be mitigated in future devices by removing the empty volume behind the button.

7 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

We summarize our work building and characterizing flat panel haptic displays with EEOPs by describing current limitations and areas for future work. The fact that we were able to build such capable devices by hand and with commonly available materials serves as a point of confidence that these devices can see vast improvement with the application of dedicated development.

7.1 Speed and Force

Our miniature EEOPs achieve impressive performance for their size, but there is room for faster flow rates and higher pressures. Depending on the amount of displacement required, we can achieve refresh-rates between 1-10Hz, and, while this is much faster than other shape-changing technologies, it may not be fast enough for real-time animations and dynamically refreshing shape displays. Similarly, our devices can withstand some pressure from the user, but users are able to block and inhibit the display, if they desire. We think a goal of over 100kPa would be a reasonable to try and achieve in future iterations.

7.2 Power and Voltage

We chose not to focus on power consumption in this initial work, but it must be addressed if EEOPs are to become more prevalent. Currently, our prototypes draw a constant current when they are actuated. The power scales with area, and pumps described here draw about 10-20 mW/mm 2 when active. This is reasonable for small actuated areas, but can become an issue if large areas are to be actuated simultaneously. Further work is needed to understand the mechanisms which dictate power draw, and further optimization is always advantageous.

Similarly, while we do not need kV range voltages as many other electrohydraulic technologies use, even 250V places a large burden on the driving electronics and safety requirements of devices. Future work should focus on reducing driving voltage even further, preferably below 50V, a goal we think is achievable. In particular, we envision geometry or surface modifications to the pumping membrane material itself, and further reducing the distance between electrodes, which will allow for similar applied electric fields for a given voltage.

Our devices do generate heat when under constant usage, but we have been able to operate them with users with minimal effects due to heating. It is assumed that heat generation is due to ohmic resistance of the fluid, and future work should look into reducing the intrinsic conductivity of the fluid as much as possible to reduce this effect. We note that early versions of our EEOPs which used de-ionized water did have significant heat generation issues, and much higher associated currents.

7.3 Reliability and Durability

Our devices have gone through many hundreds of cycles and still show good performance, however we have not explicitly studied any degradation or failure mechanisms of the device. We have been able to reduce one of the main issues with electroosmotic pumps, gas generation, by switching to a non-aqueous fluid, but since our devices rely on some electrochemisty to operate, the long term reliability of the devices should be studied in more detail (similar to development of OLED technology). Other fluids, pump membranes and electrode materials can be investigated, and we believe there is a large design space for pump material refinement.

We also encountered some leaking of our devices, usually due to poor sealing between the FR4 and silicone layers during construction. This can be solved with better adhesive mechanisms, or more repeatable construction techniques. The solvent we used has an exceedingly high boiling point (242°C) and we have experienced no noticeable issues with evaporation or liquid loss, though direct study is needed.

Durability of the touch surface is another concern, though it is not unique to just our device, but to all soft shape changing interfaces. The silicone elastomer used here is quite robust to normal rubbing and stretching interactions, but it can be prone to puncture or tearing damage because it is so compliant. The technique we showed in Section 6.3 could offer a way forward for surface durability if extremely large deformations are not needed. Materials can remain hard but become quite bendable at thin dimensions, such as bendable polymer and glass cover layers that are already being used on foldable smart devices. A combination of elastomer base with thin durable surface protection could be compelling.

7.4 Size and Construction

The stackups we created are thin (<5mm) but there is room for improvement. The reservoir layer is still quite bulky in comparison to the rest of the device, and the output encapsulation could be made thinner. Critically, much of the PCB volume is the glass fiber board, and shrinking this layer may offer a large reduction in size. The adhesive construction methods could also be improved, specifically the adhesion of the silicone elastomer to the PCB, as this is currently done by hand, which is always a challenge to make repeatable. Pixel stuffing of the glass fiber filters is also a non-ideal process, and is currently done with tweezers and a microscope. In the future, this could be done with something like a pick-and-place machine to improve repeatability.

Currently, our devices are tethered via 0.5mm pitch flat flex connectors, which are routed to the control electronics board (see Fig. 6 ). This controller is tethered to a PC via USB, and to a HV DC/DC converter which generated 250V from a 12V plug-in wall voltage converter. In the future, we believe the HV PSU can be integrated into a single design, which generates the required voltage from a 3-5V source (such as a battery) in a much more compact manner. The HV driver chips are already quite compact (6 × 6 × 0.8mm for 4 actuators) and further work should be done to either embed them into the EEOP structure itself, or reduce the number of chips needed by incorporating line driving techniques.

7.5 Large Arrays

We showcased arrays of at least 32 EEOPs, and the ability of EEOPs to reduce in size to just 2mm in diameter, but have not yet constructed arrays of hundreds or thousands of EEOPs. This type of construction would require some further improvements in system electrical routing and multiplexing, and getting rid of other manual processes. We believe FR4 PCB construction may be good enough to solve this challenge, but backplane technology (like that used in flat panel visual displays) may be needed.

7.6 Integration with Other Technologies

We described how our EEOPs could possible integrate with other flexible technologies, such as flexible OLEDs, by serving as a flexible backplane, but we have not yet tested other integrations. For instance, we envision integration with touch sensing either by a) a "bolt on" approach where our device is laminated to a more traditional touch sensing layer (e.g., a thin flexible projected capacitance sensor on top, or pressure sensing strain gauges below), or b) a fully integrated approach where sensing electrodes are incorporated into the layer stackup directly (e.g., multiplexing top electrodes to be part of a PCAP solution, similar to in-cell touchscreen designs). As EEOPs can be embedded using existing PCB technologies, we believe option b) to be an especially practical option. Future work is also focused on understanding possible sense capability of the EEOP itself if operated as a sensor instead of an actuator (similar to piezoelectrics).

8 CONCLUSION

In this work we described our vision for a new type of compact shape changing surface display that is capable of haptic feedback. We call this a flat panel haptic display. Our displays are based on miniaturized hydraulic pumps which operate by the physical phenomenon of electroosmosis. We define them as a new type of actuation and construction method called embedded electroosmotic pumps (EEOPs). These EEOPs can be customized to different shapes, sizes, and configurations, as they are simply made out of patterned sheet materials which are assembled as a stack up of layers. Much like LCD pixels, which modulate light from a common backlight, EEOPs draw from a common fluid reservoir and selectively modulate hydraulic pressure in and out of haptic cells.

We characterized how EEOP performance changes with size, measuring pressure, flow, displacement, and load characteristics. We validated that flow rates scale with area, while pressure remains independent of area. We also set a benchmark of a key system parameter, size normalized flow rate, at approximately 1 pump volume per second. Our devices were tested by participants in a psychophysical judgment task, where all participants were able to feel 0.25Hz, 1Hz, and 10Hz tactile effects at applied voltages of 70-150V. Finally, we built three separate instantiations of our flat panel haptic displays to showcase their broad potential for application. A generalized display laid out as an array of tactile pixels showed how a single device could morph into multiple shapes and forms; an audio control panel showed how complex geometric buttons could dynamically raise or lower from a surface, similar to membrane switch type control panels; and lastly, a deformable smart phone prototype showed how we could laminate a thin OLED visual display to the top of our flat panel haptic devices. The resulting deformation in the OLED itself led to tactile features which could easily be felt, such as the keys on a keyboard, app icons, and other system controls.
