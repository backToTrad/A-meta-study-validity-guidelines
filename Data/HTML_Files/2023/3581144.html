Using Virtual Reality and Co-Design to Study the Design of Large-scale Shape-Changing Interfaces

CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Large-scale shape-changing interfaces (SCIs) such as shape-changing walls offer opportunities for enhancing user experiences within buildings, e.g., for navigation. However, due to the embryonic nature of SCI technologies, designing and explaining the shape features that are beneficial to users is challenging. Previous work used virtual platforms (2D video or Projected Augmented Reality) to design SCI. This paper explores how Virtual Reality (VR) can provide an immersive experience that can help in designing large-scale SCI. We follow a co-design approach in which we use VR to obtain users’ impressions of shape-changing walls. Then, we conduct co-design sessions to understand how shape-changing walls can be designed to become ambient and blend with the environment. We report our results to guide the design of shape-changing walls as well as discuss how our approach can provide valuable insights into how a VR experience, prior to design, and can help in the design process.

1 INTRODUCTION

Large-scale shape-changing interfaces (SCIs) can be promising in improving our quality of life within buildings. Researchers have examined various types of such interfaces that have adaptive affordances including, furniture [ 21 , 22 , 26 , 53 ], sculptures [ 13 , 45 , 47 ], floors [ 23 , 52 , 56 ] and walls [ 20 , 48 , 54 ]. In particular, shape-changing walls can change to provide additional furniture, offer navigational support within buildings or to display information or art works. Imagine, for example, entering a building with shape-changing walls, the walls connect to your phone to know where you are heading to and start guiding you through the space by making corridors wider or by creating a wave-like pattern that moves in a specific direction.

However, designing and building large SCI is challenging due to their large scale and because technologies needed to build such SCI are not fully ready yet (e.g. [ 22 , 23 , 26 ]). To examine their design, prior work has paved the way and proposed the use of platforms that do not require building prototypes such as 2D videos [ 41 ] and Projected Augmented Reality [ 11 ]. We build on and extend prior work by proposing to use Virtual Reality (VR), which could offer a more immersive experience that would allow examining how shape-changing walls affect people's perceptions. In addition, previous work on large-scale SCIs (e.g. [ 20 , 45 , 47 , 48 , 52 ]) did not examine in-detail shape-change parameters that make designs intrusive or non-intrusive and hence, our understanding of how these technologies can be designed to become non-intrusive and ambient remains limited. Therefore, this work examines how people perceive various types of shape-changing walls and explores how such walls can be designed to become ambient and less intrusive.

To explore how shape-changing walls affect people's perceptions and how they can be designed to become subtle and blend with our environment, we carried out an online study following a co-design approach. We used VR to explain the complex concept of shape-changing walls and to understand how people perceive them. We particularly developed Living Building, which is a mobile VR 1 app that uses cardboard viewers to explore different shape-changing wall designs After the VR experience, we engaged potential users in co-design activities to explore how participants understand and use parameters of shape-change encountered in VR, in designing non-intrusive and ambient shape-changing walls. Finally, we conducted interviews to examine how our approach to designing shape-changing walls can facilitate the design process.

Our findings from the VR experience revealed that shape-changing walls impact people's perceptions in many ways. For instance, they caused a wide range of positive and negative emotional reactions, including feelings of fear, happiness and serenity. In addition, participants uncovered many affordances that shape-changing walls convey such as affordances of action possibilities [ 19 ] and affordances that invite behaviours [ 63 ]. Also, by experiencing shape-changing walls in VR, participants experienced various sensory illusions such as hearing sounds and feeling changes in body temperature. The co-design activities provided useful insights into the design of shape-changing walls and showed that certain types of change such as Amplitude and speed setting of fast can make designs more intrusive than others. Finally, reflections on our methodology showed it helped participants in understanding complex concepts that are necessary during the design activities.

The contributions of our work are: (1) novel insights into how people perceive shape-changing walls; (2) new understanding into how shape-changing walls can be designed to become non-intrusive and blend with the environment; (3) the Living Building mobile VR app, which allows users to explore various designs of shape-changing walls and examine their parameters; (4) a design methodology that can help in designing large-scale SCIs.

2 BACKGROUND

2.1 Large-Scale SCIs

SCIs use physical changes in shape or material as input and/or output [ 1 ]. Although the majority of the existing SCIs are considered small-scale interfaces such as devices (e.g. [ 30 , 46 ]) and objects (e.g. [ 57 ]), there exists few SCIs that are large-scale such as furniture  [ 21 , 22 , 26 , 53 ], sculptures [ 13 , 47 ] and architectural elements (e.g. wall, floor) [ 20 , 23 , 48 , 52 , 54 , 56 ]. For instance, Aegis Hyposurface [ 20 ] is a 10m x 3m shape-changing wall that deforms in real-time in reaction to environmental stimuli such as movement, sound or light. Another shape-changing wall was designed and used in the breathing room [ 48 ], which synchronises the individual's breathing pattern with the shape-changing wall. In addition, Takashima et al. [ 54 ] explored the design of a shape-changing wall display that change its shape to support the activities of its surrounding users. Their study showed that participants designed different display shapes (e.g. flat, L-shaped, concave, convex) depending on their distance from the display and the content shown.

A number of the aforementioned large-scale SCI were developed as an ambient display (i.e. having qualities that make them subtle and non-intrusive) [ 13 , 27 , 45 , 47 ]. For instance, Ishii et al. [ 27 ] presented a large-scale Pinwheels sculpture that can change to visualise information flow in a subtle, unobtrusive manner. In addition, Murmur [ 47 ] is an ambient shape-changing sculpture that is made of one hundred computer CPU fans that gets triggered by environmental sound. Also, Rogers et al. [ 45 ] designed a shape-changing ambient sculpture (The Clouds) that aims at subtly changing how people move through a building. It is composed of two sets of coloured spheres that could change and move up and down based on number of people who used either the elevator or stairs. These shape-changing sculptures were reported to be ambient and subtle. Ambient designs mainly used silent, peripheral or slow changes. However, we still lack a systematic examination of the shape-change parameters that can make designs ambient and less intrusive. In this work, we thus examine the shape-change parameters that make shape-changing wall designs more subtle and ambient.

2.2 Design Platforms for SCIs

While the aforementioned large-scale SCIs can offer opportunities for enhancing experiences within buildings, designing them can be challenging. This is because SCIs face many technological challenges [ 1 ] that make their development a difficult and a lengthy process. To address these challenges, researchers have used various design platforms including, 2D video [ 41 ] and Mixed Reality [ 11 ]. For example, Pedersen et al. [ 41 ] produced 51 videos of a shape-changing handheld device by varying shape-change parameters and examined how users perceive them. They showed that using video clips can help capturing some of how users would perceive physical devices. Additionally, Cano et al. [ 11 ] used Projected Augmented Reality (AR) to examine the perceived affordances of seven visual effects of a shape-changing object. They demonstrated that Projected AR cab be used to study shape-changing objects. Although Projected AR is feasible for designing small-scale SCIs, it can be cumbersome to implement for large-scale SCIs as it would require additional projectors and accurate head tracking systems. Virtual Reality, on the other hand, may be more accessible and self-contained for designing large-scale SCIs, while also providing immersive and engaging experiences. Hence, VR could be a great studying platform to investigate.

2.3 Designing in and for Virtual Reality

Virtual Reality (VR) allows users to immersively experience a virtual world [ 7 ]. VR has shown a promising potential in various application domains such as education, healthcare and design. In the design domain, researchers have used VR as a design tool for designing interactive spaces [ 28 ], products such as cars [ 65 ] and cakes [ 37 ], architecture and interior design [ 43 ] and as a design tool for speculative design and design fiction [ 36 ]. Furthermore, researchers used participatory design and co-design approaches to design VR scenarios in applications such exposure therapy [ 17 ], integration of VR into informal learning contexts [ 60 ] and examining social VR for older adults [ 4 ]. While several research works have been conducted to design in and for Virtual Reality, it remains unclear whether and how VR can be used to support the design of large-scale SCIs.

3 METHOD

The study is designed to answer the following research questions:

  • RQ1: How do people perceive different types of shape-changing walls?
  • RQ2: How can we design shape-changing walls with ambient qualities (i.e. subtle and non-intrusive)?
  • RQ3: Can VR be used as a platform to facilitate the design process of large-scale SCIs?

To answer these questions, we conducted an online study, consisting of three successive parts: VR experience, co-design activities and an interview, with 18 participants. Given the high complexity of the concept of shape-changing interfaces and their numerous parameters and possible values, explaining such concepts can be exceedingly challenging. Thus, we needed a method to explain and facilitate the understanding of SCI concepts to potential users before engaging them in design activities. Compared to other platforms (e.g. 2D video, Projected Augmented Reality), VR offers a more immersive experience that can help users in grasping the idea of SCI. Co-design allows generating innovative and creative ideas for complex problems [ 50 ]. We carried out co-design after the VR experience to examine how participants understand and employ the parameters that they encountered in VR in the design of ambient and non-intrusive shape-changing walls and to understand the extent to which they reflect on their emotional experience and reactions in their designs. Finally, interviews with users allows for collecting reflection on the process as a whole and how the VR experience helped them during the design activities. Ethical approval was granted by the university's ethics committee.

3.1 Participants

18 participants (9 female, from 23 to 57 years old, mean age = 32.66, SD = 8.7) were recruited using a digital recruitment poster posted on participants’ recruitment websites and on social media. We conducted a total of 18 sessions because we reached saturation, where no additional new data are being found [ 33 ]. Prior design studies reported recruiting about 7 participants (e.g. [ 31 , 39 ]), while others had an average of 15 participants (e.g. [ 25 , 35 , 38 ]). Participants were a mix of students, academics and technical staff. Three participants never used VR. Twelve participants who had already used VR considered their experience level with VR as beginner and three others as expert. Eleven participants had experience in design while seven had none. Among those with design experience, two had this experience for less than a year, one for 1-2 years, three for 3-5 years and five for more than 5 years. In this work, we focus on the experience of end-users, who are non-experts in architecture or interior design, because we want to examine how end-users understand and employ the parameters that they encounter in shape-changing walls as part of their usual interaction and navigation. We also want to understand the extent to which end-users reflect on their emotional experience and reactions in their designs.

Fourteen participants used Android and four iOS. Participants received a cardboard Virtual Reality Viewer (Figure 1 ) as a compensation for their participation and to use during the study.

3.2 Parameters of Shape-Change Studied

In order to study parameters of shape-changing walls, we first examined existing literature on shape-change frameworks. Among the available shape-change frameworks [ 14 , 29 , 44 , 46 , 51 ], we used Roudaut et al. [ 46 ] as it is the most suitable framework for examining flat surfaces such as shape-changing walls. Out of the ten features from this framework, we used: Curvature , Amplitude , Zero-Crossing , Porosity , Granularity and Speed . Area was substituted with Volume which is a part of the Size feature in a refined framework [ 29 ] of Roudaut et al.’s framework [ 46 ]. This is because of the 3D changes that it offers compared to 2D changes, which would be difficult to view in a 3D room setting of four intersecting walls. Three features were eliminated: Stretchability , Strength and Closure . Stretchability and Strength are tactile features, which do not apply to shape-changing walls. Closure is not applicable to walls and similar effects can be achieved by using Curvature when the walls curve to a certain degree. Here, we define these types and describe the other parameters used in the study.

Type of Change corresponds to the type of change that can be used in the shape-changing walls, which includes:

  • Curvature corresponds to the ability of an interface to alter the curviness of the surface. Curvature can be concave or convex.
  • Amplitude describes the range of displacement of control points.
  • Volume describes the volume of the object.
  • Zero-Crossing explains the ability of a shape to have wave-like pattern.
  • Porosity describes the ratio of the area of discontinuous parts to the total area of the shape.
  • Granularity measures the density of physical actuation points.

Speed describes the time needed to move a shape from the initial rest state to its maximum state. Values include: slow, moderate, fast and variable.

In addition, we added four more parameters: Scale , Interaction , Notification Level and Abstraction Level . We identified the Scale parameter because we needed a parameter to represent how large the change affecting the room is. Also, we needed a parameter to allow participants to describe how would they interact with the shape-changing walls during the design activities, hence, we used the Interaction parameter from Rasmussen et al. [ 44 ]. In order to create designs for shape-changing walls that can become ambient and minimally intrusive, there is a need for parameters that describe such ambient qualities. Therefore, we used Notification Level and Abstraction Level parameters from Matthews et al. [ 34 ]

Scale describes the number of walls that change its shape and values include: one wall, two walls and three walls (corresponds to all walls in the VR experience).

Interaction describes how a shape-changing interface uses shape change as input and output. Values include:

  • No Interaction : shape change is used only as output and ignores any input from the user.
  • Indirect Interaction : shape change is used as output but the change is based on an implicit input (e.g. data collected without awareness).
  • Direct Interaction : shape change is used as input and output.

Notification Level describes the degree at which the system notifies the user about the change in system state while performing a primary task [ 34 , 58 ]. Levels include: Ignore, Change Blind, Make Aware, Interrupt and Demand Action. Ignore corresponds to no notification. Change Blind corresponds to inattention,i.e. changes in the system state occurs but the user does not notice it. Make Aware uses a form of divided attention with noticeable changes in system state. Interrupt also uses divided attention, with attempts to attract user's focused attention. Demand Action requires the user to perform an action to stop the notification.

Abstraction Level is the level of abstraction in the information presented by the interface, which is the process of removing data and including fewer details compared to the original design [ 34 , 58 ]. Levels can be Low , Medium or High . Low represents data directly, without any abstraction. Medium applies some abstraction to the represented data, while also keeping it comprehensible. High encodes data strongly, with no relation to the real-world to enable comprehension. For example, to present information about the current wind speed, we would write the actual wind speed in numbers using shape-changing walls in the Low abstraction level. With Medium level, we would use shape-changing walls with the waves type of change and set its speed based on the current wind speed. Finally, we would set a number of cubes that come in and out from a wall based on the wind speed in the High abstraction level, which would make it difficult for users to understand the relationship between the data presented and the changes of the wall.

Note that we used simplified names for some of the change types to make it easier for participants. Zero-Crossing was replaced with Waves , Amplitude with Cubes and Cones depending on the shape used, Porosity with Holes and Granularity with Number of Instances .

3.3 Apparatus

We implemented Living Building, a Virtual Reality mobile app to explore different shape-changing wall designs in seven rooms. We chose seven rooms as it allows exploring many types of change in an average of 20 minutes, hence limiting causing fatigue and dizziness in participants. Figure 2 shows the linear floor plan of the building. The linear floor plan offers a one-way route, which makes the navigation simple, and walking distances shorter compared to non-linear floor plans. The interior used neutral wall colours to eliminate any reactions colours may cause and to ensure a better clarity of the shape changes.

The start room shows a welcome message and each room has a door leading to the next (See Figure 3 ). The building was designed in a way so that it gradually increases the complexity of the type of change within each room. Table 1 describes the type of change used in rooms 1 to 7. Figure 4 shows the change of states over time in the seven rooms. Each room has a control panel that enables the user to manipulate speed (slow, medium, fast) and scale (single wall, two walls, all walls), as shown in Figure 3 . Room number appears at the top of the control panel. The app terminates when the user reaches the end room (Figure 3 (i)). In addition to these rooms, the app allows users to test the movement in VR before entering. Head-tilt locomotion technique [ 59 ] was used to move by tilting the head 30° downwards to move and lifting upwards to stop. The app was uploaded to both the Play and App Stores.

3.4 Procedure

Participants who expressed an interest in the study were sent an online form containing the information sheet and consent form. They also filled in their address for the VR viewers to be sent directly from Amazon. Participants booked a slot and filled in the demographics questionnaire after receiving the VR viewer. They were asked to try the sketching tool in Google Documents, to download the Living Building app and to test the movement in VR. The study was conducted online over Zoom due to the restrictions of the COVID-19 pandemic. Each session lasted between 70 to 115 minutes. The study started by first giving participants an overview explaining the purpose of the study, its three main parts (see below) and the data to be collected.

3.4.1 VR experience. Participants were given an overview of the VR experience and instructions to navigate the rooms. They were then asked to run the app, wear the viewer and click on the Living Building button. Participants were asked to describe what they see to ensure they clicked on the right button. The researcher asked them to proceed to the first room and explained the usage of the control panel. Participants were encouraged to think aloud and describe what they see, their feelings, thoughts, associations or anything that comes to their minds. Participants reported the number on the panel each time they entered a room. In the first three rooms, the researcher helped participants in selecting buttons and in encouraging them to talk (e.g. the researcher would say: select the slow speed with one wall and tell me how would you describe the change, how does it make you feel and think, and whether you associate the change with anything?). In the rooms that follow, the researcher encouraged participants to think and speak aloud of which buttons they select and describe the wall and their feelings or thoughts. After completing the VR experience, participants were asked to remove the VR headset and they could take a break if needed.

3.4.2 Co-design activities. Co-design was conducted immediately after the VR experience and during the same study session. We carried out individual co-design sessions because one-to-one sessions allow capturing comprehensive data of the participants’ unique experiences. Following the VR experience, a link to the design activities sheet (Google Documents) was shared. The participant shared their screen with the researcher. The researcher explained the parameters seen during the VR experience (i.e. type of change, scale and speed) as well as additional parameters not yet encountered (i.e. type of interaction, abstraction level and notification level) and their icons. As a warm-up exercise, participants completed a drawing activity. Following that, the main design activities started. The first task (See Figure 5 ) was to design a room, of any purpose, with shape-changing walls (Design 1). Participants were asked to explain the purpose, and sketch how the shape-changes could serve this purpose. The researcher asked questions and encouraged participants to discuss their choices. Participants were then introduced to the term intrusiveness, which we defined as the degree to which the design demands attention from users. They were asked to assess the intrusiveness of their design (non-intrusive/moderate/intrusive) and to explain why. Based on their assessment, participants had to redesign their initial idea (Design 2) using the opposite intrusiveness level or to design two versions (intrusive/non-intrusive) of it if their original intrusiveness level was moderate.

3.4.3 Interview. At the end of the study, participants were asked to reflect on the VR experience and how it helped them in visualising and understanding shape-changing walls concepts. Participants were asked to reflect on their designs and the extent to which the VR helped them during the design activities. Participants also reflected on the whole design experience.

3.5 Data Collection and Analysis

The study was audio-recorded and the participants’ screen was recorded. We also gathered the designs of the participants. All audio-recordings were transcribed using an online automated transcription tool. To clean the data, transcription files were cross-checked with the audio to detect and correct errors made by the automatic transcription. We used Nvivo to code the qualitative data. All codes were moved to Miro [ 42 ] to carry out the qualitative data analysis. The data collected were analysed qualitatively using thematic analysis and content analysis:

  • Thematic analysis: An inductive thematic analysis [ 10 ] approach was followed to analyse the initial impressions and reactions of the shape-changing walls in VR, the design activities and the interviews. The initial codebook of the data was first produced. Then, peer validation [ 2 ] was conducted throughout the coding process to review and clarify codes. Each reviewed code was represented as a sticky note in Miro. These codes were iteratively grouped to produce categories and top level themes over the course of several team discussions.
  • Content analysis : The parameters that participants selected for their initial designs (Design 1) along with their intrusiveness assessment were analysed using an inductive content analysis [ 15 ]. These were compared with parameters selected for the opposite design (Design 2) to extract common patterns. The common patterns and categories for each parameter were first extracted. Then, two additional researchers were given these categories and asked to classify all data of each parameter independently. The average inter-rater reliability (for all parameters) using Cohen's Kappa showed an ’Excellent’ agreement between the two raters (k=0.942).

4 VR EXPERIENCE RESULTS

Here, we present the findings of the participants’ initial reactions and impressions of the VR experience. Three main themes were identified from the analysis. These themes describe participants’ reactions when encountering different types of shape-changing walls in VR. The participant number along with the type of change, speed setting (s=slow, m=medium, f=fast) and the number of walls are added to participants’ quotes.

4.1 Theme 1: Provoking Positive and Negative Emotional Reactions

Participants showed a wide range of emotional reactions. These reactions ranged from pleasant to unpleasant emotions with high and low emotional arousal. Reported emotional reactions included both induced emotions (i.e. felt emotions by the user as a result of the shape-changing walls) and perceived emotions (i.e. conveyed emotion by the shape-changing wall) of the shape-changing walls  [ 18 , 49 ]. Different emotional reactions were found depending on the type of change, speed setting and number of activated walls. Table 2 shows a summary of the findings of this theme.

4.1.1 Inducing and Perceiving Pleasant Emotions.
Encountering shape-changing walls in VR evoked pleasant emotions such as excitement, happiness and serenity. Participants reported induced and perceived energy, perceived sense of friendliness and induced and perceived sense of calm from the shape-changing walls.

Participants reported experiencing a sense of energy and excitement while exploring the living building. In particular, P2 expressed how the volume type of change made her feel more energetic when it was on fast speed and with one activated wall: "It makes me feel like a bit more energetic almost" (P2, volume, f, 1). Induced sense of energy and excitement was found in volume and waves change types, when the speed was fast and with one wall activated.

Participants also perceived feelings of energy and excitement from the shape-changing walls. For instance, P10 stated the cubes that extend more than the others were more energetic compared to the other cubes and at the same time non-threatening: "I think the ones that are coming out further (referring to the cubes) are more energetic while they are moving further so that they feel more energetic but not particularly threatening" (P10, cubes, f, 3). Perceived sense of energy and excitement was noticed in the volume and cubes types of change with medium and fast speed settings and when one and three walls were activated.

Participants stated perceiving a sense of friendliness from the shape-changing walls. For example, P10 describes the cones type of change when it is on slow speed as "separate entities that are moving in unison, rather than one thing and that seemed more friendly, I guess." (P10, cones,s,3). Perceived friendliness was found in cones and concave curvature types of change with slow and fast speed settings and when three walls were activated.

Participants expressed the walls with the waves and volume types of change made them feel calm and relaxed: "it feels very calming" (P20, waves, s, 1), "It's oddly relaxing" (P18, volume, s, 1). In addition, they reported that it felt more relaxing with three walls activated in the waves type of change: "Actually having the three walls of immersion on this one actually felt more relaxing" (P18, waves,s, 3). Induced sense of serenity was found in all types of change and in all speed and wall settings.

Participants also reported perceiving calmness and relaxation from the volume shape-changing walls: "it feels quite serene" (P20, volume,s,1). P19 described that the concave curvature wall is relaxed and calm after she realised it was moving backwards: ".... I can feel the movement, it is relaxed, it is calm" (P19, concave curvature, s, 1). Perceived sense of serenity was noticed in all types of change except holes, with all speed settings and with one and three activated walls.

4.1.2 Inducing and Perceiving Unpleasant Emotions.
Experiencing shape-changing walls has resulted in a number of unpleasant emotions. Participants described the perceived sense of anger and aggression from the walls and the induced and perceived sense of fear.

They reported perceiving anger from the shape-changing walls. P20 described that the cube " on the right is coming out quite, It feels quite close to me, so it feels like when there's three of them, It feels even more kind of angry" (P20, cubes, m, 3), while P19 felt that " the whole room has gone mad" in the volume type of change (P19, volume, f, 3). Perceived anger was noticed in the volume, cones and cubes change types, with medium and fast speeds and on one and three activated walls.

In addition, participants expressed perceiving feelings of aggression from the wall. P20 mentioned that she perceived the wall with the cones type of change as aggressive even when it was on a slow speed: "It still feels aggressive but not quite as menacing as when it's fast." (P20, cones, s, 1). Perceived sense of aggression was found in the cones and cubes types with all speed settings and with only one wall activated.

Participants indicated that certain shape-changing walls induced annoyance. For example, P11 mentioned that the waves type of change with the fast speed and with three activated walls is: "A bit too much going on visually." (P11, waves,f, 3). Participants stated being annoyed in the volume, cones, cubes and waves types of change, with slow (n=1) and fast (most participants) speeds and when one and three walls were activated.

Fear was described in many ways, including reporting the felt emotions and the perceived emotions of the wall, describing scary actions and remembering scary memories. For example, P18 mentioned that the cubes type of change "Makes me feel a little uneasy" (P18, cubes, s, 3) while P6 expressed surprise and fear upon entering this room: "Whoa, this one is actually a bit anxious, It's like big file cabinets coming towards me." (P6, cubes, m, 1).

In the volume type of change, participants felt overwhelmed and anxious by the threatening and scary wall: "That's definitely feeling threatening, a bit overwhelmed, I guess. Yeah, and potentially a little bit confused as well." (P10, volume, f, 1). P1 mentioned that activating three walls on fast speed in the cones type of change is: "more threatening. Again, I feel like I'm being trapped in some sort of obscure torture chamber" (P1, cones, f, 3). Participants also expressed fear from the wall with the volume and convex curvature types of change, like a monster threatening them: "Slow speed on full room is a bit threatening um it's a bit like a big breeding thing is waiting to eat me or something like, yeah just sitting there really anticipating swallowing me." (P1, volume, s, 3), "It is menacing I think because of the way the panel is placed you can only really see the flex happen at the top of the ceiling quite gradual. So it feels like some large monstrous change gradually happening." (P10, convex curvature, s, 1). Fear was found in all types of change, all speed settings, and when one and three walls were activated.

In addition, participants reported a number of actions the shape-changing walls were doing which provoked fear. For instance, participants thought the walls with the volume and cubes types of change were trying to hit, crush and push them: "I feel like they're just crushing on to me and a bit pushing me to the corner, even though I know I'm standing probably in the middle of the room" (P6, volume, m, 2), "It's quite fast. I felt a bit like everything's like trying to hit me." (P2, cubes, f, 3), "...especially because some of these are down at floor level, these could probably hurt you like if you were over there and one of them ran into you, it would knock you over." (P4, cubes, f, 3). Such scary feelings were reported in all types of change except concave curvature and in all speed and wall settings.

4.2 Theme 2: Uncovering Affordances of Shape-changing Walls

This theme describes the affordances of shape-changing walls reported by participants, which include affordances of action possibilities [ 19 ], affordances that invite behaviours (inviting affordances [ 63 ]) or repel them from doing a behaviour and unclear affordances in which participants tried to understand the affordances the shape-changing wall was trying to convey. Table 3 summarises the findings of this theme.

4.2.1 Perceived affordances of action possibilities.
Participants stated that the cubes type of change affords certain actions such as sitting and creating stairs and even to be used for games: "I feel if they stop moving, if they come forward and stop moving its kind of providing affordance for people to sit on it." (P19, cubes, s, 1), " It seems like they are forming a kind of stairs" (P12, cubes, m, 1).

Participants also expressed they perceive directional information from the cones type of change. For instance, P2 mentioned the fast speed: "Kind of looks like they're pointing me in the direction behind me and I want to turn around and see what they're pointing at" (P2, cones, f, 1).

Participants reported that the cubes type of change carries a hidden message: "I think the room is trying to show some kind of pattern and this pattern carries a meaning or messages of people whoever stepped in this room." (P19, cubes, f, 3)

Participants also indicated a range of actions that the shape-changing walls triggered such as jumping, climbing and dancing: "This just kind of feels like I'm in a fun house. Looks like the spongy walls that I could like jump against and bounce off." (P2, concave curvature,f, 3), "So it reminds me of climbing walls, something that makes me like want to go up to them and like try to climb up on them or something." (P3, cubes, s, 3).

4.2.2 Inviting and repelling Affordances.
Rather than stating affordances of action possibilities, participants reported the shape-changing walls were providing an inviting affordance: "um, so I think it's kind of inviting me to enjoy something or to do something here." (P19, concave curvature, f, 1). P19 explained that reducing the speed made the volume room inviting: "when the speed is reduced, so the whole purpose of the building is changed, from refusing people to step in to something that invite people to come in and do something with a building." (P19, volume, s, 3). In addition, P3 expressed that the cubes type of change invites playful interactions: "seems to kind of like invite a bit more playing with them or something..." (P3, cubes, s, 3).

In addition, participants mentioned they were intrigued and curious to approach the cones: "The ones on the lower… I might be curious to go and touch it. The colour and the shape doesn't look scary. So if it's there, I probably would go touch it" (P6, cones, f, 3). While other participants were curious to touch and feel the convex curvature wall: "It's quite interesting, I wanna go and like feel it, what does it feel like." (P2, convex curvature, f, 3).

On the other hand, participants also mentioned that certain shape-changing types (e.g. cones) with certain speed and wall settings were uninviting and repelling: "It feels I'm unwelcomed like go away" (P6, cones, f, 3). In other cases, participants reported the cubes wall was trying to convey messages that give a sense of threat and danger: "And it looks like some alert about danger or about malfunction." (P19, cubes, f, 1).

4.2.3 Unclear Affordances of the Shape-changing Walls.
Participants mentioned they were trying to understand what the holes shape-changing wall was conveying through the physical changes: "It's not clear what it's trying to say, or what it's for or why you would want it" (P4, holes,f, 3). P1 expressed that he felt the wall with the cones type of change looks like it has a purpose but it was not clear what the purpose is: "I feel they feel purposive but i don't know what that purpose is." (P1, cones, f, 1).

4.3 Theme 3: Experiencing Sensory Illusions

This theme describes the sensory illusions that participants experienced in VR. These include pleasant illusions such as changes in body temperature and hearing sounds as well as unpleasant illusions such as lack of balance and sickness. Table 4 summarises the findings of this theme.

4.3.1 Pleasant Illusions.
Exploring shape-changing walls in VR have resulted in a number of pleasant sensory illusions, including cross-modal illusions that relate directly to the body and illusions that relate to the body's relation with the surrounding space. Participants reported a number of cross-modal illusions that relate to the body such as hearing sounds, changes in body temperature and changes in body weight. For instance, participants mentioned they started to hear sounds and noises as they were looking at the shape-changing walls: "I'm getting a bit of... what's it called when you have one sense, feeding into another one, like I feel like I'm hearing it as it's moving quickly. like I always feel like I can hear it.... I noticed it a bit on the three walls and fast, but much more with the single wall." (P18, cubes, f, 1 and 3), "I Almost hear noises because you know that these types of movement cannot be without noise." (P5, waves, f, 3). While others stated that they started to think about sound during this visual experience: "it makes me think now, it's funny, but I'm thinking about sound, because it's moving like in a rhythm. Yeah, as if it was like, I don't know, like with music or something like that." (P7, volume, f, 3). Cross-modal illusion of sound was noticed in the volume, cones, cubes, waves and holes type of change with all speed settings and with one and three activated walls.

Participants expressed they felt cold in the Holes room: ".. it feels very cold, I guess chilled." (P10, holes, s, 1). P6 started to wonder about the room temperature and continued saying that it feels cold: "Weirdly It really makes me wonder, What's the temperature, like in the room for this one......but for now, the default speed makes me feel cool like if it's summer, I feel like the room will be cooler." (P6, convex curvature, m, 1). Changes in body temperature illusion occurred in holes and convex curvature change types and with slow and medium speeds and on one wall.

In addition, participants expressed experiencing changes in body weight: "with three walls and high speed... totally flying up.....makes me feel with no weight, no sense of weight." (P12, waves, f, 3). P6, on the other hand, had an illusion about the weight of one of the cubes and described it as heavy: "It's like a big file cabinets coming towards me especially the front one and the one on the bottom seems very heavy" (P6,cubes, m, 1). Weight illusions occurred in cubes and waves types of change with medium and fast speeds and with one and three activated walls.

Participants reported illusions that relate to the body's relation with the surrounding space such as space vastness, room ventilation and motion illusions. For instance, P6 explained that the medium speed in the holes type of change makes the room larger: "It makes me feel like the room is larger like because I'm extending out towards the window or outwards." (P6, holes, m, 3). Sense of vastness illusion occurred in the holes and volume types of change and with medium and fast speeds and with three walls activated.

Also, participants expressed illusions of space ventilation with the concave curvature type of change: "I think this is the opposite of the first one, instead of being like missing air, it seems to be very well ventilated here." (P12, concave curvature, m, 3). P16 mentioned that the room with the convex curvature type of change is filled with air that is stretched: "Feel like like I'm in some air filled room" (P16, convex curvature, f, 3). Illusions about room ventilation occurred in volume, convex curvature and concave curvature change types with all speed settings and with three activated walls.

Participants reported motion illusions such as walking and falling. P5 described illusion of walking while standing still in front of the volume type of change: "It feels like I'm just walking, something like that" (P5, volume, s, 1). P2 mentioned the holes type of change made her feel like falling from the sky: "when moving away from me, It's like I'm falling down from the sky, but when it's moving towards me, It feels like I'm falling onto it, a bit strange." (P2, holes, s, 1). Motion illusions occurred in volume and holes types of change with slow speed and one wall activated.

4.3.2 Unpleasant Illusions.
In addition to the pleasant sensory illusions that affected the participants’ bodies directly and their surrounding space, participants also experienced unpleasant illusions which occurred either from the shape-changing walls, using VR or both. Participants reported feeling sick and nauseous: "so it makes me feel a bit queasy." (P1, volume, s, 1), "Can't even look at, it makes me feel really sick." (P9, cubes, m, 3). Sickness feelings were reported in volume, convex curvature, concave curvature, cubes and waves change types and with all speed and wall settings.

Participants also reported experiencing lack of balance from the shape-changing walls: "It feels a little disorientating." (P18, convex curvature, s, 3), "Oh, this one makes me feel dizzy." (P15, volume, f, 3). Lack of balance was noticed in volume, convex curvature, cones and waves and with slow and fast speeds and with one and three walls activated.

5 CO-DESIGN ACTIVITIES RESULTS

We conducted co-design to examine how participants understand the shape-changing walls parameters they experienced in VR and how do they use them in the designing non-intrusive shape-changing walls, and to explore the extent to which they reflect on their emotional experience and reactions in their designs.

Each participant generated one idea in the first design activity (Design 1). Figure 6 shows examples of them. The purpose of these ideas were grouped into two categories: functional and hedonic purposes, with 9 ideas in each group. Ideas in the functional purposes group include designing for communication (e.g. communicating weather information), adaptive affordances (e.g. designing a room that changes its size when needed), behaviour change, exercise and security. Hedonic purposes group involves designing for emotion regulation (e.g. designing a room to calm people down), aesthetic aim (e.g. designing a room with a shape-changing art piece) and entertainment such as games. A summary of the different shape-changing walls parameters participants used in their designs is presented below.

Change Type: Among these design ideas, seven participants chose to design with the amplitude type of change (using shapes such as cubes or cones), four participants chose the volume type, four participants selected the waves type, three participants selected the holes type and two participants chose to design with concave curvature change type.

Speed: Ten participants chose a variable speed for their designs. Three participants chose slow speed and medium speed and two participants used a fast speed for their designs.

Scale: Twelve participants selected to apply the shape-change on all room walls, four participants selected one wall and two participants chose to apply it on two to three walls.

Interaction: Nine participants selected indirect interaction, eight participants chose direct interaction and three participants chose no interaction for their shape-changing wall design.

Notification level: Twelve participants chose a notification level of make aware. Three and two participants selected change blind and interrupt levels respectively and one participant chose demand action notification level.

Abstraction level : was applicable only on design ideas of three participants since their designs involved information communication. Two participants selected a low abstraction level and one participant selected a medium level.

5.1 Designing for Intrusiveness of Shape-Changing Walls

Fourteen participants rated their designs (Design 1) as intrusive and only four participants rated them as non-intrusive. Following the rating of the level of intrusiveness of the shape-changing wall designs, each participant altered their design and designed a wall with an opposite intrusiveness level (Design 2). The differences between Design 1 and Design 2 were examined, analysed and categorised. The categorisation of the shape-changing wall parameters can help designers in understanding how each parameter relates to intrusiveness and hence make design choices based on whether they need a more or less intrusive designs of shape-changing walls. Below, we describe the categories we produced for each parameter based on our analysis. Table 5 outlines the main findings to help designers in making design choices.

Type of change (n=9): Three categories were identified:

  • Change type of change (n=5): Both groups of participants who rated their initial designs as intrusive and non-intrusive chose to alter their opposite design by changing the type. To make the design less intrusive or non-intrusive, participants (n=3) changed the type of their initial design and used Curvature and Holes types. However, participants (n=2) changed the type of change in their initial design to Amplitude to make it more intrusive.
  • Add another type of change (n=2): Both groups of participants who rated their initial designs as intrusive and non-intrusive updated their opposite design by adding another type. Participants (n=1) who rated their initial design as non-intrusive added another change type (e.g. amplitude) to make it more intrusive. In addition, participants (n=1) who rated their initial design as intrusive chose to add another change type (e.g. Holes) to make it less intrusive.
  • Use one type of change only (n=2): Participants who used two types of change in their initial design and rated it as intrusive chose to remove one of these types of change and use only one type to make the altered design less or non-intrusive.

Speed (n=14): Two categories were identified:

  • Reduce speed: participants (n=11) who rated their initial design as intrusive reduced the speed of the shape-changing wall design to make it less or non-intrusive.
  • Increase speed: participants (n=3) who rated their initial design as non-intrusive increased the speed of the shape-changing wall design to make it more intrusive.

Scale (n=9): Two categories were identified:

  • Reduce scale: participants (n=7) who rated their initial design as intrusive reduced the number of walls to make the shape-changing wall design less or non-intrusive. For example, participants who first chose to apply the shape change on all walls, reduced the number of walls to one shape-changing wall to make the design less or non-intrusive.
  • Increase scale: participants (n=2) who rated their initial design as non-intrusive increased the number of walls to make the shape-changing wall design more intrusive. For instance, participants chose to apply the shape-change on all walls to make the design more intrusive.

Interaction (n=4): Three categories were identified:

  • Change to direct interaction: participants (n=2) who rated their initial design as intrusive changed the type of interaction from indirect or no interaction to direct interaction to make the shape-changing wall design less or non-intrusive. Participants explained that the shape-changing wall can be less intrusive by giving the user more control over the wall (e.g. to control speed).
  • Change to indirect interaction: participants (n=1) who rated their initial design as intrusive changed the type of interaction from direct interaction to indirect interaction.
  • Change to no interaction: participants (n=1) who rated their initial design as non-intrusive changed the type of interaction from direct interaction to no interaction to make the shape-changing wall design more intrusive. Participants explained that by not offering any control over the shape-changing wall and by making it always on, the wall design can be intrusive.

Notification Level (n=3): Two categories were identified:

  • Reduce notification level: participants (n=2) who rated their initial design as intrusive reduced the notification level to make the shape-changing wall design less or non-intrusive. For example, one participant changed the notification level from Interrupt level to Make Aware, while another participant changed it from Make Aware level to Change Blind.

Abstraction level (n=2): Two categories were identified:

  • Decrease abstraction level: participants (n=1) who rated their initial design as non-intrusive decreased the abstraction level of the shape-changing wall design to make it more intrusive. For instance, P10 explained that the design can be intrusive by making it less abstract and more direct in terms of the information presented.
  • Increase Abstraction level: participants (n=1) who rated their initial design as intrusive increased the abstraction level of their shape-changing wall design to make it less or non-intrusive. For example, P1 mentioned that rather than presenting individual information about breathing, averaged data (higher abstraction) would make the design less or non-intrusive.

In addition to the categories extracted from the changes in the given wall parameters, participants reported other aspects they thought are related to the intrusiveness of shape-changing walls and were not covered in the studied parameters. These include shape size (e.g. in case of using Amplitude type), shape smoothness, movement type, modality and colour.

6 BENEFITS OF USING VR FOR DESIGNING LARGE-SCALE SHAPE-CHANGING INTERFACES

Participants reflected on the overall experience. Many of them found it engaging, fun and enjoyable. From the discussions, the following points were identified:

  1. Understanding the concept of shape-changing walls and vocabulary needed for design: Participants reported that this scaffolding process helped them understand the key concepts and vocabulary needed to design shape-changing walls. P11 mentioned that without VR, thinking about the design of shape-changing walls would have been challenging: "I think it would have been difficult to think about those kinds of shape-changing walls without having experienced it in some way" (P11).
  2. Understanding how the different shape-changing walls types affect emotions and considering that during the design activities: Participants mentioned that during the design activities, they were recalling how the shape-changing walls in VR made them feel and created their designs based on that: " I think thinking through the emotions I was feeling as I was viewing that helps me to think with the design activities" (P17).
  3. Providing a concrete experience that is important for the design phase: Participants expressed that the VR provided a realistic experience that helped during the design activities: "VR was a really good way of kind of introducing, giving me some concrete experience and the ability to think about what the design parameters are" (P11), while P12 mentioned that the VR was "like living the real stuff" and it would have been difficult to understand the parameters by, for instance, watching a video.
  4. Inspiring the design outcomes and design of future buildings: P2 explained that experiencing a particular type of shape-changing walls in VR inspired her design: "When I saw blocks are moving out of the wall..this really feels like I'm in an obstacle course and that kind of inspired me to do the design I did" (P2). P17 mentioned that the VR experience provided: " a lot of inspiration for thinking of future buildings design, just thinking of how fun you can make buildings" (P17). Other participants, however, reported that the VR experience inspired and shaped the design outcomes and that a different VR experience would result in different designs: " I could have had different VR experiences which would have pushed me to think about other kinds of ways of designing SCIs or shape-changing buildings" (P11).

7 DISCUSSION

In this work, we followed a co-design approach to better understand how shape-changing walls can impact people's perception, how such walls can be designed to become an architectural element of the building while being minimally-intrusive and how the VR experience can help participants during the design process. We first used VR to obtain initial impressions and reaction of shape-changing walls. Then, we engaged potential users in the design process. Finally, participants reflected on the overall design experience. Here, we discuss our findings and limitations, and outline some future directions.

Experiencing large-scale SCIs in VR has uncovered insights into how shape-changing walls are perceived. With VR, participants were able to experience positive and negative emotional reactions. Their emotional reactions ranged from feelings of excitement, pleasantness and clam to feelings of perceived anger and fear. Encountering this wide variety of emotions suggests that VR can be an effective tool for examining how large-scale SCIs impact people's emotions. Prior work has examined Projected Augmented Reality [ 11 ] as a platform for studying affordances of SCIs and demonstrated that such a platform can help studying their dynamic affordances. In this study, participants were able to detect different affordances of shape-changing walls in VR including, affordances of action possibilities (e.g. sitting) and inviting/repelling affordances which suggests that VR can be a viable platform for uncovering the dynamic affordances of large SCIs. Furthermore, it was surprising to find that participants were able to experience various sensory illusions while in VR. The movements that the shape-changing walls create made participants imagine hearing sounds and experiencing changes in body temperature and body weight. Such sensory illusions occur as participants complete the missing parts of their perceptual experience by using a sensory cue of one modality [ 9 ]. These results are in line with prior work on sensory illusions in VR, which showed that people can experience different cross-modal illusions including, auditory-visual (e.g. [ 32 ]) and visual-haptic (e.g. [ 9 , 61 ]. This confirms that VR is capable of inducing a wide range of sensory illusions to substitute the missing senses. In real-world scenarios where shape-changing walls are part of the built environment, it is possible that various emotional reactions would be evident. Prior work has shown that physical small-scale shape-changing interfaces can impact emotional reactions in many ways [ 16 , 55 ]. Because of their large-scale and the tactile experience that they offer, physical shape-changing walls might result in stronger emotional reactions compared to emotions experienced in a virtual environment. Future efforts should examine how people's experiences of physical shape-changing walls differ from virtual walls.

Our findings showed participants were able to design intrusive and non-intrusive shape-changing walls by manipulating different shape-change parameters. For instance, participants suggested changing the type to Amplitude to make the wall design more intrusive, whereas others suggested changing the type to Curvature and Holes types to make the design less or non-intrusive. It is possible this has occurred because of the sharp features used in the Amplitude type (i.e. cubes and cones) and the rounded edges used in the Curvature type. Previous work have showed that people tend to associate sharp shapes with toughness, strength and energy [ 8 , 64 ] as well as threat and danger [ 5 , 6 ], whereas rounded/curved shapes are usually associated with features that express friendliness and approachableness [ 8 , 16 , 64 ]. In addition, participants proposed reducing the speed to make the wall design less or non-intrusive and increasing the speed to the design intrusive. These findings are in line with previous work with ambient technologies, reporting fast speeds being not suitable as they are overly distracting and intrusive [ 12 , 24 ]. Also, the findings showed that by reducing the scale, shape-changing walls become less or non-intrusive and by increasing the scale, the walls design is considered more intrusive. This is because of the possible attentional demands that two or three walls would require compared to one wall.

Furthermore, findings showed that "No Interaction" makes designs intrusive, whereas "Direct interaction" or "Indirect interaction" makes designs less or non-intrusive. Prior work did not report specific interaction types that are appropriate for ambient technologies (No Interaction with WaterLamp [ 62 ], Indirect Interaction with Pinwheels [ 27 ]). Hence, it is essential for all interaction types to not require too much from users [ 3 ]. A shape-changing wall that is changing continuously without giving the user the option to stop it could be perceived as highly intrusive. Similarly, a wall that demands too much interaction from users can also be considered intrusive. A good balance is needed when choosing the interaction type to ensure that they can blend with the environment. Moreover, participants suggested reducing the notification level to make the design of the shape-changing walls less or non-intrusive. For the abstraction level, the results show that reducing the abstraction level leads to intrusive shape-changing wall designs, whereas increasing it results in less or non-intrusive. These findings are in agreement with previous research on design dimensions of ambient technologies, which report that high notification and low abstraction are considered intrusive and attention-demanding, while low notification level and high abstraction level are less intrusive [ 34 , 40 , 58 , 62 ]. The aforementioned findings can guide designers while making design choices about the intrusiveness of shape-changing walls. For instance, designers can choose more intrusive shape-changing wall designs (e.g. Amplitude type of change, varying speed setting, scale of three walls) in spaces that are designed for fun (e.g. play area, shopping malls), and use less intrusive designs of shape-changing walls (e.g. Curvature type of change, slow speed, scale of one wall) in spaces such as museums and art galleries.

Our findings showed that VR can be a viable method for explaining the concept of shape-changing walls and its vocabulary before carrying out design activities. During co-design activities, participants made use of the learnt concepts during the VR experience such as the different types of change and how are they conveyed. Also, many participants reflected on the reactions and emotions they felt during the VR experience while completing the design activities. This suggests that VR, prior to design, is an essential step for increasing participants’ engagement in the design activities. In addition, participants reported that VR experience followed by design activities made them more immersed in the design process and found the process as a whole enjoyable. Together, these findings demonstrate that the scaffolding approach we followed can help in designing large-scale shape-changing interfaces.

7.1 Limitations & Future Work

Real-world interactions with shape-changing walls could result in a more/less intense reactions compared to experiencing them in a virtual world. Future work should examine how real prototypes influence people's perceptions and how they differ from virtual walls. In addition, we studied a limited number of shape-change parameters (from Roudaut et al. [ 46 ]), there are, however, many other parameters from other taxonomies and models (e.g. texture) that future work should explore. Another limitation of this study is using mobile VR and cardboard VR viewers (because of COVID-19 pandemic) rather than research-grade VR headsets (e.g. HTC Vive, Oculus) during the VR experience. Future work should examine how more immersive VR experiences impact people's perceptions. An additional limitation of mobile VR is that the created scenes can be unrealistic due to the used rendering pipeline for this platform. To create realistic and high-fidelity graphics, we need a specific rendering pipeline for Unity (i.e. HDRP), however, it is designed for high-end platforms and not for mobile platforms (Android/iOS). Finally, our design activities were conducted with potential end-users of the building, where expertise of developing shape-changing walls was not required. Future work should examine the design of shape-changing walls with experienced designers and architects.

8 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we presented an online study that followed a co-design approach to design shape-changing walls. We first used mobile VR to gather people's initial impressions and reactions of shape-changing walls. Then, we conducted co-design activities with potential users to examine how participants use the shape-change parameters that they encountered during the VR experience in designing less or more intrusive shape-changing walls. Our results showed that experiencing shape-changing walls in VR can cause positive and negative emotional reactions, help in uncovering many affordances of shape-changing walls and can result in different sensory illusions. In addition, our findings from the co-design activities showed that the Amplitude type of change and a fast speed setting can result in more intrusive designs, whereas types of change such as Curvature and Holes and a slow speed setting can make designs less intrusive. Together, our results showed that our approach to designing shape-changing walls can facilitate the design process of large-scale shape-changing interfaces.
