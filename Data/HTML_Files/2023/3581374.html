Soliloquy: Fostering Poetry Comprehension Using an Interactive Think-aloud Visualization

Zak Risha , School of Computing and Information, University of Pittsburgh, United States, zak.risha@pitt.edu
Deniz Sonmez Unal , School of Computing and Information, University of Pittsburgh, United States, d.sonmez@pitt.edu
Erin Walker , School of Computing and Information, University of Pittsburgh, United States, eawalker@pitt.edu

CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Complex texts like poetry are distinct from informative texts, requiring additional subprocesses to decode and interpret. Approaching a poem without knowledge of these cognitive strategies can result in confusion and frustration—rather than comprehension. In this work, we explore how interfaces can surface and demonstrate these cognitive processes to novice readers. We introduce Soliloquy, an interface that visualizes the thoughts of an expert as they read and interpret a poem by using animations of text and pop-up tooltips. We evaluate the interface in a five-condition Mechanical Turk study (n=254) by varying the detail of thoughts, including audio, and substituting a static text control. Our study detected a significant difference in comprehension between the detail of thoughts, but not between the Soliloquy interface and static text control. We further investigate this finding in a think-aloud study (n=13), revealing the impact individual differences, experience, and cognitive load could have on Soliloquy's effectiveness.

1 INTRODUCTION

As text transitioned from print to digital mediums, researchers have sought ways to increase efficacy and comprehension of reading. A common goal has been to promote active reading where learners are critically evaluating what is read. Technologies have tried to recreate or augment strategies used on paper such as annotating, note taking, and highlighting [ 20 , 51 , 62 ]. Building off this approach, other attempts have sought to more deliberately scaffold certain cognitive processes by having users construct concept maps [ 55 , 56 ] or diagrams [ 49 ] during reading. Similarly, technologies have mimicked human tutors by training users to self-explain while reading [ 34 ] or guiding them through step-based tasks to break down a text [ 59 ]. These approaches typically deal with expository text where promoting active reading might be sufficient to achieve comprehension. Accordingly, these systems emphasize additional activities for the learner to complete during reading to provide scaffolding and promote active reading. But what if the text is so unique and difficult a learner doesn't even know how to start?

Certain texts, like computer code, math equations, or poetry, require more emphasis on demonstrating the process of reading to learners. These particular examples are complex, meaning they are dense with extra processes like tracking a program's execution, interpreting logic from symbols, or unraveling the meaning of a metaphor. Comprehending a text like poetry is mostly internal, where sub-processes are hidden from the eyes of learners trying to develop the capacity to decode a complex text. Interpreting poetry requires additional techniques such as close reading , which is a method that begins by paying close attention to verbal features like diction, figures of speech, and imagery to later incorporate into larger judgments about the work [ 25 ]. Without highlighting the fine-grained thinking behind reading, educators could frustrate learners or mistakenly present the process as innate or simple. Likewise, cultivating appreciation for poetry as an art-form in learners would be diminished by confusion or inability to form any meaning. There's both an opportunity and need for technology to make these invisible processes of reading visible for learners and clearly demonstrate what occurs when reading. However, designing technology for reading comes with many challenges as individuals differ in their capacity to read. Existing research has attempted to explain what factors, such as working memory, eye movement, or decoding skills, influence individual differences in reading [ 15 , 42 , 48 , 53 ]. Furthermore, complex texts like poetry likely introduce additional processes that could augment differences across learners.

Some researchers have investigated the components of reading comprehension in depth by using a familiar method valued for its ability to surface cognitive processes—the think-aloud protocol. This method involves participants thinking aloud as they perform a task, saying what comes to mind. Several studies have utilized the technique for studying reading processes in expository text [ 14 , 47 ], fiction [ 23 , 36 ], as well as complex texts like poetry [ 17 , 41 ]. One such study noted differences in cognitive processes between those trained in poetry versus novices [ 41 ], highlighting the unique aspects of reading a complex text like poetry. Experts were found to decode figurative language and employ a series of interpretive strategies, such as analyzing the structure and wordplay, but such processes were absent in novices. While such research helps identify reading processes of poetry, there still remains the task of training novices to adopt these cognitive processes. Thinking aloud has also been referenced as a teaching strategy for teachers to help model reading and promote awareness in students [ 10 , 16 , 28 , 39 ]. In this manner, the think-aloud is adapted as a type of worked-example, a step-by-step demonstration of how to solve a problem or perform a task which has been associated with learning effects and reduced cognitive load. More specifically, the reading process of a poem is broken down into individual thoughts that are then spoken to learners. These thoughts build off one another to demonstrate the sequential process of reading. However such didactic applications of think-aloud protocol were situated as in-person classroom activities for teachers and students. There is still a lack of research trying to replicate the approach with educational technology as well as make this information available interactively and remotely.

A technology based think-aloud visualization presents a new design space for recreating and augmenting the in-person strategy. Trying to break down one's own reading process in front of the classroom can be quite a challenge as thinking aloud when reading increases cognitive load. This can hinder the quality and clarity of thoughts when teachers or students perform the activity. Similarly, learners could have difficulty following along without being able to easily see what triggers thoughts. Technology can help reduce this burden by capturing a specific process to convey interactively while also allowing the learner to set the pace. Additionally, when users interact with learning technology, they generate data that provides a window into the learning process. Along with this data, technology can personalize the think-aloud experience by adapting to a learner's proficiency level, either increasing or decreasing the degree of scaffolding provided. There are also challenges for such a design, as individuals may vary in their existing reading processes. Likewise knowledge of think-aloud protocol is not widespread to learners. Without a human facilitator actually thinking aloud, the activity becomes less intuitive and clear, calling for investigating multimedia techniques, such as incorporating audio, to provide additional clarity. Exploring these issues will help inform the design of a new type of worked example for reading, a visualized think-aloud called Soliloquy (Figure 1 ). This research will make the following contributions to such a goal:

  • Further explore the design space of worked examples for reading comprehension by introducing a novel interface that visualizes a simulated think-aloud protocol.
  • Evaluate design variants in a crowdsourcing study that reexamines classical theories of multimedia learning and worked examples in the context of reading comprehension. Our results reveal the detail of the mental model (level of scaffolding) was a significant factor for comprehension scores and that partial verbal redundancy (including speech audio of text) and differing interfaces had no detectable effect on comprehension scores.
  • Investigate the surprising lack of difference between the control interface and Soliloquy in a think-aloud study, revealing the potential impact of individual differences, experience, and cognitive load for Soliloquy.

Our overall goal will be to provide insight into Soliloquy's design and its potential impact on learning. To the best of our knowledge, the work of visualizing the think-aloud with technology to demonstrate cognitive processes has not been previously explored in the literature, and thus there's a great opportunity for further assessing this technique and helping refine the approach. In addition, there is insufficient research on using educational technology for reading comprehension of complex texts like poetry. Furthermore, prior work has proposed applying strategies from reading comprehension to computer code [ 19 ]. The results of this study could also lead to further insights for reading other difficult texts found in mathematics or computer science.

2 RELATED SYSTEMS

2.1 Cognitive Diagramming

Beyond the more common paper-based techniques involved in active reading, other systems tried promoting specific cognitive behaviors that integrate and associate the information being read. While domains like programming can more easily provide automatic diagrammed relationships for users [ 61 ], systems in reading comprehension have aimed to support user-generated diagrams. MindDot [ 55 ], a concept map-based system, encouraged students to add nodes to concept maps interactively while reading to help improve comprehension, learning gains, and comparative strategies. The system incorporated two notable features to promote comparative strategies: hyperlinking concepts with related information and using an expert template to provide initial support. Similarly, texSketch [ 49 ] was a system that sought to promote Active Diagramming where users construct diagrams through active reading and information organization to help form mental models. The approach augmented the act of highlighting a phrase by underlining key causal terms in the phrase. Users then had the ability to diagram the relationships between these key causal terms. Both these approaches sought to more specifically scaffold the cognitive processes while reading, but learners may need more demonstration to successfully diagram relationships. When faced with a unique and challenging text like poetry, an active approach like diagramming could result in an excessive amount of cognitive load that overwhelms a learner. Such methods also have yet to be tested on complex texts like poetry that include metaphorical and figurative relationships, rather than just causal or conceptional ones.

2.2 Intelligent Tutoring

Reading comprehension for first language learners has not been a prevalent domain for intelligent tutoring systems (ITS), but a few notable systems have been created. These systems incorporate pedagogical agents to actively support a learner through the reading process. iSTART [ 34 ] was a system modeled off a human-based intervention for reading comprehension which prompts users to self-explain a text as they read. The objective was to teach students to use metacognitive reading strategies to improve their self-explanations and overall comprehension. Similarly, Wijekumar et al. conducted a large scale study which evaluated a web-based ITS [ 59 ] that incorporated a reading comprehension theory called Text Structure Strategy . The interactive interface, resembling a book, instructed students to click signaling words to identify them, categorize the text into one of the structures, and fill in additional information into tables, such as the main idea. Both these examples teach reading strategies and attempt to scaffold the comprehension process, but focus on expository texts. Accordingly, they have less emphasis on modeling fine grained reading processes. Poetry is a complex, compact text that is not emphasized in standardized curriculum nor present across disciplines. Learners may be unfamiliar with the approaches and strategies needed to read a poem, resulting in high cognitive load that calls for more initial demonstration.

2.3 Text Based Visualizations

Scholars have explored visualizing aspects of poetry with the emphasis of aiding a critic's process of close reading. Poemage is a visualization tool that focuses on visualizing phonetic aspects of a poem. The interface allows users to interactively explore the sonic topology of a poem, which is the distribution of rhyme sets across a poem and their potential interactions [ 33 ]. Similarly, Metatation is a system that produces a visualization from a critic's annotations during a close reading of a poem [ 35 ]. The paper-based system extracts annotations made by hand to generate a visualization with similar context-specific supplementary data such as the phonetic relationship of highlighted words. Visualizations to aid learners with reading poetry are less common, but researchers have made attempts in other domains. Kobayashi and Kawashima explored a text layout that fades sentences and paragraphs to facilitate reading comprehension of Japanese expository texts [ 26 ]. Pedagogical visualizations have also been used for other complex texts like computer code to demonstrate execution and algorithm behavior [ 18 , 22 ]. We likewise design visualizations to augment learner's comprehension, instead focusing on the domain of poetry.

2.4 Educational Technology for Poetry

Poetry, what many would consider an ill-defined domain [ 29 ], has produced a few examples of education technology that focused on more tangible elements. For instance, For Better for Verse focused on teaching meter of English Victorian poems (the rhythmic structure of a poem), using interactive techniques to mark stressed and unstressed syllables [ 52 ]. Likewise, Chen & Lin [ 12 ] designed an educational game to teach poetry, but focused on the context surrounding the poet. The study implemented a game-based situated learning system attempting to simulate aspects of the poets’ lives. Our research focuses on modeling and evaluating the more ill-defined acts involved with the comprehension of poetry using worked examples and visualizations.

3 RELATED THEORY

For our implementation and evaluation of Soliloquy, we consider two variants. The first manipulates the level of scaffolding in order to adjust and evaluate the amount needed for novices. The second explores the capabilities of multimedia learning by including speech of the text presented on the screen. We ground both these manipulations in theory from educational literature.

3.1 Worked Examples & Cognitive Load Theory

Our proposed visualization of a think-aloud is akin to a worked example, a step-by-step demonstration of how to solve a problem or perform a task. Worked examples are frequently studied in the context of cognitive load theory (CLT), which has identified the worked-example effect [ 40 , 50 ], a learning effect observed when worked examples are used as part of instruction instead of just problem solving. The effect is explained through CLT, which describes the allocation of working memory resources during a task. CLT breaks cognitive load into three distinct types:

  • Intrinsic Load : load caused by the inherent difficulty of the material and prior knowledge of user
  • Extraneous Load : load generated by ineffective instructional activities or material that do not aid learning
  • Germane load : load generated by effective instructional activities or material that contribute to learning

The worked-example effect is thought to occur through a reduction of extraneous load [ 40 , 50 ] which leaves space for germane load. Alternatively, using only more demanding activities, like problem solving, can result in too much extraneous load, leaving little room for learning. CLT theory and the worked-example effect provide a framework for understanding how demonstrating reading processes through a think-aloud visualization can foster poetry comprehension.

Learning by example eventually loses effectiveness, which is why scholars have discussed how to transition from a worked example into more challenging tasks like problem solving. One common approach is faded worked examples, a method that introduces incomplete examples over time to smooth the transition to active practice [ 43 , 44 ]. Likewise, similar approaches have been used in intelligent tutoring systems to adapt scaffolding to a learner's knowledge [ 54 ]. These efforts attempted to mitigate the expertise reversal effect [ 24 ] which describes a reversal of the effectiveness of instructional techniques for learners with differing levels of prior knowledge and experience. Matching the right level of support is an important aspect to consider for the efficacy of a worked example. However, the nature of ill-defined domains like poetry makes this process less straightforward as there exists no concrete procedure, goal, or solution. These considerations make both identifying and modifying the appropriate level of support an important challenge and question on its own.

3.2 Multimedia Theory and Verbal Redundancy

Texts are commonly augmented using other forms of media such as images or audio to increase their effectiveness. To explain this phenomenon, multimedia theory [ 31 ], a theory from cognitive science, has relied on a dual channel assumption that verbal and visual information involve separate systems of processing that each have their own limited capacity. Engaging both channels helps better distribute a load on learners as they process information. When relating multimedia to a think-aloud, there exists a spoken aspect not easily conveyed through just text. The term verbal redundancy refers to the inclusion of text and speech of the same words. While redundancy among channels was historically seen as undesirable, the effects of redundancy seem to depend on the context. Mayer revised his earlier redundancy principle after noting potential benefit when redundant text served to guide the learner's attention without priming extraneous processing [ 30 ]. Still, verbal redundancy has proved a challenging feature to implement in multimedia with a meta-analysis of the literature revealing mixed results or even suggesting the hindering of users by increasing cognitive load [ 1 ]. The authors of the review noted that such an analysis did not apply to teaching reading and also called for more research to study the effects of partial redundancy and its uses for signaling learners to important concepts. Similarly, studies have shown successes with verbal redundancy when following particular design principles. These benefits were observed in instances where the material was complex, the redundant on-screen text short, and learners had control over the pacing of the instruction [ 3 ]. Such findings suggest verbal redundancy, implemented deliberately and specifically, could benefit a visualization of the think-aloud by increasing comprehension and reducing cognitive load.

4 SYSTEM DESIGN

The Soliloquy interface is a web application that seeks to replicate and improve upon the think-aloud instructional strategy used in the classroom. For this activity, an instructor or student thinks aloud while performing a task, such as reading a poem, to model the process and provide a worked example for others. There are limitations in instructional methods that fail to highlight thinking processes involved in reading of literary texts like poetry. This absence in instruction has been described as presenting literary interpretation as "a form of magic," rather than a skill [ 28 ]. While reading poetry can be intimidating, a think-aloud might ease learners’ anxiety by demonstrating internal mental processes that facilitated interpretation. Soliloquy attempts to replicate this classroom activity visually using an interface that incorporates interactivity, animations, and popups. As reading poetry and another's mental process are both complex, our target user for the system is adults, rather than younger learners who may still be developing proficiency in basic reading comprehension. The goal of the interface design is to improve upon other alternatives, such as a static text transcript, by conveying additional information and context from a pedagogical think-aloud in a condensed, engaging, and intuitive format.

Processing another's thoughts can be complex, and Soliloquy's improvements could help reduce learners’ cognitive load, making the interface a more effective worked example. Likewise incorporating both audio and visual components could better distribute the load across channels and aid processing of information. As a single-page web application built with React, Soliloquy 1 runs in any modern browser and provides easier access to this helpful pedagogical strategy.

4.1 Interface Features

When thinking aloud to produce a worked example, there are contextual and procedural information useful to convey beyond the thoughts spoken aloud. Such details can be useful for illustrating the process to others, yet can be challenging to express both in-person or via static text. Our interface aims to develop novel ways to convey additional contextual and procedural details as well as the basic components of the activity. Such information includes:

  • The current attention of the expert reader in the text
  • Sequential order of read lines and spoken thoughts
  • Specific words triggering a thought
  • Each verbalized thought during the think-aloud

Soliloquy implements a novel combination of features to convey this information in an interactive and intuitive manner. During a think-aloud, one cannot easily observe the place in a text the expert reader is focused on. To address this, when users initiate the activity via a button, animations will bold each word to signal where the expert reader's eye is currently focused (Figure 2 A) and begin simulating the think-aloud. Another important element to convey is the sequential order of reading. Experienced readers of literature and poetry often read recursively, moving from noticing features, posing questions, and making connections [ 28 ]. For a condensed text like poetry, it is important to demonstrate that a single, linear reading for poetry is not typical like it may be for other texts. Soliloquy uses text shading animations to simulate recursive reading patterns (Figure 2 B) that model the process more accurately and facilitates connections to previously read text. Anytime an idea, question, or connection is made and ‘spoken aloud’ a popup appears on screen and conveys the expert reader's thought. To improve intuitiveness and signal the attention of learners, we implemented verbal audio of these thoughts as an optional feature. Additionally, the word or phrase that triggers the thought is highlighted (Figure 2 C) and the animation is paused. To engage users and allow them to control pace, users click the popup to continue through the activity (Figure 2 D), following along by observing where thoughts occur and what strategies an expert reader uses to decode and interpret a poem. These novel features aim to create an interface that goes beyond a static transcript, and (partially) in-person think-aloud, by showing the expert reader's attention, spoken thoughts, and triggering words, all the while preserving the sequential order.

4.2 Expert Thoughts

Use of the think-aloud for teaching commonly involves an instructor sharing their thoughts to provide a worked example for learners. The thoughts are shared for the purpose of demonstrating a mental model, thus they are not produced from a research-based think-aloud protocol nor necessarily prioritize precision. Thoughts also vary based on the expert's approach to the poem, but share the similar goal of demonstrating an understanding of the poem. The thoughts used in Soliloquy were initially generated by the first author during post graduate work in English literature. The thoughts were developed with the aid and supervision of a faculty member with expertise in the close reading of poetry. Formation of these thoughts were inspired by related think-aloud studies [ 17 , 41 ] as well as observations of course instruction of poetry at the undergraduate university level. These thoughts were then further refined for the purposes of our study. For this study, only variations in the level of detail of thoughts are explored.

4.3 Variants

Our design process also explored two variants. The first sought to evaluate differing degrees of scaffolding to provide insight on matching the appropriate level of support for learners. Worked examples like Soliloquy provide support to minimize extraneous load and allow users more space for learning. Offering too little support creates an excess of cognitive load that impairs learning, while excessive support is also a concern as it can limit opportunities for learners to apply their own knowledge. With the potential of adaptive technology and personalization, the think-aloud interface could omit some insights or lessen the detail based on a learner model. An additional concern arises for an ill-defined domain like poetry, where highly detailed thoughts would impose a particular interpretation of the poem on the learner. Such an approach would mislead learners into thinking that poems have "an answer" and violate one of the core aspects of poetry which welcomes different interpretations. This issue complicates the question of how to match worked examples to learners’ knowledge and experience, prompting our manipulations to produce two levels of scaffolding. Our high scaffolding (Figure 3 A) condition included more detailed thoughts that not only signal the learner to important concepts but also note their relevance or imply a connection. The low scaffolding condition (Figure 3 B) reduces the amount of detail provided, identifying the same important elements of the poem but offering less insight into their significance or relationships. While the low scaffolding condition is less likely to impose a particular interpretation, it may generate an excess of cognitive load resulting in less learning. More scaffolding would likely be needed to sufficiently aid a learner. With a static text control (Figure 4 ), our alterations to scaffolding also contrasts the amount of information presented versus the way it is presented, allowing for a clearer understanding of what contributes most to learning. Overall, this variant offers interesting insights and provides design implications for future iterations of the software.

Our second variant had the goal of increasing engagement, reducing cognitive load, and improving intuitiveness. As the think-aloud is less common outside a research setting and may be novel to users, the addition of audio for thoughts could more closely resemble the act of speaking thoughts aloud. The goal of this partial verbal redundancy would be to engage a second auditory channel to draw attention to the thoughts, as well as improve the intuitiveness of the exercise. For this feature, thoughts that are “spoken aloud” and emerge as tooltips will also trigger accompanying audio of the same text to more closely mimic a traditional think-aloud experience. The deep neural network WaveNet [ 38 ] now provides high-quality text-to-speech that were used to autogenerate the accompanying audio. Not only is the design easy to implement but could lead to a number of benefits for learners. Our investigation tries to determine if Soliloquy's interface could benefit from partial verbal redundancy and also help further refine the literature on this topic.

5 CROWDSOURCING STUDY

5.1 Method

Our hypotheses were guided by the belief that Soliloquy's interactive interface that conveys more procedural and contextual information would outperform simpler methods such as static text. We also assumed that the variants involving verbal redundancy and scaffolding would impact comprehension, leading to the following hypotheses:

  • Users using the animated think-aloud interface will demonstrate significantly higher comprehension than those using static text.
  • Users receiving high scaffolding (level of detail and completeness of think-aloud) will demonstrate significantly higher comprehension than those receiving low scaffolding.
  • Users receiving partial verbal redundancy (audio) will demonstrate significantly higher comprehension than users receiving no audio.

Our target end user for this interface were adult novices. As poetry is a form of art, a broader goal of this project was not to limit its appreciation to high school or college students. Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) was selected as a suitable venue for recruitment as the crowd-sourcing platform provided access to a more diverse population. While we anticipated additional challenges in scaffolding and designing an appropriate task for workers, we felt MTurk better reached a general audience less commonly represented in many educational technology studies. We were also interested in recruiting outside a classroom environment to better measure the degree of engagement participants would exhibit for this domain. Human Intelligence Tasks (HIT)s were made available on September 2020 to workers from the United States and Canada with at least 100 hits approved and a 95% approval rate. Participants were informed they were participating in a research study, along with disclosure of details regarding data use and our anonymization process. The introductory script also explained they would be performing a reading comprehension task involving poetry upon recruitment. Participants were informed the task would take approximately 15 minutes and that they would be compensated $ 2.10 upon successful completion. The HIT on MTurk consisted of three sections: a brief introduction, a reading task, and an assessment task. Subjects were informed they would be provided with the thoughts of an expert who read the poem and thought aloud to aid them in the task. Participants were provided with the same prompt informing them they would be quizzed on the poem (Figure 4 ), as well as brief details of how to use the particular interface. The reading task consisted of a fourteen line sonnet by Robert Frost titled “Design”. The poem was carefully selected for possessing features commonly analyzed in close reading. In Robert Frost's Design, there are dramatic shifts in the speaker's thought process. The speaker of the poem (narrator) moves from observing, to imagining, to reimagining, and then concluding with internal dialogue. These subtle shifts provide opportunities to highlight how an expert extracts important information for analysis by following the trajectories of the speaker's mind. Likewise the poem is full of figurative language, symbolism, and the scene of spider, moth, and flower acts as an extended metaphor. This dense complexity presents many opportunities to be further expanded upon through the Soliloquy interface that can dramatically alter the understanding of the work. Other factors for selection were the brevity and complexity of the poem, while also being approachable to less experienced participants. Evaluating the efficacy of Soliloquy when applied across differing types and genres of poetry is of interest for future work, but was not a focus of our current study.

The experimental conditions provided the information using the animated think-aloud interface, including the aforementioned variants involving level of scaffolding and partial verbal redundancy, to create a total of 5 conditions. The control condition used static text for the thoughts expressed by an expert reader which were provided side-by-side along with the poem for subjects to read in any manner of their choosing (Figure 4 ). The static thoughts appear in the same order as the interface and include a line number to denote which line was referenced. Overall, the condition imitates providing the transcription produced from think-aloud protocol. Subjects were assigned to one of the following conditions by random assignment:

  • C: Static text transcript of the high scaffolding version of the think-aloud.
  • HS: High scaffolding of the animated think-aloud interface with partial verbal redundancy.
  • HS-NA: High scaffolding of the animated think-aloud interface with no audio.
  • LS: Low scaffolding of the animated think-aloud interface with partial verbal redundancy.
  • LS-NA: Low scaffolding of the animated think-aloud interface with no audio.

After the reading task, participant knowledge was assessed with 8 multiple-choice questions with one correct answer and three distractors. While most poems have an element of ambiguity that welcomes multiple interpretations (if sufficiently grounded in evidence and reason), there exists well-agreed upon interpretations of what is transpiring that can still be challenging for learners to comprehend. Understanding these essential details of a poem are necessary for making more complex, holistic interpretations of the entire poem's meaning. Multiple choice questions were selected for measuring comprehension as we believe the format suitable for assessing these intermediate details from the poem. We further point to recent work outside the domain of poetry which compared multiple choice versus open ended assessments, noting equivalent levels of difficulty and further reasoning both approaches can assess similar knowledge [ 57 ]. Prior to the multiple choice questions, participants were provided relevant definitions of persona , the first person narrator of the poem, and expert reader , the person reading and sharing their thoughts. Four of these multiple-choice questions assessed comprehension of the poem, another four assessed comprehension of the expert's mental model and thoughts (see Figure 5 ), and one additional question aimed to validate they weren't attempting to game the task. The validation question was embedded in the assessment and asked for participants to successfully identify the correct title of the poem. Subjects that did not answer the validation question correctly were removed and not considered to have completed the participation requirements.

Following the assessment, participants completed a survey that measured their cognitive load, familiarity and experience with poetry, and demographic information. To measure cognitive load, we adapted a ten-item instrument by Leppink et al. [ 27 ] which measures three types of cognitive load: intrinsic, extraneous, and germane load. Wording of each question was adjusted to align with the task of reading poetry as well as use of the Soliloquy interface. Additionally, a single item was flipped in connotation to represent a positive metric, rather than negative, in an effort to gauge attentiveness. Familiarity and past experience with poetry, the specific author, and poem were assessed using 6 items. Last, the survey collected age, gender, and education as well as whether English was their native language. Additionally, log data of user interactions were recorded when users navigate to the next section, interact with the think-aloud, and progress through assessment and survey.

A total of 216 subjects submitted HITs with 22.2% failing criteria for acceptance (missing the validation question), resulting in 168 participants. Additional subjects were removed due to technical issues and irregular behavior: 18 due to technical issues cause by users refreshing the HIT, 4 due to logging errors, 1 due to repeating the experiment, and 4 due to rapidly clicking a button over 100 times. A final total of 141 subjects were retained for analysis.

Subjects were distributed unevenly among conditions due to the aforementioned exclusions and also attrition. Our goal of random assignment led us to assign a condition at the time a user accepted a HIT. However, the task experienced high attrition rates where workers returned the HIT at a mean rate of 56.6% across all conditions which disrupted the balancing of subjects. A chi-square test revealed a relationship between attrition rates and experiment group, χ 2 (4, N = 510) = 13.794, p < .01, notably attrition rates were lower on average (41.2%) for the control condition. One notable difference in the control condition is users could navigate directly to the assessment, rather than first having to complete the learning activity using Soliloquy. No relationship was found between experiment group and missing the validation question.

As the imbalance of subjects weakened the study's power, a second round was conducted in Fall 2022 with the goal of improving the minimum effect size detectable. A total of 155 subjects submitted HITS with 27.1% failing criteria for acceptance (missing the validation question), resulting in 113 participants. The added subjects improved the detectable effect size to f = 0.26 (medium effect) with 80% power. A chi-square test found no difference in failing of criteria between studies χ 2 (1, N = 371) = .9168, p = .34. Attrition rates were significantly lower than the prior study at 22.1% χ 2 (1, N = 709) = 71.01, p < .001. We found no relationship between attrition rates and experiment group, although control rates were again lower on average (15.4%). We report the analysis of both samples combined unless noted with the following total participants per condition: 60 C , 49 HS , 50 HS-NA , 46 LS , 49 LS-NA .

5.2 Results

5.2.1 Assessment. We conducted a two-way ANOVA with the experiment condition and round of the study as independent variables and comprehension score as the dependent variable to gauge whether comprehension scores on the assessment differed across all five possible conditions. Results of a two-way ANOVA indicated that the groups did not differ significantly in their performance on the reading assessment F (4, 246) = 1.353, p = .25 (Table 1 ). We found that the round of the study did significantly differ from each other in their performance on the assessment F (1, 246) = 21.283, p < .001. Specifically, the second round participants scored lower on average than those in the first round. An interaction between round of the study and experiment condition was not significant F (4, 246) = 1.09, p = .34. To further investigate our hypotheses, we performed a series of planned contrasts, including the round of study as a control variable in the analysis.

To address H1 , we conducted a planned contrast comparing the control condition to all other conditions using the Soliloquy interface. Contrary to our hypothesis, participants in the control condition did not score significantly different on the assessment than those in the interface conditions ( t (246) = −.535, p = .59).

Likewise our second planned contrast examined the impact of high scaffolding versus low scaffolding within conditions using Soliloquy ( H2 ). Results confirmed our hypothesis, revealing that subjects in the high scaffolding conditions scored higher on the assessment than subjects in the low scaffolding conditions ( t (246) = 2.00, p = .047).

Our last hypothesis, H3 , sought to compare audio and no audio within the Soliloquy conditions. Our hypothesis that partial verbal redundancy (including audio of text) would improve subjects’ performance on the assessment was not supported. 6 THINK-ALOUD STUDY

Our hypothesis that our interactive, think-aloud interface would outperform static text ( H1 ) was surprisingly not supported. This motivated us to further investigate the result in a subsequent study. Seeking to address limitations of Mechanical Turk, notably the inability to observe or interact with subjects, we decided to conduct a think-aloud study that replicated the major elements of the first experiment. For this design, we used the high-detailed thoughts present in the control and high scaffolding conditions to focus on differences between the two interfaces. Likewise, since we elected to use think-aloud protocol to observe the internal nature of reading, we felt additional audio of someone thinking aloud could disrupt the research protocol. Therefore, we shifted focus away from the verbal redundancy variable, making it disabled by default and available via a button. Last, we decided to utilize a within-subjects design in contrast to our previous between-subjects deployment. We felt having the ability to compare interfaces would reduce noise from individual differences and shed more light on user preferences.

Our plans for a second study led us to reconsider differences between the two conditions and their implications for design. An advantage of the control is that it displays all information at once and at all times. We hypothesized sequential processing of thoughts would be advantageous and help participants focus on smaller pieces of information at a time, but may have introduced a limitation. This feature increased the difficulty to go back to previous thoughts and revise ideas or form new connections. We set out to address this issue in a new design (Figure 6 ) that allowed thoughts to persist to the right of the poem. Additionally, participants could hover over past thoughts, which would highlight the corresponding section of the poem. We hoped these changes would combine strengths of both interfaces by chunking information into smaller, more manageable pieces while also supporting freedom in reviewing thoughts.

Our study was made available to students, faculty, and staff at a northeastern university in the United States. Participants were informed that they will read poetry using various website interfaces, as well as answer comprehension questions. A total of 13 subjects participated in the study: 7 students, 5 staff, and 2 faculty. We stopped recruitment efforts after failing to find new themes and suspected saturation had occurred. The study took approximately 30 minutes and participants were compensated $ 10. The study was conducted remotely over video-conferencing software in which audio and recording of participant's shared screen were collected. After obtaining consent, the facilitator demonstrated think-aloud protocol using a similar example task of interpreting a riddle. Subjects were then presented with either Soliloquy or the static text control, counterbalanced for sequence effects. The Soliloquy interface was prefixed by a brief tutorial video on how to use the software. Participants then used the interface and thought aloud for the first half of the poem before answering two assessment items from our prior experiment (selected by higher rates of error). The participants then were introduced to the alternative interface to read the second half of the poem and again answer two assessment items (while thinking aloud). The facilitator asked a few questions at the end about the efficacy of the interfaces as well as the participant's preferences. The first author examined the automated transcripts, video recordings, and question responses for illustrative cases related to interactions with the interface, engagement with the expert thoughts, as well as differences between Soliloquy and static text control. Below we present when and how participants engaged with thoughts as well as differences observed between interfaces and individuals.

6.1 Results

6.1.1 How Participants Engaged With Thoughts. In our previous study, we found that more detailed thoughts had a positive effect on participants’ performance in the Soliloquy conditions ( H2 ) and likely benefited those in the control. We were further motivated to explore how participants engaged with these thoughts in ways that aided their understanding. A common positive interaction was how thoughts directed the attention of users. For example, participants noted a thought highlighted “something I missed. The author putting all three of them together” (P8) or that they “didn't realize that the author repeated white multiple times” (P3). Thoughts could also prompt forming possible explanations for the poem: “maybe the author is referencing some meta, something bigger than all this. Maybe that's the something referenced here” (P9). Likewise, thoughts could stimulate new, related ideas: “white is often a symbol of death in many cultures and often like when things are kind of decaying they kind of turn this white” (P2). In interviews after the task, all but one participant reported the thoughts helped them read the poem. More specifically, participants mentioned thoughts helped by “breaking [the poem] down and putting things into like a bigger context that I would not have been able to do on my own” (P7). Similarly, others discussed how the expert thoughts provided “a deeper understanding of the poem” (P9), “gave me more things to think about” (P13), and “guided my thoughts” (P9). Overall these findings show the capacity of detailed expert thoughts to guide attention as well as facilitate certain cognitive processes.

In some cases, however, participants interacted with the thoughts in ways that seemed less likely to directly aid their interpretation of the poem. Sometimes participants simply read the thought and immediately moved on to what was next. Other interactions were more subtle, such as stating they “agree with like the fearful tone” (P4) or put the thought into their own words: “so yeah he's thinking about like the spider and the flower and, like all the ingredients like over and over again" (P6). Participants also disagreed with thoughts, expressing they “don't necessarily think that it's [taking it back]... there's still an uneasiness to the line” (P2). Likewise, not all participants felt the thoughts were helpful, as one felt they "got between me and the poem" (P5). At times, the participant, who had relevant experience, expressed their own interpretations: "I think it's more imaginative and creative" (P5). This might suggest a desire for freedom to apply interpretive skills they've honed, rather than rely on another's. While detailed expert thoughts seemed to have the capacity to help, they may be dependent on the quality of the interaction with the learner.

6.1.2 When Participants Engaged with Thoughts. We also wanted to better understand the lack of difference in performance between the Soliloquy and control interface ( H1 ). The most notable difference between interfaces was when participants engaged with the thoughts. In the control interface, the majority (11 of 13) of participants read the entire poem first and then proceeded to read the thoughts. One participant further alluded to this after the experiment, sharing they “read the whole poem and then I went to the interpretation like kind of out of order” (P6). This sometimes led participants to quickly read through the thoughts without much observed engagement. One such participant read them all quickly, then shared a few brief thoughts, such as "I agree, a lot of grim language on the left" (P3). They later elaborated after the task: “it was really easy for me to kind of like rush through” (P3). Other challenges involved linking thoughts to the text, as one participant noted "I just mixed up the two lines" (P7), and after the task reflected “It's so confusing... feeling like you have to go back by line to the like reader notes (P7). Similar challenges parsing the thoughts were expressed in interviews, one participant noting it was “easier to miss things when you just have it all laid out in front of you” (P2). Despite these issues, some participants reported they liked the ability to “read like the poem line first and then like match it on my own” (P9) and likewise felt this method allowed them to “read at your own pace” (P4). Overall the static interface provided some participants more control and freedom in how they read but for others lead them to rush through or have issues connecting information with the text.

In contrast, nearly all participants using Soliloquy were observed reading thoughts in conjunction with the poem. Some participants slowed down and carefully considered each thought, learning new insights as they read: "Oh, so it says before that the moth, the moth, flower, and spider were compared, so I guess like I didn't realize a flower was involved" (P7). This participant added afterward they "liked how you knew what part you were supposed to be looking at at every point" (P7). Others shared similar comments that Soliloquy “[introduced] points to you one at a time” and liked being able to “tell it when you're ready to deal with the other one” (P2). However, this reading pattern created, rather than reduced, cognitive load for others. One participant was observed progressing through Soliloquy quickly, stating “I'm like trying to read it at my own pace... I don't think I'm absorbing much right now” (P4). Afterward the participant further explained it was “hard to make my own thoughts and then absorb this person's thoughts at the same time” (P4). Similar concerns were shared by others that this approach of reading “somewhat obscured the thinking process” (P13) or “distracted me from my own thoughts” (P5). Soliloquy seemed to help some participants by ensuring they took time to view and link each thought with the poem, as well as limit the amount of information presented at a time. However, for others, Soliloquy could make them feel rushed or interfere with their thinking processes.

6.1.3 Potential Impact of Individual Differences. Overall, this evidence suggests participants varied in their reaction to Soliloquy, potentially due to individual differences in how they read and process textual information, and this could have confounded the potential advantages of Soliloquy's design. Additional evidence from the study could further explain some of these observed differences. Issues such as feeling pressured or rushed may have been related to usability. Even after a video tutorial, a few participants were observed not knowing where to click to progress in the interface and seemed to later rush interactions. Additionally, one participant further stated that they preferred static text, “but I think I could get used to it [Soliloquy]” (P13). This could mean some users did not feel they had sufficient control over the interface, letting it drive interactions and pace. Another factor could have been a participant's existing experience with poetry. Some participants that felt interrupted by Soliloquy also demonstrated existing understanding of poetry's reading process. These participants made remarks demonstrating proficiency such as noting that "white is kind of juxtaposing like this dark imagery that he's conjuring up" (P4) or alluding to existing knowledge by stating how an element was not “as they're typically symbolized in literature” (P5). This could mean more experienced readers already attempt to apply cognitive strategies for interpreting poetry and are instead interrupted by Soliloquy's modeling of expert thoughts. Accounting for individual differences may be critical for applying Soliloquy's approach.

7 EXPLORATORY POST-HOC ANALYSIS

7.1 Revisiting Cognitive Load

Although we found no significant difference among conditions with regard to reported cognitive load, our findings from the think-aloud study suggested load could have been an important factor for users using Soliloquy. Users that felt a lack of control with the interface or sought more freedom to apply their own cognitive processes would likely have had increased cognitive load that would diminish Soliloquy's efficacy. This dynamic is captured by extraneous load which describes cognitive load from ineffective instructional activities. We thus looked at the relationship between extraneous load and assessment scores among the control and high scaffolding conditions. Comparing just these three conditions, we were not able to detect a significant interaction between experiment condition and extraneous load ( F (153) = 1.35, p = .26). Although we failed to detect an interaction, our results from our think-aloud study and absence of other significant findings lead us to believe extraneous load could still be an important factor. We found significant negative relationships between extraneous load and performance on the assessment for all three conditions (Figure 7 ). While the control had a weaker negative correlation of r = −0.335, p = .008, the high scaffolding conditions (HS and HS-NA) had more moderate correlations of r = −0.600, p < .001 and r = −0.513 p < .001. These findings contribute to a working hypothesis that extraneous load, potentially from individual differences, played a larger role for Soliloquy conditions than the static text control.

7.2 Time on Task

Our crowdsourcing study also collected log interactions that could help us distinguish users and better understand their engagement with the task. We were interested if time spent could help explain the lack of differences in performance between interfaces. Response time data has been used in educational data mining as a predictor of learning [ 58 , 60 ] as well as a proxy for student cognitive states [ 2 , 5 , 9 ]. These applications for learning analytics assume that response time has a nonlinear relationship, hypothesizing that there is an ideal range of time students should be spending on the task. In this manner, the students who spend too much or too little time on the task will be more likely to struggle.

Recent work that builds on this hypothesis has shown success in decomposing response time into categories that distinguish between time spent on productive and unproductive behaviors [ 2 ]. We adapt this approach to our dataset to elicit the time frames that are indicative of these periods. Our analysis focuses on the time spent on each individual item of the Soliloquy interface, which we analyze separately based on scaffolding due to the differing amounts of detail. For high scaffolding and low scaffolding conditions, we first apply a log transformation to obtain a normal distribution. For each participant, we compute the average time spent on the 13 items in Soliloquy. For the control condition, we compute an estimate for the average time spent on sentences by dividing the total activity duration by 13 as the static text interface they used does not produce fine-grained log information.

For each condition, we built a single piecewise regression model using average time as the segmented independent variable and the assessment score as the dependent variable. As mentioned, response time is often considered to be nonlinear, and recent work has proposed a quadratic relationship indication of both too fast and too slow responses will correlate with lower performance [ 13 ]. We similarly construct our model by estimating 2 break points (Table 3 ) within the associated time data. These breakpoints capture shifts in the relationship between time and assessment score. The first captures the point where response time's positive relationship peaks, the second when it starts to decline. These two breakpoints attempt to produce the following three regions:

  • Region 1 : (too fast) conceptualized as a region where responses occur too quickly or carelessly
  • Region 2 : (just right) ideal response time indicating a productive engagement with the item
  • Region 3 : (too slow) delayed response indicating mind wandering or disengagement

As the breakpoints in the model represent points when these relationships shift, they aren't always ideal boundaries for regions. For instance, we observed that users prior, but in proximity, to the first breakpoint had high performance on assessment scores. This would suggest they belong in the productive region, region 2. We thus used the points where the broken regression line intersects with the average comprehension score to determine the boundaries for the regions (Figure 8 ). Once the regions were constructed, we compared the comprehension scores, extraneous load, and reported familiarity with the task (Table 3 ).

In contrast, this pattern is not as consistent for the participants with the same characteristics (i.e. productive and low extraneous load) in the control condition. This further highlights the importance of extraneous load for the Soliloquy conditions, and further alludes to a lesser effect on performance for the control.

7.3 Impact of Familiarity & Experience

There was evidence in our think-aloud study that experience and familiarity with reading poetry could impact how users react to Soliloquy. More specifically, participants with greater familiarity found the expert thoughts interrupted existing cognitive processes. We attempted to measure familiarity in our crowd-sourcing study, where participants were asked to describe their familiarity with reading poetry, the author of the poem, Robert Frost, and the specific poem itself, Design. Surprisingly, familiarity had significant negative correlations with performance across almost all conditions in the first round (Table 4 ). Although we expected from our think-aloud study that prior experiences could negatively impact the efficacy of Soliloquy, we did not anticipate a similar effect in the control condition which did not promote direct engagement with thoughts. Although the evidence limited, it may be possible that familiarity and experience lead to well established interpretive habits less malleable to change. This could mean that the approach of sharing expert thoughts fails to engage those more experienced, which seemed to be the case for one participant in our think-aloud study. We also acknowledge the possibility that fatigue or disengagement impacted reporting of familiarity. Our analysis of response times indicated that familiarity was lower on average in the productive region 2 (Table 3 ). While this could suggest that familiarity contributed to disengagement, we also acknowledge this could have led to less accurate reporting of prior experiences. However, the presence across conditions suggests experience could play a role in the efficacy of this approach but requires further investigation.

8 DISCUSSION

8.1 Personalizing Learning

Our hypothesis that our interactive, think-aloud interface would outperform static text was surprisingly not supported, suggesting designing for this space is even more challenging than anticipated. Evidence from our studies further imply reading is a intricate process that can also vary across individuals, further complicated by the unique elements in the domain of poetry. Our think-aloud study highlighted how users differed in their reactions to Soliloquy, presumably due to differences in prior experiences or how they process and experience animations and interface features. There was also some evidence in our crowdsourcing study of these differences, as extraneous load had a stronger relationship in the Soliloquy conditions. Additionally, there was evidence that prior experience played a role, as we found a negative relationship with performance. These findings suggest a more personalized approach that can adapt to these differences may be required to ensure participants benefit from Soliloquy's approach.

Our studies helped reveal opportunities to cater the experience to users based on their preferences and differences. As more experienced readers already bring cognitive habits of reading and interpreting poetry, they may need an alternative approach that provides more space to first form their own interpretations. Research in the domain of reading comprehension has personalized material based on proficiency or reading level [ 7 , 32 ], but there may be additional opportunities for other factors, such as reading habits. Existing work has studied individual differences of reading habits [ 45 ] as well as metacognitive awareness of reading strategies [ 37 ], which may prove useful characteristics for personalization research. Adapting reading content may not be sufficient for users, and systems like Soliloquy may need to personalize visual elements and functionality of the interface. For example, users that already possess or have developed cognitive reading habits may not benefit from an in-depth demonstration. Soliloquy could provide a second exploratory mode that prioritizes user control of when and how they interact with thoughts. With additional types of interfaces, such as demonstrative and exploratory modes, Soliloquy could better appeal to users of varying experience and reading habits.

Results of our think-aloud study and analysis of response times revealed the importance of spending an appropriate amount of time [ 11 , 13 ] engaging with each thought. Existing research has tried to encourage learners to meaningfully engage with content, rather than rushing through just to progress. Prior efforts to dissuade users have utilized thresholds of time users must wait in order to progress or obtain help [ 8 ]. Other attempts have tried to prompt reflection in users by providing visual or verbal feedback when response times are fast [ 6 , 46 ]. Soliloquy could adapt to individuals that feel compelled to rush through by reminding them to pause and reflect on the thoughts. By personalizing how users interact with Soliloquy, the interface will be positioned to adapt to subtle complexities and variance that can occur when reading poetry.

8.2 Implications of Scaffolding

One of the more consistent findings across our analysis was the impact of low scaffolding on performance. Attempting to lessen the completeness of the demonstration by focusing on just highlighting and identifying key concepts seemed to notably decrease the effectiveness of the activity. Our think-aloud study revealed detailed thoughts did aid learners by drawing their attention to elements of the poem, but also augmented understanding, broke down concepts, and guided thoughts. Low scaffolding may have only signaled key elements, meaning the increased task difficulty of comprehending the significance of elements led to insufficient working memory for learning. The finding holds further implications for conducting fading of worked example for ill-defined domains like poetry. More commonly, existing work has focused on well-structured domains where problems have clear solution steps. Fading occurs through omission of certain solution steps in examples [ 43 , 44 ], leaving learners to fill the blanks on their own. However, scaffolding in less defined domains such as poetry may require alternative approaches. For cognitive mapping, another visual scaffold used in the similarly open-ended domain of expository text, prior work has employed incomplete expert templates to scaffold learners [ 55 ]. Users can then expand and complete the concept map on their own, as certain nodes are omitted. While potentially useful, the approach comes with the additional workload of processing an unfamiliar structure, a concern shared by Soliloquy. Furthermore, where concept maps are more abstract, a think-aloud consist of sequential thoughts which may cause confusion if omitted due to a lack of continuity. Our approach of reducing detail across the entire example could have promise for fading as results suggest increased task difficulty.

As scaffolding is faded, learners must also start filling in the gaps of the example. We reason the increased difficulty of the lower scaffolding condition was due to the activity only signaling key elements of the poem, leaving learners to ascertain their meaning and significance. However, the observed lower performance may have also been caused by participants simply overlooking this extra step of interpretation, resulting in shallow interactions. Our analysis of response time uncovered less variance in time spent for low scaffolding conditions, potentially because most participants didn't spend the extra time to link key concepts and evidence together. This could mean extra prompting to cue learners on how to complete the reading and interpretation process may be needed. This would echo previous work demonstrating the efficacy of self explanation prompts when using faded worked example [ 4 ]. For concept mapping, adaptive support has cued learners by highlighting nodes to expand in the incomplete expert template [ 56 ]. Similarly, Soliloquy could prompt interpretation in conjunction with fading of details to more effectively transition learners.

Although a core tenet of poetry is welcoming diverse interpretations (and likewise not imposing them on students), our results suggest a worked example lacks the desired efficacy without a detailed reading (e.g., by providing high scaffolding). Such findings shed light on what may be more fruitful ways to reduce cognitive load for novices of poetry. Students may need to engage with the interpretations of another before being prepared to construct them on their own. Although our study focused on the more intermediate, surface level details of the poem, a detailed reading still introduces subjectivity. Educators may then need to correct for misassumptions about the subjective nature of interpretation. One such approach might be demonstrating differing readings of the same work to show how there can exist multiple, yet still valid interpretations if justified by the reader. These results contribute to the broader discussion of ill-defined domains which are tasked with modeling a problem with no clear, single answer. Although thoughtful educators aim to be cognizant of their own bias, disclosing, rather than avoiding, them may be the most suitable compromise.

8.3 Partial Verbal Redundancy

As previously noted, the benefit of verbal redundancy for reducing cognitive load and improving learning has often gone unsupported in other contexts. However, our implementation for reading comprehension was quite deliberate, following many of the guidelines covered through the literature, including a meta-analysis [ 1 , 3 ]. Our implementation was partial, only translating thoughts (not the text of the poem) into speech to signal important ideas. Likewise, the redundancy was used with complex material (a poem) where users controlled the pacing by clicking to progress. We also believed the spoken nature of the think-aloud might make the interface's demonstration more intuitive to users. Despite all these caveats, evidence that partial verbal redundancy improved performance was not supported. Given the observed individual differences in our think-aloud study, it is possible the effects of audio vary across individuals. Overall, our findings in the context of reading comprehension still echo others from the literature of a continued challenge of implementing this technique. The impact of partial redundancy may be marginal, or difficult to detect depending on the context.

9 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

The deployment of our study on Mechanical Turk suffered attrition, resulting in an imbalance of participants and reduction of power. With the results of our second round, our power analysis suggested our study was able to detect a medium effect, but a larger sample size may have been able to detect additional effects across conditions. Despite loss of power, we believe random assignment was important to the integrity of the study and provided insight into the additional challenges in engaging users in the domain of poetry and, likewise, the Soliloquy interface. While many results remained consistent, there were some observed differences between participants of each round. The negative relationship observed between familiarity and assessment scores was not as apparent in the second round, perhaps due to changes in the worker population or crowdsourcing system that occurred over the gap in time.

The study focused on measuring comprehension and cognitive load, thus does not account for differences regarding a participant's appreciation or attitude towards poetry. Our think-aloud study also suggested that our assessment could have benefited from controlling for variation in the wording of items or capturing participant's second choice. Likewise our assessment consisted of only multiple choice questions. Other methods, such as open ended questions, may have been able to detect more subtle differences across conditions. Assessment of an ill-define domain can be challenging but also an exciting opportunity for future work. We believe new close ended formats to evaluate comprehension of poems as well as automated and peer evaluation of open-ended responses are promising areas for future exploration. Likewise, measuring effects beyond comprehension, such as appreciation and attitude towards poetry, could yield insights in how technology can promote this artform.

Our study was conducted with a single poem, and thus we cannot conclude nor mean to imply these results generalize to other genres and styles of poetry. The studies also were conducted in a single session, over a relatively brief period of time, and thus cannot sufficiently explain effects of longer term use. Additionally, only one set of expert thoughts were used to model reading to learners, varied only by level of detail. We believe both testing Soliloquy on additional poems, evaluating use over longer periods of time, and incorporating multiple versions of expert thoughts are great opportunities for future work.

10 CONCLUSIONS

In this work, we presented Soliloquy , an interface based on the think-aloud that simulates an expert as they read and interpret a poem. Our 5 condition study conducted on Mechanical Turk revealed the importance of detailed scaffolding for demonstrating reading processes of poetry. We found that participants receiving thoughts that not only highlighted important parts of the poem but also provided interpretive details performed significantly higher on the assessment. Surprisingly, our study was not able to find a significant difference between the Soliloquy interface and static text control. Likewise, participants receiving partial verbal redundancy did not score significantly different than those who did not. We followed up with a think-aloud study attempting to identify factors that could explain the lack of differences between Soliloquy and control interfaces. In the study, some users demonstrated how Soliloquy helped by slowing them down, drawing their attention to thoughts, and portioning the amount of information presented. However, other users reported these same features had adverse effects of interrupting their own thoughts or making them feel rushed. These findings imply individual differences may have confounded the advantages of Soliloquy. Likewise our results suggest using step-by-step scaffolding, in line with typical intelligent tutoring methods, may not be appropriate with all domains and all users. We follow up by analyzing relevant evidence from our first study to build on this theory. Our exploratory analysis suggests reported extraneous load played a larger role for the Soliloquy interface. We also investigated the relationship between response time of participants and performance. Our analysis sheds light on the importance of engagement by discovering a productive region of response time with higher performance and lower extraneous load. Given these results, personalization may be an effective next step in building reading scaffolding that adapts to the cognitive habits of learners and better aligns with how they read and process poetry.
