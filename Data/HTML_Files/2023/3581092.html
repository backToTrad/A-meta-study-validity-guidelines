Impact force is common haptic feedback on virtual reality (VR) controllers, such as hitting objects with weapons or rackets. It applies to different points of application of force (PAFs) and directions in varied scenarios. For example, using weapons with different shapes, e.g. , a sword and a pickaxe, using a weapon in different ways, e.g. , stabbing and slashing with a sword, or a ball flying and hitting a racket in different directions cause different PAFs and/or force directions. Therefore, to achieve realistic VR experiences, rendering dynamic PAF and force direction is essential. Although previous works have proposed the concept of dynamic PAF, the PAF is only in a limited space and without dynamic force direction. Therefore, we propose a controller, transPAF, to render omnidirectional impact feedback with dynamic PAF all round the controller for versatile VR scenarios. transPAF consists of a controller, a semicircular track, a linear track, and an impactor, which are all rotatable. The impactor can move to any position in a sphere, which means the whole 3D space all round the controller, and rotate in any direction. Therefore, dynamic PAF and force direction are achieved and independent to each other. We conducted a just-noticeable difference (JND) study to understand users’ distinguishability in position and direction, separately. A VR experience study was further performed to verify that feedback from transPAF with dynamic PAF and force direction enhances the VR experiences and demonstrate some applications for transPAF.

1 INTRODUCTION

Haptic feedback plays an important role in virtual reality (VR) realism. For various haptic feedback, impact force is a common one applying to a VR controller, such as hitting objects with a weapon or racket. Impact force applies to different points of application of force (PAFs) [ 28 ] and in varied directions in different scenarios. For example, due to different shapes of a sword and a pickaxe, where these weapons hit objects are different, which causes varied PAFs. Furthermore, using a weapon in different ways also causes various PAFs and force directions, such as stabbing or slashing with a sword. In addition, when playing tennis, a ball flies from different directions to hit the racket, causing different impact force directions. Therefore, to achieve realistic VR experiences, rendering dynamic PAF and force direction is essential.

Previous works [ 3 , 10 , 11 , 14 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 25 , 26 , 29 , 30 ] leverage motors, air jets, propellers, solenoids and electrical muscle stimulation (EMS) to provide force and impact feedback, but the PAF and force direction on these devices are fixed. Although rendering various force directions is commonly achieved by several works [ 4 , 5 , 6 , 8 , 9 , 21 , 22 , 27 ], dynamic PAF is not considered in these works. To render dynamic PAF, some methods [ 13 , 15 , 31 ] utilize weight-shifting mechanisms to simulate weight change. However, these only use the property of dynamic PAF of gravity, and the force direction is constantly toward the ground, which are limited. Aero-plane [ 7 ] uses propellers to simulate dynamic PAF and generate the sensation of an object moving on a 2D plane. ElastiLinks [ 28 ] first investigates the essential of dynamic PAF in 3D space. However, it focuses on force feedback between controllers, the PAF is only on the surface of a cylinder around the controller, and the force direction depends on the other controller, which limit the position and direction of the dynamic PAF. Therefore, force feedback with dynamic PAF and dynamic force direction is still not accomplished.

In this paper, we propose a controller, transPAF, to render omnidirectional impact feedback with dynamic PAF all round the controller for versatile VR scenarios. transPAF is composed of a controller, a semicircular track, a linear track and an impactor, which are all rotatable. The controller rotates the semicircular track, the linear track moves along the semicircular track, and the impactor moves along the linear track. Therefore, the impactor can move to any position in a sphere around the controller. Such a high degree-of-freedom (DoF) design covers almost the whole 3D space all round the controller. Furthermore, by spinning the linear track and rotating the impactor, the impactor can render omnidirectional impact force. We conducted a just noticeable difference (JND) study to observe users’ impact force distinguishability in position and direction, separately. We further performed a VR experience study to show that the feedback from transPAF significantly enhances the realism and demonstrate some applications for transPAF.

2 RELATED WORK

In this section, we discuss relevant methods which render force feedback, the dynamic force direction and the dynamic point of application of the force (PAF) in VR.

2.1 Force Feedback Methods

To render force feedback in VR, various mechanisms have been proposed by previous works. ExoInterfaces [ 26 ] simulates impact force feedback by pulling the belts using motors worn on the arm. When using a virtual stick to hit or contact with a virtual object, PaCaPa [ 17 ] leverages two servo motors to control the opening angle of the two wings on the controller to render force feedback, and allow users to perceive the object's shape, size and stiffness. When wielding a handheld virtual object, Drag:On [ 30 ] changes the size of two fans using servo motors on the controller to alter air resistance. Therefore, users feel the weight and shape changing of the handheld virtual object or virtual gas streams from the environment. Unident [ 16 ] simulates impact sensations by quickly changing weight and rotational inertia when swinging the handheld controller. Although these methods simulate impact feedback or sensations of hitting virtual objects with a handheld device, the feedback is still different from impact occurring instantly with sharp force increase, as proven in  [ 22 ].

To render impact feedback, Force and Torque Simulation in Virtual Tennis [ 18 ] equip two solenoids on the racket-shaped controller to render instant impact and torque when hitting a ball. AirRacket [ 20 ] is another type of racket devices which generates impact force feedback by utilizing compressed air propulsion jets from two air nozzles on each controller when playing ping-pong, badminton and tennis in VR. In addition, ElasticVR [ 25 ] uses a motor to extend an elastic band to store impact power, and renders instant impact when the band is released. On the other hand, instead of generating impact externally, Impacto [ 10 ] simulates the effect of the impact by combining EMS and a solenoid to stimulate muscles internally and tap the skin externally to render the feeling of hitting or being hit. These works provide various force or impact feedback but their PAF and force direction are fixed.

2.2 Rendering Dynamic Force Direction

To render dynamic force direction for various VR scenarios, several works have commonly achieved the goal. LevioPole [ 14 ] generates different force magnitudes from two propellers at both ends of the rod-like device to render force feedback in the dynamic direction. Wind-Blaster [ 8 ] changes the direction of the two propellers using a servo motor on the wrist-worn device to achieve different force directions. Thor's Hammer [ 6 ] leverages the resultant force generated from six propellers to first accomplish 3D force feedback on a handheld device. Besides propeller propulsion, motor pulling and pushing is another common mechanism. FacePush [ 2 ] utilizes a pulley system to pull two belts by two motors on the HMD to produce normal force and direction force on the face. GamesBond [ 12 ] uses a twisting servo motor and two bending servo motors to rotate and bend each controller, and renders force feedback in different directions between controllers. GuideBand [ 21 ] leverages two motors to move the pulling motor on tracks to pull the wristband in different directions and first achieves 3D force guidance on a wrist-worn device. ELAXO [ 32 ] combines exoskeleton, elastic bands and detachable rings, and changes the rings’ direction to achieve resistive force and rotational resistive force with and without resilience for fingers grasping and twisting. To generate dynamic impact force direction, JetController [ 27 ] generates the composite force from five horizontal and vertical air nozzles to render 3-DoF impact force feedback on the controller. Elastimpact [ 22 ] combines normal and tangential impact using elastic force to provide 2.5D impact force feedback on the HMD. Although these devices render dynamic force direction, their PAF is fixed. Therefore, rendering dynamic PAF and force direction on a handheld device is still not achieved.

2.3 Dynamic Point of Application of Force

For force devices, dynamically changing the PAF(s) to the corresponding position(s) to match the VR scenarios achieves more realistic feedback, as proven in [ 28 ]. Shifty [ 31 ] is a rod-shaped dynamic passive haptic controller. Using a stepper motor to change a weight along the controller, users perceive the illusions of weight- or shape-changing of the virtual objects. Similarly, Transcalibur  [ 15 ] extends the concept to render the illusions of different 2D shapes by changing two weights on the 2D plane of the controller. SWISH [ 13 ] dynamically moves a mass to relocate its center of gravity and simulate the sensations of fluid in the bucket-like device. However, since these leverage the property of gravity to change the center of mass and further alter the PAF, the force direction is limited to constantly toward the ground.

ElastOscillation [ 23 ] leverages six motors with six elastic bands connecting to the proxy to control the movement of the proxy and further change the PAF to generate oscillation force feedback in 1D and 2D movement when shaking the controller using the elastic force, inertia and center of mass. Aero-plane  [ 7 ] utilizes two propellers to generate forces at different magnitudes to change torque and simulate the sensations of weight-shifting on a 2D plane. Based on its results, the simulated PAF difference within 20cm along the controller is difficult to distinguish. ElastiLinks [ 28 ] first investigates the essence of dynamic PAF in 3D space. It focuses on the dynamic PAF of resistive and impact force feedback between the controllers by dynamically moving the PAF to any position on the surface of the curved side of the cylinder around each controller. Interestingly, based on its results, three different PAFs within 20cm along the controller are distinguished in both resistive and impact force feedback. This shows that dynamic PAF is distinguishable and necessary for users and simulated dynamic PAF is different from real dynamic PAF. Although dynamic PAF is achieved in these works, it is either simulated or only in a limited space, a surface, instead of all round the controller in the 3D space. Furthermore, the force direction in these works is either unidirectional or dependent on the other controller. Therefore, rendering force feedback with dynamic PAF and force direction still waits to explore.

3 TRANSPAF

To render impact feedback on a controller with dynamic PAF and force direction to simulate handheld objects with different shapes hitting other objects in different directions, we propose transPAF.

3.1 Design Considerations

To achieve our goal, the following design considerations should be taken into account.

  • Realism : To render realistic feedback, impact should be instantly rendered, which means that sharp force increase is essential, as proven in [ 22 , 25 ]. Therefore, simulating impact feedback using motor pushing or propeller thrusting with delays and gradual force increase is not proper. A jet of airflow, EMS, solenoid and elastic force, as discussed above, are better choices for instant impact. Furthermore, for realistic feedback, the PAF and force direction on the device actually changing to the corresponding position and direction according to the visual feedback is also important. Although users cannot distinguish the simulated PAF difference unless the distance is long [ 7 ], they can easily differentiate the real PAF difference [ 28 ]. Therefore, this shows that dynamic PAF is distinguishable and essential for VR realism.
  • Expressiveness : To render dynamic PAF and force direction, these two factors should be as expressive as possible for versatile VR scenarios. For PAF, to cover every position in the space around the device, a sphere is the most comprehensive and common shape. Therefore, our goal is to render dynamic PAF all round the sphere around the device. For force direction, rendering any direction in the 3D space is the most expressive approach, so generating omnidirectional impact force feedback is our goal. In addition, to accomplish expressiveness, the dynamic PAF and force direction should be independent of each other.
  • Responsiveness : For rendering haptic feedback, delays could cause a mismatch between the visual and haptic feedback and reduce the VR immersion and realism. Therefore, responsive haptic feedback is required. For impact feedback, rendering impact instantly with a shorter power-storing duration is desired. Furthermore, the PAF and force direction in VR could change swiftly. Thus, the device should be able to move or change the PAF and force direction to the target position and angle in time. Besides the hardware actuation time, the motion prediction in software could compensate for the hardware response limitation and make the whole system more responsive.
  • Mobility : For handheld devices in VR, the size, weight and power consumption need to be concerned. The weight of the impact component could affect the whole device a lot. Since it needs to be moved or rotated to different positions or directions, the heavier impact component requires larger and more powerful motors to actuate, which increases the device weight. Furthermore, to move the impact component all round a controller but minimize the device size, the mechanism design for dynamic PAF is crucial. In addition, different approaches rendering impact require various power consumption. The approaches requiring very high power consumption are not appropriate for mobile devices.

3.2 Implementation

transPAF consists of a controller, a semicircular track, a linear track, and an impactor. All components are rotatable. Therefore, by leveraging the combinations of moving and rotating the tracks and the impactor, transPAF achieves dynamic PAF and force direction. The controller is an elliptical cylinder. A DC motor (Pololu Metal Gearmotor with gear ratio 499:1 and a 48 counts per revolution encoder) and a bearing are embedded in the upper and lower ends of the controller, respectively, to rotate the semicircular track. Since we intend to move the impactor to any position all round a sphere for dynamic PAF, the controller rotates the semicircular track on the theta plane for the azimuthal angle in the spherical coordinate system. Technically, the controller can render θ angle range from − 180° to 180°, where − 180° or 180° is the direction toward the wrist and 0° is outward the wrist (or forward) when holding the controller. However, the wrist and forearm block the semicircular track from being rotated to a small range of θ angle. Therefore, based on a pilot, the θ angle range is from − 150° to 150°, which means that a range of 60° cannot be rendered. Furthermore, rendering a precise θ angle in this design requires the controller not to rotate in the hand when the controller rotates the semicircular track. The elliptical cylinder controller design could achieve this, and the direction of the second knuckles or proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joints except the thumb are approximately 0° of θ angle.

The linear track moves along the semicircular track by leveraging a DC motor (Pololu Micro Metal Gearmotor with gear ratio 380:1) with a rotary encoder (Pololu Magnetic Encoder 12 counts per revolution) embedded in it. The linear track moves on the phi plane for the inclination or polar angle in the spherical coordinate system, and the ϕ angle range is from − 90° to 90°, where 90° is vertical upward the controller and 0° is horizontal outward the wrist (or forward). The impactor moves along the linear track using a DC motor (gear ratio 380:1). Although the linear track is 20cm, to prevent the impactor from colliding with the semicircular track when rotating, it moves in a range of 15cm to render omnidirectional impact force, which is similar to the radial distance in the spherical coordinate system. We define the impactor's position on the liner track from the offset of 0 to 150, where the 0 offset is the end of the linear track close to the controller. The rotation and movement of the controller, semicircular track, linear track and impactor are corresponding to the azimuthal angle ( θ ), inclination angle ( ϕ ) and radial distance in the spherical coordinate system. Therefore, transPAF can move the impactor to any position in a sphere around the controller. Although the PAF in the space inside the semicircular track, which is for hand grasping, and a range of 60° on the theta cannot be rendered, such a high DoF design covers almost the whole 3D space all round the controller.

To render omnidirectional impact, the impactor should rotate around two axes, which is similar to rotating on theta and phi planes in the spherical coordinate system. The linear track not only moves along but also spins on the semicircular track using another DC motor (gear ratio 380:1) embedded in it in a range of − 180° to 180°. Furthermore, the linear track is firmly connected to the motor via a metal hub, which prevents the linear track from oscillating when shaking the device. Therefore, the impactor on the linear track rotates correspondingly on the theta plane in the azimuthal ( θ r ) angle. To rotate the impactor on the phi plane, a servo motor (TowerPro SG90 servo) is attached to the DC motor, which moves the impact along the linear track. The impactor rotates in a range of − 90° to 90° in the inclination ( ϕ r ) angle. This mechanism allows the impactor to render omnidirectional impact force. Since the impactor position control, including the θ , ϕ , offset, and direction control, including θ r and ϕ r , are independent, dynamic PAF and force direction are accomplished and the expressiveness design consideration is reached. Since impact force toward the wrist or forearm is common in VR and usually used on VR controllers, we define the initial state of transPAF as the circular track outward the wrist (or forward), the linear track vertical upward the controller, the impactor on the end of the linear track far from the controller and the impactor is toward the wrist. Therefore, the initial state of transPAF ( θ , ϕ , offset, θ r , ϕ r ) are (0°, 90°, 150, 0°, 0°). The form factor of transPAF is like a sword or saber with a hand guard.

For the impactor, since it needs to move and rotate to different positions and directions for dynamic PAF and force direction, and the approaches emitting a jet of airflow require a pipe(s), they are inappropriate choices. We based on and improved the design in [ 24 ] to leverage elastic or spring force to generate instant impact. It consists of a compression spring, motors, rack and pinion mechanisms, a barrel and an impact proxy. The compression spring (SAMINI SWP-A/SUS304-WPB 11-1623, spring constant: 0.98 N/mm) and impact proxy are in the barrel. One end of the spring connects to the end of the barrel, and the other connects to the impact proxy. One rack and pinion mechanism is used to move the impact proxy to compress the spring. The rack is attached to the impact proxy, which is further driven by the pinion using a DC motor (gear ratio 1000:1). The other rack and pinion mechanism, driven by a servo motor (SG90), is used to move the DC motor driving the impact proxy. Therefore, when the DC motor and its pinion are lifted, the pinion disengages the rack of the impact proxy and releases the compressed spring. Impact is then generated when the impact proxy hits the baffle on the other end of the barrel. After that, the DC motor and its pinion are moved back to re-engage and drive to the rack of the impact proxy and compress the spring again to store impact power. By compressing the spring at different distances, the multilevel impact is rendered.

For the impact proxy, we fabricated one made up of PLA and the other with a rubber ball in it, and observed that the PLA one generates strong shaking or oscillation but no directional force can be perceived. The rubber ball one hitting the baffle generates clearer directional impact force due to the deformation of the rubber ball. Although the deformation may reduce impact force, we chose the rubber ball impact proxy in transPAF. Furthermore, since the impactor direction ϕ r is defined as the direction of the impact force, it is the direction of the baffle. Notably, since the impact is generated on the baffle and further delivered to the controller via the linear and semicircular tracks, the shaft of the servo motor connecting the impactor and linear track is placed close to the baffle for efficient impact delivery. Therefore, when the impactor is toward or inward the controller, which means ϕ r = 90°, it actually can move on the linear track for the whole length of 20cm. However, when it is outward the controller, which means ϕ r = −90°, it can move on the linear track for only 15cm. Although elastic or spring force is used to generate authentic impact as in [ 24 ], our design swiftly controlling the rack and pinion engagement does not require the motor to rotate reversely, so it can quickly render instant impact consecutively, which achieves the realism and responsiveness design considerations.

The transPAF prototype without the Vive tracker, including five DC motors and two servo motors, is 328g. The DC motors are controlled by three Dual TB6612FNG motor drivers. The drivers and servo motors are connected to an Arduino Mega board. One of two signal wires of each DC motor's encoder is connected to an interrupt pin of the board. 12V and 6V external power supply are used for DC motors and servo motors, respectively. The weight of the impactor, including the DC motor, servo motor, barrel and impact proxy, is 109g, and its size is 90 × 68 × 45mm. Notably, the range of ( θ , ϕ , offset, θ r , ϕ r ) are (− 150° ∼ 150°, − 90° ∼ 90°, 0 ∼ 150, − 180° ∼ 180°, − 90° ∼ 90°), and since none of these ranges is more than 360°, the cables of the motors and encoders are not entangled. The maximum impact force it can generate is 4.5N. The relationship between the DC motor revolution and impact force magnitude was measured by attaching a force sensor (TAL220 with an HX711 amplifier) and the impactor on an aluminum extrusion frame. Rendering a similar impact force magnitude requires a powerful solenoid, which is much heavier and needs higher power consumption. Therefore, our impactor design achieves the mobility design consideration. We measured the average delay of each factor by video recording the time of moving to the distance or angle of the half of the range, which means that the time of ( θ , ϕ , offset, θ r , ϕ r ) moving to (150°, 90°, 75, 180°, 90°). The average delay of azimuthal angle ( θ ), inclination angle ( ϕ ), offset, azimuthal angle ( θ r ) and inclination angle ( ϕ r ) adjustments are 1840ms, 1950ms, 960ms, 630ms and 330ms, respectively. Furthermore, the power-storing time for the maximum impact force of 4.5N is 1220ms. Since all the adjustments and the power-storing process can be performed simultaneously, the total average delay is 1950ms. In addition, the interval between two consecutive impact stimuli in 4.5N is 1450ms.

4 JUST-NOTICEABLE DIFFERENCE STUDY

To understand the users’ distinguishability in position and direction, we conducted the just-noticeable difference (JND) study, including dynamic PAF and dynamic force direction for position and direction distinguishability, respectively. For dynamic PAF, the PAF movement consists of three factors, including the azimuthal angle ( θ ), inclination angle ( ϕ ) and offset in the spherical coordinate system. Therefore, we examined these three factors, separately, in dynamic PAF. Furthermore, we would like to observe whether dynamic PAF with and without torque affects the distinguishability. By the definition of torque, when the force applies to the controller, only the force component perpendicular to the lever arm generates torque. For the force parallel to the lever arm, users only perceive force without torque. Therefore, by adjusting the impactor direction to form these two conditions, with and without torque, six scenarios from the three factors and two conditions were separately examined in dynamic PAF. For dynamic force direction, two factors determining the impactor direction, including the azimuthal angle ( θ r ) and inclination angle ( ϕ r ) in the spherical coordinate system were tested, separately.

4.1 Participants and Apparatus

The transPAF device was held by the dominant hand of participants, and a Vive Pro head-mounted display (HMD) and noise-canceling earbuds were worn. The HMD was used to block participants’ visual feedback from the real world and render selection items. The earbuds played white noise to cancel noise from the device. Furthermore, a Vive controller was held by the other hand to select the answers to the trials. 12 participants (4 female, all right-handed) aged 20-29 (mean: 25.33) were recruited.

4.2 Task and Procedure

A two-down, one-up staircase design was used in the JND study to observe users’ distinguishability in dynamic PAF with and without torque and dynamic force direction, separately. Three stimuli were rendered in a trial, including two reference stimuli ( S ) and a test stimulus ( S ± △ S ). Since impact force magnitude distinguishability was not the goal in this JND study, which has been explored in previous works [ 24 , 28 ], we chose the maximum impact force magnitude, 4.5N, from the device to render the stimuli. S represented the position or direction in dynamic PAF and force direction, respectively. △ S was an adaptively determined positive number meaning the position or direction difference between the reference and test stimuli in dynamic PAF and force direction, respectively. Participants had to choose which stimulus was different from the others using the controller to select the item. The order of the stimuli was randomized. If participants answered correctly two consecutive times or incorrectly once, △ S was decreased or increased by a step size, respectively. After the first reversal, which means a change from decreasing to increasing △ S , and vice versa, the step size was decreased. Each staircase run ended after a total of five reversals, and the average of the last three reversals was calculated as the threshold based on a pilot.

For dynamic PAF, the azimuthal angle ( θ ), inclination angle ( ϕ ) and offset were examined, separately. For the azimuthal angle ( θ ), its range was − 150° to 150°, so S was 0° and initial △ S was 75° (50% of the △ S range). Furthermore, the first step size was 30°, and the second step size after the first reversal was 10°. In addition, if △ S was less than or equal to 0 or exceeded 150°, beyond the △ S range, the system considered it as a reversal. For the inclination angle ( ϕ ), its range was from − 90° to 90°, so S was 0° and initial △ S was 40° (44% of the △ S range). Its first step size was 25° and the second step size was 15°. If △ S was less than or equal to 0 or exceeded 90°, beyond the △ S range, the system regarded it as a reversal. For the offset, its range was from 0 to 150 for omnidirectional impact force although the impactor could be in the linear track for the whole 20cm in ϕ r = 90°, as mentioned above. Furthermore, the reference stimulus ( S ) was 0 offset and the test stimulus was defined as ( S + △ S ) instead of ( S ± △ S ), so initial △ S was 75 offset (50% of the △ S range). Such a design provided a sufficient offset range to observe the distinguishability from a pilot. The first step size was 20 offset and the second one was 10 offset. Furthermore, if △ S was less than or equal to 0 or exceeded 150 offset, the system determined it as a reversal. These study design settings were for both dynamic PAF with and without torque.

For dynamic force direction, the azimuthal angle ( θ r ) and inclination angle ( ϕ r ) were examined, separately. For the azimuthal angle ( θ r ), its range was from − 180° to 180°, so S was 0° and initial △ S was 90° (50% of the △ S range). The first step size was 30° and the second one was 10°. Furthermore, if △ S was less than 0 or exceeded 180°, beyond the △ S range, the system considered it as a reversal. For the inclination angle ( ϕ r ), its range was from − 90° to 90°, so S was 0° and initial △ S was 40° (44% of the △ S range). The first step size was 25° and the second one was 15°. In addition, if △ S was less than or equal to 0 or exceeded 90°, beyond the △ S range, the system regarded it as a reversal.

In the beginning, the experiment briefly introduced the device and the procedure of the experiment. Participants wore the HMD and held the controller for selection with the non-dominant hand. They were sitting on a chair and their elbow or even forearm of the dominant hand could lay on the armrest of the chair to reduce fatigue. Furthermore, the forearm was approximately parallel to the armrest and the wrist stayed natural without extension or flexion, so the hand was outward the body, staying forward, and the azimuthal angle range, − 150° ≤ θ ≤ 150°, could be achieved. Otherwise, we had tried that if the hand stayed forward in the center line of the body, the wrist must perform extension, which altered the azimuthal angle range. Although this situation could happen in some using scenarios, we utilized the maximal and symmetric azimuthal angle range to observe distinguishability in the JND study. To prevent the weight shifting and motor actuation from affecting participants’ perception or giving them hints to distinguish, the experimenter held the device during the PAF and force direction adjustment. After that, the experimenter put it in participants’ hand and confirmed that they held it correctly. They then perceived the impact stimulus, and gave it back to the experimenter for the adjustment of the next stimulus. After perceiving all three stimuli in a trial, they used the controller to select the answer or could play back the stimuli.

For dynamic PAF, when each factor was examined, the other two factors were randomly assigned to a position or direction for each staircase run. Notably, when randomly assigning the inclination angle ( ϕ ), its range was from − 80° to 80° instead of from − 90° to 90° to prevent the linear track from being straight downward ( ϕ = −90°) or upward ( ϕ = 90°) the controller and canceling the effect of the azimuthal angle ( θ ) factor. Since we examined the distinguishability of dynamic PAF and force direction, separately, the azimuthal angle ( θ r ) and inclination angle ( ϕ r ) for impact direction were not randomized. For dynamic PAF without torque, θ r = 0° and ϕ r = 90°, so the impact was toward the controller without torque. For dynamic PAF with torque, θ r = 0° or 180° and ϕ r = 0°, so the impact was perpendicular to the linear track, and the maximal torque was generated. Similarly, for force direction, when one factor was tested, the other was randomized for each staircase run. However, when randomly assigning the inclination angle ( ϕ r ), its range was from − 80° to 80° instead of from − 90° to 90° to prevent the impactor from being straight outward ( ϕ r = −90°) or toward/inward ( ϕ r = 90°) the controller and canceling the effect of the azimuthal angle ( θ r ) factor. Furthermore, the azimuthal angle ( θ ) and inclination angle ( ϕ ) and offset were set to their initial state, 0°, 90° and 150 offset, respectively, since dynamic PAF and force direction were tested, separately.

There were three factors, the azimuthal angle ( θ ) and inclination angle ( ϕ ) and offset, in both dynamic PAF with and without torque, and two factors, the azimuthal angle ( θ r ) and inclination angle ( ϕ r ), in direction force. Therefore, a total of 8 staircase runs were examined in this study. The three and two factors were counterbalanced in dynamic PAF and direction force, respectively. The order of dynamic PAF with and without torque was also counterbalanced. The dynamic PAF and direction force were examined orderly. Participants were interviewed after the experiment. The study took about two and a half hours.

4.3 Results and Discussion

The results of the JND study are shown in Figure 4 . Notably, in dynamic PAF, since the range, unit, and scale among the factors ( θ , ϕ , offset) are different, and we used the spherical coordinate system instead of the normalized physical PAF distance in the JND study, so the results of the three factors are shown in different plots. Repeated measures ANOVA and Bonferroni correction for post-hoc pairwise tests were leveraged for the analyses. No significant main effect is found between with and without torque in any of the three factors of PAF, azimuthal angle ( θ ) ( F 1, 11 = 0.11, p = 0.74), inclination angle ( ϕ ) ( F 1, 11 = 1.42, p = 0.26), and offset ( F 1, 11 = 3.51, p = 0.09). This is roughly consistent with the participants’ comments. Half of the participants ( P2 , P3 , P5 , P6 , P7 , P8 ) supposed that it was easier to distinguish PAF in the conditions with torque. P3 , P6 , and P8 mentioned that they perceived stronger force in the conditions with torque, which was helpful for them to distinguish the stimuli. P5 commented that “I could only feel vibrations in stimuli without torque. There was no difference for stimuli in different positions.” However, four participants ( P4 , P9 , P11 , P12 ) thought it was easier to differentiate PAF in the conditions without torque. They said that they felt the position differences more clearly in stimuli without torque. Interestingly, P4 suggested that stimuli with torque became vibrations on the controller, which confused him/her to distinguish the position. This comment was in contrast with the opinion of P5 . The other two participants said that the factor with or without torque did not affect their distinguishability. Although there is no statistical significance, the results show that the distinguishability in conditions with torque is slightly better than that in conditions without torque.

4.3.1 Dynamic PAF. Among the three factors of dynamic PAF, seven participants ( P4 , P6 , P7 , P9 , P10 , P11 , P12 ) subjectively believed that the stimuli in azimuthal angle ( θ ) were the easiest to distinguish. P4 , P6 , P11 and P12 further mentioned that after impact stimuli were rendered, there were vibrations at different sides of the controller, such as the left, right, or center of the controller, which helped them to distinguish the differences. This also happened in the inclination angle ( ϕ ), but the vibrations were at the upper, middle, and lower positions of the controller. Regarding the offset factor, some participants ( P2 , P3 , P5 , P8 ) mentioned that there was a stimulus with relatively weak force magnitude among the three stimuli, which became a hint for them to differentiate among the stimuli in both conditions with and without torque. Furthermore, P1 also perceived this only in the condition with torque. Since the test stimulus was farther than the reference stimuli in offset, we could obtain that the farther PAF generates the weaker impact feedback to the hand. Interestingly, it was different from your assumption that in the condition with torque, the farther impact generated stronger impact feedback since it had a longer lever arm and then generated larger torque.

4.3.2 Dynamic Force Direction. For dynamic force direction, most participants except P1 , P2 , P6 and P9 agreed that the stimuli in inclination angle ( ϕ r ) were more easier to distinguish than those in azimuthal angle ( θ r ). In the azimuthal angle ( θ r ), P1 , P6 , and P9 mentioned that they could roughly perceive the direction of impact stimuli in forward, backward, leftward, or rightward. P2 thought that it was only a slight difference in distinguishing stimuli in θ r and ϕ r , but it was still easier to stimuli in ϕ r . In the inclination angle ( ϕ r ), P4 , P6 , P8 , P10 , P12 said that although they could distinguish whether the impact direction was in horizontal ( ϕ r = 0°), they could not distinguish that the direction of non-horizontal impact was downward ( ϕ r > 0°) or upward ( ϕ r < 0°). This might result from that participants could distinguish the torque differences caused by different impact directions. The phenomenon was also observed in ElastiLinks [ 28 ]. Besides, most participants except for P5 and P6 generally supposed that they could more clearly distinguish stimuli with different PAFs than those with different force directions. They thought that impact stimuli with a long distance between them for different PAFs were easier to distinguish than stimuli applying to the same position while with different force directions, which means that the displacement difference is more apparent than the direction difference. P4 and P12 commented that “I felt impact stimuli applying to different areas in dynamic PAF, which is easier to distinguish. However, I only felt impact stimuli applying to the same point in dynamic force direction, which is difficult to distinguish.” .

4.3.3 Guidelines. Based on the results and comments of the JND study, we summarize and propose three guidelines for future works even when our device is not utilized. (1) Design of virtual objects. If designers intend to allow users to perceive the PAF and/or force direction differences among several virtual objects in a VR scene, then the designed PAF and/or force direction differences ( θ , ϕ , offset, θ r , ϕ r ) on haptic devices in the real world must be set beyond their respective JND thresholds. (2) Reducing latency. In real-time interactive VR applications, always changing the PAF and force direction to the corresponding visual feedback could cause latency. Based on the JND results, we know that users cannot distinguish PAF and force direction ( θ , ϕ , offset, θ r , ϕ r ) beneath their respective JND thresholds, which means that always rendering authentic PAF and force direction is unnecessary. Therefore, haptic devices could only change PAF and force direction only when those are beyond the JND thresholds to reduce the latency. (3) Reducing range. In force direction, although our device can render omnidirectional impact force, the JND results show that participants could not distinguish that non-horizontal impact stimuli in ϕ r , which means downward ( ϕ r > 0°) or upward ( ϕ r < 0°) impact stimuli. Therefore, this phenomenon could be used to reduce the range of ϕ r , which could also reduce the latency due to the smaller range. These guidelines could be adopted in future dynamic PAF and force direction devices.

5 VR EXPERIENCE STUDY

We conducted the VR study to verify the feedback with dynamic PAF and force direction from transPAF enhances users’ VR experiences.

5.1 Participants and Apparatus

The transPAF device with a Vive tracker attached to it was used in this study. A Vive HMD was worn and the headphones on the HMD played the sound effects. 12 participants (7 female, all right-handed) aged 20-32 (mean: 24.67) were recruited. Two of them have participated in the JND study, and the interval between the two studies is more than 10 days.

5.2 Task and Procedure

We built a VR scene for the study. Three methods, including impact with dynamic force direction and fixed PAF (D), impact with dynamic PAF and fixed force direction (P) and impact with dynamic PAF and force direction from transPAF (T), were compared. For all three methods, participants held the transPAF device to perceive impact feedback. In (D), the impactor's position was fixed in the initial position but its direction dynamically changed corresponding to the scenarios. In (P), the impactor dynamically moved to the corresponding position as in (T), but it was fixed in the direction always toward the frontal plane of the controller. The direction is commonly used in devices with fixed force direction and is proper for many scenarios. Notably, since moving the impactor could also alter its direction due to the change of θ and ϕ , θ r and ϕ r actually needed to change correspondingly to achieve the fixed force direction in (P). Furthermore, although the impactor direction was corresponding to the scenarios, θ r and ϕ r in (D) were different from those in (T). By comparing (D) and (T), we could observe whether the feedback with dynamic PAF enhances the VR experiences. By comparing (P) and (T), we can understand whether the feedback dynamic force direction affects the VR experiences.

Participants used four weapons, including a sword, a pickaxe, a hook and a dagger, and a tennis racket in the VR scene to attack monsters and hit balls, respectively. For the four weapons, since their shapes were different, the position and direction of the weapons hitting or attacking monsters were different. Therefore, the PAF and/or force direction changed correspondingly in (D), (P) and (T). For the sword, there were two ways to use it, slashing and stabbing. For slashing with the sword, according to the position of the blade of the sword hitting monsters, the PAF could be in three positions. Therefore, from the tip to the handle of the sword, the PAF and force direction ( θ , ϕ , offset, θ r , ϕ r ) were (0°, 90°, 150, 0°, 0°), (0°, 90°, 50, 0°, 0°) and (0°, 90°, -50, 0°, 0°). There were 100 offset between two positions, which was based on the results of the JND study that PAFs with torque with over 95.56 offset (9.56cm) are distinguishable. Furthermore, since the impactor direction ( θ r , ϕ r ) were (0°, 0°) in this scenario, the impactor could move from -50 to 150 offset on the whole linear track. For stabbing with the sword, the tip of the sword hit monsters, so the PAF and force direction ( θ , ϕ , offset, θ r , ϕ r ) were (0°, 90°, 150, 0°, 90°).

For the pickaxe, since it was in the ‘T’ shape, the PAF and force direction ( θ , ϕ , offset, θ r , ϕ r ) were (0°, 30°, 150, 0°, 0°). The ϕ angle difference between the sword and pickaxe was 60°, which was based on the results of the JND study. For the hook, since it was curved, the tip was inward and on the left side of the handle when it was held with a right hand. Its PAF and force direction ( θ , ϕ , offset, θ r , ϕ r ) were (− 90°, 45°, 150, 0°, − 45°). For the dagger, it was like a small sword or knife held with the reverse grip, and it could only be used to stab monsters. Therefore, its PAF and force direction ( θ , ϕ , offset, θ r , ϕ r ) were (0°, − 90°, 50, 0°, 90°). Notably, we followed the design of virtual object guideline of the JND study to design these four weapons, so the PAF and force direction among the weapons could be distinguished by participants. For the racket, when a ball hit the racket at the sweet spot in the forehand and backhand, the PAF and force direction ( θ , ϕ , offset, θ r , ϕ r ) were (0°, 90°, 70, 90°, 0°) and (0°, 90°, 70, − 90°, 0°), respectively. Three turrets in the VR scene shot tennis balls to the racket, so the racket might hit balls in different directions. Therefore, the impactor direction was adjusted correspondingly. In most of these scenarios, the impact force magnitude was 4.5N. However, when using the dagger and slower balls were shot, the impact force was 3N with the power-storing time of 790ms.

In the experiment, when switching to a weapon or racket, the PAF and force direction could be changed correspondingly before using the weapon or racket. Therefore, although the average delay of the device adjustment is 1950ms as mentioned above, it only affected the real-time experience little. Furthermore, the impactor could also store impact power in advance. However, the PAF and force direction needed to be adjusted dynamically during using the weapon or racket in some conditions, including determining the way to use the sword, where the sword slashed the monster and which direction the ball hit the racket. Although the average delay of changing the PAF in three positions for slashing with the sword is 1080ms, the average delay of switching between slashing and stabbing is 330ms, which only needed to adjust ϕ r , and the average delay of changing the force direction of the ball hitting the racket is 630ms, which only required to adjust θ r and ϕ r simultaneously, some prediction approaches were leveraged to reduce the delays in these adjustments when monsters or balls were close to participants within a threshold distance. To determine the way to use the sword, the angle between the sword and the vector from the sword tip to the monster was calculated. If the angle was not over a threshold of 35°, the impactor moved and rotated to the position and direction for stabbing. Otherwise, participants were going to slash with the sword. To determine which of the three positions on the sword to slash the monster, the monster moving speed and the distance between the monster and the participant were used to calculate the timing of the impactor moving from one position to another. This is based on the reducing latency guideline of the JND study to reduce the latency of moving the PAF offset. To determine the direction of the ball hitting the racket, the angle between the racket face's normal vector and the vector of the ball flying was calculated. Based on the results of these prediction approaches, the impactor changed to the corresponding position and direction in advance. When the weapons or racket contacted monsters or balls, the impactor only needed to render impact. These prediction approaches achieve the responsiveness design consideration. Furthermore, more advanced prediction methods as in Haptic Retargeting [ 1 ] could be leveraged in future works.

After the experimenters briefly introduced the device and tasks, participants were standing in the VR scene and the holding transPAF device in the dominant hand. The four weapons, a sword, a pickaxe, a hook and a dagger, were experienced orderly. Monsters were generated one at a time in a random position. After participants attacked and knocked down a monster, another was generated. They could attack with a weapon as many monsters as they desired to experience the feedback, and then they switched to the next weapon. Since they could use the sword to slash in three positions and stab monsters, they were asked to experience all these conditions. After experiencing all weapons, they switched to the racket and hit balls randomly shot from the turrets. Balls were shot one at a time. They could hit as many balls as they desired. The three methods were counterbalanced in the experiment. After the experiment, they were asked to fill a 7-point Likert scale, allowing for decimal points, and were interviewed for some feedback. The study took about one hour.

5.3 Results and Discussion

The results are shown in  Figure 7 and  Figure 8 . A Friedman test and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction for post-hoc pairwise tests were utilized for the analyses. Significant main effects are reveled in realism ( χ 2 (2) = 7.87, p = 0.02) and preference ( χ 2 (2) = 10.33, p = 0.006), but no significant main effect is found in distinguishability ( χ 2 (2) = 4.16, p = 0.125) and enjoyment ( χ 2 (2) = 4.23, p = 0.12). The post-hoc Wilcoxon pairwise tests show that significant differences are between (D, T) in realism, and (D, T) and (P, T) in preference. In addition, to further understand the experiences for each of the four weapons and the racket from the three methods, we investigated and analyzed the realism factor for the weapons and racket in deeper. The two ways of using the sword, slashing and stabbing, were compared, separately. For slashing with the sword and the hook, there is no statistical significance among the methods. For stabbing with the sword and racket, the feedback from (T) is significantly more realistic than that from (P). For the pickaxe, (T) significantly outperforms (D) in realism. For the dagger, the feedback from (T) is significantly more realistic than that from (D) and (P).

5.3.1 Slashing with the sword. For slashing with the sword, 7 participants ( P1 , P2 , P3 , P6 , P8 , P9 , P10 ) supposed that when slashing monsters with different parts of the blade of the sword, they could perceive the difference in (T), which means that they could distinguish the change of the PAF. In addition to the PAF, they also felt the reaction force direction opposite to the wielding direction, which was consistent with their expectation. On the other hand, although the PAF did not change in (D), P1 , P2 , P3 and P9 still claimed that they felt different PAFs in (D). This could be an illusion caused by the visual feedback in VR. Since the feedback from (P) and (T) were the same in slashing with the sword, they had similar scores in realism. However, there was no statistical significance between (D) and (T) in realism either although the PAF in (D) did not change. We observed that the score in (D) was a bit lower than that in (T) and (P), but was generally higher than that in the methods with inconsistent PAF or force direction in other conditions. This could indicate that in the condition with force feedback rendering corresponding force type and direction to the visual feedback but having inconsistent PAF only in offset, the realism was still quite good enough for participants.

5.3.2 Stabbing with the sword. For stabbing with the sword, most participants except for P6 and P10 responded that they could feel the force directions difference in (T), compared to slashing with the sword. When stabbing forward, they felt instant impact force backward to the sword. However, although the force direction did not change in (P), 6 participants ( P1 , P4 , P5 , P6 , P7 , P11 ) felt that the impact force was consistent with the reaction force of stabbing. The illusions in slashing and stabbing show the influence of the visual feedback. Interestingly, P3 perceived the force from the side of the sword in (P), which was consistent with neither the virtual feedback nor the force feedback. Since the feedback from (D) and (T) were the same in stabbing with the sword, statistical significance is only between (P) and (T), which is consistent to the comments.

5.3.3 Pickaxe. For the pickaxe, half participants ( P1 , P3 , P4 , P8 , P11 , P12 ) they perceived that the PAF in the pickaxe was different from that in the sword in (T) but not in (D). They mentioned that when the tip of the pickaxe hit monsters, they could feel that the impact feedback occurred at the tip of the pickaxe in (T), which matched the visual feedback and was consistent with their expectations. Thus, they supposed that the feedback from (T) was very realistic. Interestingly, since the two sides of the head of the pickaxe we chose in the VR scene was not completely symmetric, P1 , P3 , P4 , P11 and P12 reported that they felt the center of mass in (T) consistent with the pickaxe, which further enhanced the realism. However, the center of mass in (D) was weird to them. P1 further commented that “When I held the device (D), I thought that its shape did not feel like the pickaxe. When I wielded and rotated it, I did not feel that the center of mass or inertia changed as I expected.” . Although they did not see the device, they still supposed that they felt its shape due to the center of mass or inertia, which proves that transPAF also achieves the sensations of the shape or inertia as in Transcalibur [ 15 ]. Furthermore, only P3 and P6 perceived that the force direction in (P) and (T) were different. The feedback from (T) is significantly more realistic than that from (D), which is consistent with the comments. Furthermore, since (T) and (P) had the same PAF, rendered the same shape and inertia sensation, which was appreciated by participants, and the force direction difference between them was only 60° in phi r , which was less than the JND result of 76.25°, no statistical significance between them is reasonable and consistent to the JND study result.

5.3.4 Hook. For the hook, P1 , P9 and P11 felt the PAF difference between (D) and (T). They mention that the PAF in (T) was on the tip of the hook but the PAF in (D) was on the handle side of the hook. P11 commented that “The position of the force feedback was not correct. I expected the feedback to be in the front-left of my hand, but it was closer to the right side, which made me feel like holding the sword instead of the hook.” . P1 , P3 , P7 perceived the force direction difference between (P) and (T). They thought that the reaction force was in the opposite direction to the way they hooked in (T). However, they sensed force direction in (P) was not as expected, which made them feel unrealistic. Furthermore, P2 and P3 reported that when using the hook, the feedback should be the sensation of dragging instead of instant impact feedback. This could be the reason that the realism score of all methods in the hook were generally lower than that in other conditions, and there is no statistical significance among them although the score in (T) was still the highest.

5.3.5 Dagger. For the dagger, half participants ( P1 , P2 , P3 , P5 , P6 , P7 ) could feel the PAF difference between (D) and (T). They expected the impact force position was beneath the hand as in (T), but the feedback position in (D) was above the hand. Half participants ( P2 , P3 , P4 , P7 , P11 , P12 ) could feel the force direction difference between (P) and (T), and the impact direction in (T) was consistent to their expectations. Most participants except for P8 agreed that the weight distribution of (P) and (T) was more realistic than that of (D). P6 , P7 , P9 and P12 further perceived that the impact force of the dagger was weaker than that of the other weapons. For (T), P12 commented that “The force position, force direction and the weight distribution of the device were all as I expected. It just felt like holding a real dagger, so I supposed that it was the most realistic one of the weapons.” . For (P), P4 commented that “I felt that I was pushed by the blade of the dagger when I stabbed with it.” . Therefore, the feedback from (T) is significantly more realistic than that from the others.

5.3.6 Tennis racket. For the tennis racket, 7 participants ( P1 , P3 , P4 , P5 , P6 , P9 , P12 ) could feel the force direction difference between (P) and (T). They noticed that the force direction changed corresponding to the direction of hitting balls in (T), which was consistent with their expectations, but not in (P). In (P), they felt the same force direction even though the direction of balls flying and/or the racket swinging were different, which reduced the realism. All participants felt the different impact force levels, which was consistent to the speed of balls. Interestingly, only P5 responded that s/he felt the PAF difference between (D) and (T) since s/he usually played tennis, and s/he supposed that the PAF in (T) was the better sweet spot for the virtual racket. Furthermore, although the PAF and/or force direction were different among the three methods, most participants except for P4 , P10 and 11 said that holding the device in all the three modes felt like holding a real tennis racket. P1 commented that “Tennis was the funnest scenario. It reminded me when playing tennis in Wii. Compared with the Wii controller, swinging the transPAF device felt like swinging a real tennis racket, which enhanced the realism.” . The feedback from (T) is significantly more realistic than that from (P). However, there is no statistical significance between (D) and (T) since their PAF difference was 80 offset, which was smaller than the JND result of 95.55 offset and also smaller than the difference between (D) and (T) in slashing the sword. Therefore, the similar reason also affected participants in the tennis racket, and the result is consistent with the JND result.

Although some participants commented that they sometimes felt the cables hanging down from the controller, this did not affect their experience since the rendered impact feedback was much more apparent. Based on the results and comments of (D), (P) and (T), we observed that although a significant difference is only between (D) and (T) in overall realism, after deeper investigating and analyzing, the study verified that the feedback from (T) was significantly more realistic than that from (D) and (P) in different situations. Therefore, the feedback with both dynamic PAF and dynamic force direction from transPAF enhances users’ VR experience in appropriate situations. In addition, we observed that participants were easily affected or deceived by the visual illusion although the force direction did not change in stabbing with the sword in (P). Furthermore, the JND results show that it is easier to distinguish PAF than the force direction, and the VR study results (Figure 7 and 8 ) show that although there is no statistical significance between (D) and (T), the result of Dynamic PAF is better than Dynamic force direction. Therefore, we suppose that PAF has a stronger impact than the force direction on VR realism. Besides, transPAF changing the PAF also alters the center of the mass and causes the proper sensation of shape and inertia in some conditions, which also enhances the realism.

6 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

Although the feedback from transPAF was generally appreciated by participants in the studies, the current transPAF prototype still has some limitations. Although in the VR study no participants mentioned that they perceived delays, the latency problem still existed. Although the average latency of changing PAF and force direction of 1950ms is longer than the power-storing time of the maximum impact force of 1220ms, the latency of changing PAF and force direction usually happens in mode changing before rendering feedback, e.g. , switching weapons, which affects users little. Furthermore, using some visual effects during weapon or racket switching, such as the scaling animation, transparency transformation or the smoke mask as in  [ 31 ], could further reduce the delay issue in the future. However, the power-storing time could limit the consecutive impact scenarios. Although our power-storing time is much shorter than previous impact methods using elastic force [ 22 , 24 ], it is still longer than methods using solenoids or EMS. Furthermore, although the weight of the current prototype is only 328g, the impactor 109g moving to different positions could increase the weight sensation due to torque. The lighter impactor design could solve this problem in the future. In addition, since the weight modules of Transcalibur [ 15 ] are 72g, we believe that reducing the weight does not affect the benefit of the weight-shifting sensation improving realism in some scenarios.

Besides, the impact is generated by the impact proxy hitting the baffle and it is further delivered to the controller. However, compared to the impact directly applying to the linear track, the current design for omnidirectional impact force might lose some impact power in the delivery, which could reduce the effect of the impact feedback. An advanced omnidirectional impactor design could solve this in the future. The three guidelines of the JND study could also be used in future device design, and the reducing range guideline could further be used to simplify the device. Besides VR gaming scenarios, we also envision that transPAF could be leveraged in VR training scenarios. For example, in a VR factory, when users learn to use different tools, such as a hammer, a chisel, a wrench, and a nail gun, transPAF renders force feedback in the corresponding position, direction and force level, which could not only enhance VR realism but also deliver experts’ haptic sensation tips in the future.

7 CONCLUSION

We propose a controller, transPAF, to render omnidirectional impact feedback with dynamic PAF all round the controller. We conducted a JND study to understand the distinguishability in the azimuthal angle ( θ ), inclination angle ( ϕ ) and offset for PAF with and without torque, separately, and in azimuthal angle ( θ r ) and inclination angle ( ϕ r ) for force direction, separately. Based on the results, we performed a VR study to verify that the feedback with dynamic PAF and force direction significantly enhances users’ VR experience. Based on the participants’ comments, transPAF further achieves the sensation of shape and inertia, as in [ 15 ].
