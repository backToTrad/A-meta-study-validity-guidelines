Living with Light Touch
An Autoethnography of a Simple Communication Device in Long-Term Use

William Gaver , Interaction Research Studio, Northumbria University, United Kingdom, w.gaver@northumbria.ac.uk
Frances Gaver , Park Lane, United States, fgaver@comcast.net

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3580807
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

We are a mother and son who have been using a pair of simple, self-build communication devices to maintain a feeling of connection while separated by over 5,000 miles. The devices, called Light Touch, only allow us to send one another slowly-fading, coloured lights, yet we have been surprised by how much our ongoing interaction with them means to us. This paper contributes an autoethnographical account of our experiences over the last two years, including our initial experiences with the devices, and focusing on various aspects of our day-to-day use. Based on our observations, we discuss the features that have proven important in mediating our feelings of connection. We point out, however, that their success is contingent on our context of use and the nature of our bond, and suggest that simple systems like Light Touch may support emotional communication, but only if they are well-matched to settings and relationships.

1 INTRODUCTION

My daughter and I return in the evening from a weekend away and, after saying hello to the rest of our family, I go upstairs to my bedroom office to check my schedule for the following day. As I sit down at the desk, I reach out casually and send my mother a quick pulse of light. Then, after a moment's thought, I change the colour to bright pink to reflect the heat wave we're having and send it again. An hour or so later I receive an email from my mother: “Glad to see you're home.” The subject line is “Let there be light!” A little later I notice that she has sent me a yellow-green light.

We are a son and his mother who is in her 90’s, living 5,000 miles apart and separated by an 8-hour time difference. Since October 2020, we have been using a pair of internet-connected devices called Light Touch to keep in contact with each other. The devices offer only a very minimal form of communication – essentially, we can send a coloured light to one another which fades over a few hours. Despite their simplicity, over more than two years of near-continual use we have been surprised by how important the Light Touches have become to us. In this paper – told largely in the first author's voice with occasional additions by the second – we reflect on our lived experiences with the Light Touches to describe the value we have found in using them and the features that seem to make them effective.

1.1 Positionality and Method

It is important to note at the outset that I (the first author) co-led the team that developed Light Touch [ 24 ]. This may lead to bias in how my mother and I describe and even experience them. For instance, my involvement increased the likelihood of our trying the Light Touches in the first place, if only because I knew about them. Conversely, my relationship with my mother may well have influenced the design of Light Touch, since I had my mother in mind throughout its development. My experience was that any bias towards success faded quickly, however. Once I started using Light Touches at home with my mother, I stopped thinking about them in research terms and experienced them simply as a way to keep in contact with her. If they had not worked well for this, I cannot imagine that we would have persisted for so long.

Because Light Touch was a device that I helped develop, and which we wanted in our everyday lives, our account could be seen as an example of autobiographical design [ 16 , 17 , 50 , 55 ]. As we will describe below, however, Light Touch is designed to be made by its users, so our experiences of designing, constructing, installing, modifying and troubleshooting our devices were of a kind with the experiences that any of its users might be expected to have. Therefore, it is more fruitful to think of this as a retrospective autoethnography , similar to others which have appeared in the HCI literature [ 35 , 38 ]. We discuss this approach in the next section, before describing Light Touch and other emotional communication devices in more detail in the following sections.

1.2 Autoethnography

Used in disciplines ranging from Anthropology and Sociology to Theology and Religious Studies [ 5 ], autoethnography involves a combination of self-observation and reflexive analysis [ 52 ], usually to gain insights about social or cultural issues. Within HCI, autoethnography has been used to investigate topics including living without a mobile phone [ 49 ], non-routine mobile device use [ 56 ], using drones with young children [ 21 ] and supporting student sleep habits [ 47 ] among many others. Of greater relevance for this paper, it has also been used to reflect on the experience of using designs intended to support long-distance relationships, including a robotic pet [ 12 ], bespoke devices [ 11 ] and low-tech strategies [ 6 ], and we will discuss some of these findings later.

Autoethnographies vary in in their methodological and epistemological commitments and thus the forms they take. For instance, in their influential introduction to the approach (which, tellingly, is written largely as a dialogue between the authors), Ellis and Bochner [ 20 ] write that:

Autoethnography is an autobiographical genre of writing and research that displays multiple layers of consciousness, connecting the personal to the cultural. Back and forth autoethnographers gaze, first through an ethnographic wide-angle lens, focusing outwards on social and cultural aspects of their personal experience; then, they look inward, exposing a vulnerable self that is moved by and may move through, refract and resist cultural interpretations. -p. 739

Their account emphasises the creation of often intensely personal narratives that resonate with social issues, rather than explicit analyses or theory formation.

Anderson [ 2 ], in contrast, argues that focusing on this “evocative” form of autoethnography risks “eclipsing other visions of what autoethnography can be and of obscuring the ways in which it may fit productively in other traditions of social inquiry”. He proposes an alternative “analytic ethnography”, explaining that:

The purpose of analytic ethnography is not simply to document personal experience, to provide an “insider's perspective,” or to evoke emotional resonance with the reader. Rather, the defining characteristic of analytic social science is to use empirical data to gain insight into some broader set of social phenomena than those provided by the data themselves. -pp. 386-387

From his point of view, personal accounts should be augmented with “a broad set of data-transcending practices that are directed toward theoretical development, refinement, and extension”.

The dichotomy between these approaches is not unequivocal, however. Maréchal [ 52 ], for instance, suggests that “evocative” autoethnographies are not a uniform category. She describes a postmodern/poststructuralist tradition which is “a blurred genre of methodological creative practices, texts and autobiographical performances that [are]…. mostly evocative rather than expressive” and which clearly contrast with Anderson's [ 2 ] analytical approach. This is distinguished from a subjective/experiential tradition, however, which “has no explicit analytic commitment to generalization” though it is concerned with “the connection of the autobiographical and personal to the cultural and social”, a stance that might be congruent with approaches such as ethnomethodology [ 23 ]. In general, as autoethnography has been adopted by a wide range of methodological and epistemological traditions, approaches to going beyond personal accounts to draw broader lessons are likely to be equally varied.

1.2.1 Our approach. In this paper, we largely follow an analytical autoethnographic approach, seeking to explicitly draw out insights and lessons after we describe our personal observations of living with Light Touch. There are two main caveats, however.

First, autoethnographies in the social sciences seek to draw out lessons about social matters – as Adams et. al [ 1 ] points out, “ethno” refers to “cultural texts, experiences, beliefs, and practices”. While some HCI autoethnographies (e.g. [ 34 , 58 ]) arguably focus primarily on personal and social experiences, we are not primarily interested here in analysing our experiences with Light Touch to interrogate the exact nature of our relationship or family dynamics in general. Instead, and like many other HCI autoethnographies (e.g., [ 17 , 21 , 56 ]) we focus more on understanding how the features of a design (the Light Touch, in this case) shapes and reflects that relationship. From this point of view, we could argue that our account (and many others in HCI) might better be understood as an auto tech nography, after Kien [ 44 ], though we will stick with autoethnography for consistency with prior work.

Second, we do not see our discussion as leading towards “theoretical development, refinement, and extension” [ 2 ], but instead as contributing to intermediate-level theories [ 48 ] by annotating [ 8 , 26 ] both our account and the Light Touch. Like the “evocative” autoethnographers, who “approach cultural communities inductively, allowing observations to guide what they write, that is, their ‘findings’” [ 1 ], we allow the issues and insights we discuss to emerge rather than focusing on detailed, a priori research questions [ 27 ]. In part, this is again congruent with the kinds of ethnomethodologically influenced ethnographies that have commonly been used in assessing technological field deployments [ 7 , 14 ]; equally, this is because our account is a retrospective autoethnography [ 35 , 38 , 49 ], in which we reflect on our experiences, mostly after-the-fact, of a system that we did not originally intend to be part of a research study.

While most of our account is based on memory and our ongoing experiences (we are still using Light Touch as I write), we draw on other sources as well. First, I recorded the initial Zoom meeting in which my mother and I met to unpack her Light Touch, activate it, and check that it connected properly to mine, and we use excerpts from the transcript in this paper. Second, our emails show numerous, brief references to our experiences with Light Touch and problems we encountered, and we quote some of those here too. Finally, at my request my mother has written her own reports of using Light Touch, which we include in relevant sections of this paper. In combining both our voices in this way, our account can also be seen as akin to duo- and trioethnographies reported in the HCI literature [ 13 , 22 , 35 ].

The strength of the approach we take here is that we have direct access to our lived experiences with and reflections about Light Touch—we contribute a first-person [ 50 ] view rather than relying on third-person observations or descriptive quantitative data (e.g., statistics of use). Our account is limited, however, by its small sample size (n=2), the possibility of bias in our reporting, or more simply the potential that we have forgotten or misremembered events. We hope our contributions outweigh these constraints.

To support our account, we describe the Light Touch in the next section, discuss how the device came to be, and contextualise it in a landscape of other emotional communication devices. Following that is an extended section recounting our experiences with Light Touch over the last two years.

1.3 LightTouch

Figure 1 shows the Light Touch design we use. We each have one of the devices, and they are connected via the internet to send lights to each other. There are two possible options for interaction: selecting a colour and then sending it to the connected device. When lights are sent, they automatically fade over about six hours.

  • To select a colour, the capacitive touch button is pressed and held. This causes the locally-controlled LED to light at maximum brightness and then cycle continuously through the colour spectrum (red, orange, yellow, green etc.). Releasing the button selects the currently chosen colour.
  • To send the currently selected colour to my mother, I tap on the capacitive touch button on the top of my device. This causes both my locally-controlled LED and her device's remotely-controlled LED to pulse at full brightness and then remain lit at a slightly lower level. She can send a light in return by tapping the capacitive touch button on her device, causing her locally- and my remotely-controlled LEDs to light. The effect is that on each device, one light is controlled by my mother, and one by me.
  • Lights fade after they have been sent. When lights are first triggered, they glow at full power and then slowly fade in brightness over about six hours.

Light Touch is thus a very simple, self-contained emotional communication device that allows my mother and me to exchange simple signals of our presence and attention.

1.3.1 Context and Design Rationale. Light Touch is one of a collection of four emotional communication devices developed in response to the COVID-19 lockdown imposed in late March 2020 by the UK government [ 24 ]. It was clear that lockdown would bring problems of loneliness and isolation as the population was directed to stay at home except for ‘essential purposes’ [ 4 ]. For instance, I became painfully aware that I would be unable to visit my mother for the foreseeable future.

Like many others, our research group turned to online platforms supporting email, messaging and video conferencing to stay in touch. Previous research on remote collaboration (e.g. [ 19 , 28 , 32 ]), however, indicated that most commercially available systems emphasise focused communications, and that systems promoting peripheral and emotional awareness could also be valuable. We were also aware of some, though by no means all, of the previous attempts to design for emotional communications reviewed in the next section. So when we became aware of the “UKRI Agile Research and Innovation Response to COVID-19” call for proposals, we proposed designing a series of emotional communication devices that could be made widely available for people to make themselves.

The call emphasised that proposals concerning the social effects of the pandemic were as important as research investigating medical and economic concerns. More extraordinarily, it strongly emphasised projects that would make an impact rather than the originality of research. Thus, as we set out to develop devices, we were less concerned that they be novel than we were that they be effective.

In addition, our designs were conditioned by the intention to release them as self-build products that the general public could make themselves by following online instructions to assemble electronics purchased online, install bespoke software from the project website, and construct enclosures from household materials (similar to, e.g. [ 25 ]). As we explored possibilities, our technical team responded by developing a technical infrastructure, based on the Espressif ESP32 development board, which made it relatively simple to link inputs and remote outputs via WIFI, and this further constrained the design space we explored. In its released form, then, Light Touch is available as a self-build product, designed for even inexperienced makers to assemble themselves. The electronics that provide the devices’ core functionality can be housed in a wide variety of ways. The project website offers instructions for making three different housings, and people have made many alternatives.

The basic idea for Light Touch was suggested early in the project in a brief message circulated amongst our team that outlined its basic features (remotely triggering coloured lights that would fade over time). The team did not explicitly analyse its strengths and weaknesses or discuss its influences. Instead, the studio's well-established design culture allowed the idea to be appreciated and accepted tacitly and quickly. Nonetheless, the design was clearly influenced by previous research on emotional communication devices, which we briefly review below.

1.4 Emotional communication devices

Light Touch is not a particularly novel design, but an example of an emotional communication device intended to support personal relationships over a distance. Growing from early experiences with Media Spaces [ 30 , 62 ] and telecommunications-based art (e.g. [ 3 , 59 ]), this genre of device is aimed at supporting personal relatedness, usually in domestic settings, instead of or in addition to more explicit and instrumental forms of communication.

The range of designs for, and approaches to, emotional communication is vast. Since Strong's [ 61 ] seminal publication of three experience prototypes designed to support connection between long-distance lovers and friends, a myriad of proposals and implemented systems for emotional communication have appeared in the literature (e.g. [ 9 , 10 , 11 , 33 , 36 , 39 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 53 , 63 , 65 ]). These encompass widely disparate approaches, including for instance connected picture frames with icons showing information about partners’ activities [ 18 , 60 ], pneumatic garments designed to transmit hugs [ 54 , 64 ] and systems meant to mediate emotional awareness via architectural ambience [ 29 , 37 ] .

Making sense of such an eclectic design space is not easy, but two reviews help. Hassenzahl et al. [ 31 ] suggest six underlying strategies of awareness, expressivity, physicalness, gift giving, joint action, and memories, based on a review of 143 published artefacts. Augmented picture frames, from this point of view, are aimed at supporting awareness and memory, while pneumatic garments emphasise physicalness and gift giving. Li et al. [ 46 ], in contrast, organise a corpus of 52 “unconventional interfaces for emotional communication” according to device features, showing for instance that a majority of designs use touch input and graphic output, with phone/tablet input and haptic output also being popular approaches. These categorisations are useful in giving a sense of the claims made by designers about what is necessary, or at least desirable, to create feelings of relatedness. Often these claims are implicit, but sometimes they are an articulated as part of a design rationale. For instance, Chien et al. [ 11 ] argue that awareness is insufficient to maintain relationships, and instead explore a series of designs meant to promote mutual caring via responsive interactions, often based on existing shared activities. Similarly, Rooney [ 57 ] argues that designs are liable to “fragment” intimacy, and calls for “a more holistic approach” to supporting relationships that are “complex and delicately balanced”. Our experiences with Light Touch may help inform these discussions.

In addition, Light Touch can be seen as representative of numerous variations of proposals and prototypes for sending lights remotely (e.g. [ 33 , 41 , 63 ]) including commercial ones [ 15 , 51 ]. For instance, in 2012 Deschamps-Sonsino released the Good Night Lamp, a design she developed at the Interaction Design Institute at Ivrea, as a commercial product [ 15 ]. Although it received positive critical attention, it was discontinued in 2019. More recently, shortly after Light Touch was released as a self-build project, a variety of “friendship lamps”, very similar to Light Touch, were released as commercial products (e.g. [ 51 ])—a fact we became aware of only after the original Light Touch publication [ 24 ]. We are not aware of any long-term descriptions of using products such as these, and the experiences we recount here may be relevant for understanding how similar systems may fare in the long run.

1.4.1 Field Trials of Emotional Communication Devices. Apart from helping to make sense of design strategies for emotional communication, both [ 31 ] and [ 46 ] point out that there have been few long-term studies of emotional communication devices used in their intended settings. For instance, Hassenzahl et al. [ 31 ] found that only about a quarter of the systems they reviewed had been studied in the field, and that field trials lasted an average of six weeks with almost half lasting under two weeks. Similarly, Li et al. [ 46 ] found that fewer than a quarter of the 52 designs they reviewed had been field tested, and that only 10 had been tested for over a week.

Two exceptions are worth noting here. First, Kaye et al. [ 40 , 41 ] report field trials of both onscreen and physical “intimate objects”—devices that, when activated, cause an onscreen circle or LED to light and then fade slowly over hours on a remotely connected device. Their studies had the virtue of involving participants who were already in long-distance relationships, and though their field trials only lasted a week, they revealed how the devices could “leverage an enormous amount of social, cultural and emotional capital” so that participants could find meaning in the simple, “one-bit” information the systems conveyed. Their design of Intimate Objects, and particularly the use of fading, was a direct inspiration for Light Touch.

Second, Chien et al. [ 11 , 12 ] provide first-person accounts of using a series of systems to sustain the long-distance relationship between a researcher in Germany and his girlfriend in Taiwan. The devices—ranging from a robotic pet that seemingly travels between two boxes [ 12 ] to a set of robotic arms for switching on each other's tea kettles [ 11 ]—were focused on supporting “mutual caring”. Based on the couple's existing interactions, the designs were more specific than the abstract signals of attention provided by Kaye et al.’s [ 40 , 41 ] intimate objects or the Light Touch. The couple lived with the devices over varying periods up to almost two years [ 11 , 12 ]. During this time, the researcher recorded his experiences (with contributions from his girlfriend, similar to my mother's here) to produce well-observed and insightful autoethnographical accounts, ultimately drawing conclusions about “the importance of going beyond mere abstracted presence toward enabling everyday responsive interaction among the couple.”

Given this context, our account of using Light Touch contributes to the relatively few reports of long-term engagement with systems that support emotional communication. As a first-person report of using a device that is almost as simple as Kaye et al.’s [ 40 , 41 ] over a period of time comparable to Chien et al.’s [ 11 , 12 ] it complements both and contributes moreover to discussions about approaches to designing emotional communications devices. Should they be crafted to reflect the nature of people's existing interactions, or can simpler strategies succeed by leveraging an “enormous amount of social, cultural and emotional capital” as Kaye et al. [ 41 ] suggests?

2 LIVING WITH LIGHT TOUCH

In this section we describe our experiences with Light Touch, both current and remembered. Our account starts with our first experiences using Light Touch, including building and deploying them as well as more or less serious problems we encountered in the first few weeks of use. In the second, main section we discuss our everyday use of the system for almost two years, focusing on what using Light Touch is like as well as features that we think are particularly important. Finally, we describe another, more recent problem, and the speculations it led to about how our use might end.

2.1 First Experiences

As Lee et al. [ 45 ] write, “unboxing… and initial interaction in general, is a critical stage of user experience”. This is when first impressions are formed and expectations are set. For me, it started when I set out to construct the devices my mother and I would share. For my mother, it started when she received her Light Touch in the post, unboxed it, and we worked together to link the two devices and started to settle them in our homes, as we describe below.

2.1.1 Making Light Touch. I made both of our Light Touches in my home, using the online instructions (see yoyomachines.io) to assemble the hardware and install the software.

When it came to constructing the enclosures, however, I didn't use any of the examples from the website. Instead, I devised my own by cutting both ends off of a can of mixed nuts, covering it in brown paper, and wedging a circle of white foamboard roughly halfway inside it. This provided two compartments, one for the electronics to be hidden, and the other for the display. The two LEDs – one for the light controlled by my mother, the other for the light I control – were pushed through the foamboard to protrude into the display compartment. Finally, I mounted a small circular mirror over the LEDs, both to hide them from direct view and to reflect the illumination into and around the container (see Figure 1 ). I also mounted a small metal washer on the side of the container, wired to the microprocessor within to serve as a capacitive touch sensor, and glued a short segment of dowel to the opposite side to serve as a stand for the device.

I was proud of the design I achieved, and happy that I had not merely emulated one from the project website. Of course, using reversed mirrors to reflect lights back onto a surface has been done before, but I liked the way the lights mixed together inside the enclosure, and the way that the paper-wrapped cylinder was neat but unpretentious. It felt like making a gift for my mother, and I hoped she would like it too.

In building the devices, I also took extra steps to make sure they would survive the long trip to my mother. For instance, an internal breadboard is used to connect components, and I used jumper wires specially made to mount flat on its surface to avoid tangles. I also used a hot glue gun to fix the appropriate wires securely to the LEDs. As we will describe, this did not entirely prevent problems later, but it probably helped reduce them.

Once both devices were done and checked, I packed my mother's in a cardboard box with a great deal of padding and sent it to her by parcel post.

2.1.2 Unboxing and Establishing Connection. The package I sent did not provide the easy and satisfying unboxing experience afforded by many commercial products. Instead, it took persistence, a kitchen knife and plenty of ripped cardboard to extricate the Light Touch. She wrote an email to me reading “ persistence pays! I finally got it open ” with an attached picture (Figure 2 ), which I took to be a gentle reproof for thinking more about protecting the Light Touch than her ability to open it

Setting up the devices was not entirely unproblematic. When it is plugged in, the Light Touch flashes to show that it has established a WiFI connection, but my mother didn't know that and so failed to notice whether it did. Neither of us realised that she was trying to touch the wrong part to trigger the light until she held it on camera for me to see. Throughout, it was difficult to tell whether things were working until we both showed our devices onscreen.

Watching the video, I sound increasingly tense as I coach my mother. I felt that this was a crucial moment. If our Light Touches did not connect as easily as the instructions led me to believe, I anticipated having to talk my mother step by step through an unfamiliar process of rebooting the devices, entering WIFI credentials and swapping device codes. I wasn't sure we could do this successfully even if my mother was willing. The stakes seemed high—either the devices connected easily, or we might never even try them at all.

Fortunately, from our first attempt at the beginning of the transcript to the success at the end took just under three minutes, though it seemed much longer to me. This was the result of considered software design [ 24 ]. For instance, the devices’ interface allowed me to pair them via their IDs and enter my mother's WIFI credentials when I made them, so that hers was appropriately configured when it arrived at her home. Despite the initial confusion, and my worries, we managed to get our Light Touches working remarkably easily.

2.1.3 WiFi Problems. Over the next couple of weeks, our experience was a mixture of delight as we traded lights and frustration as we encountered a number of problems. Our emails included numerous asides about how enjoyable it was to see each other's lights. For instance, about a week after we started, my mother wrote:

Subject: Light Touch

I'm so glad it is working. Really fun...and I love it when I get up in the night and see that you have said Good morning.

These were interspersed with other messages discussing problems, however. In the first days and weeks, my mother's Light Touch repeatedly dropped its WiFi connection (I'm still not sure if the problem is her router or the device). When this happens, a LED on the device's microprocessor lights red to indicate the problem. This was tucked in the covered rear of the enclosure, however, and so not readily visible without dismantling the device. Instead, the only indication that WiFi had been dropped is that both lights went off and it was impossible to trigger, change or send the local light. Once we realised this, my mother learned to un- and replug the USB power cable from the wall-mounted charger, causing the system to reboot and automatically reconnect to her WiFi.

As we started using the devices, then, dropped WiFi was the subject of frequent email conversation, and our remarks about seeing one another's lights took the form of reassurances that our Light Touches were working. Before long, however, the “dropped WiFi” problem faded into an occasional occurrence that my mother and I knew how to fix. Even though our WiFi connections still drop out occasionally, and we sometimes lose internet altogether, we know how to diagnose the problem and recover independently and seldom mention it to each other anymore.

2.1.4 Potential disaster. Soon, however, a more serious problem arose, which my mother describes:

When Bill sent the Light Touch to me carefully wrapped in tissue and in a sturdy box, it was a sleek seamless cylinder with a stainless-steel circle on top and a little wooden “foot” to rest on. Truly a work of art. And it worked beautifully.

Before long, however, the back came off and the interior working parts were revealed. So far, no problem. I managed to put the back on again and it still worked. This happened several times, but still no problem when I reattached the back. I told Bill I thought there was a design flaw (only a mother would have to nerve to accuse a Professor of Design of such an error). I complained that the cord connecting to the power source was half-way up the side and it was pulling the back off.

Before long a major problem occurred. The back came off again and when the “guts” spilled out on the cabinet where it was resting a disconnected wire was revealed! I called Bill for help and with me showing a picture on my iPhone and talking to Bill via Facetime on my iPad, he diagnosed the problem and instructed me to insert the loose wire in the hole designated D3. I managed to do that (no easy task for arthritic fingers) and all was well.

The next time was different; the pesky wire was now detached from the top of the device (see Figure 4 ) . It was supposed to go through a minute hole in the top and be held in place by the steel circle. This was the “switch” that made the connection somehow through the wi-fi in my apartment in California allowing the signal to reach miraculously to Bill's Wi-Fi in London. Reattaching this part of the wire was the biggest challenge of all.

After dealing with several detachments of the problem wire I resorted to brute force and applied Scotch tape to hold the circle [capacitive touch switch] firmly over the wire. More Scotch tape held the back in place. No longer the pristine, elegant work of art.

Battle scarred and with the power cord taped securely to the back of the cabinet by packaging tape, my Light Touch continues to work beautifully!

My mother was right: mounting the power cable on the side of the Light Touch made it unstable. Moreover, it tended to pull off the lid serving as the back of the device, spilling the electronics out and eventually leading to the “pesky wire” becoming detached.

From my perspective, this was a disaster. I was very doubtful that my mother would be willing or able to fix the device. On one side, the “pesky wire” would need to be inserted into a very small hole on a miniature breadboard, positioned to align with a tiny label on the microprocessor that I found difficult to discern even with reading glasses. On the other, it needed to be forced through a tiny hole in the enclosure to make contact with the metal ring that served as the capacitive touch switch. I would only be able to help via video. I anticipated having to build and configure a completely new Light Touch to send to my mother.

To my surprise, my mother insisted on undertaking the repair, using a complex assemblage of technologies to help. She arranged a large magnifying glass and reading lamp so she could see the electronics, and sent me a series of pictures via her phone while we talked over video. With patience, persistence, and a bit of humour, we managed to work together to repair the device.

In part, her persistence seemed strong evidence for how compelling she had already found using the Light Touch to be. It also reflected pleasure in being able to work together to solve the problem: as she emailed the next day: “Thank you, Bill. I love it when we can accomplish things together…. I especially appreciate all of you treating me like a “real person” and not just an old lady in a facility!”

As she worked to stabilise the device, my mother's Light Touch became increasingly swaddled in cellophane tape (see Figure 5 ). At first, I was unhappy to see her repairs disfiguring the “pristine” device I had made her. But things stopped failing, or falling apart, and her Light Touch became reliable enough to use with few real problems. Ultimately, I realised that her liberal applications of tape had significantly improved the original design.

2.2 Everyday life with Light Touch

For me, the “pesky wire catastrophe” was the end of the beginning and our use of Light Touch stabilised afterwards. We became increasingly confident that they were working as intended and referred to them less often in our emails. We continued using them regularly nonetheless, usually sending each other lights several times a day. In this section, we describe our experiences of everyday living with the Light Touches, after first describing details of their settings to contextualise our observations.

2.2.1 The Physical Settings. My mother's Light Touch is on a cupboard in her living/dining room, in a central position where it can be seen while eating, reading, or watching television. She lives by herself in a 3-room apartment in a senior living residence, so apart from occasional visitors is usually the only person to see or interact with the device. She chose the location immediately upon receiving the device and has never reported wanting to move it.

I set up my Light Touch in the home office that I established at the beginning of lockdown, a part of the bedroom that I share with my wife that is distinct by virtue of being down a few steps and thus separated by height (see Figure 6 ). I positioned the Light Touch on the back corner of my desk as soon as I made it, and similarly to my mother have not been tempted to move it since, despite occasional speculations about where else it might go (the mantlepiece? My bedside table?).

Light Touch is very sensitive to ambient light. The two multicolour LEDs appear quite bright in low lighting, but when the ambient light is brighter, they are overwhelmed to the point that it can be difficult to see whether they are on. This does not affect my mother's Light Touch very much because it is situated deep inside her apartment, away the sunlight that comes in from her garden windows. In contrast, my ‘office’ is very small and has windows on two sides, with the result that light fills the space on sunny days. When this happens, Light Touch's colours become difficult to see (and less attractive), to the point that sometimes I have to look closely to see whether she has sent me a light.

My office is widely recognised in my family to be “my” space, so the Light Touch is seen as “mine” too, and my wife and children show little or no inclination to interact with it the way they might were it situated in, say, our living room. On reflection, this suits me well as I always wanted the devices to support my personal connection with my mother, rather than a more general family one. My wife and kids sent the occasional light out of curiosity after I first installed the Light Touch but have generally seemed content to ignore it, and I have never heard them express any particular jealousy or unhappiness about my having a connection with their mother-in-law / grandmother that they don't share.

2.2.2 Checking Lights. I tend to glance at the Light Touch when I enter my office and periodically while there, almost reflexively the way I check a clock or the view out a window. Looking at Light Touch is more than a matter of seeing whether it is on or off. Instead, I find myself assessing with a glance which combination of colours are lit, roughly how long ago my mother has sent hers, and whether I need to refresh mine. On the other hand, if I haven't looked very recently I usually can't remember whether the colours have changed since the last time I checked.

There is nothing particularly analytical in the way I appreciate the state of my Light Touch. I almost always get a momentary lift from seeing a fresh light that she has sent. In addition, the particular combination of two colours, each likely to have faded to different degrees, serves as a kind of gestalt implying a kind of mood to our relationship as expressed by the Light Touch. Sometimes it seems a bit tired and faded, indicating that we're both busy or distracted in one way or another. Other times it seems bright and vibrant, because we have both triggered our lights recently. It's impossible to read too much into the current Light Touch illumination because there are so many factors involved, but it seems impossible to read nothing into it at all.

2.2.3 Sending Lights. I usually send my mother a light four or five times a day, though this varies widely depending on my other activities. I almost always send her a light when I get up in the morning, and again when going to bed (and, according to my mother's report, she does the same). Apart from this, my transmissions are shaped by a slight preference to refresh my light periodically, and a feeling that there's no point in sending lights too often. Overriding this, however, is the fact that my transmissions are almost entirely opportunistic: for instance, I will often go out of my way to trigger a light if I am in the bedroom for another purpose, but don't recall ever going to our bedroom for the sole purpose of sending a light.

Sending a light without changing the colour feels lazy to me, like a perfunctory nod with no real commitment behind it, so I almost always select a colour before sending. My strategy for selecting colour has stabilised over time to reflect a combination of time of day, weather and mood. For instance, I almost never send blue except in the evening as it seems a quiet, peaceful colour, whereas in the morning I might send a pink or orange light to suggest that the sun is rising and I am gathering energy for the day ahead. On the other hand, during a recent hot spell I sent pinks, oranges and yellows to reflect the heat. But I also might send yellow to indicate an upset mood, since yellow is made by a combination of red and green LED light, and all three colours can be seen simultaneously and somewhat discordantly. Midday often seems green.

My mother, in contrast, reports that the most important factor in her colour selections is picking a colour that looks good with whatever I have sent. This makes sense because any colour selection is almost always made in the context of the other colour being displayed, and the colours will mix to more or less harmonious effect. Some combinations are quite lovely (blue and green, for instance, or yellow and pink) whereas others are not (combining yellow and blue, for instance, tends to produce a queasy mix of all the colours). Our differing strategies thus set up a kind of dialogue, with my attention to context and mood suggesting a new colour, and her responding with one that harmonises. However, as with the timing of our transmissions, though we both have (usually tacit) strategies for our colour selections, they are neither consistent nor easily interpretable – a fact that has never seemed to bother either of us, since neither of us seek to interpret the lights closely.

2.2.4 Seeing Lights When They're Sent. There is a special pleasure in seeing a light pulse the moment it has been sent. My mother wrote about “the closeness I feel if I happen to be looking at the right moment and I see the light at almost the same instant it is sent. Then it feels almost like a physical touch across some 8000 miles of space.” I agree: for me, realising that she has touched her device to send the light the very same moment that I am looking at mine creates a sense of immediacy that is almost tangible. For a moment it seems like our two Light Touches are one, or as if my mother's Light Touch is a ghostly mirror of my own, physically attached to it. 5000 miles collapse to nothing,

When this happens, we almost always respond to each other immediately, and spend the next minute or so exchanging lights – reciprocating is important enough to me that I'll do this even if I'm in an online meeting or otherwise busy. On the other hand, trading lights in this way has never resulted in our phoning each other or seeking more involved contact, though we have mentioned these moments in later emails. Simply exchanging these gestures, together in realtime, is rewarding enough for us – like waving to each other or exchanging a few brief pleasantries.

It seems to me that these exchanges retain their piquancy because they are rare. Both of us need to be near our respective Light Touches, one of us needs to trigger a light, and the other needs to notice it. Given that we only send a few lights a day, and that we are in different time zones, this does not happen frequently. From my point of view, this is fortunate, because seeing each other's lights being sent more regularly might seem humdrum and make responding feel more like a duty than a treat.

2.2.5 Both Lights Off. It is rare to encounter our Light Touch when both lights are off. When we do, it is a bit sad. The devices seem dull and ordinary when unlit, and it is difficult not to surmise that our relationship has lost its spark too.

There are two main reasons for unlit Light Touches. First, one of the devices may have dropped its WiFi connection. When this happens, both lights on both devices turn off, and it is impossible to turn on a light locally until the partner device is online and the connection between them restored. Given the value we both find in keeping the Light Touches lit, this provides incentive to re-establish WiFi connectivity quickly.

The second reason our Light Touches are sometimes unlit is because we both have failed to refresh our lights. This almost always happens when both of us have been away from our homes for an extended time. Sometimes this happens unexpectedly, but usually we are aware when the other is travelling. When we return to find our Light Touch completely unlit, the first reaction is usually to send a new light. Curiously, even when I know my mother is away from home, and that's why her light is off, I continue to refresh mine to keep both lit.

The sadness of an unlit Light Touch seems to have led us to a largely unspoken agreement that we should always keep the devices lit. This is something we remarked upon occasionally when we first started using them, for instance comparing Light Touch to a plant we both wanted to keep alive. It is now all but unimaginable to me that I would willingly let our Light Touches remain unlit.

2.2.6 Contextual Effects. Beyond the features of the Light Touches themselves, and their locations in our homes, several other factors influence the way we engage with and experience them. As I have described, the device's light sensitivity means that I am less likely to notice whether it is lit on sunny days than on cloudy ones, or in the morning or evening, and this makes me less likely to interact with it.

In addition, the fact that we live eight time zones apart means that a light sent at a given point in our daily schedule (e.g. mid-afternoon) will inevitably be received at a different stage of our partner's (early morning, for instance). Mostly, this doesn't matter very much, since we are primarily concerned to keep the lights refreshed periodically as an indication that we are thinking of one another. Nonetheless, using Light Touch seems to have made us more continuously aware of what time it is “on the other side”. For instance, if she receives a light during dinner, she is likely to (and has) comment that I am up extremely late. The slow fade of Light Touch has made it easy to accommodate the time difference between us because it still ‘works’ whenever we send lights. Nonetheless, thinking about the time there , when sending a light here , helps to evoke a sense of connection with her life and activities now .

There are also seasonal effects on how I engage with Light Touch. Not only are my interactions affected by bright summer light, but I am less likely to be in my office when the light and heat makes working there uncomfortable. This means that, in summer, I am much more likely to engage with Light Touch in the morning and late afternoon onward than during the middle of the day. During winter, in contrast, I am more likely to be in my office for longer periods throughout the day.

Finally, our use of Light Touch is affected by variations in daily routine. We started using the devices at the end of October 2020, on the day that the second COVID-19 lockdown was announced in the UK, and for months afterwards movement was limited. Even when greater freedom became appropriate, I continued to work from home (a pattern that persists as I write this). Continuous availability to engage with Light Touch has given way to a more varied rhythm for both my mother and me over the last nine months or so, however. My mother is out of her apartment to visit friends, go shopping, etc., much more frequently than she was, and I am also away from home – or working in other parts of my home – to a much greater extent than I had been.

Changes to our usual patterns of exchanging lights worry me at times. After all, my mother is in her 90s and thus I worry about her health and wellbeing. At the same time, I know that she might be busy, or that there might be a problem with her Light Touch, and I don't want her to worry that I am overly concerned. So, if a lack of lights on her part prompts me to email her, I am circumspect:

B: I haven't seen your light so much lately, is everything working okay?

F: My light works fine. I have been out for long lunches several times this week so maybe haven't been here at best time send light to you. Always glad to see yours and answer when I'm here.

(emails exchanged 26 August 2022)

It should be no surprise that Light Touch use is disrupted when routines change to make encounters with it less frequent. What is more interesting is the possibilities I project onto those ambiguous disruptions. Moreover, it is surprising is that the disruption hasn't been worse. For despite our increased activities away from home, we persist in using the Light Touches to send one another lights several times a day.

2.3 Another disaster and intimations of impermanence

We have been using Light Touch for more than two years at the time of writing, for the most part without substantial difficulty beyond occasional dropped WiFi connections. Recently, however, I returned to my desk to find that our recently acquired kitten had knocked my Light Touch off my desk, detaching the mirror that reflects the lights back into the enclosure (see Figure 1 ).

This mirror is attached to the device by a bolt pushed through the foamboard that bisects the enclosure (see Section 1.3) and screws into a nut that is hot-glued to the mirror itself. In the fall, the nut itself had detached from the mirror and so would need to be reattached. I knew this to be an easy job, merely requiring me to fetch the hot-glue gun from my tool cupboard and make the repair, but I was preoccupied with other activities and so put it off.

Several days passed. The device was still functional and could send and receive lights, though without the mirror the lights did not mix in the enclosure, so the appearance was not aesthetically compelling. I became accustomed to the compromised Light Touch, however, and still didn't fix it.

Finally, I did repair the Light Touch, and the job was as simple as anticipated. But the episode made me aware of just how fragile our ongoing use of Light Touch is. I could easily have continued using the broken version, and if the damage had been worse, I might never have repaired it. Equally, while my mother routinely reconnects her device when it drops WiFi, if anything more serious happened we might not choose again to undertake the kind of work we did when her device first broke down. Sometimes it seems like our use of Light Touch could just slip away.

On reading a draft of this section, however, my mother commented: “ Speak for yourself. Bill. I would choose to do whatever I could to keep our light communication working! ” I find this reassuring. Not only does it reinvigorate my own determination to keep open this link to my mother, but it makes me realise that abandoning Light Touch would have to be done by mutual consent—and I don't think we will ever agree to this.

3 DISCUSSION

Up to now, our account has focused on our lived experiences using the Light Touch in our everyday lives. We have tried to convey the excitement of setting them up, the dismay of having one break, and the determination with which we worked to fix it. Most of all, we have tried to share what it's like to use Light Touch on a day-to-day basis – how and when we send lights, what different light configurations mean to us, and how our use interleaves and changes with our other activities.

Now we step back and reflect on our experiences, and what they mean for designing for emotional communications more broadly, in the hopes that our insights might inform other designer/authors. Even after two years, it can seem odd to use coloured lights to communicate with my mother, and even stranger to find that we both value it so much. Why is it so important to see her light in the morning, or to send her one before I go to bed?

3.1 Features of Light Touch that contribute to success

In our experience a number of the Light Touch's features combine to support my mother's and my long-term engagement:

  • Simultaneity and intentionality . The near-instantaneous transmission of light across the devices creates a powerful feeling of connection across space when we see it. The fact that we know the other person has intentionally triggered the light is important in making this a meaningful act of care. The fact that we only rarely see signals at the moment they are sent make them all the more special.
  • Slow fade. Because the lights fade slowly over hours, we don't need to be present (or awake) when lights are sent and can also get a rough sense of how long ago a light was triggered. This allows for exchanges to be very slow without the device appearing neglected and is also crucial to our use across multiple time zones and in locations that we occupy regularly but not continuously.
  • Eventual extinction. The fact that the lights go out eventually is important in motivating our continual use. We find an unlit Light Touch to be ugly and sad, and this may be the reason that we both persist in sending lights even when we know the other one of us is away and won't see them – it is nicer to see Light Touch lit than not.
  • Semisynchronous communication . The fading light shares features with both synchronous and asynchronous communication media. Like synchronous communication, it is immediate and transitory, encouraging a reply before it disappears. Similar to asynchronous communication, however, the lights persist long enough for us to encounter them hours after they have been sent.
  • Controllable colours . Being able to set the colour to be sent offers a level of expressive range that is enough to be satisfying without presenting too many choices. Colour is evocative enough to suggest expressive or aesthetic intent, without being specific enough to become burdensome or constraining.
  • Multiple lights . Separate send and receive lights mean that we each control one light, so can differentiate what colours we have sent, and how long ago, from the lights sent by the other. This serves to confirm locally that a signal has been sent, and also avoids confusion about who last sent a light or selected the current colour that might occur if we both controlled a single light on each device.
  • Locally rewarding. The combination of controllable colours with multiple lights presents new opportunities for expression as we come up with harmonious or even discordant combinations. The mixture of coloured lights is aesthetically attractive on its own: Light Touch is simultaneously a locally present display as well as a medium of communication. The congruence of the reward offered by the device itself with the appeal of staying in touch seems important in reinforcing the experience.
  • Self-built. Making Light Touch myself allowed me to customise the form, leading to as feeling of ownership on my part and casting it as a personal gift to my mother. Moreover, it led to a familiarity with its construction that eases debugging and repairs. My involvement in the device's design may also have contributed here, though as described in the Introduction this does not seem to have led to significantly different experiences or insights than other maker/users might have.

It might seem tempting to conclude that this combination of technical and interface features make Light Touch a success, or to undertake a point-by-point comparison to other, similar systems (e.g. [ 15 , 33 , 40 , 51 , 63 ]). We believe that such a claim or analysis would be misleading, however, because it would overemphasise the role of these features in our successful use of the system.

3.2 Fit with context and relationship

What has become clear to us through the detailed of using Light Touch is that the success of the features above, and of the Light Touch more generally, is also entirely contingent on the context in which we use it and the nature of our relationship. We have already touched on some of the ways that our context of use affects our use of Light Touch earlier and summarise and briefly expand on these here:

  • Fading is crucial to us because we are separated by an 8-hour time difference. It is also important because we use Light Touch in rooms that we do not inhabit continuously.
  • My mother's apartment is small enough that she encounters her Light Touch regularly. For me, the fact that my Light Touch is in my bedroom office means that I am almost guaranteed to encounter it in the mornings and evening. In each case, the setting of the device means we are likely to encounter it before the lights fade completely.
  • My mother's Light Touch is placed away from her windows, and though mine is closer to windows, London is often overcast. If our rooms were more brightly illuminated, the devices’ lights would be overwhelmed and we would be unlikely to use them.
  • My mother's Light Touch is now securely fastened to her cupboard, and mine is held on my desk by the power cable, which is wedged between the desk and the wall. That means they are no longer prone to falling and breaking.
  • My mother lives alone, and I have situated my Light Touch in a space my wife and daughters tend to leave alone. If more people interacted with our devices, it might change the nature of the communication they afford and quite possibly diffuse responsibility for using them at all.
  • Crucially, we use Light Touch as part of an ecosystem of other communications media. We have weekly video meetings with my brother and sister to talk about family news and activities, and my mother and I have occasional one-to-one video meetings as well. My mother and I also send emails to each other and to other members of our families, and have recently started communicating more via text messages. This all means that Light Touch is not our sole means of communication but has found a niche in this larger ecosystem.

This list could continue, but the point should be clear: Light Touch is highly dependent on its context of use, and its ‘success’ is not just due to its technical and interaction features alone.

Equally important for Light Touch's ‘success’ is the nature of my relationship with my mother. Unsurprisingly, we are bound by deep love, caring and concern for one another's wellbeing, and this means that we both appreciate and do not find intrusive the near-continuous reminder that we are in each other's thoughts that Light Touch offers. As independent adults, however, we neither need nor want to share the minutia of our daily lives with great frequency—our weekly meetings generally suffice for this. The slow pace and minimal expressivity offered by Light Touch thus suits our relationship very well. Keeping the lights on only requires messages to be sent every six hours or so, and the only ‘content’ that need be generated is the colour of the lights. Light Touch allows us to maintain a low key, peripheral sense of each other's presence fairly continuously, while reserving immediate and demanding media such as teleconferencing for more occasional use.

As a contrast to our relationship, imagine what it might be like to use with a separated lover on the one hand, or a friend on the other. For separated lovers, the continual signal of attention and intimacy might be reassuring, but the limited expressivity would likely be unsatisfactory on its own. This is congruent with Chien et al. [ 11 ] call to support “mutual caring” via emotional communications technologies, and with Rooney's [ 57 ] call for a more “holistic approach to intimate relationships”. For friends, on the other hand, Light Touch's implicit demand to continually signal attention and affection might well seem intrusive or even creepy, while the limited expressivity offered by the lights might seem wanting. For most friendships, then, the more occasional, playful, and content-rich forms of communication offered by social media and shared apps seems more suitable. This may well explain why my daughter and her friend scoffed at my suggestion they take Light Touches to keep in touch while attending separate universities (as they put it, “we have a thing called a phone ”).

These are speculative comparisons, of course, and our description of using Light Touch indicates the value of detailed first-person accounts of using such devices to uncover the nuances of the interactions they afford. Nonetheless, considering how Light Touch might or might not support other kinds of relationships is useful in appreciating how important the nature of our relationship has been to its success. More generally, it suggests that devices such as Light Touch might be particularly well suited for supporting relationships between parents and their separated children, which with few exceptions (e.g. [ 10 , 43 ]) tend to be neglected in favour of long-distance romances.

3.3 Simple vs. crafted devices

Appreciating how technical and interaction features, our context of use and the nature of our relationship have become intertwined to produce an emotional communication experience that my mother and I both find compelling also speaks to differences in opinion about the best strategies to produce systems that can effectively support emotional relationships. On the basis of their studies of people using the “intimate objects” they designed, Kaye et al. [ 41 ] claimed that even a single bit of information (in their case, a fading light) can convey rich meaning by virtue of its situation in a complex social relationship. In contrast, Rooney [ 57 ] argues that for many such devices “the intimate relationship was conceptualised as something that could be transmitted, the experience of intimacy something that could be fragmented”, while Chien et al. [ 11 , 12 ] argue for more specific devices—such as a shared electronic pet, or robotically-linked teakettles—that can support “everyday responsive interaction”.

What our experience with Light Touch suggests is that both perspectives can be compatible in certain circumstances. Light Touch conveys more than a single bit of information, given that my mother and I can each choose lights to send, yet it is still an extremely simple device, designed with no attempt to reflect our everyday interactions. Nonetheless, we have found it meaningful, not because it transmits intimacy, but because it provides just the right degree of semisynchronous communication, and just the right amount of expressivity and reciprocity, to fit our particular contexts of use and our particular form of intimate relationship. As we have suggested above, the simple communication offered by Light Touch might not work for everybody, but it works well for us.

4 CONCLUSION

In response to my suggestion that she share her experiences with communications technologies over the course of her life, my mother wrote:

I grew up in the 1930’s in a small town in Kentucky where my mother and I lived with my grandfather and uncle. I have a dim recollection of a wall mounted telephone that connected to an operator in an office uptown who could connect calls with appropriate plugs into a switchboard. My family was lucky enough to have a single party line, although I suspect that wires were sometimes crossed so that we were connected to a party line. Soon the wall phone made way for a “modern” black dial phone. I think the telephone was used mainly for business communications and to make arrangements with family, but not to chat or share news.

When I went to college in Massachusetts in the late 1940s, my mother and I exchanged weekly letters pretty regularly as a way of keeping in touch, though of course it took days for an exchange of news. Exceptional phone calls were made and received on a single phone down the hall, but conversations were brief and business-like because of the expense and lack of privacy.

The situation hadn't changed much when my children went to college in the late 1970s. Long-distance calls were still expensive, so we communicated mainly through letters and postcards, though we would talk briefly by phone about once a week. By then, we had learned to chat a bit on the phone – though my mother persisted in disconnecting abruptly once the purpose of a call to her was achieved. Bill's brother recalls conversations with my mother limited on her part to “It certainly is good to hear your voice. Goodbye.”

This brief autobiographical history is a reminder of the radical discontinuity between the slow evolution of telecommunications my mother describes, and our current internet-enabled landscape of smartphones, email and social media. The advent of always-on, free or low-cost internet has afforded a plethora of more playful and discursive forms of communications, with dedicated devices like Light Touch benefiting from increasingly accessible and affordable microprocessor platforms and related electronics as well. Closely-observed accounts of such devices in long-term use are relatively rare, however [ 31 , 46 ], so how well they work, for whom, in what circumstances and for how long has often been a matter of speculation.

In this paper, we have shared our experiences living with Light Touch over the last two years, tracing the trajectory from our experiences building and trying the device for the first time, through early troubles and a near-disaster, and to our relatively settled day-to-day use. Most of all, we have tried to share the closeness and enjoyment that this very simple device has afforded us, a mother and son who simply want to stay in touch over time and distance. We believe this account is a substantial contribution in its own terms as a resource for designers and researchers seeking to build or study similar systems.

In addition, we have discussed in some detail the features of Light Touch that we have found important, and also describe how their utility is contingent on details of our physical and social context of use, and in particular with how well they fit with our relationship as a mother and son. We suggest that our account may help resolve a tension in the literature between those who argue that very simple devices may mediate intimacy, and those who recommend more holistic approaches that build on existing activities of mutual care. In our experience, simple devices like Light Touch may indeed play important roles in helping people feel connected—but only if they are well-matched to the contexts and relationships they are meant to support. Thus, we offer our experiences with Light Touch as an example of success, not a recipe for success.
