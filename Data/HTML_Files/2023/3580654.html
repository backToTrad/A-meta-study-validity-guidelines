Exploring User Motivations Behind iOS App Tracking Transparency Decisions

Hannah J Hutton , School of Management, University of Bath, United Kingdom, hjh53@bath.ac.uk
David A Ellis , School of Management, University of Bath, United Kingdom, dae30@bath.ac.uk

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3580654
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Apple's App Tracking Transparency framework allows users to decide whether they want to allow their activity to be tracked for advertising purposes. In this work we examine the tracking decisions made by 312 participants and their associations with privacy concern and personality factors, and conduct a thematic analysis on participants’ reasons for choosing to accept or reject tracking requests. Despite 51% of participants reporting that they had rejected tracking for privacy reasons, higher privacy concern scores did not correlate with a lower rate of tracking acceptance. Additionally, 43% of participants held incorrect beliefs about what tracking does, including nearly a quarter who mistakenly believed that accepting a tracking request would share their location with the requesting app. We suggest explanations for these misconceptions and provide recommendations that may improve usability of both App Tracking Transparency and future Privacy Enhancing Technologies.

1 INTRODUCTION

Smartphones now offer a multitude of services to support everyday social and occupational organisation. As a result, they also collect increasingly large volumes of data about the individual behind the screen. Whilst some users might be concerned about protecting sensitive information such as their banking details or passwords, even seemingly innocuous data such as the number of times a smartphone is picked up in a day can be used to identify individuals from a group [ 35 ]. Other data such as social media interactions can be used to build up a profile of a user which can be used for recommending relevant content, for targeted advertising, or for something more sinister as seen in the Cambridge Analytica scandal. In this case, millions of US voters were subject to targeted advertising which is alleged to have influenced the outcome of the 2016 US election [ 42 ]. The practice of ‘tracking’ these types of behavioural data is common across both iOS and Android ecosystems to identify interests and personalise ads. Privacy controls are therefore critical for allowing users to decide and enforce how much information they are willing to share, and with whom.

However, the privacy options afforded by smartphones and the apps we use on them are still in their infancy. Despite app tracking meeting the criteria for a ‘high-risk’ data processing activity under GDPR and thus requiring user consent due to the personal nature of the data being collected [ 33 ], previous research has found that many apps failed to obtain consent prior to engaging in tracking [ 26 ]. Apple's introduction of the App Tracking Transparency framework helps to prevent this practice. The framework, introduced as part of the iOS 14.5 update in April 2021, now requires apps to explicitly ask the user for their consent to have their device activity tracked. The activity data is used for targeted advertising, measuring the effectiveness of advertising, and for sharing information with data brokers [ 2 ]. The first time a user accesses an app that engages in tracking, they are presented with a pop-up asking for their permission (see Figure 1 ). If a user selects “Ask App Not To Track”, the app is no longer able to access their unique Identifier for Advertisers (IDFA), which can track the user across the apps and websites they use on their device. Apple's policies also forbid apps from engaging in other types of tracking such as device fingerprinting.

The release of App Tracking Transparency was met with much enthusiasm from privacy advocates. Andrés Arrieta of the Electronic Frontier Foundation described it as “a great step forward” [ 3 ]. Predictably, the changes were far less welcomed by advertisers, who suddenly found themselves unable to effectively micro-target ads to those users who had rejected tracking. Facebook's parent company Meta, which owes around 97% of its income to advertising revenue [ 32 ], predicted a loss of $ 10 billion for 2022 thanks to the changes [ 36 ] and focused on pushing a narrative that the update would disadvantage small businesses who advertise on their platform [ 31 ]. Early analytics from three weeks after the framework was deployed suggested that as few as 6% of US-based users had accepted tracking [ 21 ]; this rose to 15% after four months [ 20 ].

We were interested in understanding the choices users were making when faced with tracking requests, and the decision-making process behind these choices. Since App Tracking Transparency is automatically enabled on devices 1 , its use is not limited to those who are already taking steps to protect their privacy. Thus, we are able to assess whether privacy concern is a motivating factor in use of this feature or whether users are more interested in something else. We were also interested in whether any personality traits would influence the rate of tracking acceptance, although this was a secondary research topic. Our research questions are as follows:

  • Is the decision to allow or reject tracking correlated with reported privacy concern?
  • Is the decision to allow or reject tracking correlated with any personality traits?
  • What motivates participants’ decisions to allow or reject tracking?

To investigate these questions, we collected data on the tracking decisions made by 312 participants, as well as their stated reasons for allowing or rejecting tracking for each requesting app. Participants also completed a privacy concern scale and a short personality measure. Correlations between the rate of tracking acceptance and the privacy and personality factors were calculated, and a thematic analysis was conducted on the reasons given for the participants’ tracking decisions. We also examined the differences between those participants who had discussed privacy in their responses and those who had not.

We observed no significant correlation between privacy concern and tracking acceptance, despite just over half of our participants rejecting at least some tracking requests due to concerns about privacy or security. These concerns form a major theme in our analysis, and a significant difference was found for both privacy concern and tracking acceptance between the participants in this theme and those outside of it. This suggests that those individuals who have a higher privacy concern are more likely to think about their privacy when making tracking decisions, i.e., it is more salient for them. These findings counter the privacy paradox's claim that individuals engage in privacy-violating behaviour despite reporting privacy concerns, and we suggest that privacy calculus is a more suitable framework for assessing behaviours in the context of privacy decision making. However, many participants appeared to be engaging in a calculus based on misconceptions about activity tracking, thus indicating a need for greater clarity around App Tracking Transparency's implementation.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews prior work related to the current study. We describe our hypotheses, methodology, and analyses in Section 3 , and detail our findings in Section 4 . Section 5 contains a discussion of our findings, including limitations of this study. Finally, we conclude the paper in Section 6 . All supplementary materials for this paper, including the pre-registration, analysis code, and collected data, are available at https://osf.io/xnhzq .

2 RELATED WORK

Although we believe this to be the first work to examine the rationale behind user choices within the App Tracking Transparency framework, privacy decision making has been studied extensively in the prior literature.

2.1 Privacy Enhancing Technologies and App Tracking Transparency

Privacy Enhancing Technologies (PETs) aim to put users back in control of their data and their privacy. The term covers a diverse range of technologies across various aspects of privacy, from browser communication encryption using HTTPS Everywhere [ 22 ] to network traffic anonymisation using Tor [ 17 ]. Some of these technologies are automatically integrated with existing systems and thus require no user intervention to enable them, but many are only available to users if they specifically seek them out. In these cases, adoption rates can be low: only those users who have a high privacy concern are likely to invest time and effort in searching for and implementing such technologies [ 11 ]. Additionally, these PETs may require payment for use. For users who are less concerned about their privacy, this is simply not worth it. As summarised succinctly by Athey et al., “whenever privacy requires additional effort or comes at the cost of a less smooth user experience, participants are quick to abandon technology that would offer them greater protection” [ 6 ].

App Tracking Transparency, unlike some PETs aimed at blocking tracking such as the Ghostery browser extension 2 , is free, built in by default, and automatically enabled. This is useful for non-knowledgeable users who would otherwise not be aware of the feature's existence and would be unlikely to seek it out.

There has been limited research thus far on App Tracking Transparency. Kollnig et al. examined the tracking behaviour exhibited by apps both before and after its launch, and found that despite the framework making it more difficult for apps to track users, similar amounts of tracker libraries existed in app code after its implementation [ 27 ]. Additionally, many apps still attempted to engage in other forms of tracking despite this also being a violation of Apple's policies.

DeGiulio et al. investigated how current tracking requests were presented and the effect of modifying the request prompt in various ways for an app purpose-built for the study. They found no significant differences in opt-out rates when changing the framing of the request (either positive, neutral, or negative), when the app threatened to charge the participant to continue using the app, or when the participant was primed with a pre-request screen which gave information about app tracking [ 14 ]. The only significant difference in tracking acceptance was found when the purpose of the request was changed: participants accepted tracking requests more often if the request purported to be for personalising content within the app rather than for personalising ads 3 . They also found that 66.2% of their participants rejected tracking requests “often” or “always” when presented with requests for their own apps. However, no data were collected about which apps participants had received tracking requests from, or the reasons why participants chose to accept or reject these requests.

2.2 The privacy paradox and privacy decision making

The privacy paradox is a phenomenon that describes the discrepancy between an individual's stated privacy preferences or concerns, and their actual privacy behaviours (often referred to as ‘revealed preferences’ in the field of economics). The term was popularised in 2006 with the publication of an article about the use of social networking sites by young people, who were observed to disclose personal information despite privacy concerns [ 7 ]. However, much recent work has argued that the privacy paradox is reductionist and that this ‘paradoxical’ behaviour can be explained by other factors, such as the context surrounding the decision [ 1 ] and dark patterns intended to mislead or trick the user into behaving in ways they would otherwise not choose to [ 37 ]. Dienlin et al. [ 15 ] conducted a longitudinal analysis of participants to assess differences in information sharing behaviour over time. They found that those with higher privacy concern were less likely to share personal information at each wave of the study, suggesting that sharing behaviour was consistent with their privacy attitudes.

A popular alternative model of privacy decision making is privacy calculus, first described in 1977 as “calculus of behavior” [ 28 ]. It was later work which applied the theory to Internet use, e.g., [ 16 ]. Privacy calculus posits that individuals weigh up the potential benefits and risks of data sharing and act accordingly; an individual will only disclose data if they feel the ‘reward’ they receive is sufficient to justify the risks of their sharing behaviour. In the case of activity tracking, this reward would be personalised ads.

Privacy salience, defined as “whether informational privacy is prominent in a person's awareness or their memory of past experience” [ 41 ], arguably underpins both the privacy paradox and privacy calculus. If privacy is not salient for the individual (i.e., they are not aware that they are making a choice that affects their privacy), they may make decisions that appear incongruous with their privacy attitudes. Likewise, if individuals have a high privacy salience they may be more likely to select privacy-preserving options since they are more aware of the implications of their actions.

In a similar vein to the privacy paradox, there is also a disparity between individuals’ reported smartphone behaviours and objective measures of the same [ 19 ]. We attempt to negate this issue in this study by combining objective data (the apps that a participant has received requests from, and whether they accepted or rejected these requests) with subjective reasoning for how those decisions have been made. This will provide us with a more complete understanding of how App Tracking Transparency is being used than studying either of those aspects in isolation.

2.3 Personality and privacy risk-taking

The study of personality's effects on decision making spans multiple fields, including decision making for privacy-related settings. To assess personality in this current work, we are using the HEXACO model which measures six aspects: Honesty-Humility, Emotionality, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Conscientiousness, and Openness to Experience [ 29 ]. We use prior work on the effects of these factors (as well as the related factors of the ‘Big Five’ model [ 30 ]) on privacy decision making to guide our personality-related hypotheses in Section 3.1 . We are additionally considering the effect of these factors on risk-taking due to there being less research on the same for privacy decision making. Although risk-taking has been associated with reduced privacy-protective behaviours [ 12 ], we acknowledge that individuals may not be aware of the privacy risks stemming from this type of data collection and thus we do not make predictions as to the strength of the relationships in our hypotheses.

The effect of Agreeableness on privacy decisions is perhaps the most obvious. Individuals scoring highly in this factor are more likely to comply with requests, even in cases where they could be exploited [ 5 ]. This is also the case for Honesty-Humility [ 43 ], which has been associated with modesty [ 5 ]. This could be expressed by individuals in this context as a perception that their personal information is not valuable, so they are thus less restrictive about access. The Emotionality factor correlates with high scores of impulsivity, which naturally lends itself to risk-taking behaviour [ 30 ]. This impulsivity has been linked to less privacy-protective behaviour in previous work [ 12 ]. Both Extraversion and Openness to Experience have also been positively correlated with measures of risk-taking [ 13 , 39 ].

Of the six HEXACO factors, Conscientiousness is the one that is most often associated with greater concern about privacy and less frequent risk-taking behaviour, e.g., [ 38 ]. These individuals are more likely to take time to consider the implications of the tracking request and make an informed decision. Given that there are only limited benefits to the user within the App Tracking Transparency framework (i.e., personalised ads), we believe that high scorers in this factor will be less likely to accept tracking requests.

3 METHODS

A mixed-methods approach has been employed in this study. Quantitative data can reveal what decisions are being made when users are faced with a tracking request, but qualitative data are needed to understand the rationale behind these decisions. Ethical approval was obtained prior to data collection and all participants gave their informed consent. The data and analysis code for this study, as well as a full listing of questions asked, are available at https://osf.io/xnhzq .

3.1 Hypotheses

We first define ‘Allow Rate’ as the percentage of apps that a participant has accepted tracking requests for. This is calculated by dividing the number of accepted tracking requests by the total number of tracking requests received by that participant.

\begin{equation} allow\ rate = \frac{tracking\ requests\ accepted}{tracking\ requests\ received} \end{equation}
(1)
Our primary hypothesis is as follows:

  1. Privacy Concern will be negatively correlated with Allow Rate

Logically, we expect that participants with a higher privacy concern would accept tracking requests from fewer apps and thus have a lower allow rate than participants with a lower privacy concern score. However, the privacy paradox would suggest that tracking acceptance would remain high even for those with high privacy concern, i.e., they are not acting in accordance with their stated privacy attitudes.

The following hypotheses are based on previous research into the effects of personality traits on privacy decisions (see Section 2.3 ). These secondary hypotheses relate to the six factors of the HEXACO personality model and have been grouped together for convenience, as our primary focus is the relationship between privacy concern and allow rate.

  1. Honesty-Humility, Emotionality, Extraversion, Agreeableness, and Openness to Experience will be positively correlated with Allow Rate
  2. Conscientiousness will be negatively correlated with Allow Rate

In addition to these hypotheses, there is a large exploratory component to this research around the question of why participants choose to allow or block tracking. The qualitative data collected will be critical to understanding the quantitative results and, more generally, what factors might influence rates of tracking acceptance.

3.2 Survey design

We used a survey consisting of four sections to collect data: 1) participant demographics (age, gender, and education level), 2) questions about the tracking requests the participant had received, 3) a personality measure, and 4) a privacy concern scale. The survey was hosted on Qualtrics 4 , an online survey tool.

The tracking request section began by asking participants whether tracking requests were enabled on their device. If a participant reported that they had turned tracking requests off, they were asked to explain why they had made this decision. If tracking requests were enabled, participants were asked to provide a list of the apps that they had received tracking requests from and whether they had accepted or rejected each of these requests. This list of apps is visible from the Tracking menu within the Privacy section of the Settings app (see Figure 2 ). There was also a free-text response for each reported tracking request so that participants could discuss why they had made this decision. The option to input tracking requests was also made visible to those participants who had disabled requests, as some apps may have requested tracking permission before requests had been blocked globally.

The HEXACO-60 Personality Inventory [ 4 ] was used to capture aspects of a participant's personality across six factors: Honesty-Humility, Emotionality, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Conscientiousness, and Openness to Experience. Participants were presented with a series of 60 statements such as “I make decisions based on the feeling of the moment rather than on careful thought”, which they had to indicate their agreement with on a 5-point Likert scale (“Strongly disagree” to “Strongly agree”). Although both 100-item and 200-item versions of the HEXACO scale exist, we chose to use the shorter measure to reduce participant fatigue; recording apps that have requested tracking requires participants to switch between the survey and their tracking request list if they complete the survey on their mobile device. Correlations between this shorter scale and the longer version range between 0.89 and 0.94 [ 4 ].

Privacy concern was assessed using a scale developed by Buchanan et al. [ 10 ]. As with the HEXACO-60 personality inventory, participants were asked to indicate their agreement with 16 statements such as “Are you concerned about people you do not know obtaining personal information about you from your online activities?” on a 5-point Likert scale (“Not at all” to “Very much”). We chose to use this particular scale as it focuses on online-specific contexts and has been widely used in prior work. Although the scale developed by Earp et al. [ 18 ] arguably contains more items relevant to the context of online tracking, e.g., “I mind when my PII (Personally Identifiable Information) is used for marketing or research activities”, this scale is longer and we were careful to avoid participant fatigue. Additionally, this scale makes specific reference to websites in the majority of its items, something that does not necessarily relate to the app-based tracking of interest in this study.

Three attention check questions were used in the survey: two during the HEXACO-60 questions, and one during the privacy concern questions. Participants had to correctly respond to two of these three questions to be included in the final sample.

3.4 Analysis

The initial correlation analysis and thematic analysis were pre-registered at https://osf.io/xnhzq . Further analyses were not pre-registered and are conducted on an exploratory basis. The analysis code (in R) and data required to reproduce our results are also available at the above link.

3.4.1 Correlations. Correlations were computed between each measured factor (privacy concern and the six factors of the HEXACO model) and participants’ allow rates. Participants who had tracking requests turned off globally were assigned an allow rate of zero.

3.4.2 Analysis of variance. Participants were allocated to one of four groups based on their allow rate, and a series of one-way ANOVAs were used to determine if there were any significant differences in the measured factors between these groups. The four groups are as follows:

  • ‘Global deny’: Tracking requests are turned off at a global level (n = 87)
  • ‘Deny all’: Tracking requests are enabled, but all requests have been rejected (n = 38)
  • ‘Allow some’: Some tracking requests have been accepted and some rejected (n = 160)
  • ‘Allow all’: All tracking requests received have been accepted (n = 27)

3.4.3 Thematic analysis. Thematic analysis was conducted using the method described by Braun and Clarke [ 9 ] on both the reasons given for turning tracking requests off at a global level (n = 80), and the reasons given for each app decision (n = 2087). During the first phase, data familiarisation, we identified three common response types: privacy concerns, belief that tracking referred to location tracking, and participants receiving benefits from tracking. These formed the basis of the first round of coding and eventually became our three most prevalent themes, either outright or as part of a larger theme in the case of location tracking beliefs. We identified 62 distinct codes from the 2167 responses given by participants, which we collated into the seven themes described in Section 4.3 . After these themes had been agreed upon, we performed two more rounds of checks to ensure that each response had been correctly coded according to the final theme list and that the chosen themes were an accurate reflection of the dataset as a whole.

3.4.4 Differences between privacy-conscious participants and others. After conducting the thematic analysis, we became interested in one of the themes in particular and decided to conduct further analysis of the participants in this theme. The ‘privacy and security concern’ theme (discussed further in Section 4.3.1 ) contained 158 participants who had expressed their concerns about how activity tracking would affect either their privacy or security. We split our participants into two groups: ‘privacy salient’, the group of participants who had fallen into this theme, and ‘privacy unconcerned’, the other 154 participants who had not discussed privacy or security in any of their responses. To assess whether there were any significant differences between these two groups, t-tests were conducted for each of the measured factors (privacy concern and the six HEXACO factors), as well as for allow rate.

In addition to this, we also constructed a binary logistic regression model to determine collectively which factors (not including allow rate) were predictive of whether or not a participant was likely to have discussed privacy or security in their responses, i.e., whether privacy is salient for them. Note that we were not doing this with the intention of building a predictive model, rather the objective was to determine which factors were associated with privacy salience.

4.2 Analysis of variance

As discussed in Section 3.4.4 , we split our participants into four groups depending on their allow rate. To recap, these groups were: ‘Global deny’, tracking requests have been blocked at a global level; ‘Deny all’, all requests have been rejected; ‘Allow some’, some requests have been accepted and some rejected; and ‘Allow all’, all tracking requests received have been accepted. A series of one-way ANOVAs showed that there were no significant differences between the four groups in Privacy Concern and five of the six HEXACO factors. While there was a significant difference for Conscientiousness ( F = 5.24, p = 0.02), post-hoc comparisons did not suggest this difference was reliable (all p values > 0.15).

4.3 Thematic analysis

The correlation analyses and the analysis of variance provided some interesting results — namely that privacy concern did not have a significant influence on allow rate — but does not suggest why this may be the case. The qualitative data therefore provide some explanation as to what factors lead participants to choose to allow or deny tracking. The thematic analysis led to the development of seven themes, which are discussed in this section.

4.3.1 Privacy and security concern.

“It feels like an invasion of privacy” - Participant 146

Just over half of the participants (158, 50.64%) mentioned their concerns about privacy or security. These concerns ranged from simple explanations such as not wanting apps to have access to their data (“Worried about my personal data” - Participant 95, Spotify), to more targeted reasons such as specific companies having a bad reputation for privacy. This complaint was most often levelled against Facebook (“It's known for poor privacy” - Participant 256), Instagram, and TikTok, perhaps in part due to the widely publicised criticisms of these companies’ privacy practices (e.g., [ 8 , 42 ]). Participants in this theme often discussed their desire to control access to their data, which is one of the key principles of privacy as described by Westin [ 40 ]. Another frequent topic was the security of the collected data. Here, participants were less worried about the requesting app being privy to any sensitive data that may be collected, but rather the security of their data post-collection; several participants reported concerns about their data being hacked or exposed in some way. Participant 237 stated that they “may have feared of a data leak” for cryptocurrency exchange Binance.

Some of the comments in this theme are based on misunderstandings about how App Tracking Transparency works, but still reflect the privacy or security concerns of the participants. For example, Participant 258 said that the app Clue (a period tracking app) contains “private information on my health” when discussing why they had chosen to reject tracking permission. From this we can assume that this participant does not want other apps to have access to their health data, when in reality accepting tracking would give Clue permission to track their activity across other apps.

There were also 11 participants (3.53%) that indicated they were unconcerned with their privacy. These participants claimed that, for example, their data would not be of interest to the requesting apps. Participant 299 exemplified this with their explanation “...i don't see my data as that valuable and i have nothing to hide (sic)”. We can infer that these participants are still considering their privacy when making the tracking decision as they have referenced it in their responses, but they have opted to allow tracking anyway.

4.3.2 Misconceptions about tracking.

“I have [my] own shop on there and wanted to be informed of everything that may have happened” - Participant 171, Etsy

134 participants (43.27%) provided responses that indicated they were confused or unclear about what activity tracking does. A common belief was that the app would not function as normal without accepting tracking (“It's needed in order for the apps main function to work (sic)” - Participant 217, Flight Radar), or that accepting tracking would provide the user with a better app experience (“I thought it might make the game experience better” - Participant 131, Episode). Other misconceptions include believing that denying tracking would prevent the user from seeing ads entirely, and that allowing tracking would allow the user to receive notifications or analytics from the requesting app. This confusion may be due in part to a lack of clarity in some tracking prompts. Although the main text of the prompt is standardised and immutable, app developers are able to include a sentence explaining why they are requesting tracking permission (see Figure 3 for examples). The developer-provided string in the prompt is supposed to explain to the user the purpose for requesting tracking, but some apps may be (intentionally or unknowingly) providing false or misleading information. For example, the prompt for fashion retailer ASOS says “We'll use your data to give you a more personalised ASOS experience and to make our app even more amazing”. While this ‘personalised experience’ likely refers to the more relevant ads the user will see, a more obvious interpretation is that the content of the app will be tailored to the user. More of our participants accepted tracking requests on the basis of personalised content (n = 27) than for personalised advertising (n = 14), an effect that has also been found in prior work [ 14 ].

The most common misconception, however, was that ‘tracking’ refers to location tracking. 76 participants (24.36%) referenced location in their responses, often claiming that they needed to accept tracking as their location was integral to the functioning of the app. While many of the apps that participants reported do use location services, the tracking here refers to the tracking of a user's activity across apps on their device rather than the device's physical location. This response was often given for food delivery or collection apps, where the app requires the user's location in order to show them nearby restaurants (“It needs my location to show me local places” - Participant 188, TooGoodToGo).

4.3.3 Tracking benefits.

“I felt with this app, it may help my buying and selling experience if I allow tracking!” - Participant 148, Shpock

The most cited reason for allowing tracking (58 participants, 18.59%) was that the participant believed they would derive some benefit from this. This benefit may be either genuine (i.e., the user receives more relevant targeted advertising; “Some targeted ads are good” - Participant 113, Instagram) or incorrectly perceived. Perceived benefits, such as receiving notifications from the app, being sent vouchers, or being granted an improved app experience, are not actually afforded by activity tracking. Indeed, several participants had reported previously believing that by allowing tracking they would have realised some (perceived) benefit, but that they have not yet seen this. Participant 249 said “I expect them [Stocard] to show discounts and vouchers based on my online activity. Hasn't really materialised, so I'm thinking of changing this setting now”. Of these 58 participants, 49 discussed perceived benefits in their responses and only 16 mentioned benefits that they, or the company, would actually receive (personalised advertising or increased ad revenue). This reflects the ambiguity present in some tracking request prompts.

There were 52 participants (16.67%) who had denied tracking on the basis that they were not going to benefit from granting tracking permission to the requesting app, or that they were not interested in the benefits (whether genuine or perceived). Participant 53 rejected Barclays tracking as they “don't believe there are any benefits for it to track my activity”. Only 15 of these participants stated specifically that they were not interested in targeted advertising, and eight discussed personalised content in their responses, e.g., “I don't want content pushed on me based on what I've done elsewhere on phone” - Participant 71, Twitter.

4.3.4 Trust or distrust of the app.

“I don't trust the company with the information” - Participant 20, Instagram

43 participants (13.78%) accepted tracking requests because they trusted the requesting app or company with their data, or they approved of the company's ethos (e.g., “I like the app generally and its social aims” - Participant 133, TooGoodToGo). It should be noted, however, that tracking is often done by third-party tracking libraries rather than by the requesting app itself [ 27 ]. Additionally, tracking is done for the purpose of selling user data to advertisers to target ads to the user; the requesting app doesn't benefit from the behavioural data but will benefit from the advertising revenue. Several participants claimed to have accepted a tracking request because the app or company would benefit (Participant 236 reported that they “always allow by default without thinking about it”, as “it helps the company”), but none explicitly stated that this benefit would be increased ad revenue.

There were also 64 participants (20.51%) that rejected tracking based on their distrust of the requesting app or company. Social media apps were frequently the target of this response. Perhaps the strongest opinion came from Participant 123 when talking about Twitter: “They are a censorship company, social credit company and election interference so they don't deserve my data”. Twitter had the lowest allow rate of the ten most commonly reported apps in our sample at just 21%. Misuse of data was also a common response within this theme. Participant 203 rejected tracking for TikTok on the grounds that “I assume [the data] will be used for bad reasons”.

4.3.5 Information seeking.

“I don't know how the information they gather is going to be used” - Participant 165

93 participants (29.81%) indicated that they were unsure about some aspects of tracking such as what tracking involves or what the purpose is, or that they would like further information about how their data would be collected and used. Participant 3 erred on the side of caution, and “decided to decline tracking for all apps until I had read further about how the data was used”. Similarly, Participant 154 said that they “don't want to allow any to track as not sure what it does”. It is unclear whether any of these participants have sought out this additional information within the Tracking menu of their Settings app, or online on Apple's support website. The prevalence of this theme suggests that the permission request prompt alone does not necessarily communicate all the desired information to users. However, there is only a limited amount of space in the request itself, and including a further explanation of activity tracking in the prompt would not be feasible. This would also be inconsistent with the format of other, more readily understood, permission requests such as those for camera or microphone access. The Tracking menu does provide more information about the App Tracking Transparency framework, although there is no explanation of the types of activity data being collected or how this is being done — both things that participants discussed wanting to know.

4.3.6 Lack of engagement.

“Probably just selected this option at sign up without thinking carefully” - Participant 31

87 participants (27.88%) gave responses that indicated they hadn't engaged with App Tracking Transparency in any meaningful way. A common response in this theme was that the participant had chosen to allow or deny tracking randomly based on how they felt at the time of the request, rather than any decision relating to how they felt about the requesting app or their data (“whenever i received the notification to track i just hit whatever i feel at that time (sic)” - Participant 97). This is not intended as a criticism of these users, however. Tracking request prompts appear the first time an app is used, so users may select an option without reading the prompt or considering the implications in order to access the app more quickly. The prompts may then be viewed as a form of ‘task interruption’, where the user is distracted by the interruption and dismisses the information in order to continue their main task (in this case, setting up their new app). This is especially true in the case of actionable prompts which require user input [ 34 ], as is the case with activity tracking.

4.3.7 Tracking is creepy.

“The whole app is creepy with its tracking” - Participant 203, Facebook

26 participants (8.33%) reported that they felt tracking was ‘creepy’ or that they were uncomfortable with the idea of being tracked (“Just don't like the idea of it” - Participant 225, Tesco Clubcard). As was the case with privacy concerns and distrust, social media apps such as Facebook, Instagram, and TikTok received the majority of these responses. This creepiness is likely related to the negative connotations of the term ‘tracking’ and a wider distrust of technology companies, in particular social media companies.

5 DISCUSSION

5.1 A case for privacy salience

Based on the initial lack of correlation between participants’ privacy concern scores and their allow rates, it could be assumed that privacy concern is unimportant when users are making App Tracking Transparency decisions. We propose an alternative explanation: privacy concern does affect allow rate, but only if users are aware that the tracking requests received are presenting a decision that affects their privacy. Privacy salience is therefore a more important factor than privacy concern. Indeed, participants who talked about their privacy in the free-text responses (the ‘privacy salient’ group) had significantly higher privacy concern scores and lower allow rates compared to those participants in the ‘privacy unconcerned’ group. Participants are aware of their privacy (i.e., it is salient) when they are being asked questions to assess their privacy attitudes, hence the high levels of privacy concern reported across the sample, but they are not necessarily aware of or thinking about their privacy when faced with a tracking request.

Meta-owned apps Facebook and Instagram, as well as TikTok, all received many negative or distrusting comments in the free-text responses, and all had lower individual allow rates than the rate across all apps (see Table 2 ). These apps have notably all received much negative attention in the media for their privacy practices and how they handle user data, and this is likely to have affected whether the participants were thinking about privacy when presented with a tracking request from these apps. A similar effect can be seen in the case of WhatsApp, where the platform experienced a mass exodus in the weeks following global backlash against changes to its privacy policy [ 23 ]. WhatsApp does not request tracking permissions so we cannot assess whether that change would have affected tracking acceptance in this study.

If we assume that privacy salience is the determining factor in how users make privacy decisions, we are faced with the ongoing challenge of how to make privacy more central to the decision-making process. In the case of App Tracking Transparency requests, there may be merit in altering the wording of the tracking request to highlight that the decision users are being presented with is something that will affect their privacy. At present, the prompt reads “Allow [app] to track your activity across other companies’ apps and websites?”. It would be helpful to clarify in the prompt that the activity in question is on-device activity. This may reduce the number of users who believe that the tracking refers to their location (a permission which often does need to be granted for apps that use location services as part of their core functionality, regardless of a user's privacy attitudes), and increase awareness that the request is tracking what they do on their device.

5.2 Lack of support for the privacy paradox

The lack of a significant negative correlation between allow rate and privacy concern score does not necessarily support the privacy paradox's suggestion that people act contrary to their privacy attitudes. The privacy paradox would suggest that our ‘privacy salient’ group would be willing to allow tracking despite their (significantly higher) privacy concern scores. However, the participants in this group had a significantly lower allow rate than the ‘privacy unconcerned’ group.

The qualitative data collected indicate that the primary reason for allowing tracking despite privacy concerns (aside from those who believed that they needed to have tracking on for the app to work) is that the participant believes they are benefitting in some way from this tracking. This suggests that the discrepancy between privacy concerns and privacy behaviours is not paradoxical at all, but a result of the user weighing up the risks and benefits of their decision and concluding that those benefits (whether genuine or perceived) take priority over the potential privacy risk. This aligns with privacy calculus theory and is consistent with prior work suggesting that users prioritise more tangible benefits than maintaining the abstract and ill-defined concept of privacy, e.g., [ 25 ].

5.3 The need for clarity

It is clear from the participant responses that there are significant misunderstandings about how App Tracking Transparency works. Nearly one quarter of participants (24.36%) indicated that they believed the request to refer to location tracking rather than tracking of app activity. The use of the term ‘activity’ to describe the data being tracked is one aspect that may be confusing for users as it is a semantically overloaded term with multiple possible interpretations. Although it is an accurate descriptor for the data being collected, there is perhaps a need to clarify exactly what is meant by ‘activity’ in this case. The ‘Learn more’ page in the Tracking menu may be a suitable location to include such an explanation, ideally with examples so users would be better able to conceptualise the process. This overloading may particularly be an issue for apps in the Health & Fitness category. For these apps, ‘activity’ may also be taken to mean physical activity. Participant 24, for example, stated that “I use this to monitor my calorie intake but I am in no need to track my activity” when discussing why they had rejected tracking for the app MyFitnessPal.

Similarly, 26.6% of participants believed that they needed to accept tracking for their apps to work correctly despite this not being the case. Apple's support website states that “[y]ou can still use the full capabilities of the app, regardless of whether you allow the app to track your activity” [ 2 ]. However, this information is not mentioned in the Tracking menu's ‘Learn more’ page. Furthermore, this page does not provide a link to the explanation on the support website, so unless a user seeks out this information separately, they will not necessarily be aware that denying tracking requests still allows them to use the app normally. Users may be able to infer this from the explanation given on-device, but it is not stated there explicitly. This could easily be resolved by including the extra information in the Tracking menu, or at least providing a link to any other sources of important information.

These factors, coupled with the fact that users may be used to having to accept permissions in order to use the main functionality of apps, or not being able to use an app or a service without consenting to data collection (Hobson's choice [ 24 ]), means that it may just be a habit to accept any requests without reading what the request is for.

5.4 The importance of personality

Although the impact of personality factors on tracking acceptance was a secondary research topic for us, there were some statistically significant results. The HEXACO factors Honesty-Humility and Conscientiousness were both negatively correlated with allow rate, although the correlations were relatively small (-0.13 for Honesty-Humility and -0.17 for Conscientiousness). High levels of Honesty-Humility are associated with, among other things, a lack of interest in possessing wealth [ 5 ]. It may be that this is manifested here as disinterest in the benefits of tracking and thus a lower allow rate. Conscientiousness had a slightly stronger negative correlation, which aligns with expectations based on previous work. Logically, we would expect that conscientious participants would be more likely to pay attention to the request prompt and consider the implications of accepting or rejecting tracking before making their decision.

There were also significant differences between the ‘privacy salient’ and ‘privacy unconcerned’ groups when comparing Agreeableness, Conscientiousness, and Openness to Experience scores. However, the effect sizes were small and as such, further confirmatory research is required in order to determine the importance of personality when it comes to predicting privacy salience in the general population.

5.5 Limitations

Our choice of privacy concern scale may have had an effect on our results. While we stand by our use of this scale for the reasons outlined in Section 3.2 , this scale does focus more on specific threats to privacy such as data access and misuse. These are concerns that participants raised in their free-text responses, but we understand that our lack of correlation between higher privacy concern scores and a lower rate of tracking acceptance may be an artefact of this scale measuring privacy concerns that are not specific to the context of online tracking.

Our measure of privacy salience is also somewhat limited. We assigned participants to either the ‘privacy salient’ or ‘privacy unconcerned’ groups based on whether they talked about privacy in their responses, when in reality this is not necessarily reflective of whether a participant was thinking about their privacy when making their decisions. It may be that they had privacy concerns when given a request prompt, but believed that tracking was necessary for proper function of the app or that they would receive some sort of reward for allowing tracking, and only discussed that in their response.

A separate, uncontrollable factor that may have affected participant tracking decisions is the developer-provided string in the request prompts. Although this string follows a similar pattern for many of the apps we have observed, they do all vary and thus we cannot rule out this having an effect. However, this is likely to affect only those participants who fall into the ‘Allow some’ group (n = 160). Participants who have requests turned off globally will never see the prompts, and those who have allowed all or rejected all tended to do so for reasons other than the information they had been given in the developer string (not a single participant discussed the tracking prompt's text in their response).

Finally, the sample for this study is not representative: 67.95% of our participants identified as female and 71.15% reported having at least an undergraduate degree. The sample is also limited to UK-based participants. Qualtrics does offer the ability to obtain a representative UK sample, but not in the case where participant filters have been applied. Due to our need to filter out potential participants who did not meet the participation criterion of using an iPhone running iOS 14.5 or later as their main device, we were unable to make use of this facility. Additionally, due to the size of our sample, some of our quantitative tests suffer from reduced statistical power and should therefore be interpreted with caution. However, this limitation does not impact our qualitative findings, which provide a richer understanding of why people make privacy decisions in this context.

6 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we have presented results from a study into the decisions that users have made within the App Tracking Transparency framework and how those decisions relate to both privacy concern and personality factors. This is, to the best of our knowledge, the first study to investigate users’ decision-making processes for activity tracking requests. We analysed the tracking decisions made by 312 participants, and conducted a thematic analysis on their self-reported reasons for allowing or denying tracking for each of the apps that had requested tracking permission. Participants also completed measures to assess their level of privacy concern and aspects of their personality. We propose that the apparent disparity between privacy concern and rate of tracking acceptance may be due in part to low privacy salience and user misconceptions around the tracking framework, and we have made several suggestions to its implementation that may mitigate this confusion.

App Tracking Transparency is a step in the right direction towards a future where users are in control of their smartphone data. Our research has uncovered some of the factors that drive decisions made within this framework and provides a basis for further exploration of these. It is our hope that by reporting these factors we may be providing some benefit to developers of Privacy Enhancing Technologies or privacy decision making systems such that they are able to build improved systems in the future.
