Foldable touch screens are increasingly popular, but little research has explored how the hinge impacts usability and performance. We evaluate across- and along-hinge drag gestures on a series of prototypes emulating foldable all-screen laptops with a curved hinge radius ranging from 1mm to 24mm. Results show that using a large 24mm hinge radius instead of a small 1mm hinge radius can decrease drag time by 13% and movement variability by 7% for touch input. However, hinge radius had no effect on performance for pen input. Further, we found that dragging along the hinge was up to 30% faster than dragging across the hinge, especially when dragging across at an acute angle to the hinge. Using these results, we demonstrate use cases for across- and along-hinge gestures. Our findings provide guidance for hardware and interaction designers seeking to create foldable touchscreen devices and their accompanying software.

1 INTRODUCTION

Foldable displays are becoming more common. Many recent phones and laptops have adopted a foldable form factor that opens to reveal a single, large display, providing versatility and portability. In particular, the 13" Lenovo X1 Fold [ 12 ], 17.3" Asus Zenbook 17 Fold [ 10 ], and 17" Samsung Flex Note concept [ 7 ] are foldable all-screen laptops that can be configured as either a large tablet or a smaller laptop. In laptop mode, these devices consist of two planar display areas (vertical and horizontal) seamlessly connected by a curved hinge area. However, despite their rising prevalence, relatively little research has explored how these curved displays impact touch and pen interactions.

Past work has compared vertical and horizontal displays, showing that with touch input, tap [ 2 ] and drag [ 11 , 19 ] tasks are typically faster on horizontal surfaces. Some work has also explored drag task performance on curved displays, such as BendDesk [ 24 ], which showed dragging across a curve with touch input was slower than dragging across planar portions of a large tabletop display, and Foley et al. [ 5 ], which showed that docking tasks and mode switching times were generally longer with touch, pen, trackpad, and mouse input on a curved laptop display than on a tablet. More relevant is Hennecke et al. [ 9 ]’s evaluation of the physical transition (bezel, edge, or curve) between horizontal and vertical areas of a tabletop display. Participants had better drag accuracy when the two areas were connected with a 10cm curve than when they were connected with a bezel or edge. However, none of these works systematically explored the effect of hinge radius to provide guidance for which hinge radii are best for touch and pen drag tasks on curved displays.

To address this gap, we performed a controlled experiment with 15 participants to evaluate drag tasks with pen and touch on foldable devices, with hinge radii between 1mm and 24mm (Fig. 1 a). We constructed prototype devices by precisely bending a thin acrylic sheet and affixing a flexible digitizer and a paper sheet with drag targets. Participants performed drag tasks across and along the curve of each prototype. Tasks involved dragging between pairs of points arranged at 12 distinct angles and 2 distances (11.4cm and 22.8cm) from each other. Results show that for touch input, a 24mm hinge radius decreased drag time by 13% and movement variability by 7% compared to a 1mm hinge radius. Hinge radius did not impact performance for pen input. Furthermore, for both touch and pen input, dragging along the hinge reduced drag time by up to 30%.

Our work makes three contributions: (1) experimental results demonstrating the impact of hinge radius on drag performance for touch and pen input; (2) a set of design recommendations for hardware and interaction designers; and (3) demonstrations of use cases for across- and along-hinge interactions.

2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK

Most previous work on drag gesture performance focuses on horizontal and vertical planar displays [ 2 , 16 , 19 ]. A horizontal display refers to a display lying flat on a horizontal surface like a desk, whereas a vertical display refers to a display mounted vertically (e.g., on a wall or a stand). This work focuses on on how the curved hinge of a foldable display affects dragging interactions. We provide an overview of existing work regarding touch and pen input on planar and curved surfaces.

2.1 Touch and Pen Input on Planar Surfaces

Many works have compared pen and touch input on either horizontal or vertical displays. For example, Cockburn et al. [ 4 ] evaluate touch and pen input for tapping, dragging, and radial dragging tasks on a horizontal surface. They showed that while touch input was 23.8% faster than pen input for tapping, it was 26.6% slower for dragging. Similarly, Niiro et al. [ 17 ] compare touch and pen gestures on large vertical displays. They found that while pen input was more precise, touch input was 18.9% faster.

Other works directly compare different display orientations. Morris et al. [ 16 ] evaluate active reading tasks using vertical and horizontal displays with mouse and pen input, respectively. Vertical displays were preferred for writing-intensive tasks, whereas horizontal displays were preferred for annotation tasks. Pedersen and Hornbæk [ 19 ] compare tap and drag performance with touch input on large vertical and horizontal displays. While tapping was 5% faster on vertical displays, dragging was 5% faster and had fewer errors on horizontal displays. Joshi and Vogel [ 11 ] explore one-dimensional tap, cross, and drag tasks with touch input on the top, ridge, front, and bottom of a table. Tapping was 11.0% slower and dragging was 19.6% slower on the vertical front face compared to the horizontal top face of the table. The differences in performance can be at least partially explained by reduced task dimensionality as well as the distinct posture assumed by sitting at a table compared to standing at a display.

A larger study by Bachynskyi et al. [ 2 ] evaluates touch pointing tasks on six touchscreen surfaces, including a vertical laptop screen, a vertical public display, and a horizontal tabletop. Their findings show the laptop screen had 27.7% less throughput than the tabletop and 16.5% less throughput than the public display. While they compare a variety of form factors, they do not consider devices with curved displays.

2.2 Touch and Pen Input on Curved Surfaces

Curved displays are common in mobile computing, ranging from a subtle curve along the edge (e.g., Apple Watch Series 7 [ 1 ], Samsung Galaxy S22 Ultra [ 21 ]) to a crease that allows the device to fold (e.g., Samsung Galaxy Z Flip4 and Fold4 [ 22 ], Huawei P50 Pocket [ 6 ], Lenovo X1 Fold [ 12 ]). Yi et al. [ 26 ] evaluate the effect of a subtle curve at the edge of a phone display. They found that while the curve was detrimental to grip and comfort, it did not affect typing speed or accuracy. Another study by Yi et al. [ 27 ] compares a wider range of curvatures, including flat, horizontally convex, horizontally concave, and vertically concave, for standard smartphone usage tasks like reading, video watching, and texting. The findings showed that grip comfort was generally better for concave devices, distortion was increased when there was a curve, and two-thumb operation was better for horizontally concave devices. The devices used for both studies had a relatively subtle curve, resulting in a relatively small impact on usability.

On another extreme, spherically curved surfaces dramatically change how people interact with devices. For example, Sphere [ 3 ] demonstrates how a multi-touch spherical display allows seamless transitions between vertical and horizontal displays, facilitating unique forms of collaboration with multitouch taps and drags. Roudaut et al. [ 20 ] evaluate how a variety of spherical curvatures affect touch offset for tapping tasks. They found that surface convexity increases pointing accuracy, while concave surfaces result in larger error offsets.

Foldable all-screen laptops sit between these two extremes. Their displays are formed by two planar areas seamlessly connected by a hinge that typically forms a curved display area. Foley et al. [ 5 ] evaluate docking tasks when switching between various input modalities on both a Lenovo X1 Fold tablet and an iPad. While their focus was on mode switching time, they found that dragging across the curved hinge area of the X1 Fold took longer for both pen and touch input. They did not evaluate how different hinge radii or drag angles could impact performance.

Most other work on curved displays focuses on large, tabletop displays, such as BendDesk [ 24 ] and Curve [ 25 ]. Both systems consist of a 1.2m-wide tabletop display with a 100mm radius curve connecting to a planar vertical display at a 90°  [ 24 ] or 105°  [ 25 ] angle. Various studies explore the usability of these systems. Weiss et al. [ 24 ] evaluate touch-based dragging on three different parts of the BendDesk system: the vertical surface, the horizontal surface, and the curve. For vertical dragging, they found dragging across the curve was 14% slower than on the horizontal area and 10% slower than on the vertical area. Trajectories across the curve were comparable to those on the vertical area, but 3% longer than those on the horizontal area. They also evaluate dragging across the curve with various angles between the source and target. They found that the more acute the angle relative to the curve, the greater the observed dragging trajectory deviated from a direct line. The display orientation also affects pointing tasks. Hennecke et al. [ 8 ] evaluate pointing tasks with touch and mouse input on the Curve system. Display orientation affected touch performance but not mouse performance: touch pointing tasks were 4% faster on the horizontal surface than on the vertical surface. They did not consider pen input or drag tasks.

Some work has explored how the hinge design affects performance. Voelker et al. [ 23 ] compare touch-based flicking gestures on a similar system under three conditions: when a 100mm curve radius is present, when there is an edge between the two display areas, and when there is a gap between the areas. Participants flicked from a starting position on one display to a target on the other display, measuring errors using the angle between the target and actual trajectory. Compared to the gap condition, flicking errors were 46.6% lower for curve and 48.1% lower for edge conditions. Flicking accuracy also decreased as the starting point was moved closer to the curve.

Most relevant to the work considered in this paper is Hennecke et al.’s [ 9 ] evaluation of the same three hinge design conditions for drag tasks at multiple angles with touch and mouse. They found that the curve resulted in 23.6% better accuracy than the edge condition, but the curve had no effect on completion time. Subjective metrics show participants were more satisfied with their speed when there was a curve.

Our work differs in three ways. First, our curved displays have a form factor that more closely aligns with commercially available 15-inch laptops. The smaller size means drag gestures are smaller: in previous studies, a major component of each task involved moving across a large horizontal or vertical surface. Also, the different body postures used with a laptop-sized device will likely affect performance compared to a tabletop-sized device [ 2 ]. Second, instead of only evaluating a hinge radius of 100mm, we compare four smaller curvatures ranging from 1mm to 24mm. These are more realistic radii for devices that fold into a portable shape. For reference, the X1 Fold has a 12mm hinge radius. Third, we compare pen input in addition to standard touch input.

3 EXPERIMENT

We conducted an experiment to explore how hinge radius affects dragging interactions with both pen and touch input. Using metrics such as time and movement variability, we analyse performance to inform the design of future foldable devices and interactions.

Fifteen participants performed drag tasks across the hinge of four input-only prototypes that emulate foldable all-screen laptops. Each prototype is equivalent in size to a foldable 15-inch laptop, with a hinge radius of 1mm, 6mm, 12mm, or 24mm. We selected these hinge radii to include foldable devices available in the market at time of writing. The Lenovo X1 Fold [ 12 ] has the largest radius (12mm) and phones like the Samsung Galaxy Z Fold4 [ 22 ] have hinge radii as small as 1mm. Smaller curvatures allow devices to be more portable when folded. Since all commercially available foldable devices have small curvatures, we limit our exploration to hinge radii 24mm and smaller.

For each device, participants performed short and long drags across the hinge at 12 different angles, both with touch and pen input. In addition, short drag tasks were performed on a smaller commercially available Lenovo X1 foldable laptop with a 12mm hinge radius in order to verify external validity.

3.1 Participants

We recruited 13 right-handed and 2 left-handed participants, ages 21–33, of which 11 were male and 4 were female. Participants were recruited by social media and word-of-mouth and received $ 20 for successful completion of the study. Of the 15 participants, 12 reported they used a touch screen device for more than 60 minutes per day, with the remainder using one for at least 15 minutes per day. We classified the length of our participants’ fingernails based on the length of the free edge, or white tip, of the fingernail. 5 participants had no visible free edge (<0.5mm), 7 had midsize free edges (0.5–2.5mm long), and the remaining 3 had long free edges (over 2.5mm long).

3.2 Apparatus

Each of our four prototypes is composed of three layers. The top layer is a 1.5mm thick, 50 × 30cm clear acrylic sheet. To simulate a foldable all-screen laptop display, the sheet is bent midway so the vertical portion forms a 105° angle, effectively creating a near-vertical 25 × 30cm plane and a horizontal 25 × 30cm plane connected by a hinge area. The 105° angle conforms to ergonomics guidelines, which recommend a line-of-sight of 15° for computer usage [ 18 ]. It is also the angle used by Hennecke et al. [ 9 ] for the Curve desk. Each prototype was bent such that the hinge radius was 1, 6, 12, or 24mm (Fig. 2 ). We selected these radii to represent a variety of potential foldable form factors, where 1mm is the smallest hinge we could reliably create and 12mm is the hinge radius of the Lenovo X1 Fold tablet. To precisely bend each prototype to the appropriate hinge radius, we built a laser-cut form, clamped the acrylic on top, and applied heat with a heat gun (Fig. 3 ). 1

The middle layer is a 50 × 30cm transparent, flexible capacitive touch digitizer made with 0.2mm PET film. 2 The digitizer connects to a Windows device via USB and uses proprietary software to fine-tune its sensitivity.

The bottom layer is a paper print-out showing 24 targets used for drag tasks, each indicated with a “ + ” and a corresponding number or letter. At the center of the sheet, a “ + ” indicates the origin, which is used for calibration.

The layers are adhered together using two-sided tape. During the experiment, prototypes were placed on a wooden stand and clamped in place to ensure the angle and position of the prototype remained constant. The wooden stand was taped 5cm from the edge of the desk to control the participant's distance from the display.

The experiment ran in a custom React web app on the Lenovo X1 Fold tablet. Prototypes were connected by USB to accept touch and pen input (Fig. 4 ). With the exception of a special condition using the X1 Fold's screen instead of a prototype touchscreen, the X1 Fold screen displayed the user's touch points to the experiment facilitator to monitor data validity. The web app logged all trajectory and time data to a database for post-hoc analysis.

The web app also sent real-time data to an iPad web app viewed by the participant. Unlike the facilitator interface, the participant interface only showed the next task to perform, with text like “10–4” or “A–G,” and whether a task was successful (green background flash) or not (red background flash). The participant did not see their current touch positions on the iPad, ensuring they maintained focus on the prototype.

For pen input, we initially used a general-purpose active stylus, but during a pilot test, we found that a passive mesh-tip stylus was more reliable. 3 For the X1 Fold control condition, the Lenovo Mod Pen active stylus was used.

3.3 Task

Tasks involved dragging from a labelled start target, indicated by a “+,” to a similarly labelled end target (Fig. 5 ). Targets were arranged in a circle of 12 targets, centered at the middle of the hinge. There were two circles of targets: an outer circle with 11.4cm diameter and an inner circle with 22.8cm diameter. The outer targets were numbered 1 to 12 and the inner targets were labelled A through L . While participants could take any path between targets, the shortest path always passes through the center of the hinge. Formally, for a task with distance d ∈ {11.4cm, 22.8cm} and angle θ ∈ {0°, 30°, 60°,..., 330°}, the start target has polar coordinates $(\frac{d}{2}, \theta)$ relative to the center of the hinge and the end target has polar coordinates $(\frac{d}{2}, \theta + 180{}^{\circ })$ .

Audio cues indicated when a block ended (“ding!”) and when a trial was successful (“bop!”) or unsuccessful (“boop!”). Errors included when participants dragged between the wrong targets or they lifted off mid-drag. After an error, participants were asked to repeat the trial.

3.4 Procedure

To minimize the learning effect, each session started with a practice stage on a flat prototype, identical to those described in Section 3.2 but without a hinge. First, the facilitator demonstrated the tasks. Then, the participant performed all task variations once with pen and touch input. Practice data was not used for analysis. During the practice stage, participants were asked to perform the touch tasks with whichever finger they felt most comfortable using. In subsequent stages, they were told to only use their preferred finger.

For each of the four prototypes, the participant performed the tasks with pen and touch. At the start of each condition, the participant calibrated the display by touching the targets in order. The calibration data was used to create a piecewise affine transform for touch and pen positions relative to the positions registered by the capacitive digitizer. The facilitator visually verified the calibration points before continuing.

To verify external validity, each participant also performed tasks on a Lenovo X1 Fold, both with touch and a dedicated Lenovo Mod Pen active stylus. Because the X1 Fold has a smaller 13-inch screen, only the shorter 11.4cm tasks were possible. After the experiment, the participant was asked what prototypes and conditions they preferred. Sessions lasted at most 75 minutes.

3.5 Design

This is a within subjects design with two primary independent variables: with 4 levels (,,, and); and with 2 levels (,). Since the special condition is only used in the discussion (Section 5.4 ), we omit it from the levels.

,, and form secondary independent variables. There were 2 variations (and, or 11.4cm and 22.8cm, respectively) and 12 variations (0° to 330°, representing the angle of the start target such that 0° is right to left, 90° is top to bottom, 180° is left to right, and 270° is bottom to top, per Figure 5 d). Each task variation was performed once per. There were 3 blocks per combination of and. The order of with the special condition and were both counter-balanced with a latin square.

A fixed task order was used to make it possible for participants to perform tasks in sequence without visual aids indicating where to touch next. Tasks were always ordered clockwise starting with the drag tasks. This minimized errors where participants would touch the wrong targets. Using the targets in Fig. 5 , tasks were ordered in ascending order by the start target's label: 1, 2,..., 12, A , B ,..., L .

The primary measures computed from logs are Drag Time , and Movement Variability . Drag Time is the time from touch down on the start target until touch up on the end target. This definition is the same as “drag duration” in Weiss et al. [ 24 ], but adapted to our tasks. Movement Variability measures how much drag motion deviates from a line parallel to a straight line between the targets [ 14 ]. More precisely, let the task axis be the line from the start to the end target. Let n represent the number of sample points in a drag gesture, y i represent the distance from the task axis to point i , and $\overline{y}$ represent mean, $\frac{\sum y_i}{n}$ . Then, Movement Variability $= \sqrt {\frac{\sum (y_i - \overline{y})^2}{n - 1}}$ . We report Movement Variability in millimeters.

To provide further insight into how hesitation impacted Drag Time , we also report two secondary measures. Start Hesitation Time is the time from touch down until when the finger begins moving, defined as more than 1mm from the touch down position. End Hesitation Time is analogous, but for touch up.

Since we are interested in movement at the hinge and most of our targets are on planar surfaces, we omit measures that quantify errors finding targets, such as the distance from the drag endpoint and the target position. Error rates are also not relevant to our research question: errors are either due to dragging to the incorrect target or lifting off the screen mid-drag. Drag time and movement variability better quantify the impact of the hinge on performance.

In summary: 4 × 2 × 2 × 12 × 3 = 576 data points per participant on the four prototypes, plus 2 × 12 × 3 = 72 data points per participant on the Lenovo X1 Fold, for a total of 648 data points per participant.

4 RESULTS

We structure our analysis to evaluate pen and touch input separately. Similar to many studies involving repeated tasks, there was an ordering effect for the prototypes ( F 4, 56 = 4.63, p = .003, $\eta _G^2=.25$ ). Generally, the condition presented first was slightly slower than those presented later. However, due to counterbalancing, this impacted all prototypes in a similar manner. We also minimized this effect by having incorporated a practice session before the experiment, as discussed in Section 3.4 .

In the analysis for each, a × × ANOVA with Tukey HSD post hoc tests was used, unless noted otherwise. When the assumption of sphericity was violated, degrees of freedom were corrected using Greenhouse-Geisser (ϵ < 0.75) or Huynh-Feldt (ϵ ≥ 0.75). Residuals for Drag Time were not normally distributed, so log transformed values were used for statistical analysis. Residuals for Movement Variability were normally distributed. For each measure, trials were aggregated by participant and factors being analysed.

4.1 Touch Input

4.1.1 Drag Time. We found that using a 24mm hinge radius decreased Drag Time compared to using a 1mm hinge radius (Figure 6 ). Drag Time is defined as the duration starting at the moment the start target is touched until the finger or pen is released from the end target. There was a main effect of on Drag Time ( F 2, 31 = 3.64, p = .033, $\eta _G^2=.21$ ). Post hoc tests show (1126ms) is faster than (1287ms, p = .002). This represents a 13% time savings on a larger hinge radius.

Dragging horizontally reduced drag time compared to other angles (Fig. 7 ). There was a main effect of on Drag Time ( F 4.0, 56.1 = 29.93, p = .001, $\eta _G^2=.68$ ). Pairwise comparisons show both 0° (1019ms) and 180° (1002ms) were faster than all other angles, which ranged from 1210ms (30°) to 1337ms (240°). No other comparisons are significant. This shows dragging along the hinge can reduce time by 27%.

Along-hinge dragging is best with smaller hinge radii. There was an interaction between and ( F 33, 462 = 1.73, p = .008, $\eta _G^2=.11$ ). Dragging at 180° was always faster than non-horizontal angles for,, and (all p ).05. However, for, 180° was not significantly different from some acutely angled drags (210° and 330°, all p < .05). While horizontal drags generally save time, this benefit is reduced for larger hinge radii.

Unsurprisingly, short drags (1004ms) were 29% faster than long drags (1420ms) ( F 1, 14 = 388.79, p < .001, $\eta _G^2=.97$ ). More interesting is how dragging along the hinge reduced the time penalty from longer drags. There was an interaction between and ( F 5.7, 79.7 = 7.42, p < .001, $\eta _G^2=.35$ ). Post hoc tests found that the performance penalty of longer drags for 0° (+362ms) and 180° (+292ms) was less than most other angles ( p < .05). Thus, dragging along the curve is particularly beneficial for long drag tasks.

Both Start Hesitation Time (79ms) and End Hesitation Time (60ms) were consistent across the conditions. There was no main effect of on Start Hesitation Time ( F 3, 42 = 1.10, p > .05) or End Hesitation Time ( F 3, 42 = 0.17, p > .05).

4.1.2 Movement Variability. Similar to drag time, we found that using a 1mm hinge radius led to more variable motion compared to a 24mm hinge radius. Using the definition in Section 3.5 , Movement Variability is a measure of how much drag motion deviates from a line parallel to a straight line between two targets [ 14 ]. There was a main effect of on Movement Variability ( F 2.6, 36.3 = 3.28, p = .038, $\eta _G^2=1.9$ ), where (2.5mm) has less variability than (2.7mm). This 7% improvement shows that using a large hinge radius can reduce movement.

Movement variability was minimized when drags were along or orthogonal to the hinge (Fig. 8 ). There was a main effect of on Movement Variability ( F 4.7, 65.4 = 13.8, p < .001, $\eta _G^2=.50$ ). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons show that angles along the hinge, 0° (2.1mm) and 180° (2.2mm), or orthogonal to the hinge, 90° (2.2mm) and 270° (2.1mm), all had less variability than acute angles relative to the hinge, 30° (3.0mm), 150° (3.0mm), 210° (2.9mm), and 330° (2.9mm) (all p < .05). By avoiding drags with acute angles close to the curve, movement variability can be reduced by up to 30%.

A 24mm hinge radius made it easier to perform more acute angled tasks. There was an interaction between and on Movement Variability ( F 33, 462 = 1.52, p = .034, $\eta _G^2=.10$ ). Whereas followed the overall trend where along-hinge and orthogonal drags (0°, 90°, 180°, 270°) had significantly less movement variability than acute drags (30°, 150°, 210°, 330°) (all p < .05), post-hoc tests showed had no significant pairwise differences between these angles (all p > .05). For both and, some pairwise differences existed. Overall, using larger hinge radii generally makes it easier to take more direct paths through the curve at acute angles.

Short drags (2.3mm) had less movement variability than long drags (2.8mm) ( F 1, 14 = 70.98, p < .001, $\eta _G^2=.84$ ). Because the Movement Variability measure is independent of the time and length of a drag task, this means paths are 18% more variable when drags are longer, despite how a greater proportion of the drag gesture is at the hinge for short drags. There were no interactions between and other factors.

In summary for touch input: (1) a larger hinge radius can decrease drag time by 13% and movement variability by 7%; (2) dragging along the hinge can reduce drag time by 27%, and this effect is more pronounced for smaller hinge radii and longer distances; (3) dragging along the hinge or orthogonal to the hinge has up to 30% less movement variability than dragging at an acute angle to the hinge, and this effect is more pronounced for smaller hinge radii; and (4) shorter drags have less movement variability.

4.2 Pen Input

4.2.1 Drag Time. Unlike for touch input, hinge radius did not significantly affect Drag Time when using pen input (Fig. 6 ). Thus, hinge radius appears to have more impact on touch input.

Similar to touch input, dragging horizontally along the hinge decreased drag time (Fig. 9 ). There was a main effect of on Drag Time ( F 5.0, 69.5 = 30.37, p < .001, $\eta _G^2=.68$ ). Pairwise comparisons show both 0° (1080ms) and 180° (1114ms) were faster than all other angles, which ranged from 1295ms (270°) to 1412ms (60°) (all p < .001). Dragging along the hinge can reduce drag time by as much as 24%.

Again, short drags (1078ms) were 29% faster than long drags (1526ms) with pen input ( F 1, 14 = 379.37, p < .001, $\eta _G^2=.96$ ). Long, angled drags from the top of the screen were particularly slow. There was an interaction between and ( F 4.9, 67.9 = 2.69, p = .029, $\eta _G^2=.16$ ). Post-hoc tests show 30° (+521ms), 60° (+531ms), and 120° (+526ms) took more time than most other angles for long drags ( p < .05).

Both Start Hesitation Time (85ms) and End Hesitation Time (54ms) were consistent across the conditions. There was no main effect of on Start Hesitation Time ( F 3, 42 = 1.65, p > .05) or End Hesitation Time ( F 3, 42 = 0.60, p > .05).

4.2.2 Movement Variability. Hinge radius did not directly affect movement variability for pen input.

Unlike with touch input, movement variability is not minimized when dragging along the curve with pen input (Fig. 10 ). It is only minimized when dragging orthogonal to the hinge. There was a main effect of on Movement Variability ( F 5.0, 70.2 = 8.66, p < .001, $\eta _G^2=.38$ ). Post-hoc comparisons show 0° (2.6mm) and 180° (2.9mm) both have no significant differences ( p > .05) except with 270° (2.0mm) ( p = .038 and p = .005, resp.). Thus, dragging horizontally with pen input does not minimize movement variability. However, 90° (2.1mm) and 270° (2.0mm) have less movement variability than acutely angled drags, which had average variability as high as 3.3mm (150°). Using vertical drags can reduce movement variability by up to 39%, with no benefit found for horizontal drags.

Similar to touch input, short drags (2.4mm) had less movement variability than long drags (2.8mm) with pen input ( F 1, 14 = 85.39, p < .001, $\eta _G^2=.86$ ). Thus, paths are 14% more variable when drags are longer. There was no interaction between and other factors.

In summary for pen input: (1) hinge radius does not affect drag time or movement variability; (2) dragging along the hinge can reduce drag time by 24%, but it does not improve movement variability; (3) dragging orthogonal to the hinge has up to 39% less movement variability than dragging at an acute angle to the hinge; and (4) shorter drags have less movement variability.

5 DISCUSSION

Our experiment shows large hinge radii improves performance for touch input, but seems to have no effect on pen input. This section explores how these quantitative results compare to qualitative feedback from the experiment. Using this, we formulate design recommendations for both hardware and interaction designers.

5.1 Comparing Hinge Radii and Input Methods

As discussed in Section 4 , for touch input, drag time and movement variability were best with a 24mm hinge radius. This was supported by participant preferences: 13/15 participants preferred a hinge radius greater than 1mm and 11/15 preferred a 24mm hinge radius overall. The difference between the 1mm condition and others was particularly notable. After completing the 1mm touch condition, participants made comments such as: “it's kind of different, specifically when you want to cross [the hinge]” (P14), “this one was hard” (P12), and “steep curvature sucks” (P4).

Generally, the 1mm hinge radius touch condition was more challenging because participants lost contact with the display at the hinge (P2, P8, P10, P15). In many cases, this was due to the fingernail preventing uniform contact on the more extreme concavity created by a smaller hinge radius (P2, P8, P15). To accommodate this, almost all participants altered their hand posture, twisting their wrist as they moved across the curve. This alteration was most pronounced for the 1mm condition. P10 noted that the 1mm condition was “uncomfortable in a pain sort of way, you impact [the hinge] if you go fast.”

We observed participants with longer fingernails lose contact with the display when dragging on smaller curvatures, leading to multiple failed trials early in the experiment. However, they quickly learned how to adjust their hand posture to accommodate. Using post-hoc analysis of the touch data, we found that fingernail length, as classified in Section 3.1 , did not affect Drag Time ( F 2, 12 = 0, p > .05) or Movement Variability ( F 2, 12 = .12, p > .05).

Participants said the 24mm hinge radius condition caused less fatigue (P14), facilitated smoother motion (P5, P14), and made it “quicker to get from one place to another” (P5). However, this was not a global preference. P1 found the 1mm condition better than expected, noting it was still easy to drag across the hinge for them. Both P9 and P13 preferred the medium hinge radius of 12mm. P13 said that on a large 24mm hinge, the curved hinge surprised them: “visually, I don't see the curve at all at certain angles... It feels like I should be able to do a straight line, and at some point my finger is like, ‘oh no you're changing directions.’ ” Only P3 did not notice any differences between the conditions. Nevertheless, most participants preferred the larger hinge radius for touch input.

With pen input, most participants had the same preferences, with two exceptions. First, P11 found no differences between any conditions when using pen input. Second, P15 preferred a 1mm hinge radius because it was easier to visually follow a straight line to the end target. Our findings are similar to past results for flicking [ 23 ] and dragging [ 9 ] on tabletop curved displays, in which participants preferred a large curve joining vertical and horizontal displays over an edge or bezel. Overall, most users preferred the 24mm hinge radius.

5.2 Comparing Drag Angles

Supporting the quantitative results in Section 4 , participants found along-hinge gestures easier than across-hinge gestures (P4, P7, P9, P10). P4 described dragging along the hinge as “satisfying” and P10 said targets on the hinge were “really easy” to find because of the tactile feedback. However, while the quantitative data does not show any differences between vertical across-hinge upward drags and vertical across-hinge downward drags with touch input, participants preferred going downwards (P2, P4, P14). Particularly on devices with a smaller hinge radius, both P2 and P4 found that going upwards was challenging because the finger would lose contact with the screen at the hinge. While there was no significant interaction between hinge radius and drag angle, there was a perceived difference for touch input.

Acutely angled drags were significantly slower than horizontal drags, but participants had sometimes contrasting opinions regarding acutely angled tasks on a 1mm hinge radius with touch input. P1 found these tasks easy because “when you reach the [hinge], you're already touching the other side.” However, P13 used a strategy to avoid acute angles altogether: “I went straight down or up to the curve then found the next [target].” No participants commented about perceived differences for different angles with pen input.

Overall, dragging along the hinge is fastest and preferred, and for touch input, dragging downwards is preferred to dragging upwards on devices with a smaller hinge radius.

5.3 Comparing to Previous Studies

Most previous studies on curved displays focus on different tasks, such as pointing [ 8 , 20 ], flicking [ 23 ], and aiming [ 24 ], or metrics, such as mode switching time [ 5 ] and user preferences [ 25 ]. BendDesk [ 24 ] and Hennecke et al. [ 9 ] are the only works that measure dragging performance on curved devices, but only for touch input and the devices are tabletop-sized. In contrast to our laptop-sized 30cm-by-50cm prototypes, they were 90 × 80cm [ 9 ] and 104 × 104cm [ 24 ]. Our results show that device size reduces the impact of drag performance and movement patterns, and we also evaluate pen input in this context.

The BendDesk study examined upward touch dragging at angles 270° ± θ for θ ∈ {0°, 15°, 25°, 35°, 45°} (angles are visualized in Fig. 5 d). Unlike our prototypes, the BendDesk device had a fixed 100mm hinge radius forming a 90° angle between upper and lower display areas. They found no differences in drag duration between the movement angles, which our results confirm, and our results also show that downward dragging movements behave similarly. In addition, our work also evaluates dragging along the curve. We show this type of movement has a lower drag duration compared to other angles, especially for longer drag gestures.

Additionally, the BendDesk study observed much longer dragging trajectories for more acute angles. Our results extend this to a wider range of angles and we show the trend continues for even more acute angles, with the exception of dragging along the curve (see Section 4.1.2 ). However, in all conditions, the movement patterns we observed were much more consistent than those observed with BendDesk (Fig. 11 ). Instead of the curved paths between targets observed in BendDesk, our participants tended to move in a more direct path. We hypothesize this is due to reduced physical effort on a smaller device. In our study, participants were able to leverage wrist motion to minimize overall arm motion, which is not possible on a large display like BendDesk where the arm is more extended. Additionally, the more extreme 90° angle between the horizontal and vertical displays on BendDesk may have contributed to more movement deviation.

The primary focus of our paper is how hinge radius impacts performance, which Hennecke et al. [ 9 ] partially explored on their tabletop sized curved display. Like our prototypes, their device had a 105° angle. They compare three versions of the device: a 100mm hinge radius, a 0mm hinge edge, and a raised bezel between upper and lower displays. The raised bezel was created with a 3mm thick wooden strip covering the hinge, thereby separating the two displays. Unlike our work, they found no difference in drag time between the device conditions, despite the larger differences in hinge radius (0–100mm). This is likely because of their interaction design and limitations in touch sensing. Their prototypes could not reliably track finger position across the curve or edge, so they allowed flicking across the curve or edge, then continuing the drag on the other side. This suggests that using a combination of flicking and dragging can avoid the performance cost we found when dragging across a small hinge radius. Their study did not report movement variability during the drag motion, but pointing accuracy was found to improve with the 100mm hinge radius.

In summary, our work contributes insights on how hinge radius and drag angle affect drag performance and movement patterns.

5.4 Limitations

Comparing multiple foldable all-screen laptops was not possible for this work because only one device was commercially available: the Lenovo X1 Fold. Using custom prototypes allowed us to precisely control hinge radius, but it also resulted in a few limitations.

First, the capacitive digitizers did not support active styluses. We instead used a mesh tip passive stylus with 6mm diameter. This made it slightly harder because there was more friction when dragging across the device (P9). To understand how this difference in pen tip affected performance, we compare our prototype with our passive stylus to the device with its active stylus. In the following analysis, we use a × ANOVA with Tukey HSD post hoc tests, where has two levels: (our prototype) and. We also filtered data to only drags because the had too small a screen for drags.

As expected, there was no significant overall difference in Drag Time between the two prototypes ( F 1, 14 = .02, p = .89, $\eta _G^2=.001$ ). This validates the comparability of our prototypes overall. However, input method only impacted Drag Time for the 12mm prototype. There was an interaction between and ( F 1, 14 = 13.48, p = .49, $\eta _G^2=.003$ ), where was faster than ( p = .031) but was not significantly different from ( p > .17). While our experiment showed touch is faster than a pen with a mesh stylus, this finding suggests the two conditions would likely be more comparable with an active stylus. Trends with are similar between the two, supporting the external validity of our experiment.

The second limitation is that the prototypes are more sensitive than typical device screens. Thus, drags were typically still successful if the finger lifted off the screen slightly at the hinge. By contrast, commercially available devices like the X1 Fold only detect a finger at the hinge if significant pressure is applied. Interestingly, the X1 Fold's low sensitivity caused a large number of failed trials when the finger passed over the hinge too lightly. These trials were not included in our data and participants were asked to retry the failed trials. However, this suggests usability could be improved by increasing sensitivity at the hinge or compensating for the reduced sensitivity in software. While our prototypes are more sensitive than commercially available devices, this allowed participants to perform the tasks without applying unnecessary additional pressure.

Third, due to hardware limitations, participants could not rest their arm on the prototypes when performing tasks. This forced participants to keep their arm slightly raised for the duration of the experiment. While this is expected behaviour for gestures on vertical displays, it may have resulted in some arm fatigue. We mitigated this by encouraging participants to take breaks after each block. Results showed no sign of decreased performance in later conditions of the experiment.

5.5 Using Crossing-Based Interfaces

The hinge can be harnessed for crossing-based interfaces, in which dragging across the hinge at a particular location triggers some functionality. For usability, a guideline for a minimum width of these hinge crossing targets is useful for designers. In the context of planar touch surfaces, Luo and Vogel [ 13 ] recommend a minimum target width of 12.7mm. To provide similar guidance when crossing the hinge, we analysed logged data from short vertical drag tasks (90° and 270°). While participants were not told to drag in a straight vertical line across the hinge, all participants did so for these tasks specifically. We found that for touch input, 95% of drags passed through a 12.6mm-wide target at the hinge. Similarly, for pen input, 95% of drags passed through a 12.2mm-wide target. These findings are nearly identical to a planar surface, suggesting that similar guidelines can be used.

6 DESIGN RECOMMENDATIONS

We make the following recommendations for dragging interactions on foldable devices:

  • R1: Use a larger hinge radius for touch input. Hardware designers should use a large radius, such as 24mm, to decrease drag time and movement variability while facilitating easier drag gestures.
  • R2: Use any of the evaluated hinge radii (1–24mm) for pen input. Hinge radius did not have a detectable impact on performance for pen input.
  • R3: Leverage drags along the hinge. In all conditions, dragging along the hinge was faster than other tasks. Along-hinge dragging provided a tactile marker to guide the finger between targets.
  • R4: When crossing the hinge, avoid interactions that require acute angles. These were slower and had more movement variability. Some participants intentionally altered their dragging movements to always cross the hinge area at an orthogonal angle.
  • R5: Leverage downward drags to cross the hinge. While quantitative analysis did not show a difference between upward and downward drags, participants would more frequently lift their finger off the screen at the hinge when dragging upward, especially on devices with a smaller hinge radius.
  • R6: For hinge-based crossing interfaces, use targets at least 12.6mm wide for touch and 12.2mm wide for pen input. Crossing targets on the hinge that are wider than these thresholds should have error rates under 5%.

7 USE CASES

Since foldable all-screen laptops are a recent trend, very few applications take advantage of their unique properties. Past work has briefly mentioned use cases for the curved hinge on large displays. Weiss et al. [ 24 ] suggest users could create content on the hinge before displaying it on the vertical display, or alternatively, use the hinge as a dock for content created on the horizontal surface. Wimmer et al. [ 25 ] suggest using the curved shape of the device for 3D interaction. Neither work implements or elaborates on these potential use cases.

Our context is different. Foldable all-screen laptops are much smaller than tabletop displays, so both the content and interactions on the curve are more integrated. We use our design recommendations in application demonstrations to illustrate possible use cases for along-hinge and across-hinge interactions. 4

7.1 Along-Hinge Interactions

Since along-hinge dragging is fast and easy (R3), our first demo uses these gestures to open quick action and multitasking menus. In Windows, swiping from the right edge opens the notification center and swiping from the left edge opens a panel of widgets [ 15 ]. Since these gestures can start at any point along the bezel of the screen, they are easily confused with horizontal scrolling gestures. To avoid this ambiguity, we use swipes along the hinge.

Instead of swiping anywhere on the bezel, users swipe along the hinge from the right edge to open a horizontal quick actions bar, with options like volume, brightness, and Wi-Fi (Fig. 12 a). To adjust one-dimensional continuous parameters, like volume and brightness, users touch the appropriate icon and drag along the hinge (Fig. 12 b). A similar action can be used for a discrete set of options. For example, dragging from the Wi-Fi icon opens a set of possible networks, and releasing at various hinge positions selects a network (Fig. 12 c). Keeping interactions on the hinge avoids obstructing other applications while providing tactile guidance for where to drag.

Swiping from the left edge opens a taskbar that hides when not in use (Fig. 12 d). Dragging an application up or down from the taskbar snaps the application to the top or bottom section of the screen, while tapping opens the application in fullscreen.

7.2 Across-Hinge Interactions

Our second demo involves dragging application windows across the hinge to perform window management. In many desktop environments, users can drag a window to a corner of the screen to snap it in place. This is intuitive, but can involve long drag gestures. We present an alternative that uses the hinge as a tactile marker while avoiding long drags.

When a user starts dragging a window, icons appear at the hinge for snapping the window size and position to the left, right, and center, as well as for closing the window (Fig. 13 a). Dragging the window through an icon triggers the corresponding action (Fig. 13 b). The tactile feedback upon crossing the hinge indicates when the interaction is complete. Since interactions depend solely on where the hinge is crossed, users can drag at whatever angle is comfortable for them (R4). Furthermore, using a small number of large crossing targets maximizes performance (R6).

Our third demo demonstrates how multiple across-hinge drags can be combined into a single gesture. In a drawing application, dragging a pre-existing shape triggers a popup menu at the hinge, facilitating common tasks like duplicating, deleting, and changing the colour of the shape. Dragging the shape through the duplicate icon (Fig. 14 b), then curving back towards the hinge and selecting a colour (Fig. 14 c) creates a copy of the object, selects the colour, and positions it in 2D space in a single gesture. This facilitates quick interactions that use the hinge for its tactile feedback. As before, users can drag at any angle (R4) through large targets (R6).

Hinge-based drags can be used as a touch-based alternative to a right mouse click menu for arbitrary applications. Our fourth demo combines across- and along-hinge dragging in a file manager. When dragging a file, a series of common file operations appears at the hinge, including duplicate, rename, share, and open. Actions like duplicate and rename could be performed in sequence by dragging through one (Fig. 15 b), then curving and dragging through the next (Fig. 15 c). These drags can be done at any angle based on user preferences (R4). Other actions like share and open could be triggered by dragging to the icon at the hinge (Fig. 15 e). Once the finger reaches the hinge, the interface displays additional options along the hinge: for instance, for share or open, application icons appear. Sliding along the hinge to the appropriate application completes the interaction (Fig. 15 f). This combines horizontal dragging (R3) with a crossing based interface with large targets (R6). Additionally, because files are arranged top-to-bottom on the screen, downward drag gestures are more common in the interface (R5), making the interactions easier to perform.

8 CONCLUSION

Our work explores how the hinge radius of a foldable all-screen laptop impacts across- and along-hinge dragging. Our findings show that while hinge radius has no effect on pen input, a larger hinge radius improves performance for touch input. We also discovered dragging along the hinge is faster than dragging across the hinge, especially compared to dragging at acute angles relative to the hinge. We created a series of demos illustrating how day-to-day computing environments can leverage along- and across-hinge dragging interactions. Our work provide guidance for both hardware and interaction designers seeking to create devices and interactions that are usable with multiple input methods.
