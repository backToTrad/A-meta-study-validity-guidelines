Meeting Your Virtual Twin: Effects of Photorealism and Personalization on Embodiment, Self-Identification and Perception of Self-Avatars in Virtual Reality

Embodying virtual twins – photorealistic and personalized avatars – will soon be easily achievable in consumer-grade VR. For the first time, we explored how photorealism and personalization impact self-identification, as well as embodiment, avatar perception and presence. Twenty participants were individually scanned and, in a two-hour session, embodied four avatars (high photorealism personalized, low photorealism personalized, high photorealism generic, low photorealism generic). Questionnaire responses revealed stronger mid-immersion body ownership for the high photorealism personalized avatars compared to all other avatar types, and stronger embodiment for high photorealism compared to low photorealism avatars and for personalized compared to generic avatars. In a self-other face distinction task, participants took significantly longer to pause the face morphing videos of high photorealism personalized avatars, suggesting a stronger self-identification bias with these avatars. Photorealism and personalization were perceptually positive features; how employing these avatars in VR applications impacts users over time requires longitudinal investigation.

1 INTRODUCTION

Recent developments in the field of computer graphics have enabled the production of photorealistic and personalized avatars that can be animated in real-time in virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR) [ 64 ]. In this paper, avatars will refer to users’ self-representations that can be embodied in virtual environments. In psychology, embodiment is broadly defined as the experience of ownership over one's body, which is voluntarily controlled by the individual (sense of agency) and whose boundaries one feels located inside of (sense of self-location) [ 13 , 31 , 35 ]. Essentially, embodiment is experienced when the properties of an external entity that is embodied are processed in the same way as the properties of one's own body [ 31 ]. As seen in a vast range of literature dating back to the rubber hand illusion [ 8 ] and more recently to VR applications, individuals can experience embodiment in relation to a variety of external objects including virtual avatar bodies following multisensory integration of congruent streams of sensory information [ 30 , 32 , 47 , 50 ]. A related bodily illusion is the enfacement illusion, where following multisensory integration, individuals can experience self-identification with another person's face, which together with embodiment, is crucial for understanding body perception [ 42 , 45 , 55 ].

Due to the extensive possibilities for avatar customization enabled by VR technologies, it is possible to flexibly embody a variety of avatar types from highly photorealistic personalized avatars [ 57 ], to cartoonish avatars [ 37 ], to edited humanoid shapes that incorporate additional limbs/tail [ 52 ] and even well-known figures, e.g., Albert Einstein [ 5 ]. Photorealism refers to the quality of the graphics of avatars to resemble actual humans as opposed to cartoonish/abstract versions of humans (regardless of their identity) [ 61 ]. In addition to photorealism, the effect of personalization has also been investigated [ 3 , 4 , 57 ]. Personalization is achieved when avatars closely resemble their users by maintaining their identity and specific appearance features (as opposed to resembling other individuals) [ 57 ]. Personalized avatars can be generated using depth cameras [ 10 , 33 ], photogrammetry methods that typically require a rig of cameras capturing the body and the face and a processing pipeline for the reconstruction of the images to generate 3D scans [ 1 ] or even low-cost solutions that use smartphone cameras to create videos capturing multiple angles of a person [ 6 ]. In the current context of increased technological development towards enabling social VR scenarios where individuals could be represented by high fidelity avatars, it is crucial to investigate how photorealism and personalization systematically interact and impact avatar embodiment, self-identification, and perceptual acceptance.

In this paper, we present the first detailed investigation of the interplay between photorealism and personalization on embodiment, self-identification and avatar perception for embodied avatars animated in real-time by users. We develop an avatar production pipeline that generated avatars that showed potential for overcoming the Uncanny Valley as well as corresponding lower photorealism versions. This research also contributes to the HCI literature by: (1) Adapting a behavioural self-other face distinction task to investigate self-identification with high photorealism personalized avatars in comparison to lower photorealism and generic avatars and (2) Adopting a holistic approach: showing the individual and collective impact photorealism and personalization can have on three different facets of bodily perception (embodiment, self-identification, appeal and external avatar perception variables).

2 RELATED WORK

Multiple studies have investigated the effect avatar photorealism can have on embodiment in VR. For instance, employing full-body motion capture, [ 36 ] induced the full body ownership illusion over avatars ranging in realism that included a robot, a block-man and a photorealistic human. The study showed that there were no differences in skin conductance responses (SCRs) – a measure used as a physiological proxy for embodiment – during a task that threatened each avatar body type. In terms of questionnaire ratings, there were no overall differences in embodiment between the three conditions, but participants reported that they felt they had two distinct bodies in the human avatar condition. This suggests that the human avatar was not accepted as one's own body potentially due to the Uncanny Valley effect [ 40 ]. This effect has been found to occur when individuals interact with or are embodied as highly photorealistic human avatars, which are perceived as eerie due to subtle inconsistencies in their appearance or movement [ 39 , 40 , 60 ]. However, more recent research [ 29 ] suggested that current advances in the field of graphics (i.e., the state-of-the-art MetaHumans enabled by the Unreal Engine) allow the creation of photorealistic avatars that might be able to overcome the Uncanny Valley effect and are in fact perceived as less eerie and more attractive than less photorealistic avatars. This finding corroborated previous research [ 63 ] that also recommended photorealism as a desirable avatar feature. However, in [ 29 ], these high-fidelity photorealistic avatars were not embodied, they were only viewed on a computer screen. Therefore, whether these findings can be extrapolated to 3D embodied photorealistic avatars demands further investigation.

In terms of personalization, Waltemate et al. [ 57 ] produced 3D photogrammetry scans of their participants and compared their level of embodiment (body ownership and agency) as well as presence when their avatars, viewed in a virtual mirror, were a generic hand-modelled version, a generic scanned version and an individualized scanned version. Presence is defined as a subjective property of VR, which refers to users’ responses to the virtual simulation and has been conceptualised as two orthogonal constructs – place illusion (the sensation of “being there” in the environment) and plausibility illusion (the sensation that what is happening in VR is happening to the user) [ 48 , 49 , 51 ]. As shown in [ 57 ], participants reported increased body ownership, agency and presence for the individualized scanned version compared to the generic scanned version, and the generic hand-modelled avatar. All avatars had the same level of photorealism. These findings were also corroborated by [ 26 ], who attempted to embody their participants in avatars viewed from a third person perspective that ranged from a robot, an avatar in a sci-fi suit to a doppelgänger. Participants reported increased body ownership and increased vulnerability while completing threatening VR tasks when embodied as the doppelgänger compared to the other avatars. Therefore, avatar personalization appears to increase embodiment.

More recently, Ma and Pan [ 37 ] manipulated the level of photorealism of personalized self-avatars to investigate avatar embodiment and perception. Using minimal body tracking and facial animation enabled by the VIVE Facial Tracker, participants were embodied as a photorealistic and a cartoonish personalized avatar. The avatars were generated using a photo portrait of the participant and the CC3 AI generator plugin Headshot. In a second iteration of the study, participants were also embodied in a gender-matched photorealistic and cartoonish generic avatar that used higher motion-fidelity than the first iteration. The authors reported no embodiment and avatar perception differences (i.e., humanness, eerie, spine-tingling), between the photorealistic and cartoonish avatars both for the personalized and generic avatars and no differences between the personalized and generic avatars, which contradicts previously discussed findings, e.g., [ 57 ]. Attractiveness was the only avatar perception variable where there was a significant difference – the cartoon avatar was rated as more attractive than the photorealistic one. It is important to highlight the relatively small sample size, overly represented by females (5/7 participants) and the lack of full body motion capture, as the researchers only employed headset and controller-enabled tracking. Moreover, there was no explicit investigation of the interplay between photorealism and personalization under the same experimental setup with the same level of motion fidelity.

Furthermore, a secondary focus of [ 37 ] was the enfacement illusion. The authors measured participants’ self-face perception using four enfacement questions taken from [ 17 , 18 ] and reported no differences between the different types of avatars in terms of self-identification and perceived face ownership. However, the authors did not use any behavioural tasks such as a self-other face distinction task, which might be able to capture more subtle details and is not prone to bias like questionnaire responses [ 15 , 54 ]. The self-other face distinction task consists of presenting the participant with a face morph ranging from 0% self 100% other to 100% self 0% other and vice versa. Participants are instructed to pause the video when they think the face either starts to look like themselves (other-self morph) or like the other (self-other morph) and the more delayed they are to pause the video, the stronger the enfacement illusion and thus, the stronger the self-identification with the other face [ 15 , 24 ]. Recently, Gonzalez-Franco et al. [ 24 ] used different levels of facial animation of cartoon generic avatars and a self-other face distinction task and reported that enabling facial animation leads to stronger enfacement illusion and thus to increased self-identification with the avatars compared to no facial animation. However, it is unclear whether this effect would be more pronounced when embodying personalized avatars that closely resemble users’ real appearance compared to generic avatars.

It is critical to investigate how being embodied as one's own photorealistic and personalized avatar can impact users’ embodiment levels, self-identification with the avatar, avatar perception and VR experience (i.e., presence), especially as social VR platforms and applications that accommodate social interactions could soon be populated by high fidelity avatars that closely resemble their users. Understanding how individuals react to these avatars and which combination of avatar characteristics could lead to the optimal embodied experience is a top priority for HCI research. From a technical standpoint, it is also paramount to determine whether producing these high visual and motor fidelity avatars is desirable, especially considering the high computational power and high-end GPUs necessary to enable these [ 29 ].

We formulated several hypotheses. Firstly, participants will experience stronger embodiment (questionnaire and SCRs) for high photorealism and personalized avatars compared to low photorealism and generic ones. In terms of self-identification, participants will self-identify the most with the high photorealism personalized avatar. With regards to avatar perception, participants will experience higher visual appeal and likeability for low photorealism compared to high photorealism avatars as well as higher realism and eeriness for high photorealism compared to low photorealism avatars. Participants will experience higher familiarity and self-resemblance for the personalized avatars compared to the generic. Finally, participants will experience stronger presence when embodying the high photorealism and personalized avatars compared to the low photorealism and generic ones.

3 METHODS

3.1 Participants

Twenty participants (nine males, 11 females) took part in the experiment. Due to the Covid-19 restriction at the time the experiment was conducted, all participants were members of staff or students at an UK university, but none worked directly with the researchers on this project. All participants were recruited through opportunity sampling, word of mouth and from two mailing lists in the Psychology and Computer Science departments at the UK university. Participants’ age ranged from 21 to 49 ( M = 29.00, SD = 6.70). According to the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory [ 41 ], two participants were left-handed, while 18 participants were right-handed. In terms of ethnicity, all participants self-identified as Caucasian. Four participants reported that they had never used VR before, whereas 16 participants had used VR. This aspect might not be representative of the entire population, but it is unlikely that it has affected the findings reported. Six participants did not have English as their first language, whereas 14 did.

3.2 Materials

3.2.1 Avatar construction. We created a high photorealism personalized, low photorealism personalized, high photorealism generic and low photorealism generic avatar for all 20 participants. See Figure 1 (A) for an example of all avatars embodied by a female participant, Figure 1 (B) for an example of all the avatars embodied by a male participant and Figure 1 (C) for a close-up of the avatars’ facial details.

All participants were scanned employing a photogrammetry setup consisting of a 78-camera array. The scans were processed into a mesh using Agisoft Metashape. R3DS Wrap 2020.12.2 was used to create a lower resolution version of the scan that could be skinned to a skeleton and driven with motion capture. This lower resolution model is common to our avatar pipeline and could be used to automatically generate a skinned avatar with skeleton and a personalized Vicon solving setup. To do this, we used our avatar pipeline rig, which was made in Maya 2022. Because we used the same mesh topology across all avatars, that meant we could set up our rig to place the joints within the mesh automatically. More specifically, we used Maya's point on poly constraints to place locators at specific landmarks on the mesh (e.g., wrists, elbows, etc.), and then used a combination of constraints to place and orient the joints with respect to these locators. The mesh was then skinned to this skeleton. Again, because we used the same topology across all avatars, we could use the same skin weights for all of them as well. The photorealistic texture was acquired by using Agisoft Metashape to reproject the photos from the photogrammetry onto the lower resolution mesh. See Figure 2 for an array of high photorealism personalized avatars created and employed in the study.

For the low photorealism personalized avatars, the same model and skeleton as the high photorealism personalized ones was used, but the texture was manually painted in Adobe Photoshop to be a low photorealism equivalent of the photorealistic one.

The generic avatars were made employing two generic scans (one male, one female) acquired from the 3D scan store (male: https://www.3dscanstore.com/people-3d-models/male-01-clothing-05 ; female: https://www.3dscanstore.com/clothed-3d-models/female-arch-viz-40), which were chosen to be of a highly similar quality to the ones produced in our studio. For each participant, the generic avatar was uniformly scaled to be roughly the right size. Then, each joint was changed to be the same length as the equivalent one on the participant, thereby deforming the generic model and ensuring that the proportions matched the participant's personalized avatar. From there, the generic avatar could be treated the same as the personalized one, from the point of view of solving in Shogun and streaming into Unity.

For the generic low photorealism avatars, the same process as for the low photorealism personalized avatars was used – i.e., the texture was manually painted using Adobe Photoshop.

3.2.2 Motion capture. To animate the avatars in real-time based on participants’ motions, we employed a 20-camera Vicon motion capture system running Shogun Live 1.6. We used a 10-finger production marker set, which means 57 body markers plus 20 finger ones that were attached to the motion capture suit by two experimenters (see Figure 3 ). No facial tracking was used. The capture area was approximately 5 m 2 . Body movements and Vive headset position/rotation were streamed into (and aligned in) Unity using Vicon's Unity plugin. Shogun Live and Unity were both run on the same machine, which has a 3.5GHz Intel Core i9-10920X CPU, 64GB RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3080.

3.2.3 Virtual environment. The virtual environment, consisting of a 3x3 meter room with a mirror, was custom created using the Unity3D game engine (see Figure 4 ). The virtual mirror was included based on previous evidence suggesting that it is an essential element for embodiment induction as it enables the participant to experience the real-time visuo-motor synchrony between the participants and their avatars [ 24 ].

The virtual mirror effect was created by duplicating and scaling by -1 across one axis the entire scene (including the avatar). The curtains were created using Maya (version 2022) fabric simulation and were then saved as a static model and brought into Unity. For the threatening event meant to stimulate SCRs, we used a box which was programmed to fall to induce a stress reaction, which was an asset acquired from the Unity Asset store. This was programmed to fall close to the participants’ avatar body to threaten its integrity exactly one minute and 15 seconds after the scene started. After this event occurred, participants were shown three mid-immersion questions on the virtual mirror (see Figure 5 ).

The virtual environment was experienced using the HTC Vive Pro Eye headset that has Dual OLED 3.5" diagonal screen with a resolution of 1440 x 1600 pixels per eye (2880 x 1600 pixels combined) a 90 Hz refresh rate and 110 degrees refresh rate.

3.2.4 Questionnaire. Participants were first presented with the information sheet and consent form. Like [ 57 ], we employed both three brief questions administered while participants were still immersed in the environment and a complete questionnaire once participants took their headset off. The mid-immersion questions, adapted from [ 57 ] and originally from the main questionnaires employed described below, were Q1: “I felt as if the virtual body was my body.”, Q2: “I felt like I could control the virtual body as if it was my own body.” and Q3: “I feel present in the virtual environment right now.” with the definition of presence provided.

After each condition, we administered a version of the Embodiment questionnaire, initially developed by [ 23 ] and revised by [ 44 ] (see Supplementary Materials). The questionnaire comprised of 16 questions such as Q3: “During this trial there were moments in which I felt as if the movements of the virtual body were influencing my own movements.”, Q5: “During this trial there were moments in which it felt as if my real body was starting to take on the posture or shape of the virtual body that I saw.” and Q10: “ During this trial there were moments in which I felt as if the virtual body was my body.”. Q10 was also used as a mid-immersion questionnaire item. Q8 and Q9 were completed with the words “anxiety sensation” and “object fall” resulting in the items: Q8: “During this trial there were moments in which I felt an anxiety sensation in my body when I saw the object fall.” and Q9: “During this trial there were moments in which I felt that my own body could be affected by the falling object.”. For Q14, Q15, Q16, the gaps were filled with “floor”. The items were rated on a 7-point Likert scale (from 1 = strongly disagree, to 7 = strongly agree). The order of the questions was randomized in each trial.

To assess presence, we administered the SUS questionnaire [ 56 ] because it is commonly used to assess presence (both the place and plausibility illusions) and it is shorter than the other popular alternative (i.e., Witmer and Singer Presence questionnaire [ 58 ]) which has 32 items, as we tried to keep the experimental procedure duration under two hours. The SUS only has six items including Q1: “I have a sense of ‘being there’ in the virtual room” and Q5: “I think of the virtual room as a place in a way similar to other places that I've been today...”. Participants rated their sense of presence on a 7-point Likert scale with the ends customized for each item (see Supplementary Materials for more details).

We also measured a range of avatar perception variables, namely, visual appeal, likeability, eeriness, familiarity, self-resemblance and realism, which were based on measures previously used by [ 63 ] and [ 38 ]. They were all rated on a 7-point Likert scale (from 1 = strongly disagree, to 7 = strongly agree). These questionnaire parts were repeated four times, once/condition.

Finally, participants completed a series of demographic questions including their gender, age, ethnicity, experience with VR, first language, the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory [ 41 ]. Participants were asked to indicate which hand or foot they used to perform 10 activities including writing or using a spoon on a three-point Likert scale with the options: “Left”, “No preference” and “Right”. We also assessed participants’ skin tone using the Fitzpatrick scale [ 20 ]. This was included to identify any self-avatar perception differences based on participants’ skin tone (i.e., potential differences in the participant-avatar skin tone match), but as all participants were in the Type 2 category, this aspect was not included in the analysis. Participants were then re-directed to the debrief sheet and the final consent form, where they could give consent to having their email addresses stored to be contacted about future research using their personalized avatars.

3.2.5 Skin Conductance Response. A BioPac MP160 Wireless was used to record participants’ SCRs. The MP160 Data Acquisition Unit received signals from the two BioPac electrodes placed on participants’ non-dominant index and middle fingertips. The BioPac MP160 Data Acquisition Unit was connected to a 2.8GHz Dell XPS i7 4GB GPU, 8GB RAM, which was running the AcqKnowledge software. The experimenter manually used the F7 key to mark the relevant events (i.e., the fall of the virtual box and the start of a new condition).

3.2.6 Self-Other Face Distinction Task. In line with previous studies, e.g. [ 25 ], the morphing task consisted of two types of videos per condition – one video showing the morphing sequence from the avatar to the self (Avatar-Self) and one video showing the morphing sequence from the self to the avatar (Self-Avatar), depicted in Figure 6 . We used one frontal photograph of the participants taken during the initial photogrammetry session and one frontal screenshot of each of the four avatars. The facemorpher library built on Python and OpenCV was used to create 100 frames that correspond to 1% increments of morph transformation from the photo of the participant to the avatar and vice versa. The videos were then assembled with the same library with a 5 frames/second refresh rate. The final 20-second videos were viewed on a MacBook Pro screen once in the self-avatar and avatar-self direction for all four avatars before the VR exposure. After the VR exposure, participants were shown the specific morphing videos that included the avatar embodied in the respective condition three times in each direction.

3.3 Design

The study had a within-participants design. One of the IVs was the level of photorealism (high photorealism versus low photorealism). Photorealism is mainly impacted by two dimensions: materials (texture and optical properties) and shape (geometry) [ 60 ]. Here, we only manipulated the texture (material) subdimension, as this property was suggested to have more impact on avatar perception (i.e., visual appeal, eeriness and attractiveness) than shape [ 60 ]. The level of personalization (personalized versus generic) of the avatars was the second IV. The dependent variables were the strength of embodiment, which was measured through a self-report questionnaire and SCRs to a threatening event (i.e., the fall of box from the ceiling close to the avatar), self-identification with the avatars measured through a self-other face distinction task, avatar perception variables (visual appeal, likeability, eeriness, familiarity, self-resemblance, and realism) and presence. The order of conditions was counterbalanced by using a Latin square design.

3.4 Procedure

3.4.1 Pre-experimental phase. Individuals who expressed interest in taking part in the study were asked to complete the COVID-19 pre-screening documents and if eligible (i.e., not part of a vulnerable sub-population), were invited to the studio. The pre-experimental session was designed to obtain scans of individuals’ bodies, which later enabled the creation of the avatars. Participants read the information sheet and gave informed consent. They were then photographed doing three different poses: a neutral pose, a ‘grinning’ pose (to expose teeth details) and an ‘eye’ pose (to expose eye details). All participants were then invited to the main experimental session which took place on a different day. The pre-experimental phase took around 15 minutes.

3.4.2 Experimental phase. The experimental phase began with the completion of the first part of the questionnaire that included the information sheet and consent form. Then, participants completed the initial self-other face distinction task using all the morphing videos that were later presented in the study (i.e., from self to all avatar types and vice versa). They were instructed to stop each video when they felt that the face started to look like themselves/an avatar and to tell the experimenter the time that the video was stopped (in seconds).

After this, participants put on the motion capture suit. They were then invited in the motion capture space. The experimenters placed the markers on the suit and the headset. The BioPac markers were also placed on participants’ index and middle fingers of their non-dominant hand. Participants performed a range of motions for Vicon calibration in synchrony with the experimenter. They moved their head for five seconds, performed circular motions with their arms for five seconds then bent their elbows, twisted their wrists and wiggled their fingers for another five seconds for the right and left side. They moved their torso from side to side and bent forward and backward for five seconds. Finally, they lifted each foot and moved it around, also bending their toes and feet for another five seconds for the right and left side.

The experimenter then instructed the participants to put the Vive Pro headset on and adjusted it, ensuring that the participants had the optimal VR experience in terms of comfort and visual acuity. The VR experience started, so participants could immediately see their avatars in the virtual mirror. They were instructed to perform a range of motions. For the following one minute and 15 seconds, they were asked to alternate looking towards their virtual body and in the mirror while performing a range of motions (each for 15 seconds): “lift your right arm and slowly perform large circular motions”, “lift your left arm and slowly perform large circular motions”, “lift your right foot and shake it slowly” , “lift your left foot and shake it slowly” and “look at your face in the mirror” to ensure they acknowledged their facial features. Right after the final action was completed, a threatening event occurred in the virtual scene – a heavy box was dropped from the ceiling in a location that was very close to the avatar. At that time, the experimenter created a mark in the BioPac recording used for later analysis. Then, participants saw the three mid-immersion questions appear on the mirror and indicated the answer that best described their experience out loud (see Materials section). Their answers were inputted into Qualtrics by the experimenter.

The experimenter then took the participant's headset off and the participant completed a more detailed questionnaire (see Materials section). Participants then completed the self-other face distinction task specific to the condition – morphing from the picture of the participant to the avatar just shown and vice versa (three times in each direction). Again, for each video, participants were instructed to stop the video when they felt that the face started to look like themselves/another person and to tell the experimenter the name of the video watched and the time that the video was stopped at in seconds.

Participants put the headset back on for the next condition. This series of events occurred four times (one time/condition). Once this part was completed, the experimenters removed the markers on the motion capture suit and the BioPac markers. Participants got changed, then resumed the questionnaire completion and received a debrief. Participants were thanked for their participation and were provided renumeration (£10/hour). The experiment took between 1h 30 minutes – 2 hours.

3.5 Analysis

The analysis plans are outlined in this section followed by the results. According to the Shapiro-Wilk test applied for all measures and conditions, the normality assumption was violated in multiple instances (See Supplementary Materials). Because ANOVA is robust to violations of normality [ 7 ], we performed two-way repeated-measures ANOVAs with photorealism (high photorealism vs low photorealism) and personalization (personalized vs generic) as the IVs on the study measures outlined below.

3.5.1 Mid-immersion measures. We ran three two-way repeated-measures ANOVAs with photorealism and personalization as the IVs on mid-immersion body ownership, agency and presence ratings. Because we were interested in all pairwise comparisons, we ran post-hoc contrasts. They were investigated using the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests due to the violation of normality.

3.5.2 Embodiment. We computed the embodiment scores by employing the procedure suggested by [ 44 ]. Namely, as outlined below, we computed the average for each sub-category and obtained the sub-scale scores. These sub-scale scores were subsequently averaged together to compute the final embodiment score.

Appearance = (Q1 + Q2 + Q3 + Q4 + Q5 + Q6 + Q9 + Q16)/8

Response = (Q4 + Q6 + Q7 + Q8 + Q9 + Q15)/6

Ownership = (Q5 + Q10 + Q11 + Q12 + Q13 + Q14)/6

Multi-Sensory = (Q3 + Q12 + Q13 + Q14 + Q15 + Q16)/6

Embodiment = (Appearance + Response + Ownership + Multi-Sensory)/4

A 2 (photorealism) × 2 (personalization) repeated-measures ANOVA was performed on embodiment ratings.

3.5.3 Skin conductance response. We subtracted the mean SCR from 3 to 1 seconds before the box fall, as the baseline interval, from the mean SCR from 1 to 6 seconds after the box fall [ 19 ]. We selected this region of interest because, typically, stimulus-elicited SCRs occur between 1 and 3 seconds after stimulus onset [ 12 ]. We regarded SCR amplitudes below 0.05 μs as zero responses [ 9 ] and excluded participants who had zero responses in more than half of the conditions from the analysis [ 19 ]. This left nine participants for the final analysis, a lower number than originally anticipated. Nevertheless, we proceeded with the analysis.

3.5.4 Self-Identification: Self-Other Face Distinction Task. In line with previous studies which looked at self-identification only with generic avatars, e.g. [ 25 ], the analysis was run twice, once for the Avatar-Self and once for the Self-Avatar morphing directions. We conducted two one-way repeated-measures ANOVAs with the trial type (pre-VR high photorealism personalized, post-VR high photorealism personalized, pre-VR high photorealism generic, post-VR high photorealism generic, pre-VR low photorealism personalized, post-VR low photorealism personalized, pre-VR low photorealism generic, post-VR low photorealism generic) as the factor on the response times in morph %. Considering our research aims and hypotheses, the a priori contrasts of interest were the pairwise comparisons between the pre-VR and post-VR morph % values for each of the four avatar types as well as between the four avatar types in post-VR exposure morph %. Where we found a significant effect of trial type, we investigated these contrasts for both the Avatar-Self direction and Self-Avatar direction separately using the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests due to the violation of normality.

Both for the Avatar-Self and the Self-Avatar directions, because the trial type factor had more than two levels (i.e., eight), we checked the sphericity assumption. Mauchly's test indicated a violation of the sphericity assumption, χ 2 (27) = 165.13, p < .001 and χ 2 (27) = 168.81, p < .001. As a result, Huyn-Feldt corrected results were reported, as this correction is recommended for repeated-measures ANOVA, when sphericity is violated, there is a small sample size and the measurement occasions number is relatively large, as it is the case here [ 28 ].

3.5.5 Avatar perception variables. A 2 (photorealism) × 2 (personalization) repeated-measures ANOVA was performed on the avatar perception variables (i.e., visual appeal, likeability, eeriness, familiarity, self-resemblance and realism).

3.5.6 Presence. We computed the presence ratings by averaging together the six items in the SUS scale for each of the four conditions. A 2 (photorealism) × 2 (personalization) repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted.

4 RESULTS

4.1 Mid-immersion measures

4.1.1 Body ownership. 5 DISCUSSION

Our study investigated the impact of avatar photorealism and personalization on embodiment, self-identification, avatar perception and presence. Embodiment ratings were higher for the high photorealism avatars compared to the low photorealism ones and for the personalized avatars compared to the generic ones, which was in line with our hypothesis and some previous literature, e.g. [ 57 ], but in disagreement with other studies [ 36 , 37 ]. In terms of mid-immersion measures, individuals reported the strongest body ownership over the high photorealism personalized avatar compared to the low photorealism personalized, high photorealism generic and low photorealism generic avatars, but no difference in agency, as we provided high fidelity motion capture in all conditions. However, no skin conductance differences were found, which partially refutes our hypothesis. This finding was likely because the box fall on participants’ avatar body did not induce sufficiently strong SCRs, which is evident due to the lack of a difference in SCRs between the baseline and post-box fall moments for 55% of the participants. There also were no differences in presence both mid-immersion and after-VR exposure. This contradicts our hypothesis and some previous studies, e.g. [ 57 ], and might be because the level of agency and the appearance of the virtual environment was maintained constant in all conditions. The interplay between avatar appearance and presence requires further examination.

In the self-other face distinction task, in the self-avatar direction, participants took significantly longer to pause the morphing videos when watching themselves morph into the high photorealism personalized avatar compared to the other three avatar types. In the avatar-self direction, participants took significantly longer to stop the video for the high photorealism personalized avatar than the other avatars (other than the low photorealism personalized one). Moreover, participants took significantly longer to stop the morphing video going from themselves to the low photorealism personalized avatar than the low photorealism generic avatar, overall suggesting a greater enfacement illusion so thus, greater bias towards self-identification for the personalized avatars, which is in agreement with our hypothesis. This is also corroborated by previous EEG research on 2D personalized avatar facial perception which revealed a less positive ERP component (P200) both for real faces and look-alike avatars processing compared to non-self-recognized pictures (e.g., familiar and unfamiliar others) [ 22 ]. This suggested that photographs of personalized avatars were assimilated as real own face representations of the participants. Our finding builds on this research by illustrating the special status of personalized avatars in 3D facial perception and is remarkable especially considering that we did not enable any facial animation. However, there were no differences between the time the morphing videos were stopped in both directions between pre-embodiment and post-embodiment and no statistically significant correlations between embodiment ratings and morph % in both directions in the four different conditions, suggesting that the delayed response for the high photorealism and personalized avatars was possibly either due to the increased familiarity of these avatars or simply due to the increased similarity between the self and the avatar, not due to increased embodiment per se [ 11 , 53 ]. Gonzalez-Franco et al. [ 25 ] suggest the opposite – that there is a link between embodiment and self-identification, so more research is needed to untangle the relationship between these two aspects. Nevertheless, our finding is in line with [ 21 ] who conducted a series of studies including a facial recognition task, face matching task and a PCA applied to a set of participants’ photographs and a set of their avatars. On all these tasks, the personalized avatars photographs showed the same familiarity advantages as the photographs of real individuals. Consequently, the authors suggested that avatars can capture the individual identity of real people. This can also be extrapolated from our self-other face distinction task, where self-identification was more challenging when the high photorealism personalized avatar was the ‘other’ (i.e., the photorealistic personalized avatar was more easily identified as oneself).

Regarding the avatar perception variables, in discrepancy with our hypothesis, the photorealistic avatars were rated higher on visual appeal and there were no significant effects on likeability. There also were no significant effects on eeriness – all types of avatars were rated equally as eerie (also all means were around the middle of the range), which disagrees with our prediction. However, our hypothesis was partly supported by the finding that the high photorealism avatars were rated as more realistic than the low photorealism ones. Familiarity and self-resemblance ratings were higher for the personalized avatars compared to the generic avatars, which is in conformity with our hypothesis. Overall, these findings illustrate a more positive reaction to the high photorealism personalized avatars compared to the low photorealism and generic avatars, which corroborates recent research employing cutting-edge avatars that seem to overcome the Uncanny Valley [ 29 ]. These final findings (realism, familiarity, and self-resemblance) also suggest that the avatar appearance manipulations used were perceptually successful.

6 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

While the photorealism and personalization manipulations were successful, it is important to acknowledge that the threatening event meant to induce skin conductance responses was not able to induce the stress reaction intended. Future research should potentially resort to another type of event that might trigger a stronger bodily reaction such as a knife cut [ 27 , 62 ], paper-cutter [ 43 ] or a painful body twist [ 2 ], so that SCRs could be used as a physiological proxy to measure embodiment. Moreover, in the specific context of the interplay between photorealism and personalization, it would be interesting to investigate how manipulating these parameters of the virtual environment in addition to the avatar embodied might impact individuals’ stress reactions and overall experience. For instance, in terms of personalization, it is now possible to re-create real spaces in VR using technologies such Lidar scanners. This enables a whole range of possibilities such as placing individuals in familiar or emotionally meaningful virtual places and exploring their behaviours and reactions as embodied avatars (either resembling their real selves or other avatars), even when interacting with other avatars in social VR scenarios [ 34 , 46 ].

Another notable limitation of the current study is the lack of facial animation. Even though we employed a high-end motion capture system targeting the whole body, with the technologies available at the time the study was conducted, we could not implement facial animation. As suggested by [ 25 ], facial animation (lip-sync or lip-sync + pre-baked facial animation) is particularly important to achieve as it can lead to increased embodiment and facial self-identification with the avatar compared to still face avatars. In a future study, it would be interesting to manipulate both the level of visual fidelity (ranging from high photorealism personalized to low photorealism generic) and the motion fidelity (both body and facial) of the avatars, which was constant in all conditions in this experiment.

Moreover, it is important to note that we only manipulated one factor influencing photorealism. Here, we only manipulated the texture of the avatars, while the shape, which is another key factor [ 61 ], was maintained constant. We adopted this approach because previous research by [ 60 ] suggested that this property has more influence on avatar perception than shape, which was of interest here. Therefore, this aspect must be taken into account when interpreting our comparison between high photorealism and low photorealism. Building on our findings, future research could systematically manipulate other photorealism parameters of personalized avatars such as shape, facial proportions, level of detail, skin appearance, lighting and shading [ 61 ] in order to establish their impact on personalized avatar perception, embodiment and self-identification.

Considering the novelty of high photorealism personalized avatars, there are many avenues for future research in this area. In this paper, we showed that individuals experience higher levels of embodiment and self-identification with such avatars, and that embodying them is a perceptually positive experience, but there could be crucial behavioural implications deriving from this. For instance, increased embodiment and self-identification using highly photorealistic personalized avatars might impact users’ behaviour in social VR scenarios [ 61 ], such as leading to increased accountability and prosocial attitudes [ 25 ] as well as potentially enhancing nonverbal behaviour and users’ ability to communicate effectively in mixed/virtual reality [ 14 ]. Employing such avatars might also be helpful for public speaking anxiety exposure therapy, with potentially higher anxiety benefits than generic avatars [ 3 , 4 ]. Body image disturbances research seems to be another clinical application area that can considerably benefit from employing and manipulating patients’ highly photorealistic personalized avatars in targeted interventions [ 16 , 59 ]. Ultimately, designing long-term longitudinal investigations to understand how individuals react to being repeatedly embodied as photorealistic personalized avatars both in social VR and clinical applications is also paramount and the next step in our current research.

7 CONCLUSION

Overall, this study has provided evidence regarding the advantages of using highly photorealistic and personalized avatars as embodied self-representations in VR – increased embodiment and self-identification as well as positive avatar perception effects. These findings are important in the current HCI research context, where understanding users’ experiences in embodied VR and the factors that modulate these is a top priority, especially as high-fidelity avatars that closely resemble their users are becoming increasingly achievable with consumer-grade technologies. From a technical perspective, the research presented here suggests that even if high photorealism personalized avatars require higher computational power and graphics processing capabilities, their implementation seems justified as it considerably impacts users’ experience in a positive manner. With the increased attention currently devoted to the possibilities of the metaverse, we are likely to witness increased social VR collaboration and communication as virtual avatars and agents on platforms such as AltSpace, VRChat or Meta's Horizon Worlds. Therefore, this study contributed towards untangling the independent and collective effect photorealism and personalization has on users’ bodily and external perception to optimize their embodied VR experiences.
