In medical simulation training two approaches are currently rather disjunct: realistic manikins are used to teach physical skills and procedures and VR systems are used to train situation assessment and decision making. We propose a mixed reality approach, which allows trainees to use real tools and their hands when interacting with a physical manikin overlaid with a responsive virtual avatar. In close exchange with first responder organizations, we developed and evaluated an MR training scenario. In the scenario, users can talk to injured people in a car accident, assess the threat of the environment, and utilize real medical equipment. Participants experienced high levels of physical- and self-presence, increased stress levels, and reported a high technology acceptance. The proposed main requirements of first responders regarding haptic multi-sensory skill training in MR and the lessons learned from the workshop aim to guide the design of training solutions for medical training in MR.

1 INTRODUCTION

In a matter of minutes, medical first responders must decide and act to save another person's life. From deciding on which patients to focus their attention on in the first triage to basic life support until the emergency doctor arrives or the patient is transported into a hospital, this occupational context is challenging in various ways.

Different solutions exist to prepare and train for these situations with varying focus points for medical first responder training. For one, medical manikins have become increasingly intricate in their life-likeness: modern patient simulators - e.g. ADAM-X 1 , feature realistic respiratory systems, pupillary responses, and a simulation of a patient's physiological status. With these manikins, medical skills can be trained in a safe but realistic way.

Complementary to this practical approach to training, VR simulations have been developed and employed to prepare medical professionals within immersive virtual environments for the challenging situations they face in their line of work - e.g. [ 31 , 33 , 35 ]. These virtual simulations focus on more “top-down” aspects of training medical emergencies: assessing virtual accident sites for environmental threats, realistic representations of patients, and learning the sequence of treatments with controller-based interaction can accustom trainees to stressful situations and teach them the rules and steps to coping with them. Training assessment of the environment, threat assessments, and sequences of medical procedures in immersive virtual environments offer a high degree of visual and auditory realism but lack haptic feedback and only simulate the treatment of patients through virtual interactions.

These two approaches could complement each other to create the next level of medical skills training by combining virtual environments - for decision training, training of situation awareness, communication training, etc. - and physical manikins to provide hands-on training of skills with a variety of tools and haptic feedback. Thus, we propose using mixed reality (MR) to combine the advantages of hands-on manikins and immersive VR training into one. By integrating a training manikin into MR, overlaying it with a virtual visualization of an agent, and placing the trainee and the manikin in a virtual accident scenario.

To evaluate the potential of the proposed MR application as a future MR training system for first responders, we conducted an evaluation workshop with medical experts and a complementary user study with medical first responders to answer our research question:

  • RQ: Does the use of a responsive tangible manikin in immersive mixed reality enable skill training of medical first responders?

To answer that question, we have devised two hypotheses to test (a) the immersive aspect of the RQ and (b) the medical skill aspect of the RQ.

  • H1: The first responder experts will show a high acceptance of the technology, indicating that it fits their needs for medical skill training.
  • H2: The first responder experts will experience high amounts of presence in the immersive environment, indicated by ratings and physiological measurements.

To answer the research question and to test our hypotheses, we took a human-centered approach: Initial requirement workshops served as a platform to gather needs and wants of medical first responders towards a tangible MR training solution. Data from these workshops informed the design of the MR Manikin application, which was iteratively developed together with medical professionals from first responder organizations. The aim was to envision what the future of medical first responder training could look like. We defined the two main goals: (1) to maximize immersion and (2) to enable medically realistic treatment as the main goals for the development of the prototype and scenario. On top of the technical implementations, we developed a script for a specific car accident scenario again with a research and education organization for medical first responders.

The preliminary evaluation in the expert workshop and the complementary user study consisted of questionnaires, group discussions as well as the use of the galvanic skin response (GSR) as a measure of emotional arousal during the MR experience. Based on the gathered insight from this first evaluation we discuss the usage of the manikin in MR to guide future development of first responder training applications.

The contributions to the HCI community of this paper are as follows:

  1. Description of the prototypical training application, including technical implementation details, demonstrating the use of tangible-virtual interaction to create useful and engaging interactions for medical training.
  2. A collection of qualitative and quantitative findings from an evaluation workshop by medical experts.
  3. Ideas and needs for future development & improvements of MR training systems from the perspective of medical experts.

2 RELATED WORK

The MR Manikin is intended as a haptic digital solution for a dynamic patient simulation in which trainees train the assessment, correct diagnosis, and treatment of trauma patients. For the realization of the MR Manikin for medical training, casualties, injuries, symptoms, and wounds need to be represented correctly and realistically. The same is true for the patient's responses and vital data according to the injuries and treatments. Only through correct representation is it possible to diagnose the injury correctly and to provide the right treatment. This section gives an overview of the current findings on VR training in general and specifically for medical training as well as what the latest research on the integration of tangible elements in VR and medical training is and why it is promising.

2.1 Training in VR

Virtual reality has demonstrated its ability to transform learning and training and is an ongoing topic for research within the CHI community [ 46 ]. From traditional classroom environments to extreme training situations, VR reduces investment and increases enrichment across a range of industries. When it comes to absorbing, retaining, and applying new skills, VR delivers distinct advantages. VR environments and simulation have been part of professional training (e.g. [ 8 , 15 , 29 , 40 , 42 , 51 , 57 ], etc.) Also, VR training offers controlled virtual environments that allow for repetition(s) that people need to master a new skill or process [ 38 ].

Evidence shows that the use of VR is useful when the training domain is complex and difficult to master (cf. [ 6 , 21 ]) and when audio-visual features - assisted by haptic feedback - of the training environment are crucial to the overall training success (cf. [ 1 ]). This makes virtual environments a promising solution for practicing and learning in domains where the context of the training is not easily available or replicable due to security and safety issues. (e.g. [ 16 , 40 , 45 ]). VR training can allow users to evaluate different strategies and learn without severe consequences. It eliminates risk, liability, and injury and it allows users to easily train and master procedures to be better prepared for the real case.

A VR environment encourages information retention by offering fresh, immersive learning opportunities. VR training encourages input, by turning the learner, or “trainee”, into an active user who must physically and mentally engage to participate. Thereby, VR experiences stimulate tactile, visual, and auditory senses and therefore ensure to satisfy a range of different learning styles or types. Multi-sensory VR experiences include even more senses like olfaction or nociception, which can improve the realism and threat assessment of the virtual environment [ 56 ]. As a result information retention promotes to a new level of effectiveness and VR training demonstrates the potential to expand and complement traditional teaching methods [ 12 , 47 ].

VR training provides opportunities for the trainer as well. Sharing a virtual environment fosters empathy, allowing the trainer to walk around and observe students as they experience the training scenarios. The instructor can annotate mistakes and provide constructive feedback in real time. This way, the student can make corrections while instructions are still fresh in their minds. Additionally, the training session can also be recorded and played back later, in 3D and/or audio, to evaluate and review trainees and/or trainers [ 39 ]. Continuous, evolving feedback is especially valuable when people are trained for medical emergencies. Automated tracking and analysis of trainees’ head or eye movements [ 50 ] adds another layer of feedback.

2.2 Medical training in VR

VR in healthcare is currently an area of great potential. VR training could result in cost and liability savings and could provide novel opportunities to enhance medical training and education, for example, record metrics from virtual training and assess improvements in outcomes. Although, VR training in healthcare already exists the topic overall is still in a very early stage, requiring more evaluation and improvement of prototypes, and identification of appropriate learning theories to better guide its development.

There are many types of VR medical training systems realized up to date. On the one hand, there are systems centered on teaching physical skills and procedures e.g. surgery [ 19 , 23 , 26 , 41 , 44 ], planing surgery [ 11 ], first aid for patients with seizures [ 2 ], first aid and reanimation [ 7 ], using a dialysis machine [ 48 ] and very recently a training for COVID19 related measures - Nasopharyngeal swab and the proper Personal Protective Equipment [ 61 ]. Those usually employ highly sophisticated hardware user interfaces providing realistic haptic feedback or/and mimicking real devices.

On the other hand, there are VR systems helping the trainee to diagnose patients [ 35 ], learn general anatomical knowledge [ 22 ] or train for the patient consultation process before a surgery - e.g. Social VR Clinic [ 31 ]. Also training for developing psychological skills required in real-world scenarios (e.g. [ 24 , 42 ]), medical ethics training [ 33 ] or first responder decision training [ 27 ] have been proposed in related work.

2.3 Haptics and tangible interaction for medical training in VR

Using tangible artifacts and haptic modalities in immersive training is an important aspect that was targeted in prior research in many domains apart from medical training. For example Liu et al. [ 32 ] proposed MR training for learning the solar and planet system - developed for a primary school in Singapore - and an MR exhibition about evolution -for a public exhibition in the Singapore Science Center. Ayala et al. [ 4 ] proposed a training system for teaching geometry by interacting with tangible devices in a virtual world. Sousa & Romão [ 54 ] proposed a tangible learning environment that was targeted to promote chemistry to children between 9 and 12 years. In a similar direction, Pan et al. [ 43 ] proposed to conduct chemical experiments in MR. The authors argue that this might be a benefit in terms of safety, environmental pollution, and also in terms of time needed for the experiments.

Also, multiple systems have been already proposed that integrate real tools, haptic feedback, or tangible interaction in medical training. E.g. [ 13 ] provides a review on haptic simulations in medical training. Scherfgen et al. [ 49 ] proposed integrating a manikin in VR training. Their work focused on the technical system to estimate the pose of a medical manikin which is then used to integrate the real manikin correctly into the virtual environment. In a similar direction, Semaro et al. [ 52 ] describe the usage of a physical mannequin in VR based on a commercial Laerdal HeartSim 4000 mannequin in combination with data-gloves and a head-mounted display. Participants judged the VR experience as interesting and considered useful for healthcare training. But it requires comprehensive technological advancement (e.g. finger movements, improvement of facial signs) and validation in emergency training. Another solution that integrates manikins in VR is researched the project ViTAWiN– Virtuell-augmentiertes Training für die Aus- und Weiterbildung in der interprofessionellen Notfallversorgung (Virtual-augmented training for education and training in inter-professional emergency care 2 . The goal of Project ViTAWiN is to investigate a haptic augmented and virtual multiuser training environment by adding digital features on a traditional simulation mannequin. Gasques et al. [ 14 ] proposed a mixed reality application "HoloSim"-. In HoloSim a physical manikin is augmented with visual overlays by using the Micorosft HoloLens AR glasses and the Vuforia AR framework. The authors used the visual overlay to make interaction with a manikin more realistic e.g. by showing bleeding of the manikin or simulated vomiting. In contrast to the work proposed in this paper, no tangible tools were used, and also only the manikin was augmented without providing a fully immersive environment. Kwon et al. [ 28 ] proposed a sensor kit that is integrated into manikins to support learning Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation without an instructor. The system provides simulated virtual scenarios and multimodal feedback (auditory and visual). Visual feedback is provided through a tablet in one mode and especially relevant for this work in another mode the manikin is enhanced by a projection.

Also other haptic and tangible devices beyond manikins have been proposed. Huang et al, [ 20 ] proposed a haptic device that provides realistic haptic feedback in a 3D VR angiography training simulator. [ 17 ] describe a novel haptic controller that is used in a VR training environment for training high tibial osteotomy. This haptic controller aims to support a realistic impression of multiple surgical instruments that are used in the training. [ 9 ] describes a dental simulation for periodontal probing where haptic technology provides different sensations for different dental substances (e.g. teeth, gingiva, bone, etc.).

The applications to date are either limited to individual special use cases or require complex additional technology, finger tracking, or data-gloves, which are a hindrance to the haptic diagnosis in training medical emergencies. The goal must be to make the interactions as natural as possible. It would be best to simply use the own hands without additional technology on it and the own medical equipment. With the proposed MR manikin solution, we are trying to get closer to this ambitious goal.

3 DESIGN PROCESS

To provide a scenario that includes a suitable environment, realistic behaviors of the virtual agents, and appropriate use of medical devices during the training, the concept of the MR Manikin application was designed together with experts from a medical first responder organization.

3.1 Requirement Workshops

At the beginning of the project this work is nested in, a series of requirement workshops for an MR training system for medical first responders were executed. These workshops focused on various topics related to MR training, amongst others the use of a tangible manikin, tangible tools, and realism in general. The needs and wants of medical first responders regarding tangible aspects and realism were assessed. These identified requirements motivated the MR manikin prototype presented in this work.

Overall, the requirements centered around the following topics: realism, virtual environments and assets, live dashboard for the trainer, debriefing dashboard, smart wearables, integration of tangible equipment, manikin and requirements for systematic assessments of patients. In terms of realism, a crucial aspect extracted from the workshops were realistic rules of the virtual world, i.e. correct timings of procedures and valid effect-cause events in the patients state. This is what Slater [ 53 ] would call ’plausibility illusion’. Further, the importance of a realistic, spatialized soundscape was stressed, with potentially loud environmental noises that make communication more challenging, as this represents the work reality more accurately. In terms of virtual environments, different scenarios where identified, amongst them traffic accidents - as implemented in this work - but also CBRNe events, gas explosions and accidents in tunnels. In terms of assets an accurate injury representation was identified as a main need. Further discussed were avatar representations, which should ideally match the respective organisations uniform, the trainees role and gender. Regarding virtual agents, the need for realistic ’human-like’ interactions, reactions and emotions was expressed. For assessing the patients state, the workshops resulted in different functionalities the manikin should possess, like the possibility to assess the airway, listening to breathing sounds with a stethoscope or cyanosis. These and other results from the workshops helped guide the development of the MR prototype described in this work.

3.2 Prototyping the MR Manikin

3.2.1 Technology setup. The prototype for the MR training scenario was developed in Unity 3D 3 2022.1.15f including the High-Definition Rendering Pipeline (HDRP) to provide high-fidelity graphics. The HMD Varjo XR-3 4 (Figure 2 c) is used together with the applications Varjo Base and Varjo Lab Tools to enable additional features like chroma-keying and depth testing. The chroma keying feature is used in combination with a green screen setup, including a green screen fabric and tape for the floor and the manikin, to render real objects like the stethoscope above the virtual 3D scene. As the manikin is chroma keyed, this allows for a virtual overlay of the physical manikin with a virtual avatar. The depth testing feature of the XR-3 enables the integration of the real hands of the user and real medical tools on top of the virtual environment by using depth information of the integrated LiDAR sensors. The position and rotation of real objects (e.g., ambu bag, stethoscope, or manikin) are tracked with attached, printable fiducial markers by the video pass-through cameras of the HMD. Additionally, a scent emitter device by OVR Technology 5 is attached to the HMD and controlled via Bluetooth to support the virtual environment with appropriate ambient scents. The trainee is also wearing an audio headset to hear spatialized sounds like external and internal breathing sounds as well as speech coming from the agent.

Additionally, the video feed of the HMD was displayed to the role-playing trainer via screen sharing. A microphone was connected to the PC, to control speech animations on the virtual agents within the scene based on the sound input of the microphone and to enable the trainer to role-play one of the two virtual agents. The virtual agents were created with the 3D software Character Creator 4 6 and the animation library Mixamo 7 was used to combine multiple animations for the agents. Figure 3 gives an overview of the system.

3.2.2 Virtual agents. Within the scene, there are two virtual agents that have to be assessed and treated by the trainee and are role-played by the trainer role: Grace and Tobi. These two virtual agents were designed by using example personas of a car accident developed during the requirement phase of the project.

Grace (Figure 4 a) is 42 years old and in her 34th week of pregnancy. At the arrival of the medical first responder, she stands next to Tobi and is clearly stressed. She is not injured and has just observed the accident. Because of her pregnancy, the medical first responder should try to calm her down to avoid any premature complications due to stress.

Tobi Figure ( 4 b) is 20 years old and upon arrival of the medical first responder, he is lying on the ground. He is conscious but suffers from thorax trauma, which is indicated by pain and respiratory problems. He shows an increased respiratory rate including coughing and during the scenario, his skin starts to get pale and sweaty. He obeys simple commands slowly, but his oxygen saturation decreases slowly. During the scenario, he faints and needs to be ventilated with an ambu bag.

3.2.3 Tangible objects. To enable treatment of Tobi's thorax trauma, real tools of medical first responders were added to the scenario. The oxygen mask ( 5 c), the ambu bag and the oxygen bottle (both 5 b) were included to assist Tobis respiratory functions. These tools were integrated without fiducial markers and can be used as they would in reality. Trainees can change between the ambu bag and the oxygen mask by applying the tool with its tube to the oxygen bottle. Effects on Tobi were simulated by the use of animation sliders in unity. On the stethoscope ( 5 d) we placed a fiducial marker. Through an object collision script with relevant regions on the chest of Tobi, the lung sounds play when the stethoscope is pressed against the chest of the manikin. The main tangible object though is of course the manikin ( 5 a), which was the Resusci Anne QCPR by Laerdal 8 . To chroma key the manikin, we dressed it in a green shirt and taped the head with neon green tape. Two fiducial markers are attached to the manikin, to secure tracking data at different angles and distances.

3.2.4 Sound. To support the visual representation of the virtual environment, different types of sounds were played during the experience. Ambient sounds included audio for traffic and distant sirens. Both virtual agents also exhibit spatialized breathing sounds that are heard when the trainee is standing close enough to them. The breathing sounds were included since the respiratory rate is an important indicator for the initial assessment of potential injuries. Additionally, the trainee can use a stethoscope on Tobi to check his lung sounds and assess internal respiratory problems. As soon as the stethoscope is placed onto the chest of the Tobi, the breathing sound of the virtual agent changes to the internal lung breathing sound.

3.2.5 Olfaction. A scent emitter device by OVR Technology 9 is attached to the HMD, which emits a light smell of smoke and gasoline to support the visual representation of the car accident with suitable scents. The emitter is enabled via Bluetooth as soon as the application starts and includes scent cartridges that emit scent particles periodically.

3.2.6 Trainee Interactions. The trainee can move freely within the virtual scene. During the emergency check, tangible objects like the stethoscope and the ambu bag can be held intuitively. The position and rotation of the stethoscope are tracked continuously and if it is used to examine breathing sounds, internal breathing sounds are enabled as soon as the stethoscope chestpiece touches the chest of the mannequin. If the trainee decides to perform ventilation with the ambu bag, the animation of lifting and lowering of the thorax can be controlled in the editor in a wizard-of-oz method. The current oxygen saturation and heart rate that are displayed on a purely virtual oximeter on Tobi's hand can be changed in the editor during run-time. Additionally, animations and changes in the material that represent the different health states (e.g., Tobi getting pale and sweaty, falling unconscious) can be triggered in the editor. A small menu, that is tracked with a marker, can be used to remove the shirt of Tobi with a virtual button. The end of the scenario is triggered in the editor and is visualized with the arrival of the emergency doctor's vehicle.

3.2.7 Trainer Interactions. The trainer is role-playing both virtual agents by speaking into the provided microphone based on a provided script that gives an overview of the general setting of the accident and the involved people. The switch between audio from breathing sounds and the trainer's voice can be controlled with the editor. Since the trainer is able to see the first-person view of the trainee all the time, it is possible to improvise and respond to the actions of the trainee immediately. Figure 7 shows (a) the green screen setup in the lab, (b) the trainee view, and (c) the interplay between the trainee and trainer.

3.3 Scenario creation

To create a realistic training scenario vignette, we collaborated with a medical expert with a background in medicine. Together, we thought of a scenario that could (a) allow us to test out the developed interactions and (b) be accurate from a medical and first responders’ point of view. This resulted in a car accident scenario involving two persons: Grace and Tobi. Whereas Grace is not hurt, Tobi has a thorax trauma resulting from the accident. Just like how the real-life training is planned in different phases, we designed the scenario around 3 phases.

Phase 1: Assessment. In the assessment phase, it is the trainee's job to get an overview of the accident site and talk to the involved persons to assess their bodily and mental state.

Phase 2: Treatment In the treatment phase, Tobi's state is decaying and the trainee's task is to perform life support measures in the form of respiratory support.

Phase 3: Patient transfer: Phase 3 marks the end of the scenario, where the emergency doctor arrives to take over the patient. The trainee just reports what was done and what happened with the patient.

3.3.1 Scenario description. The designed scenario starts at a gas station. A pile-up accident involving two cars and two injured persons just happened. The fire department has already approved access to the scene of the accident. Figure 2 a shows the car accident with a firefighter in the background, Figure 2 b shows a top-down view of the scenario. The trainee is wearing the head-mounted display (HMD) and can see an uninjured pregnant woman (Grace), and a young man (Tobi) that is lying on the floor and experiencing severe pain and respiratory problems. In Phase 1, the trainee examines the young man by performing an emergency check including a visual examination of potential respiratory obstruction or life-threatening bleeding. This was done by the experimenter removing the shirt off Tobi once the trainees said they wanted to check Tobi's body. At the start, Tobi is still able to talk and reports (through the role-playing trainer) severe pain in the front of the right thorax. The trainee recognizes that the airways are free, but after removing the shirt a bruise at the right thorax becomes visible which indicates thorax trauma. The use of a stethoscope indicates insusceptible breathing sounds. An attached oximeter displays a normal oxygen saturation, but a slightly increased heart rate. During the emergency check, the man starts to get pale and bluish skin, which represents a drop in oxygen saturation. This marks the beginning of the decay of Tobi and therefore starts phase 2. The decrease in oxygen saturation is also visible on the oximeter. Additionally, the heart rate increases and the man starts to sweat. Shortly afterward, the man gets unconscious and his breathing rate further decreases. The trainee needs to ventilate the man with an available ambu bag until the emergency doctor is arriving and taking over (Phase 3).

4 Expert Workshop & User Study: Formative Evaluation of the MR manikin

After testing the MR manikin and the different interactions we developed internally, we conducted an expert workshop to answer our research question: would an MR manikin in this form enable basic medical skill training and its hypotheses: how is it experienced in terms of technology acceptance and presence? We chose a workshop format, as we wanted the experts to have a space to try out the MR manikin and the interactions without putting too much focus on performance in the scenario. The intention was, that the experts would get a feeling for the possibilities of MR as a solution for skill training, and connect this experience to their knowledge about training goals and conditions of skill training for medical first responders. The same setup was used in a complementary user study, which involved actual medical first responders, who completed the same scenario with the same measurements and questionnaires.

4.1 Participants

The expert workshop was conducted with 7 experts (2 male, 5 female, mean age of 34.3 years, SD = 5.9) from Johanniter Österreich - Ausbildung und Forschung gemeinnützige GmbH 10 , a sub-division of a first responder organization specialized in education and research in the field of first responder training. The experts were part of the research branch of the organization and had varying academic backgrounds (medicine, biology, psychology). The user study was conducted with 5 medical first responders from the same organization (all male, mean age of 33.6, SD = 9.6).

Some of the experts and first responders had already tested an early version of the MR manikin, which included only the virtual environment and the manikin with overlay of the virtual agent. Therefore, 5 experts reported having used MR before, while 2 used it the first time during the workshop. Of the first responders, 2 had previous experience with MR and 3 had not.

4.2 Measurements

4.2.1 Technology Acceptance. As a theoretical basis for examining, whether the experts and first responders would use the MR manikin for training (i.e. if it would enable medical skills training, RQ1), we chose the Unified Theory of Adoption and Use of Technology model 2 (UTAUT2) [ 58 ]. We took the UTAUT2 original items from Harboth & Pape [ 18 ] and adapted them for training with the MR manikin. Some of the sub facets of the UTAUT2 model did not fit our investigation (Habit, Social Influence, Price Value, and Use Behavior) and were therefore excluded. The used scales therefore were: Performance Expectancy (PE), Effort Expectancy (EE), Hedonic Motivation (HM), Facilitating Conditions (FC), and Behavioral Intention. Items for Technology Acceptance were answered on a 5-point Likert scale.

4.2.2 Presence. To measure the amount of presence our participants experienced during the MR experience (RQ2) we opted for the translation [ 59 ] of the Multimodal Presence Scale (MPS) for virtual reality environments by Makransky et al. [ 36 ]. The questionnaire is based on Lee's [ 30 ] unified understanding of presence and differentiates between physical, social, and self-presence. Physical presence revolves around how physical objects or environments feel like, social presence describes the experience with social actors, and self-presence refers to how much users feel like their own body is inside the virtual environment. The MPS items were answered on a 5-point Likert scale.

4.2.3 Emotional Arousal. As an additional objective measurement for presence (RQ2), we used a galvanic skin response (GSR) sensor to measure the skin conductance. GSR is usually interpreted as an indicator of emotional arousal: more peaks in skin conduction and higher peak typically indicate more arousal or excitement [ 3 ]. Skin conductance in the context of VR experience has shown promise as a proxy for presence [ 37 ], with the assumption that higher physiological arousal in a VR environment signifies a higher involvement and presence in the scenario. Together with the questionnaire data, we aim to find additional evidence for the amount of immersion our experts experienced. To achieve that, we looked at two GSR metrics: phasic peaks per minute and mean peak amplitude. As a reference to a calm resting state, we included a 2-minute baseline measurement, adapted from Katsigiannis et al. [ 25 ]. The experts would watch a snippet of the movie "Searching for Bobby Fisher" (Waitzkin, 1988, 00:14:27 - 00:16:30), a calm scene where a child is playing a chess game against his father. Recording and processing of GSR data were done in the imotions experiment platform 11 . Further statistical analysis was performed in python with the packages pandas 12 and statsmodels 13 .

4.2.4 Open Questions. To gather information about the quality of our experts’ experience, we included a total of three open questions relating to (1) problems during the MR experience, (2) positive impressions of the MR experience and (3) improvements they would like to see. Together with the group discussions, this qualitative data was collected to guide future development, should RQ1 be fulfilled.

4.2.5 Group Discussion. Additionally to the questionnaires, the expert workshop also included a 1-hour group discussion at the end. In it, we elaborated on the answers to the open questions to gain deeper insights. Focus was the role of the trainers and their possible future interactions with our MR training experience

4.3 Procedure

Upon arrival the participants of the expert workshop were randomly assigned a color for anonymization purposes and filled out an informed consent. Afterwards, they would go - one after another - into a neighboring room, where they were equipped with the GSR sensor and completed the baseline measurement. Upon completion they would experience the MR manikin first as the trainee, then as the trainer for the next expert taking the trainee role. After finishing the scenario as the trainer, the experts would fill out the questionnaire. The remaining 4-5 experts stayed in the main room and brainstormed ideas on post-its and a digital whiteboard regarding the questions for the concluding group discussion. After all 7 experts had completed the trainee and the trainer experience and subsequently answered the questionnaire, the group discussion was performed. For the complementary user study, the first responders would arrive alone to their respective time slots. Due to this difference in design, participants of the user study did not have a group discussion, brainstorming sessions, or take on the trainer role, as only one was present at a time.

4.4 Quantitative Results

4.4.1 Technology Acceptance. For the experts during the workshop, Hedonic Motivation was rated highest regarding technology acceptance, with a mean of M HM = 4.7 (SD = 0.8), followed by Behavioral Intention with a mean of M BI = 4.4 (SD = 0.9). Effort Expectancy (M EE = 4.2, SD EE = 0.9) and Performance Expectancy (M PE = 4.1, SD PE = 0.8) were rated similarly. Facilitating Conditions was rated lowest with a mean of M FC = 3.9 (SD FC = 0.7).

For the first responders during the user study, similar results emerged for Hedonic Motivation, with a mean of M HM = 4.7 (SD = 0.5) and Behavioral Intention with a mean of M BI = 4.4 (SD = 0.4). Effort Expectancy (M EE = 4.3, SD EE = 0.8) was rated slighly higher, as was Performancy Expectancy (M PE = 4.1, SD PE = 0.7). 4.5 Lessons Learned

We collected the answers to the open questions, notes from the experimenters, post-its, and notes from the digital smart board. All answers were split into single statements on one topic. These were then grouped by means of thematic analysis and discussion between the researchers.

In a first step, each post-it, open question answer or bullet point on the smart board were split into statements concerning one topic each. This was especially the case for the open question answers, as they came as one text block including comments about multiple topics. Next, these units of information were inductively categorized in an iterative manner, meaning that found inductive categories were compared to other found categories, which would either lead to a differentiation or a merge of categories. This process was further supported by the experimenters’ notes, which offered context through observations and informal feedback from the participants, making it easier to grasp the meaning from the other sources. The three main categories that emerged were:

  1. Realism
    1. Medical Realism (portrayal of injuries and decay of patient state)
    2. Immersiveness of the VE (haptic, auditory and visual realism)
  2. Tracking
    1. Manikin position
    2. Display of transparent tools
  3. Communication
    1. With the patients (the trainer role playing)
    2. With the experimenters
    3. Trainer dashboard

The statements in their inductive categories were further labelled as positive or negative in valence, depending on whether it was critique or praise of the system. Figure 10 summarizes the results of this thematic analysis, including improvements abstracted and extracted from the open questions. The experts felt highly immersed in the MR experience. The immersion the MR manikin provided was overall received as very positive, with one expert summarizing it as: "The experience was very immersive. I felt like I was in the scenario and could understand and assess the situation." The decay of the patient (change of pupil size, change in breathing, turning blue & sweating) was perceived as being medically realistic, as was the chronological progression of symptoms. The visual design of the environment, the virtual agents, and the patient wound were deemed as very lifelike, with one expert mentioning: "The virtual environment with the accident was very stressful". Adding to visual realism, all of the experts mentioned sound design as crucial for their sense of presence. This included the ambient sounds in the background, as well as the realistic breathing sounds for assessment of the patient's status. Interestingly, the olfactory stimulus was not actively noticed by the experts, with one mentioning "I thought something in the room smelled bad". When designing MR training, the combination of domain specific realism (i.e. the ’plausibility’) with sensory realism (i.e. haptics, spatial audio and visual fidelity) promises a highly immersive experience.

The interaction with the manikin with the provided tools was intuitive. Using the stethoscope, the face mask, and ambu bag in combination with the oxygen bottle was experienced as intuitive. Due to the fact that the experts could see their own hands and use actual medical tools, they could interact with the manikin without any instructions. For example, the stethoscope was picked up and used as it would be expected in reality, and due to the collision detection via the fiducial marker, it worked as expected. One expert commented: "the application of the tools was working very well". Another one highlighted the interaction with the ambu bag: "The ventilation worked very well and the chest expansion was very realistic". A slight criticism was raised about the stethoscope: as it could not be used in reality (meaning plugged into the ears), the expert wore them around their necks. This resulted in some of them mentioning that this created a divergent experience: they could listen to the patient's lung sounds, without feeling the stethoscope's ear plugs. These insights suggest, that using real, physical tools within mixed reality skill training can provide a level of intuitiveness, that could enable faster learning of interactions, more natural behaviour and higher presence due to a more direct mapping.

There is a need for a fully moveable manikin including extremities. One constructive criticism regarding the current implementation of the MR manikin was, that the manikin must be moveable and complete, with all extremities. Moving is of special importance, as the whole body needs to be checked for wounds, and often respiration is checked on the backside. One sub facet of this need is the additional possibility to stretch the head of the manikin with the corresponding stretching of the virtual agent's neck. For respiration-related treatment and assessment, this is crucial to include in future MR training. On a higher level, this learning illustrates the challenges that arise due to the switch from VR to MR: While turning a virtual agent in VR is done easily with a simple script, implementing the same feature in MR with a physical manikin using fiducial markers alone is more difficult. Therefore for future virtual training it should be carefully weighed whether MR is necessary for the intended training goals and what benefits it would bring to the training.

Providing a selection of tools is not enough. The experts agreed, that providing trainees with only a selection of their medical tools poses a problem. As the challenge of a first responder situation is making the decision of which tools to use for a given scenario, a pre-selection of tools makes the assessment of symptoms and treatment too obvious and easy. A future training solution should therefore include the whole medical backpack first responders arrive with at an accident site. On a more general level, this learning illustrates the complexities arising in specific HCI application fields, and how as researchers and designers we have to be aware of our mental blind spots. When implementing the MR manikin prototype, we approached it by defining one realistic accident scenario, and integrated the tools that would be needed to solve it. By doing that, we did not see that part of such a scenario is the choice of the right tool. For realistic virtual training, the decision space must therefore optimally be as open as in reality, which of course is also more demanding in terms of development, as more variations and interactions of events have to be accounted for.

Transparent medical tools pose a challenge for MR training. A common issue raised by the expert was, that the face mask and the ambu-bag are both made out of partly transparent plastic. In combination with the green ground, this meant, that there were graphical glitches - especially the face mask was difficult to see when it was in front of the green manikin or the floor. For future training, solid variants of these tools should be used. Alternatively, new real-time chroma key methods need to be developed to accommodate this challenge - this is already done in video production including chroma key, though in post editing.

Real clothing should be included. One need the experts mentioned was, that real clothing should be included. In emergency situations, the handling of the patient's clothes poses a challenge when first responders need to quickly assess the body of their patient. When there is no time to meticulously remove the patient's clothes, special clothing scissors are used to remove the clothing quickly. Dressing up the manikin (as long as the clothing is not green) and including clothing scissors as part of the medical equipment is therefore a requirement for a future training system. MR with chroma keying offers the possibility of tangible clothing in the virtual world, which could be of need in various fields of application. In the domain of simulation skill training, body searches could be trained by police officers or clues about a persons identity could be hidden in their clothes for crime scene investigation training.

To increase social presence, head movement and facial expressions should be integrated. Although the eye movement of the virtual agents was received positively in terms of social realism, experts also voiced constructive criticism, that the social interaction was still too stiff. When interacting with a patient, subtle signs like facial expressions and head movements are important markers to assess the state of the patient. Adding to that, one expert mentioned, that "there should be some form of reaction when I touch the patient - especially in the vicinity of the wound". A challenge for a future MR system is therefore the virtual agent interaction realism. Realistic social behavior of virtual agents is a general challenge for simulation training involving social interaction. Combining role-playing by a trainer with automated responses of the virtual agent seems like an achievable next step, whereas fully automated responses both in speech and behavior might be possible within the next 5 years with increasing use of AI models in XR technology.

There is a need to indicate, which virtual agents are haptic and which are not. To improve the confidence of users in the interaction with virtual agents, there needs to be a (visual) cue to differentiate between non-haptic and haptic virtual agents (via the manikin). One expert described this as: "It should not happen that I try to touch a patient and my hand goes through the virtual agent". When this happens, it not only broke the immersion but made the experts less confident in the interaction with elements in MR. In a future MR solution, users have to know what they can touch and what is purely virtual. This learning illustrates a general challenge in MR simulations including tangible objects. New ways of communicating tangibility of a virtual objects are needed, in a manner that is easy to recognize but does not break the immersion by adding artificial overlays or visualisations.

To summarize, experts experienced high amounts of physical and self-presence in the MR manikin scenario and reported a high technology acceptance for the application of skills training of first responders in MR. Through the qualitative feedback we gathered it is clear that there are some challenges in implementing tools (e.g. transparency of tools) as well as a need for a full body manikin, ideally with finer haptic granularity and features like feeling the pulse or being able to move different body parts. Also, the responsiveness of the virtual agents needs to improve, to heighten the sense of social presence.

5 DISCUSSION

5.1 Hypothesis 1: The first responder experts will show a high acceptance of the technology, indicating that it fits their needs for medical skill training.

Overall, the comments of the experts regarding the assessment and treatment of the patient via haptic feedback of the manikin and the use of real tools suggests, that our MR approach can indeed enable medical skills training. The generally high ratings on the technology acceptance scales by both the experts and the first responders further strengthen that argument.

Both the experts and the first responders rated hedonic motivation the highest of the technology acceptance scales, indicating interacting with the MR manikin was fun and enjoyable for them.

Effort expectancy was another scale that was rated very positively: Experts could learn the interactions quickly and easily. It seems that the use of their real tools enables a quick transfer into MR, as no new actions have to be learned, in comparison to traditional controller-based VR training.

Performance expectancy was also regarded as positive, meaning that the experts deem the MR manikin to be a useful tool in daily training routines, helping achieve training goals quicker and more efficiently. This was further echoed by verbal feedback (e.g.: "Assisting the respiration of the patient worked really well, including the chest expansion when I pumped in the air") during the experience and in the concluding group discussion.

The scale Facilitating Conditions was not rated negatively but reveals some room for improvement. Experts did not feel as prepared for the interactions as they could have, which reflects well in a statement by an expert: "The training could go more into the use of the tools. I didn't know immediately how to take off the T-shirt, for example". Our MR manikin is still in the prototyping phase, and the short introduction by the experimenters was seemingly not enough. In a polished training system, a tutorial should therefore be included to teach users the interactions in MR. A similar issue was reported by Bucher et. al [ 7 ], who did not include verbal instructions in the early stage of their VR reanimation prototype, which led to some confusion.

Lastly, Behavioral Intention was also rated highly, indicating that the experts are willing to use an MR training system regularly in the future, which is a strong argument for further development of the system. Similarly to Semeraro et al. [ 52 ] we think that the addition of MR to traditional forms of training may improve realistic training of diagnosis of the patients state and provide feedback on the effect of a certain treatment (e.g. the color of the skin changing in response to missing respiratory support).

Apart from the quantitative and qualitative (formative) evaluation of our specific use case, some more general learnings could be extracted from the workshop and user study regarding user acceptance of mixed reality training simulations.

For one, the transition from VR to MR training – despite its many promises – also brings more challenges. With the increasing sensory realism we found that expectations also rise. In VR training missing haptics are often accepted as a limitation of the technology and not further mentioned, whereas in our implementation having ’only’ the torso as a haptic prop to simulate the virtual agents body was often criticised. This illustrates, that transitioning to MR requires going ’all the way’ for tangible elements - in this case including a full body manikin. As Chen et al. [ 10 ] mention in their classification paper of medical MR, the sense of touch ’provides important cues in medical procedures’, which is especially true for the patient.

The theme of ’going all the way’ was also present in feedback concerning the tangible tools: While the integrated tools were generally appreciated, it was also noted that only providing a selection of tools limits the decision space during the training, resulting in a much easier decision making process.

At the same time, our results show that if these tangible elements are implemented properly it results in high intuitiveness and ease of use for the users, as the mappings of interactions and more direct and the interface is embodied in its original shape. This was the case for the tools in our scenario, where we observed participants using them without former explanation or instruction. The VR reanimation training ’VRAnimate II’ for example used controller based interaction, which resulted in problems for some in understanding the buttons [ 7 ]. The increased intuitiveness of the MR approach due to tangibility in terms of augmented virtuality is in line with other work in the field (e.g., [ 60 ]).

Another challenge in transitioning from VR to MR and overlaying physical props with virtual representations is the need to indicate, which objects of the virtual world are virtual and which have a physical counterpart. In our case, this uncertainty led to some insecurity which virtual agents could be touched and which not. We see a gap here for new forms of communicating physicality in virtual environments.

5.2 Hypothesis 2: The first responder experts will experience high amounts of presence in the immersive environment, indicated by ratings and physiological measurements.

Combining the comments and ratings of presence, we get a differentiated view of the presence-evoking capabilities of the MR manikin in its current form. The questionnaire results of both the experts and the first responders show, that physical presence and self-presence were both rated mostly highly, whereas social presence was rated slightly lower on average. This reflects in the qualitative data, as the experts felt immersed while using the medical tools (i.e. physical presence) and touching the manikin with their own hands (i.e. self-presence), but at the same time criticized the behavior of the virtual agents. The virtual agents did not turn their heads, show facial expressions or react to touch, resulting in a lower social presence. One expert did not experience any self-presence, as they did not notice that their own hands were present in the MR simulation. This is interesting, as on the one side they rated their self-presence as low, on the other side they did the treatment and assessment all with their hands without noticing - which could be interpreted as a strong sign of presence. Overall, the answer to H2 seems to be that the experts generally felt high amounts of presence regarding the interactions and their own self-presence in the scenario, but were partly taken out of the experience by the virtual agents not being responsive enough.

The realism of the auditory landscape played a major role in the experience of presence: the ambient sounds of sirens and a busy city in the background, combined with the medically accurate and changing breathing patterns seemed to evoke realistic behavior and stress in the scenario when looking at the qualitative data. Indeed, breathing patterns and sound mark an important pillar of the medical realism of the scenario, and as a focal point for first responders should therefore receive attention when designing a training scenario for this group of end users.

Additionally, the experts reported that the training scenario was experienced as stressful, as was intended. Indeed, the GSR results suggest increased arousal levels during the scenario, with the peaks per minute staying continuously high during the scenario. This indicates, that during the scenario, there were frequent moments of emotional arousal, which in the context of a challenging medical scenario can be interpreted as a stress response to the environment. The mean peak amplitude on the other hand was only significantly higher for the approach condition. Higher peaks are usually associated with a higher intensity of emotional arousal. In the case of the MR manikin scenario, the approach interval marked the beginning of the experience. The experts, therefore, demonstrated more peaks and more intense peaks in the initial assessment phase compared to the baseline, with intensity decreasing once they treat the patient. The expert seems to be under stress at the beginning of the scenario, where there still is a lot of uncertainty, and switch to a less intense but still stimulated state of arousal once they chose their treatment and during the action.

The first responders exhibited similar patters as the experts in terms of phasic peaks per minute between the three time periods (baseline, approach and treatment). Their average peaks per minute though were significantly lower than those of the experts. This is a plausible result, as the first responders actually encounter situation like the one portrayed in the scenario on a regular basis and are therefore more used to it.

The phasic peak amplitude shows a different pattern with the first responders: here additionally to the difference between baseline and approach, there is also a difference between baseline and treatment. The intensity of arousal does therefore not drop during treatment as it does in the experts. This could be an indication, that the first responders continued to experience the situation as stressful, knowing the dangers even during treatment, whereas the experts might have been calmed by doing the obviously intended treatment. This difference in physiological reactions should be investigated further and could help in assessing the performance of trainees during virtual simulation training.

We think that part of the high presence experience in our prototype is due to what Mel Slater calls the ’plausibility illusion’ [ 53 ]. This term denotes one aspect of presence, pertaining to experiencing the events happening in a virtual environment as ’really happening’ or plausible in the specific context. The realistic decay of the patients state in the scenario due to close development with the end users and experts seems to have fostered this experience of plausibility. For training medical procedures in XR we therefore deem it highly important to adhere to the plausibility, which includes visuals (e.g. skin color), sounds (e.g. changing breathing sound), timings (e.g. the time it takes for the patient to decay).

Regarding social presence, or the sense of being with another [ 5 ], we found that our participants have a high need for more realistic social interaction. Talking with the role playing trainer was generally perceived positively, but lacking animations in terms of head movement or reactivity to touch limited the experience of social presence in our prototype. The importance of realistic virtual agents in XR training is in line with other research in the field. For example, Strojny et al. [ 55 ] studied the effects of bystanders in a similar context: fire fighter first response training in VR. They found, that social presence is a significant moderator of the effect of bystanders on performance in such a situation. In situations involving social agents, social presence therefore crucial as it impacts behavior and performance. Similar effects could be shown more recently by Leluo et al. [ 34 ] in regard to facial expressions of virtual agents. They showed, that virtual agents were more likely to evoke trusting behavior in the trust game when they showed changing facial expressions than when they did not. In the first responder / patient relationship, facial expressions are therefore crucial as a form of social connection and to better read the patients state.

5.3 Limitations

There are certain limitations that need to be mentioned. First, this work marks only a formative evaluation of the proposed system. The workshop and the user study were conducted with a small sample size and we did not have a comparative condition like a purely VR version of the training scenario. Although half our participants did experience MR before, a novelty effect can not be ruled out, which would be more evident with a comparison condition. To evaluate the benefits of a MR training to VR or real training, a larger study in the form of a randomized controlled trial will be conducted in the future during the project.

Regarding the experienced emotional arousal in the scenario, a comparison group or a baseline condition would also reveal the impact of the trainee's reaction in MR compared to reality while we only compared it to a calmness baseline. This could further strengthen the need for immersive skills training in comparison to conventional skills training.

Also it has to be noted that the proposed prototype still relied on multiple wizard-of-oz elements, which need to be automated to fully prove the feasibility of this approach. For example, the trainer still had to react to the trainees interactions, e.g. to adapt the heart rate or in communication.

5.4 Future Research

In future work, we aim to replace the torso manikin we used with a full-body manikin. To truly enable medical treatment, the manikin has to be complete and moveable, as assessment procedures involve looking at the patient's back and listening to lung sounds there, or checking for other wounds. This will pose the challenge of tracking sub-parts of the manikin (e.g. the head for head movement, the arms, the legs). Also, it would be favorable, to integrate highly realistic manikins with real skin feel, the possibility to feel the pulse and respiratory simulation into MR, to truly harness the potential of combining the two approaches. An example of such a manikin would be the Adam-X patient simulator by Medical-X 14 .

With highly realistic manikins like the Adam-X, future research could consider showing the physical manikin without a virtual overlay. This would solve possible tracking issues but at the cost of the variability in the visualization of wounds and bodily states. Comparing the usage of highly realistic manikins with the approach presented in this work would be beneficial to assess this alternative.

More work needs to be done in the area of creating realistic cause and effect representations of injuries of the virtual agents. For example, when a certain injury is not treated right, it often worsens the injury, whereas if the right procedure is chosen it gets better. These treatment patterns and outcomes should be developed in collaboration with medical experts, to create realistic virtual patients which react automatically and do not need to be controlled manually.

As [ 10 ] propose, speech and gesture recognition is a field of research that could greatly benefit medical MR training. This would enable automated communication with the virtual agent and reduce the wizard-of-oz components in MR training systems.

When developing a training it is also important to think about how trainees learn the skills in MR when compared to other modes of learning. Future work should include comparison studies between conventional forms of learning and the MR approach presented in this work. This would also entail measuring performance when in stress, as this would more closely represent the real situation.

6 CONCLUSION

In current medical simulation training, realistic manikins are used to teach physical skills and procedures, whereas VR systems are used to train situation assessment and decision-making. In this work, we present the development and evaluation of a medical MR training scenario that integrates a physical tangible manikin and physical tangible tools into a virtual accident scenario environment. This MR training scenario allows trainees to use real tools and their own hands when interacting with the physical manikin overlaid with a responsive virtual agent. The proposed MR training was designed and evaluated in close exchange with first responder organizations. Participants experienced high levels of physical- and self-presence, increased stress levels, and reported a high technology acceptance. This implies, that MR is seen as a viable technological solution to create immersive skill training, which end-users see as a useful tool and addition to existing training solutions. The proposed main requirements of first responders regarding haptic multi-sensory skill training in MR and the lessons learned from the workshop aim to guide the design of training solutions for medical training in MR which shall be addressed in future work.
