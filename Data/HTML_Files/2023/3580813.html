3D Printable Play-Dough: New Biodegradable Materials and Creative Possibilities for Digital Fabrication

Leah Buechley , Hand and Machine, Department of Computer Science, University of New Mexico, United States, buechley@unm.edu
Ruby Ta , Hand and Machine, Department of Computer Science, University of New Mexico, United States, nta@unm.edu

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3580813
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Play-dough is a brightly-colored, easy-to-make, and familiar material. We have developed and tested custom play-dough materials that can be employed in 3D printers designed for clay. This paper introduces a set of recipes for 3D printable play-dough along with an exploration of these materials’ print characteristics. We explore the design potential of play-dough as a sustainable fabrication material, highlighting its recyclability, compostability, and repairability. We demonstrate how custom-color prints can be designed and constructed and describe how play-dough can be used as a support material for clay 3D prints. We also present a set of example artifacts made from play-dough and discuss opportunities for future research.

1 INTRODUCTION

Play-dough is cheap, easy-to-make, biodegradable, and can be dyed with food coloring into vivid colors. We have developed workflows that allow it to be printed on low cost desktop clay 3D printers, like the Eazao Zero, which sells for under $ 1000 USD. There has been some very limited exploration of the 3D printing of play-dough in the past. In particular, the 3D printing company Hyrel has demonstrated that it is possible to print this material [ 24 ]. However, there is no research literature on this topic and the creative potential of play-dough has not been explored.

This paper introduces recipes and workflows for 3D printing home-made play-dough. We investigate the printability of several different play-dough recipes through a series of structured experiments using a model developed by Duty et. al. [ 16 ]. This includes an exploration of the extrudability and structural stability during the printing, and (critically) drying processes. We print a range of geometry with play-dough and compare the material to PLA (polylactic acid) along the dimensions of recycleability, repairability, compostability, and printer energy consumption.

The use of play-dough in 3D printing opens up a range of new opportunities which we also begin to explore. In this paper, we focus on two such opportunities. First, we explore the possibilities of printing full-color play-dough prints. We argue that this expands the expressive capabilities of 3D printing by enabling users to easily generate their own custom-color filaments. It also supports a low-resolution mapping of color to computationally generated features of form, similar to the ikat method of dying yarn before weaving [ 20 ].

Second, we introduce a technique where we print supports for clay forms out of play-dough. These supports burn away when a clay piece is fired, leaving only the clay form behind. Our method for printing easily removable supports for clay is the first of its kind since dissolvable supports are not a viable option–clay dissolves in liquid until it is fired. This technique could dramatically expand the class of 3D-printable ceramic geometries, and we believe will prove particularly useful once dual-nozzle clay printers become widely available.

The key contributions of this paper are: 1) An accessible workflow for measuring and determining the printability of play-dough, clay and similar materials; 2) Recipes for 3D printable play-dough and a detailed exploration of material characteristics; 3) A comparison of play-dough to PLA with respect to features including recycleability, repairability, and compostability; 4) Examples of new creative opportunities afforded by play-dough, including the ability to print bright custom-colored artifacts and burn-able supports for clay forms.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Sustainablility and HCI

The Human Computer Interaction (HCI) research community is increasingly interested in issues related to sustainability. Work in this area has included the use and development of biodegradable materials that can be used in electronics manufacturing [ 39 ] and other making processes [ 7 ], and the development of workflows that incorporate intentional re-use and/or deconstruction [ 46 ].

A particularly relevant strand of this work has investigated using biodegradable materials in digital fabrication processes. For instance, Lazaro Vasquez et al. developed a home-made mycelium-based sheet material for laser cutting [ 29 ] and Tao et al. constructed shape-changing flour-based structures using laser etching and 3D printing [ 42 ]. It is also worth mentioning that many textiles, including silk, cotton, and linen, are plant-based biodegradable materials widely used in fabrication [ 46 ].

Our work contributes to the ongoing conversation about how we can leverage biodegradable materials in digital fabrication and the broader context of HCI. Such materials are desirable not only for their practical sustainable characteristics, but also because they foreground new ideas and values as well as new creative and aesthetic possibilities.

2.2 3D Printing of Ceramics

Chen et. al. provides an excellent review of the array of different technologies and approaches that have been developed to 3D print ceramics [ 11 ]. Of particular relevance are extrusion-based clay 3D printers made by companies including Eazao [ 17 ], 3D Potter [ 2 ], WASP [ 45 ], and StoneFlower [ 40 ].

Designers and HCI reserachers have begun to use such machines to explore clay as a site of creative and conceptual possibility. Desjardins and Tihanyi's Listening Cups, for example, are a series of beautiful 3D printed mugs with surface patterns that act as tactile data physicalizations of sound [ 14 ]. Rael and San Fratello used clay harvested from land close to the US/Mexico border to create 3D printed vessels with gently varying colors and dramatic surface textures they called "Bad Hombres", referencing the political dialogue centered around these spaces [ 36 ]. Additional compelling explorations of clay 3D printing include the works of Shelby Doyle[ 15 ], Piotr Wasniowski [ 44 ], and Brian Bryan Czibesz [ 12 ], among others.

This emerging subfield is illuminating the possibilities of clay printing that are distinct and very different from from FFF and other types of 3D printing. Unique opportunities that are afforded by ceramics and clay 3D printers include a focus on surface textures as much as form, the creation of usable everyday objects made from a beautiful and durable material, and the meanings imbued in material harvested from specific places. Our explorations of play-dough arose from initial explorations of 3D printing clay and we have found that the clay-like material shares many affordances with ceramics.

2.3 3D Printing of Biodegradable Materials

In the last few decades, a large body of research has developed around 3D printing food [ 30 ]. A subset of that work has explored how to print doughs [ 32 ], exploring variables that can be adjusted to achieve desired shapes [ 47 ], texture [ 43 ], or color [ 21 ]. Researchers have explored using many different flours, including those with and without gluten [ 19 ][ 33 ]. All of this work has been constrained by the fact that the resulting artifacts need to be edible.

Materials researchers have also investigated the development and use of a wider range of biodegradable materials for 3D printing in other contexts [ 37 ][ 23 ]. These include materials for FFF printers, the extrusion-based machines we employ in our research, and a range of other technologies. Cellulose, starch, gelatin, calcium alginate and other materials derived from renewable sources have been successfully printed [ 37 ].

Our work introduces a new type of simple and accessible biodegradable 3D filament that we hope can find an audience in maker, DIY, and education communities, contributing to a different kind of exploration and adoption of sustainable materials for 3D printing by a broader non-scientist audience.

2.4 3D Printing in Color

Commercial full color 3D printers rely on technologies similar to inkjet printing where colored droplets of plastic are deposited to form an artifact. These printers and materials are much more expensive than FFF machines or materials [ 25 ]. Color printing in FFF machines is achievable primarily through using colored filaments. A wide range of colored filaments are commercially available, but designers cannot easily make custom-color filament or prints.

Previous research has explored a variety of methods for producing custom-color prints for FFF printers. These include pre-coloring filament using markers [ 31 ], employing dual-extrusion FFF printers to generate complex multi-color and grey-scale effects [ 28 ], and melting different colors of filament together as they are extruded[ 38 ]. In this paper, we introduce a different kind of approach, generating custom-color filament by loading different colors of play-dough into a print tube. This process is straightforward and easy to implement, requiring no modification of the printer's hardware or firmware.

3 SOFTWARE

To generate 3D printer paths to evaluate our materials, we wrote a custom open-source software library that enables us to create paths through space and then translate these paths into g-code, the file-format that defines the toolpath of 3D printers [ 8 ]. It is intended to give programmers fine-grained control over the surface texture of 3D prints as well as their geometry. The Python-based library is employed in the context of Grasshopper and uses Rhino to generate 3D visualizations of tool-paths and prints.

The library uses a blend of traditional geometry and Turtle Geometry [ 3 ] to define the path of a printer's nozzle through space. The nozzle is associated with a "turtle" that can be moved around using forward, back, left and right commands as well as their 3D analogues. "pen up" and "pen down" commands are used to start and stop extrusion. A print's geometry and surface texture is defined by the movement of the turtle through space. Additional commands give a user control over printer parameters like print volume, nozzle speed, nozzle size, and extrude rate. Figure 3 shows an example of a turtle program that uses forward, right and lift commands to draw a cylinder along with a visualization of the part in Rhino.

We originally developed the library to explore the creation of textured surfaces using FFF printers [ 35 ]. We have since added support for several clay 3D printers along with a number of new features. We added visualization tools that enable us to view fully rendered designs in Rhino before printing them. We have also added the ability to associate different colors with different parts of a path to support multi-color printing using a "set color" command. We have added tools that enable us to track the amount of material used during printing and use this information to generate a visualization of the print tube as it needs to be loaded for any print. This visualization shows the volume of material that should be loaded into the print tube, displaying it as the height to which material should be loaded in the tube in mm. (We have implemented this feature for the Eazao Zero, Potter Bot Micro, and Potter Bot Super clay 3D printers.) The software also displays the weight of the material that should be loaded, a calculation based on user-entered density information. See the bottom of Figure 2 .

We have also built a new suite of helper functions that enable more advanced g-code generation. In particular, we developed a general-purpose slicing tool that allows the user to use arbitrary solid mesh geometry as input and then creates a shell of the geometry with a variety of surface textures. This enables us to build on existing geometrical models as well as create printer paths from scratch using turtle commands. Our library includes several built-in surface textures, including a woven-like texture. Figure 2 top shows images of an input shape and the code used to produce a g-code file that will print a textured version of the shape. The bottom of the figure shows software visualizations along with a physical print. More information about the software can be found in our online reference [ 8 ].

4 MEASUREMENT PROCEDURES

A successful play-dough or clay 3D print requires the material be soft enough to print but hard enough to remain structurally sound. If a material is too firm, it cannot be extruded, pushed through the printing nozzle. On the other hand, if a material is too soft, walls can slump or collapse during the printing process [ 10 ][ 16 ].

Materials of consistent hardness are required for consistent 3D printing yet the clay 3D printing community has not yet developed standard methods for assessing and insuring this consistency. The artist Johnathan Keep, who hosts a popular clay 3D printing blog, has developed perhaps the most widely known measurement technique, the drop spike approach [ 26 ]. He built a custom metal spike which he drops into a block of clay from a specific height. Measuring the depth that the spike penetrates into the clay enables him to compare the hardness, and therefore printability, of different materials. While useful in comparing materials in a single lab or studio, this technique does not generate measurements that are applicable to larger contexts because they are not associated with a real-world unit.

We wanted to identify a low-cost and accessible measurement of hardness tied to real-world units. A range of well known methods and tools have been used to measure the hardness, and therefore plasticity, of clay within more technical ceramics communities, including calibrated drop-spike methods. See [ 6 ] for an excellent historical overview. Rheological testing of materials provides accurate and comprehensive information, but it depends on expensive laboratory equipment and engineering expertise [ 10 ].

We decided to investigate the "indention method", which has been shown to provide reliable results using fairly simple equipment [ 13 ]. We use a penetrometer to measure indention hardness, the resistance of a material to deformation when subjected to a continuous load, in force per unit area kg / cm 2 . A penetrometer measures the force required to press a surface of defined area into a material a specific depth.

The paste-like materials we employ for 3D printing are too soft to be measured with penetrometers that were intended for measuring soil or traditional clay. To solve this problem, we found a very sensitive hand-held penetrometer that was designed to measure the hardness (and therefore ripeness) of soft fruit like strawberries and bananas [ 5 ]. The tool uses a cylindrical measurement foot that is pressed into a material to a depth of 5mm, see Figure 4 . The tool was intended to be used with a 8mm diameter foot. To increase the sensitivity of the instrument so that we could measure our very soft materials, we exchanged the 8mm foot for an 11mm foot. When the surface area of the measurement foot is increased, more force is required to press the tool into a material; a larger foot means a more sensitive penetrometer. We converted our measurements to the correct unit by multiplying them by .53, the ratio of the surface area of the two feet. Our penetrometer has a rated precision of + / − 0.02 kg / cm 2 .

We also measured the density of each material by weighing 100 cm 3 of each material and dividing mass by volume. Note that this number is numerically equivalent to specific gravity. All measurements were taken at room temperature.

This system has been critical to our explorations. It has enabled us to mix consistently printable play-dough and to mix consistent materials over time. It has also provided us with a way to identify, isolate, and compare other physical characteristics of materials.

For the traditional recipe, all ingredients are mixed together and then cooked over medium heat until the dough is at the desired hardness. For all other recipes, the ingredients are mixed together in a kitchen mixer. The oil should be added after the other ingredients have been mixed slightly to prevent clumping. No cooking is required. Once thoroughly mixed, water can be added to achieve the desired hardness. Note that for the corn-based recipes, it is important to use finely ground corn flour , not coarsely ground corn meal.

Water can be substituted for some or all of the vinegar in these recipes if the material will be printed soon after it is mixed. Vinegar acts as a preservative and lengthens the life of the dough. We recommend that dough be stored in a refrigerator. Dough that is not dried or refrigerated will decompose quickly.

Previous research has shown that the printability of epoxy resins and other materials can be increased by mixing them with clay [ 22 ]. Clay increases the viscosity of resins and improves the structural stability of prints. We experimented with adding clay to play-dough to see if this could similarly increase its printability and stability. We made a dough from a combination of the 75% corn 25% wheat play-dough and clay. For this recipe, clay is first mixed in a kitchen mixer with water to an indention hardness between .5 and .6 kg / cm 2 , a suitable hardness for 3D printing. The softened clay and play-dough are then blended together by weight in a 25% clay to 75% play-dough ratio. Water is added to achieve a final indention hardness between .5 and .6.

All materials were colored to facilitate easy tracking during testing. Table 2 shows the indention hardness, density, and color of each of the materials we tested.

These recipes are a result of an iterative research process. We began by attempting to print traditional play-dough. We developed gluten-free and gluten-reduced doughs to address challenges that arose in printing wheat-based dough. We then began adding clay to our recipes in attempt to improve print quality. Finally, our sand and glue recipe arose from a desire to develop a material with print characteristics similar to the clay and dough mixture that did not require clay.

It is likely that these recipes could be improved with further research. We present them not as ideal recipes, but as viable and useful ones that can provide a starting point for future investigations. The printing characteristics of these recipes are compared in detail in the next section.

6 DEVELOPING AND CHARACTERIZING PRINTABLE PLAY-DOUGH

Duty et al. identified four components of material printability in extrusion-based printing: pressure-driven extrusion flow, bead formation, bead functionality, and component functionality [ 16 ]. Pressure-driven extrusion flow describes the requirement that a material be extrudable by a printer. Bead formation and bead functionality describe the desirable characteristics of a strand of extruded material or "bead" once it leaves the printer. Component functionality describes the structural soundness and dimensional accuracy of the printed part. We conducted a series of tests that explored each of these aspects of printability to compare the performance of the recipes listed above.

The physical characteristics of the printer and process parameters defined in software are as important as the characteristics of materials in 3D printing. Important non-material variables include the nozzle size, print speed, extrude rate, layer height, and mix factor. We choose to keep most variables constant for our tests to focus on material properties. Unless otherwise noted, prints were printed on an Eazao Zero printer using a 1.5mm inner-diameter nozzle, a print speed of 1000mm/minute, an extrude rate of 1.25mm of filament extruded per mm traveled, and a layer height of 1.0mm. Our mix factor–which defines the relative speed of the auger and plunger stepper motors–is .92 (auger) to .08 (plunger). Figure 5 shows a diagram illustrating features of extrusion-based printing.

To improve print quality, we added a custom heater to the extruder of our printer. The heater dries out layers as they are extruded, helping to improve the structural stability of prints. The heater is constructed from two fans and a heating element we built from coiled 26 gauge nichrome wire. One fan and heater coil sits in front of the extruder and one behind to insure even coverage. The heater is connected to the underside of the extruder's metal frame with magnets so that it can be easily attached to and removed from the machine. The heater is attached to its own power supply and on/off switch. Figure 5 also shows images of this set up. Tests were conducted with and without the heater to assess its impact on print quality.

6.1 Extrudability and Bead Formation

The pressure-driven extrusion flow or extrudability aspect of printability describes a relationship between a printer and a material. The printer's motors, which drive the extrusion–via the plunger and auger–must be able to supply enough pressure to force the material out of the nozzle. All components of the machine (ie: tubes and material containers) must also be able to withstand this pressure.

Bead formation identifies the requirement that a material, once extruded, must form a "bead" with stable geometry, see Figure 5 . We explored extrudability and bead formation in tandem, working to develop materials that were extrudable while forming a stable bead. Bead stability was assessed visually. An excessively dry recipe, or a material that is too hard to extrude quickly, can result in a bead that crumbles while an overly wet recipe can result in a bead that is unable to hold a stable shape. Liquids, which are easy to extrude, but do not form a stable bead, are a useful point of reference. For the remainder of this section, we use the term "extrudability" to mean a material can be extruded with stable bead geometry.

The rheology of a material–properties that determine how it flows when force is applied–determines if it can be extruded by a given machine. Hardness, unlike the full suite of rheological characteristics, is easy to measure. Hardness measurements are useful in comparing the extrudability and printablity of slight variations of a material (ie: a play-dough containing different amounts of water). But, they cannot be used to compare different kinds of materials. Different materials are extrudable at different hardnesses.

Many materials exhibit nonlinear rheological behavior that is not easily characterized. Wheat-flour based doughs are particularly complex [ 18 ]. Some materials, including wheat-based doughs, exhibit shear-thickening behaviors that make them unsuitable for 3D printing. A shear-thickening material's viscosity increases when it is under stress and decreases when the stress is removed. This is an undesirable property for a 3D printing material because it means the material is difficult to extrude–it behaves more like a solid during extrusion–but has limited structural stability–it behaves more like a liquid after extrusion. Ideal 3D printing materials have shear-thinning characteristics; their viscosity decreases under stress and increases when the stress is removed[ 32 ].

Traditional home-made play-dough, which is a mixture of wheat-flour, salt, and water, is a shear thickening non-Newtonian fluid [ 18 ]. We began our experiments attempting to print this complex material. Traditional play-dough can be extruded, but it has to be softened considerably. Commercial play-dough has an indention hardness of .7 kg / cm 2 . Our home-made traditional play-dough required a hardness of .25 kg / cm 2 to be extruded on the Eazao Zero. Doughs harder than approximately .4 kg / cm 2 could not be extruded. Softer doughs could be extruded, but bead formation and print quality declined quickly along with hardness.

Most of the problematic properties of traditional home-made play-dough stem from the gluten in wheat flour. Gluten-free doughs behave more like plastic fluids, exhibiting more linear rheological behavior [ 32 ]. We chose to use corn flour for the gluten-free and gluten-reduced recipes shown in Table 1 because it is widely available and inexpensive in the United States. All of our recipes were made with Maseca brand corn flour. Gluten-free and gluten-reduced play-doughs can be extruded when mixed to higher indention hardness values than wheat-based doughs, but this decreases bead quality, resulting in a bead that can crumble and crack during printing. Because of these trade offs, all these recipes were mixed to a hardness of between .2 and .4 kg / cm 2 .

All of the materials listed in Table 2 are extrudable on the Eazao Zero printer. Extrudability is a prerequisite to any other print test. It is worth noting that it took a number of experiments and a considerable amount of time to develop extrudable materials.

6.2 Bead Functionality

Bead functionality requires that a bead: 1) can serve as a strong foundation for subsequent layers and 2) can span small gaps without significant sagging. Bead functionality is a function of material characteristics and printing parameters.

6.2.1 Bead functionality and layer height. To test bead functionality for our different materials, we first printed a series of cylinders 30mm wide by 30mm tall. This helped us identify an appropriate layer height for printing and also revealed noteworthy material characteristics. We generated tool-paths for the cylinders that consisted of the sides only, no bottom or top, with layer heights ranging from .6mm to 1.8mm. We printed out these tool-paths for each of our test materials. We printed at least four copies of each print with at least two different material batches to insure that the print behavior we observed was consistent and repeatable. We printed the cylinders both with and without the heater.

Figure 6 shows an image chart of the cylinders. Images were taken immediately after printing. Different color-coded materials are shown on the y-axis and different layer heights are shown on the x-axis. Each column of images represents different materials printed with the same tool-path, which is shown in front view on the bottom row.

We observed three kinds of vertical bead instability that determine the nature of oscillating patterns on some of the forms. If the layer height is too large, prints for all materials become unstable. The prints in the far right-hand column all began to collapse because the layer height (1.8mm) is significantly larger than the diameter of the bead (approximately 1.5mm, the diameter of the nozzle). The nozzle ends up extruding filament above the previous layer. The filament begins to curl and twist before it reaches the previous layer, creating the oscillating patterns that are visible in the figure. The problem worsens with each layer, as the nozzle moves further and further away from the print. An image taken during the printing of one of these cylinders is shown on the left in Figure 7 .

The second type of instability is a result of the vertical walls of a print oscillating in the directions normal to the vertical wall–in the radial direction. The form flexes and bends during printing, generating an oscillating pattern on the wall's surface. This behavior can be seen in the center image in Figure 7 . Lighter and more elastic materials are particularly susceptible to this type of failure. The traditional play-dough prints from 1.0 - 1.4mm layer heights exhibit this behavior.

Using the heater significantly decreased this instability for all materials but the traditional play-dough. Figure 8 shows a representative selection of prints made with and without the heater. As can be seen in the Figure, the heater had a particularly dramatic impact on the stability of the Sand and Glue dough.

Some of the prints exhibit a combination of instability 1 and instability 2. For example, the 100% corn and corn and wheat doughs with layer heights of 1.6 and 1.8mm begin exhibiting instability 2 and end exhibiting instability 1.

The third type of instability is present only for the traditional play-dough printed in the no-heater condition. When the layer height is small, the dough pushes the previous layer out of the way instead of compressing it, resulting in a double-walled print. A first layer prints normally. The second layer prints around the outside of the previous layer instead of on top of it. The third layer prints normally again and the pattern repeats as the form grows. A close-up image of an in-progress print exhibiting this behavior is shown in Figure 7 right. The use of the heater during printing reduces this behavior.

It may be possible to leverage the oscillating behavior of different materials for creative purposes. Takahashi and Miyashita experimented with raising the nozzle above the printing surface (instability 1) to generate different decorative effects in PLA prints [ 41 ]. Instabilities 2 and 3 may be worth exploring in a similar way. However, as the next section will demonstrate, these material-based instabilities make it difficult to print more complex geometries.

6.2.2 Bead functionality: layer compression. To further our understanding of bead stability, we printed a series of bowls with walls at various angles. These allow us to see whether or not a bead is holding up subsequent layers of a print. If lower layers are compressed by upper layers, the bowl shape slumps and takes on a rounded character. We can get a measure of bead stability by measuring the difference between the shape as defined by the toolpath and the actual printed shape. Figure 10 shows a diagram that demonstrates this dynamic.

We began by printing bowls with single walls, but we found it more useful to compare prints with "woven walls". Each layer is printed with a circular path that follows the shape of a sine wave. The sine wave is offset every other layer to create a weave-like pattern on the print's walls. Woven walls provide a print with more structural stability–the walls are thicker and better supported–and enable the printing of forms that would not otherwise be possible. For this set of tests, woven walls enabled us to print vessels with larger overhangs.

Figure 9 shows an image chart of bowls with overhang angles ranging from 70° to 50°. For each bowl shown in the figure, we printed at least four copies to insure consistent print behavior. Images were taken immediately after printing, from the same location and a frontal orientation, level with the prints, so that we could compare print characteristics. The angle specified in the g-code is shown next to each print to facilitate comparison.

Using the heater improved print performance for all materials. It improves bead performance by drying and hardening lower layers during printing, allowing them to better support the weight of subsequent layers. The improvement is particularly striking for the traditional and sand and glue recipes. In both cases, the heater made it possible to print shapes that collapsed without it, Figure 9 red and lime green.

In the no-heater condition, slumping increases as the overhang angle of the toolpath increases. With the traditional play-dough, even the 70° print exhibits considerable slumping. Though the heater improves the print behavior of this dough, it is still poor in comparison to other materials. The slumping on the 50° angle print with the heater is particularly noteworthy. Due to its poor performance, we eliminated the traditional dough from further testing.

6.3 Structural Stability

Component functionality or structural stability describes characteristics of a full print, its soundness and dimensional accuracy. A print is not in it's final state until it is fully dry. This component of printability encompasses both the printing process and any necessary post-processing steps including drying.

With play-dough, like with clay, distortion or cracking can occur as a print dries. These problems are usually caused by differential shrinkage, which happens when one part of a print dries before another. This may happen if one area is printed before another, if one part of a print is thicker than another, or if the part is dried unevenly. For the tests described in this section, all prints were dried in a de-humidifier at a temperature of 115°F (46°C) for six hours. This insured that prints were dried evenly and consistently through a controllable and repeatable process.

We tested the vertical shrinkage behavior by printing a set of five 30mm x 30mm cylinders for each material (except the traditional play-dough) using a layer height of 1.0mm. We measured the height of prints before and after drying. We conducted these tests for prints both with and without the heater. Figure 11 shows a chart of these results.

The heater improves shrinkage performance slightly. The difference between the heater and no-heater condition is only statistically significant for the 100% corn dough. This dough experiences the largest shrinkage (19% no-heater and 17% heater) and the glue and sand mixture the smallest (5% no-heater and 3.7% heater). This makes sense because the sand in the mixture experiences no volumetric change during the drying process. The corn and wheat dough exhibits the next best shrinkage behavior.

In addition to shrinkage, we visually inspected these prints for cracks once they had dried. The 100% corn dough exhibited visible cracking and distortion for both heater and no-heater conditions, with no significant difference between the two. All other prints dried with no noticeable distortions or cracks. We also dried and inspected the bowls we printed to test slumping, as described in the last section. The bowl printed from 100% corn dough exhibits cracking and distortion, as can be seen in Figure 12 left. The 25% clay dough exhibits some cracking though no significant distortion, Figure 12 right. The other two materials remained stable. Due to the problems evident with 100% corn dough, it was eliminated from further tests.

To test the dimensional accuracy of prints with more complex geometry, we printed two additional models. A larger vase form (approximately 100 x 100 x 60mm, 4 x 4 x 2.5 in) and the Stanford Bunny model (approximately 100 x 100 x 80mm, 4 x 4 x 3in)[ 1 ]. These forms were printed in the corn and wheat, glue and sand, and 25% clay mixtures. The heater was used for all prints. The g-code for the bunny print was generated with Simplify3D using a layer height of .6mm and an infill percentage of 10%. (Our software does not yet generate infill or support structures.) Supports were printed for the bunny's right ear and underbelly. All of these forms initially printed with no cracks or distortion. They were then placed in the dehumidifier. The fully dried prints are shown Figure 13 .

All vase forms printed and dried successfully though slight distortion is noticable in the 25% clay mixture near the lip of the vase. For the bunny prints, both the corn and wheat and the 25% clay mixtures exhibit cracking. The cracking was most significant for the 25% clay mixture, which experiences significant cracks between layers as well as other stress cracks. There are some other printing issues however. In particular, there is some drooping on the underside of the left ear, which was printed without support.

6.4 Printability Conclusions and Discussion

Based on the tests we conducted, we have come up with the printability classification shown in Table 3 . Materials are rated from 0 (not extrudable) to 5.0 (suitable for general-purpose printing; similar in printability to PLA) and sorted according to their printability. We rate both the corn and wheat and the glue and sand recipes as printable. However, based on the results of our tests, the corn and wheat is suitable only for printing hollow vessels and other simple forms with walls of fairly uniform thickness. We believe the glue and sand recipe can serve as a robust general purpose printing material, but there are print scenarios not fully covered in the tests we conducted, which is why we are not assigning it a 5.0 rating.

There are other considerations that must be taken into account in play-dough printing. It is more challenging to start and stop filament flow on extrusion based printers. Printers without augers, like those in the Potter Bot series, have no ability to start and stop filament flow. All movement of the print head is accompanied by a bead of filament. An auger equipped printer, like the Eazao Zero, is able to start and stop flow, but the transition is not as clean as it is for FFF printers. These transition points result in nubs of extra filament at the transition location. The ideal print path is one that keeps a continuous bead as much as possible–takes as few travel steps as possible. Figure 14 shows the back of one of our Stanford bunny prints immediately after printing when many nubs were visible. Most of these can be brushed or cut off the surface once a print dries, but too many in one area can result in raised unattractive areas.

Another critical issue in printing with play-dough is that because structures need to dry, completely solid parts are difficult or impossible to print. Ideal play-dough prints have walls of uniform thickness and openings that allow all surfaces–both interior and exterior–to dry at the same rate. Printer-paths should be optimized for drying. Our Stanford bunny prints, for example, have no bottom and minimal interior support structures. They were dried on a rack placed in the center of the dehumidifier so that the interior of the prints were also exposed to warm air during this process.

Finally, play-dough is not a strong as PLA. It is not a good material for printing mechanical parts or other objects that experience significant stress. Play-dough prints are well suited for decorative applications and light use. They can be finished with a range of coatings including acrylic paint, gel medium, wax, and wood finish. When waterproofed, they can be used as water-tight vases, planters, and other functional vessels.

7.1 Material Considerations

7.1.1 Renewability. Our corn and wheat play-dough is a minimally processed entirely plant-based material. The glue and sand mixture includes white glue, which is composed of polyvinyl acetate (PVAC), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and water [ 9 ]. These chemicals can be made from renewable sources like ethanol [ 4 ]. PLA is also a highly-processed material that is made from renewable, typically corn-based, materials [ 37 ]. All materials are rated as renewable in our table, but the corn and wheat play-dough is an easier to renew resource because all of its ingredients require less complex processing.

7.1.2 Recyclability. Play-dough is easy to recycle by hand. Waste produced during printing and unwanted prints can be dissolved in water and either incorporated into new batches of dough or directly re-printed. Figure 16 shows steps in this process. Play-dough is much easier to recycle than PLA.

PLA is recyclable, but waste must be shredded, melted, and turned back into filament before it can be reused. This is a possible, if cumbersome, DIY process that requires a shredder and filament maker. PLA waste can be industrially recycled, but this must be done in specialized processing plants; PLA cannot be recycled through standard plastic recycling processes [ 34 ].

7.1.3 Repairability. Play-dough prints are also very easy to repair. If broken, they can be glued back together with white glue or a similar water-based adhesive. Because the material is porous, repair seams are strong and virtually undetectable, Figure 16 . Prints are also easy to patch. Small cracks and holes can be filled with wet play-dough. If the repair is done in a matching color these patches are also very hard to detect.

PLA prints are much more difficult to repair. Once broken, they can be glued back together with notable seams, using an adhesive like epoxy or super glue. Damaged prints are typically discarded.

7.1.4 Compostability. Our corn and wheat play-dough composts readily like food waste. The corn and wheat components of our glue and sand recipe do as well. The chemicals in white glue, PVA and PVAC are classified as biodegradable and compostable materials. They are known to be mineralized by some microorganisms. However, the microorganisms that degrade these materials are not ubiquitous within the environment and may or may not be present in a given compost pile or other location[ 4 ]. It is perhaps worth noting that the glue and sand play-dough appears to compost and degrade in the same way that play-dough without glue does. A mixture with no vinegar will begin to visibly decay after two to three days if it is left in a moist environment (ie: a sealed tupperware) at room temperature. Figure 17 shows and image of cylinders printed from the Corn and Wheat, Glue and Sand, and 25% clay recipes, mixed with water instead of vinegar, that have been left in a sealed tupperware at room temperature for one month. We are interested in further exploring the decomposition process in future work.

PLA is classified as compostable material, but it only breaks down in industrial composting facilities [ 34 ]. PVAC, PVA, and PLA are all rated as highly-degradable and pose limited environmental risk as micro-particles [ 48 ].

In summary, the corn and wheat play-dough has the most desirable characteristics from a sustainability perspective. The glue and sand play-dough has many positive qualities, but the addition of glue means the material is not as compostable as the corn and wheat recipe. Both are an improvement over PLA on all dimensions.

7.2 Machine Considerations

To contrast the energy consumed during the printing process for play-dough and PLA, we recorded print time and energy usage data for the Eazao Zero both with and without our custom heater. We obtained similar measurements for an Ender-3 Creality FFF printer. Energy consumption was measured using a Kill-A-Watt device [ 27 ]. Print times were recorded for the vase figures and Stanford bunny prints shown in Figure 13 .

The Eazao Zero with our custom heater consumes the most energy while it is running over a continuous time period. This is due to the fact that our heater never turns off. The Ender-3 consumes slightly less energy over time. The Eazao Zero without any attachments consumes the smallest amount of energy. Energy consumed by heaters dominate these measurements.

Once print time is factored into account, play-dough printing is significantly more energy efficient, even when the heater is used. The play-dough bunny requires 360 Wh to print, while a PLA bunny requries 600 Wh. The differences for the vase prints are more dramatic, with the play-dough version requiring 72 Wh and the PLA version requiring 957 Wh.

From this assessment, we believe that play-dough printing is a more sustainable process than PLA printing.

8 EXAMPLE ARTIFACTS AND TECHNIQUES

The process of testing and documenting the properties of play-dough helped us discover ways that we could leverage and make use of it. This section focuses on two application areas while introducing several example artifacts.

8.1 Color

One of the most striking features of play-dough is its color. It is easy to tint play-dough an infinite variety of hues with food coloring. By adding several colors of dough to a print tube, multi-colored prints can be made. Different colors blend together as they are printed, creating an "ombre" effect. Figure 20 shows an example workflow. Color transitions are designed and visualized in software which also generates a print tube visualization based on the design. When the tube is loaded according to the guidelines, the print reflects the designed colors. Depending on the material being printed, a user may need to adjust a color diffusion parameter in the software, which defines the speed of color transitions, for accurate printing.

This method of color printing is simple and requires no extra printer hardware to implement. It allows for the creation of low resolution color prints. The resolution is limited by the 50mm diameter of the print tube. Each millimeter of print tube height corresponds to approximately 500mm of extruded filament (based on the print parameters we are using for most tests). The smallest amount of filament that can reasonably be loaded in the tube is approximately 5mm x 50mm in diameter. This gives a limit of one color change every 2500mm of extruded filament.

Limits are also imposed by the fact that the colors loaded in the tube generate a single strand of continuous filament. This means that many desired color possibilities are not achievable. For instance, it would be impossible to color the left half of a vase one color and the right half a different color.

However, the capabilities can facilitate interesting intentional designs. In a project exploring data visualization in play-dough prints, we produced the set of vases shown in Figure 19 . Each vase represents temperature changes over the course of the past year in three different locations: Albuqueque, NM–the city the authors’ live in–(right), along with one of the warmest cities in the United States (Key West, Florida, left) and one of the coldest (Leadville, Colorado, center). Vases begin with data for November of 2021 at the top and end with November of 2022 at the bottom. Each vase physicalizes local weather changes throughout the year. Comparing the group of vases provides information about the different climates in each location. We were able to use our software to design these vessels and insure that each one had a similar color profile from bottom to top, despite requiring different amounts of filament.

In addition to using software to design color effects, it is possible to work more improvisationally. A tube can be loaded with a stack of different colors and the user can wait until printing to discover the final result. We think of this technique as similar to employing a variegated yarn in knitting or weaving. An overall color pallet is chosen, but the design isn't fully known until the artifact is constructed. Figure 20 shows some examples that were constructed in this fashion.

We believe that techniques similar to ones investigated by other researchers for FFF machines [ 31 ][ 38 ] could be used to create higher resolution colored prints in play-dough. The material readily absorbs dye, which could be applied when material is closer to extrusion–at or near the nozzle exit. We hope to experiment with these possibilities in future work.

8.2 Play-Dough Supports for Clay Forms

Currently, the only way to construct supports for 3D printed ceramics is to print them out of clay and then break them off after a piece has dried but before it is fired. It is challenging to break clay supports without also breaking the form itself, so the utility of these supports is limited. Dissolvable supports, like those developed for FFF printers, do not work because clay is water soluble before it is fired.

Play-dough cannot withstand the firing temperatures required for clay. It simply burns away. We leverage these differing behaviors and employ play-dough as a support material. During the printing process, it acts as a structural support, but it is burned away in the kiln, leaving only the clay behind. Figure 21 shows an example that leverages this technique. We first printed the play-dough support structures and then printed the mug, placing the supports where needed as the print progressed. This process would be more elegant with a dual-nozzle 3D printer. We are in the process of constructing one in our lab so that we can better explore and take advantage of these possibilities.

9 FUTURE WORK AND CONCLUSION

Beyond these specific examples, we believe that, because it is so easy to make and dye your own play-dough, it is possible to create prints with a range of interesting material properties. For instance, infra-red or UV dyes could be used to embed hidden codes or tags in objects. Thermochromic inks and dyes could yield artifacts with dynamic and programmable color patterns. Similarly, conductive materials like carbon fiber or metal powders could be blended into doughs and used to make multi-functional prints. In general, this class of materials might be able to serve as an accessible sandbox for a range of exciting material experiments, particularly in conjunction with dual extrusion printers.

We also believe that there is more work to be done in capturing the material understanding we have developed in software so that we, and others, are better able to design for these materials. Software tools that would let a designer better explore relationships between tool-paths, color, material recipe, and other characteristics could lead to a new set of design possibilities.

Finally, and perhaps most compellingly, we believe that the ability to employ play-dough in low-cost clay printers can open up new contexts in which to explore digital fabrication. In particular, play-dough could make the exploration of 3D printing more feasible in a range of educational contexts. Play-dough is a familiar, fun, safe, and accessible material. Moreover, simple forms can be produced very quickly. The play-dough vases in Figure 13 were each printed in a little over a half an hour. This is 25 times faster than it would take to print them in PLA. Increasing print speed significantly decreases the most important obstacle to the use of 3D printers in classrooms and other large-group educational settings. Many student projects can be printed in a relatively short time period; the bottleneck of printer access is significantly reduced.

We look forward to introducing educators, young people and DIY enthusiasts to play-dough printing in workshops and other events we are currently planning and hope to see the widespread adoption of this material and others like it in the near future.
