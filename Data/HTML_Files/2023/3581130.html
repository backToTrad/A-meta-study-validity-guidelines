Visible Nuances: A Caption System to Visualize Paralinguistic Speech Cues for Deaf and Hard-of-Hearing Individuals

CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Captions help deaf and hard-of-hearing (DHH) individuals visually communicate voice information to better understand video content. In speech, the literal content and paralinguistic cues (e.g., pitch and nuance) work together to create real intention. However, current captions are limited in their capacity to deliver fine nuances because they cannot fully convey these paralinguistic cues. This paper proposes an audio-visualized caption system that automatically visualizes paralinguistic cues into various caption elements (thickness, height, font type and motion). A comparative study with 20 DHH participants demonstrates how our system supports DHH individuals to be better accessible to paralinguistic cues while watching videos. Particularly in the case of formal talks, they could accurately identify the speaker's nuance more often compared to current captions, without any practice or training. Addressing some issues on legibility and familiarity, the proposed caption system has potentials to enrich DHH individuals’ video watching experience more as hearing people enjoy.

1 INTRODUCTION

Captions are a useful means for visually accessing audio information, and deaf and hard-of-hearing (DHH) individuals often rely on captions to enjoy video content. Typical captions appear in the text format of spoken content, transcribed by manual typists, or, more recently, automatic captioning systems such as speech-to-text technology. Captions are usually sufficient to convey a speaker's meaning, but in many conversation contexts, ‘how’ something is said determines the full meaning of ‘what’ is being said. It is very important to detect the paralinguistic elements of speech such as intonation, tone, and strength, to determine speakers’ full meaning. Since typical captions are limited to the conveyance of the literal sense of spoken words, DHH individuals inevitably miss out on the paralinguistic information accompanying speech described by captions, so that they cannot enjoy videos to the extent that hearing people do.

Recent caption systems (e.g., those adopted by Netflix) occasionally convey paralinguistic audio information in an explainable way (e.g., [Tom speaks in a trembling voice] or [kids scream]). Such systems’ explicit conveyance of the feeling of sound as text is useful for DHH individuals, but they still have disadvantages. First, it takes caption authors considerable time and effort to manually type paralinguistic information after interpreting sounds individually [ 23 , 40 ]. Second, these sound explanations are expressed in text, which occupies a portion of the limited two-line area reserved on the screen for captions, and it is burdensome for DHH individuals to read additional text within the short duration in which successive captions are displayed [ 3 , 22 , 45 ]. Rather than providing explicit explanations of paralinguistic information, some researchers have manipulated visual elements of captions (e.g., font size [ 11 , 26 , 37 ], thickness [ 8 , 21 , 26 , 46 ], height [ 39 ], and width [ 8 , 39 , 46 ]) to visualize basic sound elements of speech such as volume and pitch. For example, a loud voice is expressed with a larger font size, and the pitch of the voice is expressed with the elevation of the text above its baseline. These caption elements usually express elementary sound elements and are not sufficiently sophisticated to express more complex emotional voice nuances such as anger, tension, trembling, hesitation or sarcasm. As emphasized in [ 8 , 47 ], there is a need to deliver such nuances and similarly more complex speech context through captions.

This paper investigates the potential of a caption system which visualizes paralinguistic information of speech to support DHH individuals’ access to the nuances of speech in video. We first defined a proper relationship between sound and caption elements, then designed two versions of the caption system: basic and advanced. We conducted a comparative study followed by post-interviews with 20 DHH participants on two video categories (formal talks and dramas) which demonstrated that our proposed caption system enables DHH individuals to infer nuances in speech. We presented the possibilities and limitations of our caption system based on analysis of participants’ preferences and experiences and discussed design considerations for future improvements to the system.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

  • Introducing more paralinguistic elements of speech (e.g., nuances) and caption elements (e.g., font styles and text motion) to conventional captions
  • Validating the potential benefits of the proposed caption system for DHH individuals through a comparative analysis, as to the best of our knowledge, there is a lack of in-depth studies on caption systems which convey paralinguistic information in speech for DHH individuals
  • Providing guidelines for designing a caption system to improve speech accessibility for DHH individuals

2 BACKGROUND

Sometimes a sentence can convey several meanings without changing a single word; for example, “She isn't flying to Berlin tomorrow” can convey six subtle nuances which imply veiled information depending on which word is emphasized. If the speaker emphasizes the word “she,” the sentence may imply that someone else may be flying to Berlin, but if “flying” is emphasized instead, the speaker might be suggesting that the subject of the sentence will be traveling to Berlin by some other means than flight. As this simple example demonstrates, the meaning of a sentence can be changed by saying it with different nuances. A compliment can be expressed as a sarcastic insult merely by changing the way it is said. Paralanguage, also known as vocalics, is a common component of meta-communication by which nuances can be modified or emotions conveyed within sentences through paralinguistic cues such as pitch, volume, intonation, and pace. A speaker's intended meaning can be accurately conveyed through a proper combination of text (i.e., what is said) and paralinguistic cues (i.e., how it is said) [ 28 , 33 , 38 ], but DHH individuals, who do not have access to subtle paralinguistic cues with typical captions, inevitably miss some information while consuming multimedia content. They can understand the literal content from the current caption system but must attempt to interpret nuance and context by reading speakers’ facial expressions [ 1 , 23 , 26 ].

A number of studies have sought to improve the expressive power of captions for DHH individuals by conveying information beyond the literal meaning of text by presenting, in which the paralinguistic cues of speech are presented in captions with different text size [ 11 , 26 , 37 ], height above baseline [ 28 , 39 ], thickness [ 8 , 21 , 26 , 46 ], and emoji [ 23 , 24 , 32 ]. Pataca and Costa studied typography modulation, where the pitch of speech shifts captions’ vertical position, and syllables’ font weight corresponds to the volume of speech [ 8 ]. Lim et al. adjusted caption font size according to the speaker's volume [ 26 ]. Wölfel et al. mapped speech volume, pitch, and speed to caption vertical stroke weight, horizontal stroke weight, and text width, respectively [ 46 ]. They normalized these three speech features to reflect visualization of each spoken phoneme and also tried to visualize the intonation of speech in text. Some caption systems have focused on conveying emotions in text [ 39 ]. Oomori et al. proposed an emoji-based caption system for voice-only online meeting scenarios for DHH users [ 32 ]. Pereira et al. investigated emotive captioning methods which included emojis and icons (e.g., an icon expressing effort by depicting a person pushing a ball uphill) with captions in media content [ 34 ]. They found that the intention of speech may be distorted when conveying emotion through emojis or icons due to viewers’ arbitrary interpretation or cultural backgrounds. Still, most of these studies focused on elementary sound elements rather than considering various caption elements modification all inclusively, which have not reached a profound nuance.

Besides caption research, some studies on typography have arranged letters and text to make them legible, clear, and visually appealing to the reader [ 13 , 18 , 19 , 27 ] and convey additional context over the text. Maçãs et al. proposed a system which generates typefaces by adjusting the shape of letters or characters in accordance with emotion e.g., using irregular wave shapes to express fear and rectangular shapes to represent sadness [ 27 ]. Kinetic typography (animated text) can also be used to effectively express emotion [ 24 , 28 ]. Forlizzi et al. applied cartoon-styled text motion where kinetic typographic properties such as slow-in, slow-out, procession on a curved path, compression, and stretching were implemented according to the speaker's emotion [ 11 ]. When shown animated text devised by typography designers, study participants confirmed that emotion can be conveyed through the motion of text. Malik et al. designed animated text to express emotions such as anger, sadness, happiness, and fear [ 28 ]. Text was animated to express anger in twisting or shaking motions which varied according to the level of emotion represented. These approaches prove that various typographical methods can effectively convey additional context in text, which could potentially visualize paralinguistic cues in a caption system. However, only a few studies have attempted to introduce typographic properties such as animated text or typefaces in captions, and there have been insufficient studies on DHH individuals to verify their effectiveness.

It is still necessary to represent a broader spectrum of sound elements to deliver the subtle nuances of speech for DHH individuals and incorporate various visualization methods such as animated text to properly express these elements. In this paper, we investigate various sound and visual elements to develop a more effective caption system to enable DHH individuals to better enjoy video content. We approach the provision of auditory visualization in captions with the following research questions:

  • RQ 1. Which caption element best represents each sound feature? (Section 3.1 )
  • RQ 2. Is the conveyance of auditory information to DHH individuals through speech-visualized captions feasible? (Section 5.1 )

3 AN AUDIO-VISUALIZED CAPTION SYSTEM

Figure 1 presents an overview of a proposed audio-visualized caption system which automatically applies caption elements to captions by analyzing sounds. The system's inputs are a video's audio file and a caption list including the start time, end time, and text for each caption. First, a vocal sample is extracted from the mixed audio channel of the video using a source separation model called Spleeter [ 14 ]. Second, the extracted vocal sample is segmented into caption units based on the start and end times of corresponding captions to recognize the emotional nuance of the speech contained in each caption unit. Caption unit samples are segmented into syllable units to calculate volume and pitch features of speech over time. Finally, the analyzed nuance, volume, and pitch features of each caption are mapped to a pair of the font and motion, thickness, and height of the caption, respectively, according to pre-defined set of mapping rules, and the system integrates the mapping results to generate a single line of audio-visualized captions.

This study uses the Korean language, which has a phonetic alphabet which represents the sounds of speech with symbols. Phonemes are “the smallest units of phonology that can no longer be divided into smaller ones” [ 35 ]. Syllables are “words or parts of words which contain single vowel sounds” [ 41 ] and “the smallest units of speech that give a sense of a single voice” [ 42 ]. Syllable units vary slightly among different language systems; for example, both English and Korean are phonetic alphabet systems which represent sounds or words in phoneme units, but as shown in Figure 2 , Korean differs from English in that it collects phonemes and marks them in syllable units. For example, “straight” is spelled with eight letters but pronounced with one syllable in English, since it has only one vowel. In Korean, however, it is spelled with five letters since it is pronounced with five syllables (“ seu-teu-rae-i-teu ”). In other words, this word has different numbers of syllables and letters in English, but the same number in Korean. Therefore, we expressed paralinguistic information (e.g., intonation over time) in syllable units according to the characteristics of Korean in which the number of syllables uttered matches the number of letters in the caption. The unit of audio visualization can be adjusted according to the language system e.g., word unit for English.

3.1 Selecting Caption Elements to Express Sound Elements

There is no consensus as to which caption element is best for conveying a particular sound element when mapping sounds to caption elements. Some researchers map sound volume to caption thickness [ 6 , 8 ] while others map it to font size [ 31 , 39 ]. In this study, we first investigated the relationship between sound and caption elements by conducting a survey on a sample of 20 hearing college students (8 females and 12 males). We informed the participants of the definition of sound elements (volume, pitch, and tone) and provided sample videos of formal talks and dramas with captions applied by each caption element. Caption elements investigated in the survey were frequently used in other studies [ 5 , 6 , 8 , 23 , 25 , 31 , 36 , 37 , 39 , 46 ]. The participants were asked to select one or more suitable caption elements for volume, pitch, and tone by watching the sample videos. Tone encompassed both emotional and emphasized tone in this study.

Figure 3 shows the number of votes cast for the caption elements for each sound element. Thickness was selected most often to represent the speaker's volume (i.e., how loud or quietly a person speaks), followed by size. Height was selected most often to represent pitch (i.e., how high or low pitch a person speaks), followed by size. Motion was selected most often to represent tone (i.e., a speaker's emotion or nuance), followed by font. Based upon these results, we decided to map the speakers’ volume to thickness, pitch to height, and tone to motion and font.

3.2 Mapping Between Sound and Caption Elements

3.2.1 Tone. In order to visualize speakers’ tone of voice, we first extracted emotions from voice audio rather than inferring them from text, then arranged the extracted emotions to correspond to caption font and motion. A design workshop comprised of a separate sample of 13 hearing individuals was conducted to investigate pairs of font style and motion appropriate for the 60 emotions in the Korean Emotional Conversation Corpus dataset [ 9 ], consisting of six basic emotion categories (angry, sad, anxious, wounded, embarrassed, and happy), and ten sub-emotions for each emotion category. In the workshop, participants were given a pool of about 600 fonts 1 and 97 motion styles 2 from which to select one or more appropriate font and motion style for each emotion. After completing the selection process, they explained the characteristics of the fonts and motions they chose. Based on the results of the workshop, which took approximately three hours to complete, derived a total of 60 mapping results. In order to reduce the experimental workload, we considered mappings of only six representative emotion categories instead of those of 60 sub-emotional tones (e.g., “frustrated” was considered as simply “sad”). A mapping scheme for the six basic emotions was introduced for drama videos, as shown in Figure 4 , in which each emotion category encompasses the characteristics of their sub-emotions. The characteristics of the selected fonts and motions for the six emotion categories are summarized as follows:

  • Angry category: a font that gives a sharp, pointed, or spiky feel to a sound with a motion which oscillates up and down as if trembling in the manner of uncontrollable anger
  • Sad category: a thin, slanted font that conveys the warped feeling of a brushstroke (in general, thin writing conveys a sense of emotional instability, helplessness, or instability) with a motion which droops as if shedding tears
  • Anxious category: a thin, irregular, curvy font with characters with tops and bottoms of varying lengths with a jolting motion as if shaking one's body from side to side in anxiety
  • Wounded category: a squiggly, pointed, or tight font with a disjointed and vanishing motion
  • Embarrassed category: a heavy, hard-feeling font without interstitial blank spaces, suggesting the speaker's eyes growing bigger and distorting in shame, with a motion suggesting confusion, as if the speaker is at a loss
  • Happy category: a font with a stylish, soft, round, and energetic style with a heart-pumping motion as if aroused by expectation or satisfaction

In formal talk videos, the tones which emphasize the meaning of the content is more prominent than the speaker's emotional tone. Thus, the ‘headshake’ motion (see Figure 4 ) was applied to captions to reflect speakers’ emphasis without changing the font type. The default caption style is a motionless 24-pixel Noto Sans font.

The tone (nuance) of a caption unit is mapped to a specific font and motion pair as described in Figure 4 . We employ an emotion classifier which classifies the vocal sample in a caption unit into one of six emotions. The appropriate motion is implemented while the corresponding caption appears onscreen.

3.2.2 Volume and Pitch. Volume and pitch are calculated as the average of the output values from the RMS and piptrack functions of Librosa [ 29 ], a Python music and audio analysis package. RMS measures the overall magnitude of signals within a window, while piptrack returns pitch values through pitch-tracking by a thresholded parabolically-interpolated STFT algorithm 3 . These features are calculated for each syllable to capture natural intonation and nuances over time. The connectionist temporal classification (CTC) segmentation model [ 20 , 44 ] pretrained with the Korean language model [ 43 ] is used to split the audio samples into syllable units. The feature values of volume and pitch are further processed as follows: (1) Temporal smoothing is applied to smooth out noisy values. (2) As reconstructing too many syllables can cause visual fatigue, we incorporate a tolerance function to return values only when the degree of change of the current syllable value from the previous syllable exceeds an empirically defined threshold. For example, if there are 10 syllables in the caption, all features of 10 syllables are not reflected, but only the value of some syllables which are above the tolerance threshold are reflected. (3) Inspired by [ 8 ], all syllable values are normalized by those of nearby syllables. The RMS values are normalized and divided into four levels of the CSS font-weight property ranging from thin to thick (300, 500, 700, and 900). Pitch values are normalized between 0 and 5, which means that syllables are elevated by up to five pixels from their baselines.

4 EXPERIMENTAL STUDY

In order to determine whether our audio-visualized caption system enables DHH individuals to access paralinguistic cues in speech, we conducted a comparative study with three different classes of captions: common, basic, and advanced, as illustrated in Figure 5 . Common captions provide plain-text captions usually seen in video content. Basic captions visualize volume and pitch in speech with caption thickness and height variations. Advanced captions provide all supplementary caption elements, including font and motion, to fully express nuances in speech. We manually corrected erroneous predictions of the emotion classifier to rule out the effect of emotion prediction performance in the experiment.

4.1 Participants

We recruited 20 DHH participants with normal or corrected-to-normal vision to read captions (11 females and 9 males of ages between 20 and 61 ( M = 44.9, SD = 12.7). Their average age of hearing loss occurred was 3 years old ( SD = 2.98). Thirteen participants have profound deafness, two have severe hearing loss, one has moderate hearing loss, and four have mild hearing loss. When asked how much time they spent each day watching videos on average, three participants responded, “less than 1 hour,” eight answered “1–2 hours,” another eight answered “2–3 hours,” and one answered “more than 3 hours.” When asked how often they use a caption system, 12 participants answered “always,” seven answered “sometimes,” and one answered “never.”

4.2 Materials and Measurements

Two videos from each of the two categories were used in this study: a lecture and a movie review from the formal talk category and two dramas. We extracted one-minute segments of similar contexts from each video to apply the three caption systems because watching the same video to evaluate every caption condition could result in a learning effect when answering the questions. A total of 12 one-minute segments (3 segments from each of 4 videos) were prepared for the study.

We prepared an 8-item questionnaire (see Table 1 ) to measure the participants’ experience with the three caption systems. The first five questions (Q1–Q5) measured participants’ subjective evaluation of the video content with respect to understanding, speech accessibility (interpretation of nuance, volume, and pitch), and overall satisfaction. The five questions employed a 7-point Likert-type agreement scale ranging from 1 = “strongly disagree” to 7 = “strongly agree.” The next three questions (Q6–Q8) measured the legibility and cognitive load of the caption systems, inspired by NASA-TLX questions and prior research [ 12 , 17 ]. They employed a 21-point NASA-TLX response scale ranging from 1 = “very low” to 21 = “very high.”

In addition to the subjective evaluation questions, two task-related questions were used to objectively evaluate the DHH participants’ performance in identifying the emphases or nuances of the speakers through captions. These questions were appropriately adjusted according to each video category. For the formal talk videos, participants were provided a list of captions (approximately 20 caption sentences from each video) which appeared in the videos and were asked to choose the caption which they felt the speakers had emphasized the most. For drama videos, 12 out of 60 emotional tones were selected to simplify the experiment process, and participants were given a list of 12 emotion words (comprised of one basic emotion and one sub-emotion from each of the six emotion categories; angry-sarcastic, sad-frustrated, anxious-fear, wounded-lonely, embarrassed-desperate, and happy-lovely) and asked to choose one of the 12 words which best described the speaker's nuance after reviewing a specific excerpt from the video. We additionally considered sub-emotions to see if participants could distinguish low-level sub-emotional nuances beyond high-level basic nuances through the captions. Task performances were scored according to ground-truth answers annotated by five hearing subjects who watched the videos with sound.

Post-interviews were conducted at the end of the experiment to elicit opinions and feedback on the caption systems. Furthermore, participants were asked to rank the three caption systems according to their preference, describe their willingness to use our caption system, and suggest what kind(s) of additional information they would like to receive from captions to make the caption system more accessible.

4.3 Study Setup

According to a within-subjects study design, the participants experienced three caption systems: common, basic, and advanced. We initially explained the study procedure to the participants, procured their written informed consent, and obtained their demographic information. The participants were not given any explicit information pertaining to sound and caption elements used in the proposed caption system, e.g., that caption thickness represents speech volume. They watched 12 videos over six sessions where three caption systems and videos were counterbalanced across participants (see supplementary material). The videos were presented without sound on a 27-inch monitor with 1920×1080 resolution. In each session, participants watched two videos of the same category using a caption system and completed a questionnaire. Task performance was then assessed for each video. After completing all the sessions, we conducted post-interviews to assess participants’ overall experiences and preferences with respect to the caption systems. The post-interviews were transcribed for analysis. A sign language interpreter facilitated communication with the participants during the study. The experiment took approximately one hour to conduct, and participants were paid 50K KRW (approximately 37 USD) as compensation.

5 RESULTS

5.1 Speech Accessibility in Videos

5.1.1 Objective task performance. Figure 6 shows the DHH participants’ objective task performance in identifying explicit nuances. These tasks were very challenging for DHH individuals, which was reflected by their relatively low task performance scores. Nevertheless, the proposed system still achieved an improvement, where the rate of “don't know” responses, which signified a participant's inability to answer a task question, significantly dropped from 35% (using common captions) to 7.5% (using advanced captions) for formal talk videos. The accuracy achieved by participants with the advanced captions (45%) is also much higher than that achieved with the common captions (22.5%). The proposed system still demonstrated a small improvement (7.5%: 62.5% with advanced captions versus 55% with common captions) in the classification of emotions into the six emotion categories in the drama videos. Interestingly, our advanced captions improved participants’ more nuanced identification of emotions among 12 subcategories of emotions (C: 15% B: 15% A: 35%), although actual presentations of advanced captions between a basic emotion and the corresponding sub-emotion were the same. It seems to be possible because the viewers basically received information both captions and video content simultaneously.

We confirmed that the audio-visualized captions enabled DHH viewers to achieve improved task performance accuracy by showing which part of a dialogue the speakers were emphasizing. In conversational videos such as formal talk videos with little variation in speakers’ facial expression or gestures, it is difficult to recognize speakers’ nuances or emphasis from common captions. Figure 7 shows an instance of task question responses to a formal talk video, one of the most challenging tasks. Most (4 out of 7 participants) of the group who watched the video with common captions answered “Don't know”, while the group who watched it with audio-visualized captions could identify one caption line which they felt was most emphasized. More DHH viewers who watched videos with advanced captions responded closer to the ground truth answers than a group who watched the videos with common captions, although DHH viewers failed to choose a ground truth which elapsed quickly (e.g., at line 17). Notably, the audio-visualized captions were particularly helpful to DHH individuals in videos in which speakers’ facial expressions are not visible (e.g., at lines 10–11). When the speaker introduced an interesting point in the middle of their speech (e.g., at line 19), DHH viewers watching the video with advanced captions recognized that the speaker thought that the point was particularly exciting, while participants who watched the video with common captions not.

Unfortunately, audio-visualized captions were not always the ideal option for DHH viewers. If nuances and emotions were already well-expressed through facial expressions and context such as in drama videos (see Figure 8 ), the proposed caption system is less effective. Some DHH participants responded that they felt paralinguistic cues conveyed even by common captions, claiming in the post-interviews that they could detect nuances from speakers’ facial expressions as they usually did. Some of them were frustrated by the audio-visualized captions. For example, P15 mentioned, “ All the nuances were visible in the facial expressions, ” and P18 commented, “ I think there was no need for caption motion because I could feel [the speakers’ nuances] through [their] performances and… facial expressions. ”

5.1.2 Subjective evaluation on the accessibility of paralinguistic cues. Figure 9 shows the mean scores and distribution of perceived accessibility of paralinguistic cues in the caption systems.

Nuance (Q1) indicates the degree to which DHH participants can detect speakers’ nuance through the caption systems. The basic and advanced captions conveyed nuance more effectively to DHH viewers compared to the common system in both formal talk and drama video categories. As mentioned above, many DHH viewers reported that the audio-visualized caption helped them to detect the speakers’ nuances although there were no statistically significant differences due to a large individual difference. One participant (P6), who has had profound deafness since birth, directly expressed that the feeling of sounds was conveyed through the proposed caption system even though he had never experienced the sounds.

“ It felt like a sound echo, so although I am hearing impaired, I liked [the advanced captions] because they aren't just monotonous text but indicated high and low pitch. I don't know the feeling of the sound, but I liked the audio-visualized captions because the letters made the content feel alive. ” – P6

" The advanced captions] made it easy to distinguish between emotions—whether gloomy or angry—and I could feel them clearly and naturally through the captions. … [The basic captions] made it easy to distinguish whether a voice was loud, soft, or a whisper. ” – P13

Volume (Q2) and Pitch (Q3) indicate to what degree participants could feel the change of speakers’ volume and pitch through the caption systems. As shown in Figure 9 , participants thought that the audio-visualized captions (basic and advanced) allowed them to experience volume and pitch better than the common captions.

" I liked [the basic caption system] because [speakers’ volume and pitch] were well-expressed by the [captions’] height and thickness. ” – P1

" I could tell how loud voices were through the [captions’] thickness. … I could distinguish between [different] pitches of voices. ” – P12

Understanding (Q4) indicates how well DHH participants understood the overall video content. As shown in Figure 9 , the audio-visualized captions (basic and advanced) had lower mean scores than that of the common captions, although a statistically significant difference was not found. Basically, the common captions were sufficiently familiar to provide the basic content of speech. A participant's comment that they were familiar and comfortable with the common captions suggested that simple, uniform and familiar captions may result in DHH viewers’ general understanding with relatively lower cognitive load than the other caption systems.

" [The common caption] is comfortable because it is familiar, and its letters are uniform. ” – P3

Interestingly, some participants responded that the advanced captions work better for understanding formal talk videos than other caption systems. Due to the characteristics of the formal talk videos, understanding the content is relatively burdensome due to the large amount of caption text compared to drama video with same length, and speakers’ facial cues may not be as useful as they are when interpreting paralinguistic information in drama videos. Typically, formal talk videos can be boring because the speakers’ expressions are very rarely evocative. Some participants responded that the simple motion effect of advanced captions may provide some entertainment in this context and better clarify important content. This result is aligned with [ 17 ] which suggested highlighting important content in captions to improve its understandability by DHH individuals. Contrastively, the basic caption seems to be less effective in triggering viewers’ attention to improve content understandability.

" As motion and font were added to the basic captions, I could feel [the content] more clearly. ” – P13

" The [advanced] captions were fun, and I liked that they became more and more understandable as I watched the video. ” – P20

5.2 Legibility and Cognitive Load

Figure 10 shows the caption systems’ results with respect to legibility (Q6) and cognitive load (Q7–8). Although extensive variance in individual results illustrated in the wide range of boxplots, DHH participants generally experienced lower legibility and higher cognitive load from the audio-visualized captions (basic and advanced) than the common captions. (Note that there was no statistical significance, except for mental demand in drama videos.) This result might be natural, since many participants felt unfamiliar with the new caption types (basic and advanced) and had no idea how to interpret the styles of the new captions. Note that they were not provided any tutorial or training to get accustomed with the proposed new captions. Since the new captions sometimes changed the effects on caption font too frequently, DHH participants were challenged to read the captions generated by the basic and advanced caption systems. DHH viewers must often quickly alternate between observing speakers’ facial expressions or gestures while reading the captions to glean a holistic understanding of video content [ 45 ]. The common captions with static height made it relatively easier for DHH individuals to switch between reading the captions and watching the video content; however, the eye movement pattern required to accomplish this task was different from that which they were accustomed to. For example, P8 mentioned, “ My eyes were tired and irritated from reading because the letters moved too much ” and P1 commented, “ I think the cursive writing is difficult to read. I hope it improves. ” P11 stated, “ The common captions were easy to read because there was no change [in caption baseline], but the other [basic and advanced] captions were uncomfortable [to read] because my eyes had to follow the letters as they moved vertically. ”

Unexpectedly, some participants reported experiencing higher legibility and lower cognitive load watching formal talk videos captioned with the advanced system than with the common system. Although the multiple visual elements (e.g., font and motion) of these caption systems may impose a higher cognitive load due to visual distraction [ 5 , 7 ], participants seemed to not be as burdened with the advanced captions as we had expected. As previously mentioned in Section 5.1.1 , participants could understand nuances and other important paralinguistic cues more intuitively and smoothly through caption motion.

5.3 Satisfaction and Preferences

5.3.1 Satisfaction. Figure 11 indicates the satisfaction scores for the caption systems for both video types. The percentages along the left side of the Y-axis represent the ratios of the unfavorable (1-3 points) scores with respect to satisfaction, and the percentages along the right side of the Y-axis represent the ratios of the favorable (5-7 points) scores. The satisfaction level is particularly higher in dramas, and both formal-talk videos and dramas generally scored more than 4 points. We identified from the interview that they were basically satisfied with the fact that the video had any type of caption. For example, P8 said, “ I wish there were captions for [every video]. ”

DHH participants had very high satisfaction with all captions in the case of drama videos, while the advanced captions especially scored higher satisfaction against the other two in formal-talk videos. As mentioned earlier, unlike formal talk videos, drama videos often emphasize, to some extent, speakers’ emotional states through the speakers’ facial expressions. This suggests that the more predictable the information delivered by facial expressions, the more DHH participants preferred the basic caption system to the advanced caption system. The basic system was considered less distracting and sufficient to convey paralinguistic cues. Each caption system received preference votes in the context of formal talk videos, but the advanced caption system was the most preferred, demonstrating the benefits of advanced captions in formal-talk videos as mentioned in Section 5.1.2 .

5.3.2 Preferences. We analyzed participants’ preferences toward the caption systems, as shown in Figure 12 . More than half of participants (n = 13) preferred either of the audio-visualized captions (basic or advanced), and 11 participants of those indicated the least preference for the common captions. Most participants lost their hearing at an early age ( M = 3.2); however, even those who had rarely experienced sound also preferred our audio-visualized captions. Through the interview, we observed that the participants’ preferences vary depending on individuals’ curiosity about sound, accessibility, and personal tendencies than hearing experience. Participants preferred the advanced captions slightly more than the basic captions, with some variance due to individual preferences. Participants who favored the basic captions thought that they were sufficiently effective in expressing speakers’ nuances without disturbing the viewers’ caption reading, while participants who favored the advanced captions liked the caption motion effect.

" The thickness stands out [for me]. [I think] thickness is enough. ” – P2

" The basic captions could show the degree of sound through their thickness. It was okay to change [the captions’ height]. I felt dizzy when reading the advanced captions. ” – P12

" The speakers’ feelings were easily conveyed [by the advanced captions]. It felt more definite when the shapes of the moving letters were added to the basic captions ” – P13

" Because a movement matches the emotion when a speaker is angry, their angry emotion is clearly expressed. ” – P14

As reported in Section 5.2 , participants who preferred the common system chose it mainly because of its high legibility and familiarity. For the same reason, six participants disliked various effects of the advanced system. P10 preferred only the common captions, commenting “ [The basic and advanced caption systems were] weird and confusing because there's a change. Since the common caption system is very comfortable, I will use comfortable [common] captions even if I don't receive audio information. ”

Interestingly, when asked if they would opt to use the audio-visualized captions in their daily lives, 16 out of the 20 participants, including those who chose the basic or advanced captions as their third preferences, responded affirmatively (either conditionally or unconditionally). Some participants expressed a strong interest in using the advanced caption system; as P13 said, “ I want to use it. I want it very much ” and some participants stated that they would use the new captions if they were improved. P3 stated, “ If they are improved, I [would be] willing to use [them]. ” Although participants responded positively to the audio-visualized caption system because it expanded their access to sound, some participants finally chose the common caption system because of its legibility. If we can refine the legibility of our approach and provide participants with more time to familiarize themselves with the new captions, we may present DHH individuals with more legible and overall effective captions.

5.4 Analysis on the Effectiveness of the Audio-visualized Captions on DHH Viewers

We conducted an additional experiment in the same manner as the original without providing sound on a sample of 20 hearing individuals ( M = 31.4, SD = 8.2, 9 females, 11 males) and obtained results which indicated relatively clear effects. Figure 13 shows the comparative results of subjective responses between the DHH and hearing participants to all videos where we identified clear significant differences in speech accessibility metrics between the common system and the basic or advanced systems, implying that hearing individuals already familiar with sound can more easily understand the audio-visualized mapping design. These results suggest that the effectiveness of the proposed captioning system may be limited for DHH individuals, who are not familiar with sound.

In order to better understand the difference in statistical significance between the DHH and hearing participants, we conducted focus group interviews with six Korean sign language interpreters and six DHH people (who hadn't participated in the main experiment). In this interview, we discussed the culture of conversation with DHH people and obtained some examples of the methods of expression of nuance in sign language. We confirmed that the expression of nuance between DHH and hearing people differed in modality (i.e., gestures versus sounds), but had similar concepts of expression. For example, DHH people convey and perceive volume and pitch of speech through the breadth (wide or narrow) and location (high or low) of hand movements and repetition in sign language. Just as hearing people convey the entire nuance of speech through volume and pitch, DHH people form nuances by adding the degree and position of movement to sign language, even though it may be not as detailed as hearing people. DHH interviewees responded that captions that express comprehensive nuances, including volume, pitch, emotion and emphasis are understandable and useful. In particular, S6 (“S” indicates a sign language interpreter) expressed the expectation of an audio-visualized caption effect to the DHH learners with shared her teaching experiences of having difficulties with DHH students due to poor delivery of tone and emphasis. She, who has taught young adolescents regularly, explained most of her DHH students are eager to achieve high academic performance as equal to hearing students. When her student asked for help that it was hard to understand and notice what was important in the class, she was frustrated and embarrassed because it was challenging to teach the student which part of the content had been emphasized every single time. She mentioned that our system would help DHH students better understand and follow the flow of the contents of the class through the teacher's emphasis and nuance. Another interviewee, D1 (“D” indicates a DHH interviewee) said that entertainment programs which alter the caption size according to the volume helped them understand nuance and mood. Interviewees agreed on the usefulness of providing paralinguistic cues, while some DHH people also mentioned that they needed time to accept and acclimate to the audio-visualized caption system because they had habitually relied on unmodified text and visual cues (e.g., facial expressions) to grasp speakers’ intentions and had rarely experienced paralinguistic cues within captions conveying properties of sounds. The short study time had not provided sufficient time to learn the new caption system which made it difficult to find significant differences among DHH participants in the main experiment.

6 DISCUSSION

6.1 Potentials and Limitations of the Audio-visualized Caption System

DHH participants show improved accessibility to paralinguistic cues when using the audio-visualized caption system, although the results are not statistically significant in the instance of common captions. We did not explain the sound-to-caption mapping logic or methodology to the DHH participants prior to the experiment, but a few DHH participants acclimated very quickly to the proposed caption systems and intrinsically understood the mapping design and element characteristics. P15 commented, “ I didn't understand [the proposed audio-visualized captions] at first, but I feel like I'm becoming accustomed to them. ” However, some participants were not familiar with the new caption systems (basic and advanced), and were not easily able to understand how the audio-visualized captions worked. Those participants who preferred the common captions commented that the basic and advanced captions were very awkward, although their objective performance in identifying nuances was generally higher with the proposed captions.

We suggest two possible solutions to these limitations. First, the audio visualization mapping requires further refinement from the perspective of DHH individuals. The scope of their audio conceptualization may be different from that of hearing viewers. In addition, there were individual differences among members of the DHH participant group such as literacy and personal preference. Although it was confirmed through the focus group interview that our audio visualization system has a mapping method similar to that of DHH people, it must still be refined to deliver the semantic information of the sound elements to DHH more effectively. Some DHH participants suggested several alternative visual mapping elements such as changing the color of text or outlining characters in different colors in the focus group interview (D1, D2). Another DHH participant suggested using emojis or symbolic images (e.g., fire to express anger and lighting to express annoyance) within the caption. The efforts should be made to reflect their characteristics in the caption system. Also, our system mainly considers emotions as one type of underlying semantics, beyond which it needs to be expanded to provide the more diverse semantics that sound can form. For example, raising the pitch in the last sentence can mean questionable tone or speaking with a small and low voice can form that the conversation is in secret. Additional sound analysis techniques are required, but it would be useful to present various semantics directly to DHH people. Second, DHH participants require more time to familiarize themselves with the visualizing logic since they've never experienced such captions before. Tutorial sessions would be useful to help them to acclimate to the new captions. Some sign language experts (E1, E2, E6) who participated in the focus group interview suggested that a tutoring stage would be useful for learning and acclimating to the elements of the caption system, just like learning a foreign language, and that DHH users could expand their understanding of sound through the caption system. A longitudinal study would be required to determine the DHH participants’ performance after acclimation to the visualization design.

The proposed caption system is promising if this limitation can be improved or alleviated in future work, and is also very cost-effective in automatically analyzing sounds and mapping sound elements to caption elements, unlike other manually-implemented approaches such as speech to sign language.

6.2 Design Recommendations for Captioning Systems for DHH Individuals

Based on our experimental results and post-interviews, we highlight the following design points:

Balance legibility with expressiveness. When designing audio-visualized captions, researchers should carefully consider legibility along with the amount of audio information expressed. As reported in the results, substantially modified captions can distract viewers, sometimes distracting DHH individuals from video content. Some participants commented that handwriting-style fonts such as italics and cursive writing (e.g., the Ggeuteumuri font styles in Figure 4 ) reduce legibility. When caption motion is implemented to convey paralinguistic information, appropriate thresholds of frequency and intensity must be maintained to preserve caption legibility. For example, caption motion should be applied only when content nuances are most strongly emphasized among a number of consecutive sentences. Likewise, using font height to express pitch also must be implemented moderately to reduce visual fatigue. Mapping content in units of words instead of units of syllables is expected to reduce the frequency of visual variation. Adjusting the segmentation unit appropriately for the language system used can help users to perceive easier and more intuitively. Since there is a trade-off between the legibility and the expressiveness of paralinguistic cues, it would be worthwhile to identify appropriate parameter selections of caption elements in future empirical studies.

Provide caption systems with different characteristics depending on the video categories. A caption system should be configured by adjusting the number and degree of caption elements according to the video genre. We confirmed that the nuance and satisfaction results for the basic and advanced systems differ depending on whether videos were in the drama or formal talk categories. DHH participants who thought that sufficient sound expression was achieved just by changing the height and weight of the caption font (i.e., the basic caption system) tended to feel the effect of the advanced captions less in drama videos which typically feature a wide variety of speakers’ facial expressions. In contrast, in formal talk videos featuring relatively monotonous speakers’ facial expressions, the conveyance of nuances and satisfaction of the advanced captions are better than those of the basic captions (Section 5.1 and 5.3 ). As mentioned in Section 5.1 , the added effect of captions can add entertainment values to monotonous or boring video genres.

Minimize mismatches between video content and visualized captions. Captions which naturally match the nuances of video content can reduce DHH individuals’ cognitive load and increase their comprehension of content. A successful visualization design is visually congruent and enables viewers to understand content faster and more accurately [ 10 ]. P9 complained about the unnatural matching between content and captions, stating, “ When designing captions, I hope captions match the video [content] well, and I hope that large and small expressions of emotions match the video well. ” It suggests that errors sometimes occurred in visualized captions due to poor predictions of models in our system, but more importantly it suggests that a fine level of design mapping is required to reduce this mismatch gap. For example, when an actor in a video speaks angrily, the corresponding motion of the caption should persist for as long as the emotion is expressed. Our caption system is presently capable of conveying only binary nuances (e.g., anger) in caption units, but should also be able to express gradually changing nuances (e.g., increasingly angry tones). To improve the aforementioned example of caption motion more naturally, the intensity of the motion can increase as the speaker becomes progressively angrier. Future synchronization of the mapping of captions and the context of video content must be more refined.

Investigate more caption elements to identify speakers. We discovered from the interviews that DHH individuals had a strong need to identify which person onscreen was speaking. Many DHH participants felt uncomfortable while watching videos in which it was difficult to distinguish who was speaking when several people were involved in a conversation. Displaying the speaker's name in parentheses when more than two people were speaking simultaneously, this didn't satisfy DHH viewers. An effective caption system must provide certain cues to enable viewers to identify speakers, and almost every DHH participant suggested that caption color would be an effective design element to indicate this differentiation. Another suggestion was adding a small facial image of each speaker to appear adjacent to each caption or placing captions closer to their speakers’ faces or bodies onscreen in a form of “dynamic captions” [ 15 ].

Provide a condensed version of captions. DHH individuals’ reading comprehension skills could differ from those of hearing individuals [ 3 , 4 , 30 ]. DHH participants with low literacy, especially the elderly, favored summarized text akin to sign language structure, which differs from hearing language arrangement [ 16 ]. One DHH participant expressed preference for a shorter or condensed caption format rather than whole sentences. In many instances, closed captions or current captions often transcribe speakers’ words verbatim. To better meet the needs of DHH viewers, future studies are required on how to map paralinguistic cues from full spoken sentences into summarized captions, possibly utilizing an automatic text simplification tool or text summarization model [ 2 ] in conjunction with our system to provide simplified text captions. However, excessive text summarization can easily result in a loss of information; thus, a proper balance must be struck between caption condensation and preservation of conveyed information.

7 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

Several limitations must be addressed in future work. First, our design process was conducted with hearing individuals. Our research primarily considers how best to visualize sound features, so we had no choice but to collaborate with hearing individuals. Since the goal is the direct visualization of sound, the sound-to-caption feature mapping design was designed for participants who can hear sounds and understand their characteristics such as volume, pitch, and nuances. Our caption system must be fine-tuned from the perspective of DHH individuals to minimize the gap between DHH individuals and hearing individuals. The efficiency of our system is expected to improve by analyzing the relative importance of sound elements and recognizing more various meanings they provide, from the perspective of DHH people. Second, the mapping design needs to be further supplemented to be more intuitive and natural to improve legibility and reduce the cognitive load on DHH individuals. Third, the current version of the caption system covers only six emotional nuance categories, and should be expanded to cover a wider range of nuances in future studies. The current system considers only binary nuances (e.g., an angry tone), but we will add graded nuances (e.g., a progressively angrier tone). Finally, our current caption system is a semi-automated mapping system for which we manually mapped emotions instead of using an emotion classifier to avoid mismatch errors. In the future, we will develop a real-time caption system which fully automates audio visualization using an emotion classification model or a real-time audio analysis model.

8 CONCLUSION

This study presented a caption system which conveys paralinguistic information in speech beyond simple video content delivery to DHH individuals. An audio-visualized caption system was proposed which encompasses the expression of nuances which cannot be expressed sufficiently with basic sound features such as volume and pitch. Our caption system was observed to convey the nuances of speech in video content to DHH individuals, even those who have never experienced sound due to deafness from birth. Our system was particularly effective in formal-talk videos where the speakers’ facial expressions, which are key indicators of nuance for DHH viewers, were not as evocative. Post-interviews reflected various preferences in caption systems among DHH individuals, and suggested design improvements focusing on caption legibility and allowing users sufficient time to acclimate themselves to novel caption systems. Although DHH individuals lack experience with paralinguistic cues, they recognized the accessibility and utility for paralinguistic cues, showing a strong intention to use the proposed caption system despite its limitations. If the proposed caption system could be refined to reflect the perspective of DHH viewers in its design guidelines, it could provide them with a more accessible and enjoyable video watching experience. We hope our approach and insights will benefit future works studying caption systems for DHH viewers.
