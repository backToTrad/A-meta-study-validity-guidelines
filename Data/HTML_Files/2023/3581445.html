CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Multilingual speakers tend to interleave two or more languages when communicating. This communication strategy is called code-mixing, and it has surged with today's ever-increasing linguistic and cultural diversity. Because of their communication style, multilinguals who use conversational agents have specific needs and expectations which are currently not being met by conversational systems. While research has been undertaken on code-mixing conversational systems, previous works have rarely focused on the code-mixing users themselves to discover their genuine needs. This work furthers our understanding of the challenges faced by code-mixing users in conversational agent interaction, unveils the key factors that users consider in code-mixing scenarios, and explores expectations that users have for future conversational agents capable of code-mixing. This study discusses the design implications of our findings and provides a guide on how to alleviate the challenges faced by multilingual users and how to improve the conversational agent user experience for multilingual users.

1 INTRODUCTION

Today, intelligent conversational agents, such as Siri and Alexa, are continuously being updated to actualize more natural, socializing, human-like interaction [ 9 , 10 , 71 , 83 ]. Experts in this field believe that the human-likeness of the conversational agent allows users to communicate with the agent as they would with other humans [ 18 , 28 , 58 ]. Thus, system development has focused on modifying attributes of human communication, such as nuances and quirks, to agent communications in order to improve the conversational agent user experience [ 7 , 124 ]. However, few studies have been completed regarding the user experiences of multilinguals , people who can proficiently speak more than two languages, and bilinguals , people who can speak two languages. Although recent research has been conducted in relation to system development, such as speech recognition and language generation systems, to make computers understand the multilingual stylistic language pattern called code-mixing , which is to alternate two or more languages in words, phrases, clauses, or sentences [ 7 , 75 , 94 , 110 ], few studies have been user-centered, and even fewer studies have attempted to thoroughly understand code-mixing users’ experiences of conversational agents and their expectations when using voice-based conversational agents. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, none of the prior research has been conducted to evaluate their innate needs regarding the agent and the reasons behind those. Therefore, further research is needed in order to understand the design implications and related human-computer technologies that are used to develop conversational agents and how they should be applied to enhance conversational agent usability for multilingual users with code-mixing behaviors.

This study is the initial study conducted to understand the motivation of multilingual users’ code-mixing, their code-mixing contexts, and how these would affect conversational agent design. The study's main aim is to identify the problems that arise when there is a gap in the conversational agents’ understanding of multilingual users’ comfortable communication strategies and language mixing and the elements that would enhance the usability of a voice-based code-mixing conversational agent. The two questions that will guide this study are as follows: What factors need to be considered in order to enrich the user experience for code-mixing multilingual users? In what contexts do multilingual users want to use a conversational agent with code-mixing abilities, and how do they wish to engage with it?

In the hope that this study would provide a primary guide to answering these questions, we conducted a focus group interview and participatory design with 16 multilingual participants proficient in both Korean and English. Through focus group interviews, the conversational agent user experience and the challenges these multilingual users had encountered were observed, and their implicit user needs were discovered through a participatory design workshop. By answering the research questions, this study aims to offer the following contributions to assist future conversational agent designers and researchers: (1) an improved understanding of the multilingual conversational agent user experience and the influence of multilinguals’ code-mixing when using the agents, (2) design considerations that can alleviate the challenges faced when using conversational agents and enhance the conversational agent user experience, and (3) future design directions of conversational agents for code-mixing multilingual users.

2 RELATED WORKS

2.1 Code-Mixing of Multilingual Users

One of the most commonly observed linguistic strategies of multilingual users is mixing and switching different languages in a conversation [ 52 ]. With an increasing number of multilingual speakers globally, many studies have recognized the importance of language mixing in the HCI field for multilingual speakers [ 33 , 43 , 103 ]. Multilingual users have unique characteristics that affect their conversational agent interactions [ 7 ]. For example, they speak more than two languages and belong to various cultural groups due to their diverse linguistic backgrounds [ 52 ]. Moreover, for multilingual users, verbal communication in just one language takes effort and time [ 43 ].

The significance of multilingual conversational agent users’ preferences has been highlighted in the linguistic field. Additionally, many linguistic researchers have stated that language mixing is an effective communication mode for multilingual speakers and should be appreciated more in society [ 43 ]. However, current code-mixing conversational systems appear to lack the ability to understand the natural, code-mixing communication style that multilingual speakers would use when conversing with other people [ 7 ]. Research on conversational agents has been widely made to analyze the user experience and observed user's daily communication [ 9 , 91 , 99 ] and their perception regarding conversational agents [ 23 , 115 ]. Although these studies have offered design suggestions that would support the use of conversational agents among particular user groups, such as children, elderly people, people with disabilities [ 24 , 36 , 92 , 117 , 122 ], and people with distinguishing speech patterns [ 11 ], it has been difficult to find research that relates to code-mixing multilingual users.

2.2 Conversational Agents for Multilingual Users with Code-Mixing Behaviors

Speech-based interaction with social robots and voice-based conversational agents allow users to believe it is possible to socialize [ 83 ] and communicate with these as they would with humans, unlike other computational devices [ 71 ]. Such human-like interaction allows users to utilize conversational agents using a more straightforward and naturalistic approach, enhancing the user experience [ 69 , 83 ]. On the other hand, users are also utilitarian oriented with conversation agents and think that successful task performance of the agent is essential to increase their service satisfaction [ 86 , 104 ]. In parallel with this opinion, some users expressed aversion to the agent's human-likeness for they preferred the agent to stay as a non-relational figure and a“service provider” without unnecessary social cues [ 57 , 62 , 93 ]. Moreover, some studies have indicated that human–agent voice interaction is closer to an exchange of a request and a response than human-like communication [ 91 ]. However, if the conversational agent aims to stimulate more trust and frequent use or to discover the potential for broader use than its practical purposes, the agent would have to replicate human–human social interaction and communication [ 8 , 69 , 99 ], like informal chat [ 105 ], humor [ 32 ], and more. This could be achieved by studying the speech behavior and social cues of humans [ 97 ].

Many designers and developers in conversational agents, therefore, concentrate on designing more human-like assistants, including retrofitting the behavioral intricacies of human–human communication to human–agent communication, such as mimicking the nuances and idiosyncrasies of human speech patterns (e.g., Google Assistant Duplex [ 124 ] and Amazon Alexa [ 100 ]). The immersive interest in the field for conversations between people is founded on the Computers-Are-Social-Actors paradigm; users unconsciously talk to the agent the way they talk to people [ 82 ]. This motivates the conversational agent to cultivate human-like characteristics [ 21 , 42 , 50 ], which encourages the user to bond and socialize with the agent, as they would do with other people [ 79 , 80 , 114 ]. Thus, conversational agents are designed to imitate various human linguistic and behavioral patterns [ 102 ], including the language mixing patterns of multilingual users.

In addition to the work completed by developers and designers, HCI scholars have studied specific user groups with distinct behavioral patterns and linguistic characteristics [ 9 , 91 , 99 ], like stammering [ 11 ]. For example, Choi et al. [ 24 ] conducted an empirical study observing how users with vision impairments utilize conversational agents and suggested invaluable design implications regarding user experience concerns. Accordingly, similar studies have been conducted in relation to code-mixing for conversational agent design implications. However, those studies have mainly written of code-mixing among racial or cultural groups that use code-mixing as their main language [ 44 , 46 , 109 ], and mostly researched user experience with text-based interactions [ 2 , 34 ], such with search interfaces [ 67 ], social media[ 37 ], and chatbots [ 7 ].

Conversational agents remain predominantly monolingual and biased in certain languages even when expanded to new languages [ 3 , 15 , 121 ]. The significance of multilingual conversational agent users’ preferences has been highlighted not only in the linguistic field but also by experts in conversational systems. A greater focus on the challenges faced by multilingual users has led to efforts being made to support multilinguals by developing technologies that support the interaction in multiple languages and code-mixing [ 46 , 73 , 74 ]. However, these attempts have primarily concentrated on enabling users to speak in their most proficient language [ 15 ] or on supporting code-mixing for a very insufficient number of language pairs [ 3 , 106 ], such as Hinglish (Hindi+English). Some conversational systems have recently released updates to support multilingual users through dual language mode settings [ 3 , 15 , 106 ], but they are selective in language pairs, do not support code-mixing [ 15 , 19 ], and only allow code-mixing in particular language pairs [ 3 , 20 , 51 , 106 ]. Therefore, progress has been limited in scope and is still in the early stages.

In this initial stage of designing a conversational agent that fully supports multilingual conversation, a significant factor to consider is multilinguals’ code-mixing conversational contexts and patterns. Many studies have contributed to the development of technology in relation to language mixing. However, it is challenging to find a qualitative user study that has thoroughly identified code-mixing multilingual user needs regarding the conversational agent and the design directions to enhance the current agent.

3 METHODS

This study aimed to explore how code-mixing multilingual users perceived their current conversational agents and what they expected from future agents capable of code-mixing. So that we would be able to discover user needs and wants from the analysis of the results, we adopted the focus group interview method and the participatory design workshop method as the study methodologies [ 61 ]. Focus group interviews are well-known for helping participants recall their past experiences [ 59 ] and become deeply involved in the“given topic” [ 111 ]. Therefore, this study used group interviews to discover the challenges that the multilingual users had experienced with their conversational agents and what, specifically, had caused these problems regarding code-mixing. Moreover, having been inspired by similar studies [ 48 , 61 ] that used workshops to reveal implicit user needs from the participants’ own solutions, as well as studies that used a speculative design method on conversational agents to discover user needs around emerging technology [ 120 ], focus group interview was followed by a participatory design workshop that explored the users’ potential need for a conversational agent with an ability to code-mix.

3.1 Participants

As many countries have been globalized both culturally and linguistically, it is easy to discover global influences in countries like South Korea that were traditionally monolingual. As knowledge of the English language has become a requirement in South Korea, most Koreans learn to speak English as a second language through elementary courses, and many children are sent to study abroad [ 6 , 22 , 101 ], which has led to an increase in the number of Korean-English bilinguals in Korean society. Therefore, we aimed to recruit participants who spoke both Korean and English as a representative example but also drew similar inferences for other language pair users. Participants were recruited from a screening survey that we shared on online social sites for international school graduates and on networking sites for people who were working or seeking work opportunities at international companies. The survey consisted of questions that asked about age, availability, code-mixing usage (and with whom they most frequently used code-mixing and the code-mixing percentage of a conversation with and without those people), current country of residence, previous countries of residence, previous and current conversational agent usage, proficiency in English and Korean, and proficiency in other languages. From this information, we selected 16 participants using two criteria: 1) a multilingual speaker who had a proficient level of both Korean and English and who was a frequent user of code-mixing with friends and family [ 33 ]; and 2) who was using or had used a conversational agent of any kind. In order to evaluate the candidates’ language proficiency, we asked them to define their proficiency in Korean and English by choosing one of the following: basic knowledge, conversant, proficient, fluent, or native. We recruited participants who were above the“proficient” level in both languages because we saw code-mixing not as the result of insufficient language ability but as a behavioral pattern for convenient conversation [ 56 ]. We also considered the experience of living in various countries as another recruitment criterium, as societal and cultural factors are influential factors in triggering code-mixing [ 5 , 35 , 56 , 89 ].

At the time of the study, 13 participants resided in South Korea, and three resided outside of South Korea, one in France and two in the U.S. Most participants, apart from three, had lived outside of South Korea for more than three years in total. All participants were native Korean speakers with an above proficient level of English—seven native speakers, seven fluent speakers (evenly advanced levels in reading, writing, speaking, and listening), and two proficient speakers (able to communicate with native speakers). Eight participants spoke more than two languages; the additional languages of participants other than English and Korean are stated in Table 1. All participants indicated they had previously used voice-based conversational agents. Siri was the most used conversational agent, followed by Google Assistant and Bixby (Table 1).

3.2 Study Procedure

This study consisted of two phases: a focus group interview and a participatory design workshop. The two phases of the study proceeded with three discussions (with each assigned topic), two for the focus group interview and one for the participatory design workshop. The entire study was conducted in four groups of 3–5 participants and lasted an average of 150 minutes. Group interviews help participants quickly recall their experiences and ideas by being stimulated by other participants’ discussions [ 1 ]. Also, Morrison [ 78 ] inferred that discussion between participants helps them to reflect on valuable aspects of their daily experiences that they had initially overlooked. All interviews proceeded via the video conferencing platform Zoom so that participants in different time zones and locations could take part. Using online video interviews allowed for the participation of code-mixing multilingual users from different regions and countries despite location and time differences. The researchers selected participants from diverse locations, hoping this would encourage and influence the participants to discuss their experiences from a more varied context than if the participants were all from one nation or area. All sessions of the study were audio-recorded with the participant's consent. Each participant was notified in advance that they had been assigned to one of the four groups of A–D and to prepare paper and a black pen for the design workshop. The researchers also recommended that the participants think about how they usually use code-mixing and their conversational agent in their daily lives before participation. The entire study introduction proceeded with screen-shared instruction slides with a brief subject and goal of the research, definition of code-mixing and the content of the study, and topics for each discussion. The presentation slides were written in both Korean and English and were verbally explained, mainly in Korean-English code-mixing. The introduction to the study included an icebreaker with the participants introducing themselves, which helped them understand the other participants’ linguistic and cultural backgrounds so they would comfortably use and talk about language mixing [ 56 ]. The main study included three code-mixing topics on which the participants were required to share their overall experiences and thoughts.

Phase 1: Focus Group Interview .

The first two topics were conducted in the focus group interview so that the participants could be inspired by each other to discuss their previous experiences related to the topic. Each topic took 20–25 minutes of discussion. The first discussion topic was“Code-mixing stories in my everyday life,” which discussed motivations, essential qualities, and experiences of code-mixing in real life. The moderator also introduced sub-questions to help the participants remember:“Do you comfortably use code-mixing in conversations? If not, why not?”“Whom do you usually code-mix with and in what context?”“Do you code-mix with a stranger?” The purpose of the first topic was only to emphasize the differences between the participants’ interactions with people and agents [ 26 ], so the participants could compare their experiences and think outside the boundaries of current technologies.

The topic for the second discussion was“Facing the conversational agent as a multilingual user who code-mixes.” For this topic, participants were to share their experiences and challenges interacting with conversational agents as code-mixing multilingual users. The following questions were given to the participants in between related opinions to encourage the discussion:“What conversational agent are you currently using?”“Have you tried code-mixing? Why and why not?”“Were there any moments you wanted to use both Korean and English?”“If your agent fails to understand your (code-mixing) attempts, what do you think would be the reason?” The purpose of the second topic was to help the participants recall and share the challenges they had encountered when using conversational agents, such as issues around the agent not supporting code-mixing. Through these focus group interviews, the conversational agent user experience and the challenges these multilingual users encountered were investigated.

Phase 2: Participatory Design Workshop .

After the focus group interview, the moderator asked the participants to gather paper and a black pen for the third discussion. The third discussion of the main study was conducted in a participatory design workshop that instructed the participants to imagine future scenarios of how they would utilize a code-mixing agent in their everyday lives and draw these scenarios. The participants were asked to sketch a possible future conversation with a code-mixing conversational agent. The researchers required the scenarios to contain the following elements: user (individual or multi-party), conversational agent (in any form), and situational context or conversation. The moderator showed an example of the drawing when the participant asked for clarification about what a“scenario” is, but the example was unrelated to the conversational agent to prevent the impact on their outcome and creativity.

Since the current conversational agent that multilingual users commonly use does not support the code-mixing of most language pairs, the users are not used to conversing with code-mixing with the agent. Although there are other methodologies to investigate the users’ perception, like video-prototyping [ 57 ] and focus group interview [ 26 ], those methods could bias the users’ perception and limit their imagination regarding the possible scenarios of the future conversational agents. Therefore, a participatory design workshop with the sketching method was chosen for this study to investigate the inherent wants of the future conversational agent regarding code-mixing. With its low cost and quick results, sketching is a novel method used to discuss the future image of HCI [ 108 ]. The non-final state of the drawing allows for the burdenless participation of novices [ 13 , 29 ] and stimulates open discussion and ideation from its ambiguity [ 39 ]. As a result, with well-intended questions and instructions, sketched scenarios can be generated as implications of the adoption of future technologies [ 107 , 108 , 112 ]. Since sketching scenarios helps users to envision future contexts [ 60 ] and create open solutions by challenging the current availability bias [ 113 ], the scenarios allowed for the verification of potential user needs [ 12 , 95 , 96 ]. Thus, the participatory workshop was based on the prerequisite that such a future agent had no technological limitations and could understand all the languages the participants spoke to alleviate the impact of current technological boundaries. After the 30-minute drawing session, participants photographed or scanned each drawing result and delivered it to the moderator. The collected drawings were organized into Google slides by the moderator and shared with group participants so they could see each other's results. Participants were allowed to add a supplementary description of their drawing directly to the slide if desired. (Figure 1). The drawings were then shared and discussed within the group for 50 minutes as a debriefing session. Each member introduced their scenarios, and the other participants added their opinions and related experiences.

The study was wrapped up with the participants sharing their thoughts on a code-mixing conversational agent and the entire study process. Through this participatory design workshop, code-mixing conversational agent users’ needs were discovered.

3.3 Data Analysis

The audio recordings of the study were transcribed and analyzed using a thematic coding approach [ 40 ], following the practice of similar qualitative research regarding HCI [ 24 , 26 , 61 ]. The initial coding was completed using an inductive approach. The researchers then extracted and codified intrinsic user needs and expectations from the data. After all the data had been analyzed under the decided-upon initial themes, the codes were iterated so they could be re-evaluated and merged into similarities and differences, and they were then categorized into developed themes. For the final themes, the researchers followed the subjects from the main study topics, believing these topics would best communicate the findings. Then, the assembled codes were arranged into final subcategories. Each of the themes was mapped to the findings in the following section. The first three findings included the data from both the focus group interviews and the debriefing of the participatory design workshop. The fourth finding only consisted of the data from the workshop because it accounted for multilingual speakers’ potential user needs in terms of a future conversational agent capable of code-mixing. (Parts of the representative quotes in the findings that are spoken in Korean were translated by a Korean and English bilingual researcher to best interpret the cultural terms used by the participants.)

4 FINDINGS

4.1 Findings on Multilingual Users’ Daily Code-Mixing Behavior

Throughout the study, all participants described the need to code-mix in order to have comfortable and effective conversations. While discussing their experiences regarding code-mixing, the participants conveyed the fundamental features they would consider and need when mixing languages in conversations in everyday life. Before referring to the findings regarding the perceptions and difficulties the users had with conversational agents, we present these insights for the readers to comprehend the features that could affect code-mixing users’ interactions with conversational agents. Some findings may be in parallel with topics from existing literature on linguistics and psychology regarding language mixing [ 33 , 56 , 89 ]. In addition, the usage of the following attributes and code-mixing may differ by the context of the conversation and the relationship with the interlocutor.

4.1.1 Why Do Multilingual Users Code-Mix?. The participants mentioned several reasons why they use code-mixing while communicating. One of the reasons derived from the study is that multilingual user has a distinct first language for a vocabulary. Because the participants had learned words in various languages, they recalled some vocabulary in the language they first learned. This phenomenon meant that translating words into another language during a conversation would take considerable time and effort, making code-mixing inevitable.

“I usually use code-mixing when I can't perfectly switch language modes. When I meet people like me (people who speak two or three languages), we introduce ourselves as‘zero-lingual’ because there are moments when I can't think of a certain word in English or Korean." (P14)

“We don't learn languages like that. So, when I learned to say‘cradle (in English]’ in the first place, I have to use the word‘cradle’ (in English) even if I am talking in Korean. I don't have an alternative word in every language.” (P10)

Aligning with this notion, the participants mixed languages to describe and deliver the best meaning of certain words. They believed that the nuance, culture, and emotion of a particular word and phrase could only be described in a specific language.

“Outside of work, especially when I'm at home or with my husband, I mostly talk in Korean. But when I feel it's more natural to say (something) in English, I use English expressions and reactions.” (P6)

Therefore, participants found code-mixing an essential feature and even considered having conversations with other code-mixers as safe zones in which they could comfortably communicate without having to be considerate or mentally burdened.

“When I talk to a person with mixed languages, I see how the person responds. Then I can tell if this person is okay and if he's a ‘safe zone.’" (P8)

4.1.2 Burdens and Difficulties Related to Code-Mixed Communication. Even though code-mixing is widely known to be an effective strategy for multilingual speakers [ 43 ], code-mixing speakers face moments when they have to self-censor or not code-mix [ 17 ]. One situation in which these difficulties occur, mentioned by our participants, is when the speaker first needs to determine the language proficiency of a stranger before making a conversation. According to the participants, one way in which a person's code-mixing ability can be determined is by forming common ground related to their cultural and language diversity. Code-mixing users immediately felt sympathetic when they discovered that others had led similar lives, like growing up and living in different countries or attending an international school. Those experiences exposed the person's cultural and linguistic backgrounds to others.

“I don't think it's easy to find out the language level of another person. You don't usually ask a person,‘How well do you speak English?’ But knowing information like whether the person lived in the U.S. in middle school or the person lived in another country for years is how you approximate it. I can talk comfortably from then on.” (P5)

Moreover, the participants noted they also control the level of language mixing based on the learned knowledge of acquaintances, feeling mentally burdened. If the participants were unsure what the person would think or how the person would react if they used several languages, they eliminated the possibility of code-mixing. If they knew the language proficiency but needed more trust, they aligned to the interlocutor's code-mixing style and amount.

“…If the person can get to this level where I'm comfortable talking, I continue, and if the person doesn't, I adjust to that (person's) level… And if there's one friend who doesn't catch up with the others in the group, I tend to lower my level of code-mixing (for that person).” (P11)

Although code-mixing made the multilingual users’ conversations easier, most participants said they tended to avoid using it, as they felt burdened by the adverse reactions code-mixing received in society. The participants felt pressured and also felt they might be judged by the general public.

“I use this very basic terminology, but I cringe when the other person doesn't understand what it means. I don't mean to be arrogant, but sometimes I feel people might think that way.” (P5)

4.1.3 Requirements That Encourage Multilingual Users to Code-Mix. Code-mixing is a communication method that multilingual speakers use when they are in their most relaxed state and are with people with whom they have close relationships [ 56 ]. The study participants needed enough trust and intimacy with the interlocutor to believe their code-mixing would not be judged. This was a significant reason for them to never try mixing languages when speaking with a stranger or an ordinary acquaintance, and they would only code-mix in front of their close, well-known relatives (P2, P3, P4, P5, P6, P7, P10, P11, P12, P14, and P15).

“It's very rare to mix languages with someone you just met or hardly know…” (P4)

Therefore, the participants used code-mixing without hesitation with their close friends and family. The close relationship built upon similar linguistic and cultural backgrounds formed high trust and intimacy.

“So, when I'm with my friends, I don't really care how much code-mixing I use or how I change the percentile of it. Because I know they understand no matter what.” (P15)

As a result, code-mixing was also used by multilinguals as a representation of closeness and emotional bonding.

“When I use code-mixing, I do it to share cultural things with that person.‘You know?’ and‘I know.’ kind of bonding.” (P16)

4.2 Code-Mixing Multilingual Users’ Perceptions of Current Conversational Agents

4.2.1 Monolingual Person. Although some conversational agent users see the agent as an“it,” a non-humanoid figure, [ 93 ], the multilingual participants consider their conversational agent to be a person with a particular persona. They personify the conversational agent to have a nationality and a mother tongue whose language and cultural roots are from the country of its company. Our participants likely set their agent to speak in its base language, assuming the agent would, therefore, communicate with better understanding.

“You know, Bixby and Tmap are from Korea, made by Koreans. And because Google and Amazon's Alexa are made in the United States… I feel like the agent responds better in English.” (P11)

The participants also considered the agent to be entirely native and monolingual. Echoing the reasons for not code-mixing with strangers, they would not use different languages and would carefully choose their words so the agent would understand them.

“Since my (conversational) agent is set up in English, I think I've treated her like I'm facing a foreign, native friend. I mean, she's an American who only speaks English, so I never thought of talking to her in Korean or any other language….” (P5)

4.2.2 Low Language Proficiency. As previously mentioned, code-mixing speakers constantly analyze the language proficiency of their interlocutors and conversational agents. The participants had experienced numerous failures with their conversational agents and had begun communicating with low expectations. They were also putting more effort into talking slowly and clearly to increase the agent's recognition.

“(Siri) just pops out when I say something about the country‘Syria 1 .’ So, I've changed it to English, but most of my default settings and information are in Korean, and I can't change everything to use Siri….” (P2)

“...I think I see it as a five-year-old. So when I use Siri, I still think I have to say this slowly and clearly so that Siri can understand well as I would do to a real baby.” (P9)

4.3 Challenges Code-Mixing Multilingual Users Face in Conversational Agent Interaction

4.3.1 Different Code-Mixing Needs in Varying Cultural Contexts. The participants experienced inconvenience when the agent did not understand various subjects from cultural contexts that related to the other languages the participants spoke or the mix of those languages. Although a few conversational agents support two language settings [ 15 ], they do not understand the contexts of both cultures and languages.

“People who live in multiple cultures like us have no choice....” (P10)

“When I want to search 무한도전[mu-han-do-jeon] 2 ," I can't just say it in Korean (because I set my conversational agent to English). So, I just interpret it in English and say,‘Search Infinite Challenge,’ but the result just comes out with foreign videos that have the same subtitle as it…. It doesn't give me the results related to Korea.” (P7)

Code-mixing is known as a cross-cultural phenomenon [ 52 ], for multilingual speakers live in complex and multicultural contexts with frequent language switching. As a result, they need to alternate language modes or change the code mix to fit different contexts. The participants reported that their multicultural contexts increased code-mixing and made using a conversational agent rather demanding. However, current conversational agents cannot cope with changeable user contexts, and this issue made the participants either change the settings repeatedly or give up on tasks. The participants cited problems they had encountered using a current conversational agent; their experiences were affected by their online and offline contexts. 4.3.2 Problems Caused by Using Mixed Accents as a Result of Mixed Languages. Multilingual speakers described unpleasant moments they have had with their own conversational agents. Participants with various language experiences tended to speak with an intonation affected by other languages, which led to the agents’ mishearing [ 88 ]. Some participants were capable of using more than one accent for a language. For example, P14 mentioned that she speaks in English mainly in a Konglish accent (English in a Korean accent) and a Franglish accent (English in a French accent), and in a native English accent for certain words. The participants described that they mix-used the accents as a natural result of their language-mixing speech. They described the two reasons to explain the phenomenon. The first reason was that participants were affected by what language they started speaking and what accent the members of the conversation predominantly used from the beginning. For instance, if the participant started speaking French, the participant code-mixes English in a Franglish accent to fit English into the discourse and return to French more naturally. Another reason for accent mixing was to express the perfect nuance of the word or expression in its native pronunciation; this relates to the insights from section 4.1.1. Thus, the participant could start speaking the language in one accent and move to another while the conversation flows without being self-aware. Moreover, the participants mentioned that these vary by the social situations and the audience around when the conversation is ongoing, as mentioned in section 4.3.

However, to stop the ongoing cycle of their failure in the conversational agent interaction, the participants mentioned that they had tried to fit in their accents to what the agent could understand (and this was possible because they were above the proficient or native level in languages) or changed the language settings, and felt empowered by the agent because they thought the agent is supposed to help them, not the other way around. Some participants blamed not being able to make the most of the service on their mispronunciations rather than blaming the agent.

“Honestly, now my pronunciation got all mixed up... I think Siri doesn't understand me because I am not a native English speaker and have a French English accent. So I went back to the Korean setting because that's the language I have the most accurate pronunciation of.” (P14)

“People who speak good English can understand any English in any accent, like Mexican accent, Philippines accent or American accent. It feels like (the agent) only understands native pronunciation, so I don't think there's much of a compromising….” (P10)

“...so, I have to talk slowly and in a precise (American English) accent as it talks to me. And I try to adjust my voice so (the conversational agent) would understand me.” (P12)

4.3.3 Feeling Pressured and Interrupted when Choosing Words. A major reason for code-mixing, as previously mentioned, is to avoid hesitating when choosing words during conversations. However, the participants felt rushed and pressured when the agent closed the discourse without allowing the users to have moments of pausing to recall, translate, or switch language modes.

“I am most comfortable talking in mixed language and when I can't recall some of the words as I say.” (P11)

“When I code-mix with English and French, I stutter when I suddenly change to another language. I think one of the biggest reasons is the accent. It's hard to quickly switch to another intonation.” (P14)

4.3.4 Experiencing Inconsistent Personas When Switching Languages. Because the participants were proficient in multiple languages, they often alternated language modes on their smartphones and conversational agents. When alternating the language, the participants had erratic user experiences when they faced different personas by language, even if the user and the device they used were the same.

“I often switch my language setting because I work both in Korean and English. But the agent has a very calm male voice in Korean, whereas a cold female voice in English. Although I'm the same person, I feel like I'm talking to two different systems separately. Maybe it's the default mode because I switch modes very often.” (P16)

The participants agreed that the difference in voices made them think they were using a new, distinct conversational agent from their original agent. As a result, the participants felt inconvenienced by having to rebuild a relationship and trust with the agent.

4.3.5 Feeling Excluded from the General User Group. With constant failures and challenging conversational agent user experiences, the code-mixing multilingual users were convinced they were not designed to be the primary users of the conversational agents. The participants saw themselves as less capable of utilizing the agents than native, monolingual users.

“I can speak both English and Korean, but I get the feeling that I can't use (the agent) as much as (native, monolingual) people do. This is the part where I thought I just had to live with it.” (P8)

4.4 Expectations for Code-Mixing Conversational Agents

The participants also described their expectations regarding future code-mixing conversational agents by comparing their conversational agent experiences with code-mixing with people.

4.4.1 Applicable Use Cases for Code-Mixing Agents. Through a participatory design workshop, the participants created possible conversation scenarios with a code-mixing conversational agent. However, since this study gathered an insufficient number of participants and scenarios to discover the overall use cases for a code-mixing conversational agent, we acknowledge that the roles and use cases we present in this section cannot be representative use cases. Accordingly, we report these applicable usage scenarios to demonstrate the discovery of the code-mixing users’ unmet wants and the difficulties they have had with their current agents. Among the drawn scenarios, the results showed some similarities between the roles of the conversational agent and were gradually grouped into three categories. The three possible roles that emerged from the participants’ ideas included agents casually code-mixing with the users and helping with their work and language learning.

Casual Chat and Play .

As mentioned earlier, multilingual speakers release their linguistic burden by code-mixing in front of people with whom they have close relationships. The study participants would like the agent to be a friend who would indulge them by talking in a similar language with a similar level of code-mixing as the user. They wanted the agent to be one of them, play games, and talk casually using interjections or colloquial expressions.

“I wanted the agent to follow my code-mixing and communicate with me like friends. And I wanted it to be part of me and play games.” (P6) (Figure 2)

Most participants emphasized that the code-mixing conversational agent could naturally use and understand slang and expressions from online memes in all the languages they use.

“I want the agent to talk casually in both languages (Korean and English) in the language I use to talk (to the agent).” (Figure 3) (P11)

“If I mix languages mainly on proper nouns or if I switch languages in sentences, I want the agent to code-mix just as I do. And if it does so, I would think of the agent as a friend and expect the conversation to be more casual.” (P14)

Supporting Work and Interpreting Nuances .

On the premise that the agent has the multilingual language ability to code-mix, participants said that the agents should help them with their work by finding information in various languages. Influenced by the“social norm” [ 16 ], code-mixing speakers usually work monolingually, which requires them to convert their code-mixed thoughts into two separate language modes. They would expect the agents to help with the interpretation of words or phrases in perfect nuance in another language.

“There are times when I know the term in Korean but cannot interpret the nuance in English. I try Google Translate or use Papago, but I still don't quite get the meaning.” (Figure 4) (P6)

“When I freely code-mix English and Korean to brainstorm the webtoon storyline, I want the agent to combine keywords and come up with a Korean or English title that is the most representative.” (Figure 5) (P9)

“When I look for image references, I search in both Korean and English. I have to combine keywords that best describe what I want to look up. So, if the agent searches both English and Korean results even when I say (the keywords) in Korean, it will simplify my work so much. I can just describe the keyword in Korean.” (P2)

Learning Languages and Enhancing Proficiency .

Some participants said they would require the agent to have a professional level in all languages they could speak and wanted the agent to be a language coach that supervised and taught an advanced language level. Multilingual users wanted to expose themselves to an environment that cultivated continuous language development because they were relatively open to different languages and cultures.

“…I always wanted to learn new languages. So, if I have a code-mixing AI agent, it can help me learn the languages much easier.” (P14)

“I do a lot of job interviews in English. So, I want the agent to simulate a job interview and give me feedback in both English and Korean.” (P1)

“I have so many video meetings with coworkers overseas. And as I'm doing the meeting, I want my agent to record my conversations and tell me what I can improve or what I've got wrong. I think that's the best way to improve my English skills.” (Figure 6) (P16)

4.4.2 Interaction with Familiar Code-Mixing. As previously indicated, there are multiple concerns that multilingual speakers have to consider when code-mixing with people. While the participants felt burdened when they communicated with acquaintances and needed to observe the interlocutors and control the code-mix level, they prioritized their own preferences in regard to interaction with a future code-mixing agent. All participants agreed that the agent should control its use of code-mixing and yield to them.

“I just want the agent to talk like me. I think it will feel natural if the agent talks in Korean when I talk in Korean and in English when I talk in English.” (P8)

“The most important thing is the agent matches my level of code-mixing. If I mix an English word in a Korean sentence, I want the agent to do the same…” (P5)

Participants wanted the agent's code-mixing response to be similar in nature to their code-mixing. P5 indicated that she wanted the agent to use words like“hiking” and“heavy rain” in English even when the dominant language was Korean because she found those words were best understood in English (Figure 7). Other participants also agreed that the agent should respond in the dominant language the user talked in.

“I mixed the Korean word‘대표님보고[dae-pyo-nim bo-go]’ into my (English) sentence because I never said it in English. I think it will be awkward if the agent intentionally translates the word.” (P3) (Figure 8)

The study also revealed that a conversational agent's ability to understand code-mixed sentences should be more fundamental and prioritized than the ability to use mixed languages in response. For code-mixing users, speaking in one language takes effort, but it takes less effort to understand it.

“I don't want the agent to mix language in their responses. I don't expect it to give in return. I just want it to understand all of my code-mixed words.” (P9)

“I don't really mind if the agent talks to me in full English. Because I can understand most English accents.” (P1)

4.4.3 Discoverability of Multilingual and Code-Mixing Abilities. The multilingual users conveyed that having a conversational agent that could communicate in multiple languages was of paramount importance. However, some participants were not aware that their agent had recently updated to support two languages at once until the day of the interview. Although the function only allowed the users to talk in each language individually (not code-mixing), they found it useful in terms of not having to change language settings all the time. Moreover, understanding the boundaries of that function's capability was even more challenging. Participants thus expressed their desire to be well informed of functions related to multilingual abilities in the future so they could use these as soon as possible.

“I just learned today that Google Home can have two language modes. To be honest, Google has been a native Korean to me until now. I once tried calling it in an English accent, but it didn't respond, so I just thought there are no such functions.” (P8)

“If the (code-mixing) agent tells me that it can use two or more languages in the beginning stage, I'll recognize the advanced technology of it and maybe try code-mixing to it….” (P16)

The participants would also prefer the agent to attempt a mixed-language conversation when the agent encounters a multilingual user without additional setup. We discovered that the multilingual users’ unintentionally used code-mixing could be the cue for the agent to notice its multilingual user and suggest the functions in response.

“…Another way to find out the function can be the positive feedback (from an agent) when I unconsciously code-mixed to the agent.” (P16)

“The agent can (first) talk to me in mixed languages to let me know about the function.” (P6)

Some participants stated that if they witness the agent's multilingual and multicultural abilities, they would feel accepted and think their code-mixing was being acknowledged as an effective communication tool in the community rather than evidence of low language proficiency [ 56 ] as they would do when they talk to their close relatives.

“If there's an agent like that (that code-mixes), I think I can shake off some of the fear of code-mixing in public, myself.” (P13)

“The development of code-mixing agents will not only help people (who live in multiple cultural environments) like us but also let others know that code-mixing is essential and vital.” (P10)

5 DISCUSSIONS AND DESIGN IMPLICATIONS

The findings of this study reveal the conversational agent user experience for code-mixing multilinguals and their expectations for using a conversational agent with code-mixing behaviors. The results show that code-mixing can be considered an essential subject when designing conversational agents in different aspects, regarding the agent's role, persona, and accents. In this section, we merit discussion on the implications of our insightful findings in terms of enhancing multilingual users’ engagement with conversational agents.

5.1 Why Do the Users Need a Code-Mixing Conversational Agent?

Our findings emphasize that multilingual users, especially code-mixing users, face challenges when engaging with voice-based conversational agents, just as they do in society [ 4 , 94 , 116 ]. Prioritizing homogenous language development in HCI was unavoidable since monolingual communication was considered the norm even in linguistic research for an extended period [ 16 ], and has made multilingual users take fewer benefits from utilizing the conversational agent [ 53 ]. The discrepancy in user experience led previous studies to support multilingual users and their code-mixing behaviors with advanced and effective code-mixing recognition and learning [ 49 , 118 ]. Related research that studied the relationship between code-mixing conversational agents and multilingual users discovered the users’ preferences on code-mixing abilities [ 7 , 46 ]. However, these studies overlooked discussing why the users need and want such capabilities, which leads to the question: What valuable aspects do the multilingual users see in the upcoming code-mixing conversational agent?

The findings from this study indicate that multilingual users are similarly pressured into conversing with a monolingual conversational agent as if talking to a stranger. The code-mixing users taking part in this study felt they needed to indulge the agent, not the other way around, and this led the users to discover their need for a safe zone. They wished to avoid all burdening considerations, such as adjusting the code-mixing level to the interlocutor and making the conversation understandable when engaging with the agents. Moreover, the advent of code-mixing conversational agents could present multilingual users with a feeling of inclusiveness and acceptance in society. As mentioned, the language use of multilingual speakers varies depending on their“group membership” [ 38 ]. Their language and code-mixing vary by what social group they are engaging with and how they want to be identified because they have various cultural backgrounds [ 38 , 52 ]. In addition, our findings also show that their multicultural contexts affect them to make their online environment to be language-mixed and affect their current conversational agent to be even more complicated. Including the functional weakness in relation to code-mixing, the perceptions and challenges that stem from the existing conversational agents made the multilingual users with code-mixing habits feel excluded from being general users of the agents.

As expected, the advance in speech recognition would enhance the multilingual user experience in utilitarian aspects with code-mixing interaction and potentialize the relational orientation [ 93 ] with its more human-like conversational interaction [ 71 ]. Most importantly, we should understand that users expect the upcoming code-mixing conversational agent, not as an advanced technology but to be an easy-to-access refugee for a comfortable conversation with emotionally fulfilling acceptance.

In extension of this notion, it is vital to design informative walk-through guidelines to forewarn the users about the available abilities and limitations of the code-mixing agent in the beginning. Although conversational agents fail to understand users for many reasons, code-mixing multilingual users often blame their unique speech behavior—code-mixing—and drop the conversation (findings 4.3.5) or think they have lost control of the device [ 85 ] because they do not understand the reason for the failure. Experiences like this could eventually destroy the users’ well-established trust and intimacy with the conversational agent. For this reason, the upcoming code-mixing conversational agent should be designed to make clear statements of their failure to prevent the multilingual users from getting frustrated by unnecessary misunderstandings [ 62 ]. Moreover, the agent's functional boundaries and breakdown possibility regarding its code-mixing capabilities should be well-informed in advance, and the users should be guided to educate the agent as part of the designed user journey.

5.2 Design Considerations for Multilingual-Friendly Conversational Agent

Through our findings, we analyzed various aspects of how the usability of conversational agents could be enhanced to support code-mixing multilingual users. In addition, since this type of conversational system development would require both design changes and technological advances, we suggest design considerations that could be immediately incorporated into conversational agent design, such as the conversational agent's persona.

5.2.1 User-Like Code-Mixing to Form Common Ground. For human-like communication with conversational agents, conversational systems should consider mixed-language interactions [ 7 ]. Although some studies have investigated limited assessments of agents’ social conversations [ 26 ], our participants noted the possibility of forming either rapport with the code-mixing conversational agent or even friendship by talking and playing games casually. However, the appropriate extent of code-mixing should be considered in order to enhance human–agent interactions, for an excessive human-likeness of the agent could cause antipathy [ 57 ]. According to the communication accommodation theory, people often synchronize with the interlocutor in close relationships in human–human communication [ 27 ]; this strategy is called convergence [ 68 ]. Convergence in human–agent communication can also occur when the user experience enhances communication effectiveness and affiliation (similar to a human conversation) [ 14 , 41 ]. Bawa et al. discovered that the“effective reciprocation” of a code-mixing chatbot, a text-based conversational agent, made a significant impact in terms of making users feel that the conversation was natural. Likewise, the participants of our study mentioned their preference for the voice-based conversational agent to match their code-mixing, using the words they frequently use. Therefore, a conversational agent imitating the code-mixing of the user rather than talking in its own code-mixing can be an effective strategy to enhance the user experience in both text and voice communication. To do so, conversational agent designers willing to specifically target code-mixing multilingual users could evaluate the previous conversational agent design guidelines retrofitting them with the findings from this study. Some of Yang's conversational agent guidelines [ 123 ] advise to“Learn about user habits over time from past interactions” and“Provide users with options to customise the commands and responses” for general users. These guidelines could be refined into“Learn about the user's code-mixing habits and speech pattern over time from past interactions” and“Provide users with options to customize commands and responses in different languages or with code-mixing” for code-mixing multilingual users. The HCI community could support such guidelines by concentrating the conversational agents to learn the user's context and code-mix habits through advancing artificially intelligent conversational systems and learning, such as code-switch speech recognition systems [ 118 ] and natural language processing [ 49 ].

5.2.2 Adjusting the Code-Mixing Level and Personality to Fit User-Expected Roles. Our findings revealed that multilingual users with code-mixing behavior expect conversational agents to have different code-mixing levels, language use, and attitude depending on the desired role. Thus, we suggest that the upcoming agent for multilinguals should support the users’ different code-mixing and language preferences for their social and transactional roles. The social role includes casually talking as the users do with their close friends with similar cultural backgrounds. Some scenarios from our participatory workshop showed cases of casually playing games with the agent and asking for a weather forecast, and such conversations led to the comfortable use of code-mixing and multilingual slang from both the user and the agent. Another suggested role is transactional. Because of the task-oriented usage of current agents [ 25 , 77 ], participants naturally came up with interaction scenarios with a more advanced conversational agent assistant. For the agent's transactional role, the code-mixing users’ expected a supporter (or a butler) with a common ground in linguistics and furthermore, a language expert who could advise their language proficiency and constant language learning. The participants would like their agent to avoid unnecessary code-mixing but to understand the use of it in these roles, which stems from their prior experiences of being viewed as using code-mixing because of low language abilities [ 16 , 43 ] and the need for trust in being accepted in the relationship. From the formal and professional attitudes of the assistant, code-mixing users anticipate receiving supervision and feedback on language skills and getting initial help with translating subtle nuances and phrases from one language to another for language proficiency. Moreover, they wanted the agent to have full access to information in all languages the user speaks and bring search results in those languages to support them with pieces of information from all cultural aspects. Thus, we suggest conversational agent design to fulfill the users’ needs by establishing different code-mixing modes that vary by user's purpose, such as social and transactional roles, and broaden the conversational agent use among multilingual code-mixing speakers and enable intimate engagement in agent interaction. Designers could also find an opportunity to strengthen the conversational agent's personality [ 100 ] with the study finding by adding code-mixing as an attribute to diverse its personality into its relational and humorous aspects and utilitarian oriented aspects. Since this study revealed that multilingual code-mixers want varying languages, degrees of code-mixing, and conversational agent personalities that depend on the conversational context, design researchers could conduct further user studies to discover scenarios in a more diversified usage area.

5.2.3 Understanding Complex Accent Combinations. There have been various attempts in research to consider dialects and accents within a country or single nation [ 72 , 90 ] and to pursue the need for understanding and learning [ 47 ] various accented speech of non-native language users.

Multilingual users would also like the agent to understand their various accents, which were being affected by different languages. It has been previously discovered that user groups with varying accents face difficulties in voice interactions due to their accented speech [ 88 ] because current conversational agents are primarily based on the standard pronunciation of the language [ 46 ]. Current conversational agents also see the significant need to support various accents and are in progress, but still fall short in actualization. Google and Amazon [ 47 ] are making progress but still fall short in their technology to support selective accents like specific intonation from traditional bilingual societies (e.g., India and Hong Kong) or non-native English society [ 54 ]. According to our study findings, however, multilingual speakers who are proficient in speaking Standard English also use varying accents and show a more complex pattern in their accents: mixed accents. This phenomenon was a result of their code-mixing behaviors. Therefore, the participants could choose from various accents, including native English accents, unlike the other“culturally code-mixing” societies [ 46 ] stated above.

As an example of many cases, some of our participants discussed how they use particular words in both native (Standard English) and non-native (Korean-English, French-English, or Japanese-English) accents depending on the context and the major language the members of the conversation use. Moreover, participants also mentioned cases of using two or more accents in a sentence while code-mixing multiple languages. However, the current conversational agents’ limitation on recognizing the accented speech [ 47 , 84 ] causes the users to put additional effort into differentiating accents between the languages only to convince the agent.

“When I use my Google Assistant in Korean, it often doesn't get my awake words like‘Hey Google’ and‘Okay Google’ because I say it in English accents, I guess. I have to say‘O-k-ay Goo-gle’ like in Korean accents.” (P7)

Therefore, conversational agents should be designed to understand that users can comfortably switch accents during the same word or phrase. Scholars of HCI are advancing accent identification with speech recognition [ 30 ], and even accent verification in cross-accented discourses [ 84 , 119 ]. However, such technological advance merits only the selective language pairs and user groups, similar to the current conversational agents. In most cases, it underestimates the users by not knowing that a single user could have more than one accent per language.

Once again, we emphasize the importance of being aware that code-mixing can cause a complex discourse of mixed accents or speech patterns. The conversational agent should be designed to understand that such complex speech behaviors could come from a single user and attach this information to the user's voice profile [ 87 ]. Along with this insight, further user research could uncover whether multilingual users prefer the conversational agent with mixed accents since this study only discovered how the users would talk to the agent.

5.2.4 Maintaining a Consistent Persona Across All Language Modes. One of the important design elements in developing a conversational agent is to design the agent's persona, such as personality and profile, tone and manner, voice, and scenarios [ 65 ] because these elements determine how the user perceives the agent. Several studies confirm that a consistent persona should be designed for a conversational agent [ 66 , 76 , 126 ]. Unfortunately, however, some of our participants mentioned that the capricious persona of the agent led to unpredictable and inconsistent user experiences. As stated in our findings, code-mixing users alternate the language settings on their mobile devices or conversational agents depending on the surrounding context or language they wish to speak in. Although the user and the machine remained the same in this process, the multilingual users experienced changes in the conversational agent's persona, which included voice, personality, and even gender. The sudden change confused the original anthropomorphic image users had of their agents, disconnected the trust and relationship built before the language changed, and made them feel they were facing a stranger. Although this would merely affect the agent interaction for users who prefer utilitarian orientation over relational orientation—who consider the agent as“it” [ 93 ]—it could have a significant impact on the relationship engagement and user experience for users who personify the agent as a social figure from its speech behavior and voice [ 69 , 97 ]. The confusion of the original anthropomorphic image the users had of their agents, could disconnect the trust and relationship built before the language changed, and made them feel they were facing a stranger. Retaining a consistent conversational agent persona regardless of language setting could mitigate the limited relationship in multilingual users’ agent interaction. Most importantly, a consistent image of the voice should be provided in advance. Moreover, our study participants indicated they needed a precise image of the agent's nationality and dominant language foundation to decide their communication attitude toward it. Therefore, providing a distinctive and continuous personality through voice and intonation could help multilingual users imagine the personified interlocutor with the intent of forming a relationship with it [ 70 ]. Furthermore, designers could also consider designing the agent to have a persona with multiple countries or language backgrounds and similar code-mixing skills, to achieve higher trust and intimacy with code-mixing users since they could form a common ground with the agent as they do with other multilinguals.

5.3 Limitations and Future Work

Although this research is an initial study investigating code-mixing multilingual users’ general experiences and perceptions of conversational agents, we caution against generalizing our findings to other multilingual users since multilingual users are significantly influenced by the linguistic background of any interlocutor [ 94 ], and, in this case, including the interviewer, who is Korean-English bilingual. Therefore, different findings might be discovered in similarly conducted studies with other language pair users, and this further motivates the need for further studies with various multilingual speakers as participants. Moreover, the locations and countries of the participants could also be crucial features of the research. Our study was conducted online to recruit code-mixing users who were residing in various nations and had different backgrounds. Although only a few participants in this study were from different countries, it was clear that the groups with these participants had more extensive discussions than the others. Further research with a broader spectrum and numbers of participants from diverse regions and locations could bring out richer insights. In addition, the participants of our study mentioned that their current stage of code-mixing had been gradually manipulated by biased social awareness [ 43 , 45 , 81 ] over time when compared with their language mixing in childhood. Furthermore, the users’ perceptions might have been affected by their existing conversational agent and could have limited the users’ imaginations of unconventional interactions [ 26 ]. Therefore, future works conducting studies on people who are not affected by such social norms and technologies but are capable of anthropomorphizing the agent [ 93 , 125 ] may demonstrate a broader usage range for the code-mixing conversational agent.

Furthermore, we acknowledge the limitation of the study method. Since the study relied on the participants’ recall of the past, the result will not include all their conversational agent experiences and challenges. Also, the group study environment may have caused the participants to converge or be pressured and reluctant to oppose others’ opinions. Therefore, further research could directly observe multilinguals’ code-mixing conversations by studying the users’ conversation strategies and conducting individual post-interviews for richer results [ 64 , 65 ].

In addition to the code-mixing multilingual user experience being explicitly studied, further research can also be completed regarding multilingual users’ perception of the conversational agent's embodiment. The scenarios that were drawn as part of this study were intended to be the result of imagining the interaction with the upcoming conversational agent with advanced abilities and communication skills. Although some participants even saw the agent as a human-like social figure, a friend who could play games with them, most scenarios illustrated an advanced version of the agent's utilitarian use, like answering questions and supporting work tasks. The barrier to the user's imagination of a human-like conversational agent may stem from their previous experience with current task-oriented agents with poor dialogue abilities [ 25 , 26 ]. For instance, many participants illustrated the future conversational agent in the forms of existing agents, such as Siri and Alexa (Findings 4.4). Therefore, future works could achieve different results by constructing the participatory design workshop around sketching use cases with the conversational agents having more human-like attributes, such as physical embodiment. Previous studies inferred that users perceive the agent persona differently depending on the conversational agent's form [ 63 ], and the personification of the agent could increase user engagement [ 55 ] and increase trust and relationship [ 31 , 70 , 98 ]. Accordingly, more diverse findings may be found if code-mixing users proceed with the same investigation with agents in varying humanoid physical embodiments, like humanoid robots.

6 CONCLUSION

This study was conducted to investigate current conversational agent usage by multilingual users who frequently code-mix and the expectations they have for future conversational agents. Due to the lack of user-centered research, we aimed to discover, through focus group interviews and a participatory design workshop, the previously unknown challenges these multilingual users encounter when using conversational agents. Our key findings relate to the users’ innate wants toward an advanced conversational agent capable of code-mixing, the challenges encountered due to technological deficiencies or lack of user understanding, and the conversational agent code-mixing user experience, including the users’ perception that the commercialized conversational agent is a native monolingual person and that they are excluded from the primary user group of the agent. We also determined that a conversational agent that is user-friendly for code-mixing multilingual users would provide an enriched experience for these users.

This study helped to clarify proposals for new agent designs that are to be considered by the designer and scholars of conversational agents. First, the agent's code-mixing should consider the purpose of the interaction. Second, the agent should offer a distinctive and consistent persona to provide a seamless user experience. Finally, this study has illustrated the steps that can be taken to design a more social, human-like, and efficient conversational system by considering the distinctive linguistic patterns of code-mixing users.
