CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Faced with public speaking anxiety, one common piece of advice is to picture the audience in a new light, using your mind's eye. With Augmented Reality (AR) face filters, it becomes possible to literally change how one sees oneself or others. In this paper, we explore privately applied AR filters during online public speaking. Private means that these effects are only visible to the speaker. To investigate this possibly controversial concept, we conducted an online survey with 100 respondents to gather a diverse set of initial impressions, possible boundaries, and guidelines. Following this, we built a prototype of a private AR web-based video-calling application, and pilot-tested it with 16 participants to gain more in-depth insights. Based on our results, we outline key user perspectives and opportunities for the private application of AR face filters during online public speaking and discuss them in the context of previous literature on this topic.

1 INTRODUCTION

Strong communication skills are essential in many facets of one's life, yet many people face a great deal of anxiety when required to speak formally or in public. In fact, the fear of public speaking (FOPS) is considered to be the most common lifetime social fear [ 48 ]. There are many strategies to combat this fear. Franklin D. Roosevelt is said to have pretended that the audience has holes in their socks, while Winston Churchill is attributed with picturing each member of the audience naked [ 23 , 50 ] – a piece of advice referenced in pop culture dozens of times [ 1 ]. While this particular advice might be considered questionable, the underlying idea is to mitigate nervousness by making the public speaking situation less high-stakes, as well as more casual and similar to an "everyday conversation" [ 6 , 31 ].

In a time when video calls are widely used to deliver speeches and presentations, Augmented Reality filters (AR filters) can provide a means to literally help people picture something new by changing what they see, rather than requiring them to rely on their mind's eye. Superimposing virtual elements on camera streams has become a popular feature in video-calling (e.g. Zoom, Microsoft Teams) as well as social media applications (e.g. Snapchat, Instagram) [ 19 ]. As such, real-time AR filters could be used to lighten up the situation and picture the audience as less threatening [ 25 ].

While the standard behavior in AR apps is to allow everyone on a call or livestream to see the applied filter effect, we propose and explore the novel concept of privately applying AR filters aimed at reducing public speaking anxiety in online video-calling contexts. To do this, we conducted a survey with 100 respondents to understand a general public perception and social acceptability of this concept, and to explore the boundaries of what types of effects people might be open to. In a second phase of our investigation, we designed and implemented an experimental video-calling application that enables people to apply filters privately, either on oneself or on other person(s). We recruited 16 people with a fear of public speaking to deliver a short speech in the app, and collected their thoughts and feedback through surveys and follow-up interviews. From this, we derived a deeper understanding of the factors that can influence the use of private AR filters in this context.

The core contributions of this work are (1) an overview of a general public's perception of the acceptability of different filter types applied on-self or on-others, as well as possible motivations and ethical concerns around its use; (2) early evidence that AR face filters may be helpful for combating public-speaking anxiety from a pilot study with 16 people with a fear of public speaking who tested the feature in a simulated online speaking scenario; (3) an outline of different strategies that we observed people taking with AR face filters to manipulate their perception of the audience, themselves, or the situation, and modulate their public-speaking anxiety.

2 Concept & Terminology

We begin by outlining the current usage paradigm for AR filters in online video calls and describing how it is distinct from the concept that is explored in this paper.

At present, many popular video conferencing applications support the use of AR-based effects, commonly referred to as AR Filters . These apply image manipulations on a live video source based on head/face position tracking. One popular use of AR is to change the appearance of one's background. Virtual backgrounds are a type of AR filter, since body tracking is required to segment the background from the foreground. Another subset of AR filters are AR Face Filters , which can change the appearance of oneself to various degrees. These effects can range from adding simple accessories on a person, to completely transforming their appearance to look like another character.

In contrast to the current state-of-the-art for video conferencing, where people can only apply AR Filters to themselves in a way that's viewable by all call attendees (i.e. publicly viewable), we explore the novel concept of Private AR Filters for this context. With this term, we refer to manipulations that are only visible for the person who is applying it, and not for anyone else on the call. Furthermore, we explore how such filters could either be applied on the video stream of oneself or on others . Since video calls have become a dominant medium for delivering talks, and our focus is on addressing the fear of public speaking, we confine our use of the term “Private AR Filters” to refer to video-conferencing contexts. We do not use this term in the context of other private AR experiences (e.g. in virtual-try on apps), or AR-glasses based experiences (which are still not commonly used for in-person public speaking situations).

3 RELATED WORK

Since we aim to investigate the possible impact of being able to privately apply filters to oneself (to change one's self-perception), and to others (in order to change a person's perception of their audience) in a public speaking situation, we outline prior works on the concept of technology-mediated perceptual illusions, as well as a body of prior work on systems designed to improve one's communication abilities.

3.1 Virtual Perceptual Experiences

Beyond entertainment, one prominent stream of research in AR/VR explores their serious applications in learning, training, and self-enhancement across several domains, including public speaking.

Serious VR applications [ 12 ] have been developed for a variety of educational and training purposes in areas such as culture [ 12 ], leadership, [ 9 ], assembly tasks [ 40 ], emergency response [ 34 ], and public speaking [ 13 ]. To address anxiety and fear, VR has been leveraged to facilitate exposure therapy [ 7 , 29 , 45 , 47 , 55 , 59 ], with various interventions having been shown to help mitigate public-speaking anxiety [ 39 , 41 ]. Interestingly, VR has also been shown to positively impact people's abilities through body-swap illusions, wherein people virtually embody different characters. This has yielded a host of notable positive effects including boosting problem-solving capabilities [ 4 ], mitigating stereotype threat [ 44 ], magnifying physical expressivity [ 21 ] and improving creativity [ 16 ]. As a foundation to the aforementioned embodiment works in VR, Turkle [ 58 ] observed how virtual avatars provided people with various opportunities to explore, construct, and reconstruct their identities, while Yee et al. [ 62 ] discovered that people's behaviors can be shaped by their virtual avatars’ appearances (a phenomenon now referred to as the Proteus Effect).

AR experiences can also be used to shape people's knowledge, emotions and behaviors, and have been used for various serious applications such as education [ 46 ], knowledge transfer [ 11 ], and exposure therapy [ 64 ]. Yoshida et al. [ 63 ] found that manipulating a person's facial expressions on-screen using image deformation techniques can influence them to feel happier or sadder. In a video-conferencing system called Smart Face, Nakzato et al. [ 33 ] explored facial expression manipulation and face-blending between the callers, and discovered that people made to look smiley brainstormed more ideas than in the other conditions. Leong et al. [ 24 ] observed that the use of AR face filters to look child- or inventor-like may help people think more creatively. According to a study conducted by Park et al. [ 42 ], visual anonymity from using an avatar coupled with self-affirmation may help boost active participation in discussions. However, there is an ongoing discussion on the effects of AR face filters in video-calling and social media. The use of beauty AR filters has been linked to negative body-image and an increased demand for real-world cosmetic procedures and surgeries [ 52 ]. Yet, despite these side-effects, they remain a popular feature in social media and are predicted to endure over time [ 52 ]. According to Javornik et al. [ 19 ], there are many possible motivations for people to use AR filters (e.g., ideal and transformed self-presentation, affiliation, enjoyment) and that whether or not filter usage has a positive or negative impact on well-being depends on the person's underlying motivation for using it.

In contrast to prior works, we investigate the idea to empower people to virtually change their visual perception of their audience or themselves, on their own and as they would like, to modulate their own public speaking anxiety. We take a primarily qualitative approach to explore combating public-speaking anxiety through private AR filter usage, collecting perspectives through a survey and experience-based interviews with people using a custom prototype.

3.2 Improving Communication Skills

Researchers have taken numerous approaches besides using AR/VR for virtual perceptual illusions to help people with public speaking. One body of work has examined the use of wearables to give public speakers real-time feedback on their verbal and non-verbal delivery. Rhema by Tanveer et al. [ 56 ] provided feedback on speech volume and speed in the form of visual notifications on smart-glasses. Similarly, Logue by Damian et al. [ 10 ] was a smart-glasses interface that gave live feedback on the person's speech rate, energy, and body language (openness).

Other researchers have created systems to assist people in their preparation and practice for speaking situations. Hoque et al. created MACH [ 18 ], an automated conversation coach, wherein a user can practice for an interview while speaking with a virtual agent, and receive summarized feedback on their facial expressions, prosody and speaking rate. VoiceCoach, introduced by Wang et al. [ 61 ] was a visual system that could suggest a way to modulate one's voice for a given sentence, based on a database of training examples from TED talks.

Researchers have also experimented with introducing supportive virtual agents to reduce the level of anxiety a speaker has in public speaking scenarios. Wang et al. [ 60 ] explored the feasibility for smart speakers such as Amazon's Alexa to bring people through a cognitive restructuring exercise prior to a virtual public speaking task. They found that making the conversational agent (CA) more sociable increased interpersonal closeness and helped to reduce social anxiety. Trinh et al. [ 57 ] explored how introducing a virtual agent as a co-presenter for scientific presentations can help to reduce public speaking anxiety. Murali et al. [ 32 ] experimented with introducing a virtual emotional support agent situated in the crowd to reduce public speaking anxiety. They found that their approach significantly reduced public speaking anxiety based on physiological and self-report measures.

Positioning our work with respect to prior systems, we aim to provide a real-time intervention that focuses on reducing people's anxiety to improve their communication abilities, rather than give them direct metric-based feedback on their speech qualities. We anticipate that although it can be controversial due to concerns around agency and ethical usage, such a feature may help boost a person's self-confidence, reduce their subjective fear of the audience, or add humor to the situation to diffuse feelings of anxiety.

4 INVESTIGATION ONE: SURVEY ON THE PRIVATE USE OF AR FILTERS

As the idea of applying AR face filters privately on oneself as well as on others is a provocative and possibly controversial concept, we sought to distribute a survey to a wide range of people. We aimed to gather diverse perspectives on the current use of filters, as well as capture people's initial perceptions and judgements of the ability to apply filters in private, both on themselves and on others.

4.1 Method

A web survey was created using Qualtrics and distributed via the Prolific platform. We crafted a collection of 36 multiple-choice, Likert scale, and open-ended questions. These questions covered people's demographics, current usage habits of video-calling and AR features, their level of anxiety in social situations and public speaking, as well as their comfort levels, opinions and preferences regarding being able to either privately apply AR filters on themselves or others, or have others do so to them. As the survey was written in English, participants were pre-screened based on whether they reported being fluent in the language. Each person was paid $ 2 for their response.

4.2 Results

4.2.1 Demographics. 100 participants (Female: 48, Male: 52), aged 19 to 74 years old (M=32, SD=11) completed the survey correctly. Originally 101 responses were collected, but one was removed due to a failed attention check. The average survey completion time was 9.75 minutes. Respondents came from multiple countries (i.e., US, Canada, UK, Japan, Portugal, South Africa, Australia, Mexico, Ireland, Korea, Spain, Chile, Sweden and Poland) and self-identified as White (53), Asian (16), Black or African American (12), Hispanic, Latino or Spanish Origin (8), Middle Eastern or North African (3), Multiethnic (8). Participants used video-calling to a range of different degrees: Never (2), Less than once a week (33), A few times each week (35), Almost every day (19), and Multiple times daily (11). Their experience with AR face filters (a definition and examples were provided in the survey) varied, with the majority never using them (54). The rest reported seldom using them (32), using them about half the time (10), or usually using them (4).

4.2.2 Social Anxiety and Public Speaking Anxiety. Participants were asked about their anxiety in social and public speaking situations, both online and in person. A considerable fraction of the respondents reported being anxious in all permutations (see Fig. 1 ).

4.2.3 Response to Advice to “Picture the Audience...”. Respondents were also asked to rate the degree to which they like the advice “ Picture the audience in their underwear ” as a means to reduce nervousness when public speaking (1 = Strongly Dislike, 5 = Strongly Like) to which 33 strongly disliked it, 22 somewhat disliked it, 34 felt neutrally, 9 somewhat liked it, and only 2 strongly liked it. Those who strongly disliked the advice stated that the advice is unprofessional, uncomfortable or inappropriate, can divide attention or be distracting, or that it is unhelpful or ineffective for them. One respondent wrote " I would rather picture how people feel than how they look. For example, if I picture people feeling positive toward me, it helps me calm down. " This echoes the work of Pertaub et al. [ 45 ], who found that the disposition of a virtual audience (neutral, hostile, or friendly) can impact how a speaker feels.

4.2.4 Private Application of Filters. We asked respondents about their opinions regarding the potential use of Private AR Filters (for definitions, see Section 2 ) during online video calls.

Comfort / Acceptance : Respondents rated how comfortable they would feel using Private AR filters in video calls. Most respondents could imagine applying filters on themselves. For applying filters on others, the responses were more divided (see Fig. 2 ).

Motivations : Respondents checked from a list of options reasons they may have for using Private AR Filters (see Fig. 3 ). Respondents associated using filters on others to feel less intimidated, while filters on oneself correlated with feeling less self-conscious.

Filter Types: When asked about the acceptability of different AR filter types (see Fig. 4 ), respondents tended to accept background or cosmetic effects and reject filters that changed more core attributes. Of the more unconventional options, filters that make people less realistic or non-human were fairly well accepted. Race/Ethnicity, Gender, Voice and Age effects were generally disliked, although Age was more acceptable if applied to oneself. Furthermore, the fact that 33 respondents chose None , while 27 chose All highlights the controversial nature of the concept.

Comments and Concerns Some people stated that they could not see any reason to use private AR filters. Those that saw utility in it imagined it could counter boredom, aid in relaxation, or reduce fear of judgement. Conversely, others raised concerns that this feature could distract people from focusing, hinder one's ability to understand others, heighten self-dissatisfaction, lead to embarrassment if a malfunction were to occur and unintentionally expose the fact that they were using it, and most seriously, could be used for bullying or harassment (via the sharing of screenshots or recordings without consent).

4.3 Survey Insights & Discussion

Overall, the survey revealed that the concept is indeed a controversial one. In particular, one third of respondents felt there would be no good reason to privately apply filters on others (see Fig. 3 ). Nevertheless, over half of the respondents felt comfortable with the idea of privately applying filters on oneself, and roughly one third of participants reported feeling comfortable with the concept of either privately applying filters on others or having others privately apply filters on them (see Fig. 2 ). Generally, respondents seemed most comfortable with applying filters on themselves. Additionally, their comfort regarding applying filters on others seemed to reflect their comfort with having others do that to them. Furthermore, roughly half of the respondents reported having anxiety in both in-person and online socializing and public speaking scenarios (See Fig. 1 ). Therefore, although the concept is controversial, the responses indicate that such an intervention may nevertheless be worthy of investigation.

Reflecting the current usage paradigm of AR filters, over half of the participants identified entertainment as a reason to engage with private AR face filters (See Fig. 3 ). Besides entertainment, roughly a third of respondents either indicated a motivation to reduce negative emotions (i.e. anxiety, fear, and self-consciousness), to take the situation less seriously, or to conversely feel happier. Interestingly, roughly one third of participants would be motivated to use the on-self condition to feel less self-conscious, while one third of respondents considered the private use of filters on others as a means to feel less intimidated.

Respondents were somewhat conservative with regards to the types of filters they would consider using (see Fig. 4 ). Besides background effects, most respondents could imagine using filters that offer cosmetic changes to hair, make up, accessories, and clothing . More interestingly, roughly one third of them could also imagine using filters that make a person look less realistic, non-human, or which change facial expressions . Changes to race/ethnicity, gender, age and voice were most off-limits in terms of acceptability. We pay special attention to these preferences in the follow-up investigation.

Finally, participants’ open comments and responses to the prompt “ picture the audience in their underwear ” helped to unveil pertinent areas for careful consideration and concern. Translated into action items, their comments suggest that filters should be appropriate, professional and and respectful. In addition to this, there needs to be safeguards in place to prevent bullying and harassment that could occur from sharing screenshots or recordings. Introducing opportunities for parties to provide their consent may help mitigate this issue. For instance, a future system could clearly explain the nature of the filters that can be applied, and how their privacy is protected before people sign up for the service. In this way, people who agree to use the system can give advance consent to either using or being subject to this feature. In addition, it is also necessary to consider potentially unintended consequences of such an intervention, such as distracting users, or making them feel hyper-self-aware and hence more intimidated in social situations.

5 INVESTIGATION TWO: EXPERIENCE-BASED INTERVIEWS

The survey provided us with a broad set of opinions and insights on the idea of private filter usage. It also showed that while many users are skeptical of the concept, there is a large number of users who could imagine using AR filters privately on others or themselves.

Nevertheless, the space of applications for private AR filters in social contexts is multifaceted and complex. Firstly, people come into these situations with a different set of experiences and reasons for being nervous when speaking in public. Secondly, people have different practices and coping strategies for public speaking, which could alter their perception and openness to a technological intervention as the one we are exploring. Thirdly, there are thousands of types of AR filters available that can be designed and categorized in many different ways (e.g., level of realism, subject matter, visibility, animation, etc.).

Due to this complex combination of factors, we felt it was necessary to engage in direct conversations with potential users as part of a deeper investigation into the subject. Since it can be particularly difficult for users to imagine an experience [ 37 ], we decided to develop a prototype of a private AR video calling application for people to use, prior to asking them interview questions [ 22 ]. By giving users hands-on experience with such a feature, we aimed to elicit more in-depth and authentic insights into the needs, concerns, attitudes, and preferences people have with regards to using private AR filters in the context of online public speaking.

5.1 Private AR Video Calling App

We designed and implemented Masquerade , a web-based video conferencing platform that enables users to gather in a virtual meeting room and privately apply AR filters on themselves or on others. Since this web app was developed to support a subsequent investigation, it supported distinct user and administrator roles.

5.1.1 User Interface. The app comprised a set of standard video conferencing application features. To begin, the researcher could log in as an administrator to create a virtual meeting room with a unique ID code. Subjects could log in to the same virtual meeting room by entering the same code. On the main screen, each user was presented with drop-down menus to select their camera and microphone devices. A video grid displayed everyone on the call. The user's own video stream was positioned in the top-left most corner, followed by the video streams of other users in the room.

Special controls were provided to administrators to (1) toggle the app between an on-self and on-others mode, and to (2) add or remove virtual audience members, as detailed in section 5.1.3 . Users, on the other hand, could select an AR filter effect from several options in a filter menu (See Fig. 5 , left). If the app was in on-self mode, their AR filter selection would be privately applied to themselves. If the app was in on-others mode, the filter would be privately applied to people other than themselves on the call. The set of available filters was determined based on the prior online survey responses.

5.1.2 Filter Categories & Options. A limited set of 14 AR filters were included in the app, based on the former survey responses. This enabled us to provide a consistent experience to all participants. It also ensured that each participant would have an overview of all the options, such that their choice of filter was not due to the chance of discovery. Additionally, this would enable us to collect multiple opinions on the same set of AR filters, which could help us distill an understanding of what aspects of filters may contribute to their utility to reduce anxiety in public speaking.

Various factors were considered to determine the set of available filters in addition to their overall acceptability (see Fig. 4 ). We excluded the use of voice manipulation since the voice extends to another medium (i.e. sound), as well as the option of doodling on the face since it requires additional interaction steps (e.g. drawing). Despite background filters being widely accepted, we also excluded this option because we presumed that participants may default to using this more conventional option, making it difficult to elicit new knowledge about opportunities for changing the appearance of others directly with AR face filters.

We organized the final set of filters into the categories: Accessories, Non-Human, Emotion, Face, and Video filters (see Fig. 5 , right). Accessories included glasses, a flower crown, a viking hat, and wigs (to also account for hair options). Non-human included a dog filter with ears and a snout, and full-face koala and lion masks. Emotion included a particle effect of floating pixelated hearts, and an effect that could morph a person's face to make it look as those their mouth is curled into a smile. Face included a face morph that resembled Sylvester Stallone, and another that applied makeup. Video filters contained an effect that made people look like they're the subject in an illustrated poster.

5.1.3 Virtual Audience. In order to simulate the experience of giving a speech online for the experiment, the app enabled the addition or removal of virtual audience members. Each audience member was represented with a looping pre-recorded video of a real person (roughly 20-30 seconds in length). The person in each video was asked to look attentive, yet maintain a neutral facial expression. We chose this approach as it allowed us to create a diverse audience, and ensured that the experience of the audience would be consistent across test subjects. It also enforced that positive feelings felt by the participant could not be attributed to positive expressions or feedback from the audience. While they were not responsive, this parallels other prior works, which have leveraged the use of a fake audience in VR [ 60 ] to simulate the experience of giving a speech.

5.1.4 Technical Implementation. The application was based on NodeJS, using PeerJS 1 for exchanging video and audio feeds between clients. The AR filter features were integrated using the DeepAR SDK. 2 This stack was chosen to enable its use on any web-enabled device. Finally, Masquerade was deployed using Heroku 3 to make it publicly accessible. For the purposes of data collection, separate surveys were created in Qualtrics.

5.2 Method

Recruitment invitations were distributed via email lists and social platforms used at a university in the United States. To find people in our targeted user group, we pre-screened participants with the question: “ I fear public speaking ” (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree). Only those who rated 3 and above were recruited.

In sessions, participants joined the researcher in an online Zoom call from a personal computer. First, they were asked to complete a demographics questionnaire spanning their level of experience with AR filters, gender, age, occupation and ethnicity . They were also asked to answer a modified set of 6 questions derived from the PRCA public speaking subscale [ 60 ] as an indication of their trait anxiety for public speaking .

They were then given the link to the web application and were asked to join the researcher in a virtual meeting room. Once inside, the researcher toggled the mode of the app according to their randomly assigned condition (i.e. on-self or on-others ), and asked the participant to try out each of the available filter options. After participants confirmed that they could see the applied filter changing using their controls, the researcher showed the participants via Zoom screen sharing that their filter selection was private to them. Following this, participants were asked to give a 3-minute speech on the assigned topic: “Should there be an age limit on social media? Why or why not?” . They were given up to 5 minutes to prepare, but could opt out of the full time if they did not need it. They were also told that a virtual audience would join, and that they should try their best to use the full allotted time. After preparing, they were asked to rate their state level of anxiety before their speech based on a Subjective Units of Distress (SUDS) scale ranging from 0 to 100, as commonly done in prior works [ 35 , 49 , 60 ].

The researcher then added 5 virtual audience members to the room. Participants were asked to confirm that they could see the audience, that their selected filter was correctly applied, and that the app was fully visible on their screen. The researcher then hid their own camera, and started the timer, signalling to the participant to begin their speech. When complete, the researcher thanked the participants and gave them an exit survey. The survey asked them to rate the anxiety they had felt during their speech (based on SUDS) as well as their impressions of the filters and its effects. They were also asked to rank the filter options that were given. Lastly, the researcher engaged in a direct conversation with the participants in a semi-structured interview (approx. 25 min.) The participants were encouraged to reflect and draw upon their experience using the app in the speaking task to formulate their responses.

The overall intention for the semi-structured interview was to develop an understanding of what goals people may have when applying filters, and how different aspects of filters could impact their feelings of anxiety. Pre-prepared questions were used as a loose structure for the interview process covering the following themes: goals, reasoning for filter selection, emotions, comfort and discomfort, use cases and scenarios, relationships to others, attention, focus and distraction, ethics and social acceptability . Follow-up questions were asked to clarify and elicit more detailed explanations of ideas and concepts that interviewees raised. Since an impromptu speaking-task is highly demanding on individuals (particularly for those that self-identify as fearful of public speaking), we decided to have each person only deliver one speech using the app, in either the on-self or on-others condition. However, after the exercise, participants were shown the alternative condition so that a discussion could be had about the concept from both perspectives. Each session lasted approximately 45 minutes, and each participant was compensated with a $ 15 Amazon gift card as a thank-you for their involvement.

5.3 Results

5.3.1 Demographics. Overall, 16 people were recruited for this experience-based interviewing. Their ages ranged from 20 to 51 years (M=28.56, SD=8.15). 9 identified as female, 6 identified as male, and 1 non-binary/third gender. 7 people self-identified as Asian, 4 as White or Caucasian, 3 as Hispanic, Latino or Spanish / White or Caucasian, 1 as Hispanic, Latino or Spanish Origin, and 1 person chose not to disclose. All were affiliated with the local university (14 students, a research scientist, and a visiting professor). On average, the selected people had a trait anxiety level of 3.73 (SD = 0.6), which indicates that the recruited group of participants were indeed representative of people who feel anxious about online public speaking.

5.3.2 State Anxiety: Manipulation Check. Participants were asked to rate the level of distress, fear or anxiety they felt before and while they gave their speech based on the SUDS scale. Prior to speaking, participants on average had a moderate level of state anxiety (M = 48.8, SD = 20.2). The state anxiety levels between the conditions were comparable ( On-Others : M = 47.6, SD = 25.1, On-Self : M = 50.0, SD = 15.5). Participants also felt on average moderately anxious during their speeches (M = 49.5, SD = 25.9). State anxiety for those in the on-others condition (M = 46.5, SD = 19.3) were within a similar range as those in the on-self condition (M = 64.5, SD = 18.1). This suggests that the experience generally induced a moderate level of anxiety in the participants. There is also a slight indication that the on-others configuration may map to less anxiety than the on-self configuration. Due to the subjective nature of the scale and the fact that each person only experienced one condition, we do not conduct a statistical comparison.

5.3.3 Filter Popularity & Rankings. Based on actual usage in the scenario, the most popular filters in the on-others condition were the Non-Human options. 4 out of 8 people chose the Dalmatian , 3 chose the Lion , and 1 person chose the Poster . In the on-self condition, the choices were diverse, without apparent trends. 2 people chose Stallone , 2 chose the Viking Hat , and the rest were spread evenly across Dalmatian, Koala, Round Glasses, and the Smile . Each participant only used one filter during their speech. While they were not explicitly instructed to stick with one filter, and the filter menu was always accessible, no one changed their filter mid-session.

We also asked participants to report their top three favorite and least favorite filters. By a tally, the top three most favorite filters across both conditions, were the animal-based Non-Human options (Dalmation: 8, Koala: 7, Lion: 6). Stallone (5) came in fourth, and the Smile, Poster, and Flower Crown filters were tied for fifth with 4 votes each. Face filters were the least favorite. The Make Up and Stallone filters tied for first with 7 votes each, followed by the Koala (5). The Lion, Poster, and Smile were tied with 4 votes each, and the Flower Crown, and the Floating Pixelated hearts with 3 votes each. Aviators, Make Up and Pink Wig got 2 votes each. This suggests that people were particularly divided over the Stallone, Koala and Lion filters, the Poster and the Smile filters.

5.3.4 Filter Impressions & Impacts.

As part of the exercise, we asked participants to rate the extent to which they found the filter they had selected to be Funny, Attractive, Friendly, Distracting , and Pleasant via 5-point Likert items (1 = Not at All, 5 = Extremely). Generally, participants perceived the filters they chose to put on-others to be very friendly and somewhat pleasant. This aligns with prior work, that suggests that having a friendly audience can be helpful in a stressful public speaking situation [ 32 ]. In the on-self condition, participants tended to perceive their chosen filter to be funny. In both conditions, people did not seem to prioritize attractiveness, and on average found the private application of filters to only be a little distracting (See Fig. 6 , left). Interestingly, the whiskers for the on-self condition across all attributes tends to be longer than for the on-others condition, indicating less consensus on what attributes are a focus in this configuration.

Participants were also asked to rate how the ability to apply the AR filter made them feel, and how it was like to use it via 5-point Likert items (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree) (see Fig. 6 , right). In the on-others condition, participants tended to somewhat agree that its usage made them feel less anxious overall , less worried about how the audience would think of them , and more confident . They also generally found the feature to be somewhat pleasant and very fun to use. The on-self condition had a lesser impact on their anxiety , fear of the audience and confidence . In fact, it seemed that on average, people felt less confident with the filter applied to them. They also did not find the feature to be particularly pleasant to use, although they agreed it was somewhat fun.

5.3.5 Opportunities for Private AR Filters for Public Speaking. Based on the interview data, we sectioned the interviews into quotes and grouped them to arrive at different themes following an affinity diagramming process. We outline these themes below.

Making the Audience More Approachable (On-Others) . A significant fraction of the participants were drawn to using the Lion or Dalmatian filters because they were “cute” (P1, P3, P5, P7) or “friendly” (P1, P9). Particularly dogs were ascribed special qualities: “dogs are really friendly and happy, so if I put it on my audience, I feel like they seemed more friendly and more welcoming, [and] I didn't feel like they were judging me as much” (P9). Similarly, P5 felt: “[dogs] don't really do anything wrong [...], they're moral - what better audience to have, you know?” This echoes recent findings that people prefer virtual dogs over humans as social support figures [ 38 ].

P8 considered the Floating Pixelated Hearts effect to be reassuring, similar to receiving likes on Instagram and other forms of social media “it kind of felt like - oh, you're doing well.” While it was not an option in the prototype, P13 imagined that a private filter to make someone who is constantly disapproving look like they are nodding would be a source of helpful encouragement.

Distancing from the Audience (On-Others) . Many participants appreciated obscuring others in order to distance themselves from their audience and alleviate stress caused by pressure and judgement. Having selected the Lion filter, P3 stated feeling less pressure by masking people's reactions: “it makes it feel like I'm not really even giving [the speech] to an audience,” while P5 felt “[the speech] was better than if [the filter] wasn't there for sure, because [...] it does put a little bit of distance between you and your audience.” While P1 initially chose the Lion, he also liked the idea of the Poster effect, saying “[it] makes me relaxed because I don't see the real person, I see a 2D replication like him...”

Although P2, P4 and P12 experienced the on-self condition, they could also see value in distancing. In fact, P2 naturally wished she could “..choose some masks on the virtual audience's faces... so I don't have to see their faces when I'm speaking.” P4 considered changing the audience into non-human “plants or flowers” to make her less nervous. For P12, he saw utility in transforming high-ranking people into “unrecognizable characters” to minimize power dynamics and thereby reduce one's nervousness.

Self-Disguise (On-Self) . When putting filters on oneself, some participants felt relief in being “ disguised ” (P2) or “ unrecognizable ” (P18). P2 chose the Dalmatian filter, and although she knew it was applied privately, she stated “I presume that people may see me as that, so it also releases me to care more about the other people [...] I'm only a cute dog.” P18 liked the Stallone filter for being clearly distinct from himself - making it useful as a disguise, and would have even preferred for it to be of someone completely unknown, rather than a celebrity.

Looking the Part & Improving Appearances (On-Self) . Some people were inclined to use AR face filters to improve their own appearance and disposition. P4 chose the Round Glasses filter because she felt they looked professional and would empower her to be more persuasive. P12 refrained from picking filters that could obscure his eyes, noting that he perceives wearing sunglasses during in-person speeches to be rude. Several participants considered the Smile filter, citing dissatisfaction with their own smile (P1) or the feeling that they should smile more for presentations (P2, P8). P16 had wished there were more subtle hair and make up effects than the ones given to improve her appearance, to give her a confidence boost. Acknowledging the difference between public and private filters, P2 liked that private filters could help her maintain a professional appearance in the event of recordings.

Minimizing Distraction (On-Self & On-Others) . Whether the filter would be distracting was also a factor in people's choices. P12 and P16 both opted for using the Viking Helmet, as a subtle and non-distracting choice on themselves. P12 expressed that the animated Floating Pixelated Hearts, in contrast, was too distracting. P15 found that the Poster was useful to mitigate distractions from the audience, by visually blocking out their backgrounds and giving them a more uniform appearance.

Humor & Taking the Situation Less Seriously (On-Self & On-Others) . Some participants also chose their filter based on whether it was funny or humorous. P7, P11, and P13 opted for the Dalmatian filter because they found it humorous. The Stallone filter was often considered to be “ugly,” but that drew participants such as P6 to select it, hoping it would “make me feel more goofy and [...] not overly serious.”

5.3.6 Strategies for Public Speaking. People's practices and desires for public speaking often shaped their perspectives on using private AR face filters.

Ability to Read the Audience . Many people valued being able to read their audience's expressions (P7, P8) or understand if the audience agrees or disagrees with their statements (P13). P7 disliked that the pink wig “covered a lot of the face” and instead appreciated that the Dalmatian filter “didn't obscure facial expressions.”

Ability to See Oneself . People in the on-self condition reflected on the use of self-monitoring in online calls. With the Stallone filter, P6 complained that it made it “difficult to control [their] own facial expressions” and feel “more detached from [themself]...” due to it obscuring their face from their own view. Participants (P8, P9, P16) were also concerned with being able to self-monitor their appearance. Creatively, P8 pondered the possibility for a system to normally not show her self-view, but notify her and make it visible if she ought to inspect something. Conversely some challenged the use of self-view. P12 stated he would sometimes choose to turn off his self-view: “there's no value in me looking at myself talk.” P14 chose the Koala filter as a means to divert self-focus: “I thought it would make me think less about myself in the speech, and I could just treat it like a verbal exercise when I applied the filter.”

Sources of Anxiety & Coping Strategies . People mentioned different sources of anxiety during online public speaking. P8 referred to a lack of feedback when speaking during webinars: “it's especially stressful, because you're just talking to the void. And sometimes, you're like - is my camera on? - am I even talking to anyone?” P18 was exceptionally stressed by the scenario (needing to stop his speech early) and explained that simply the idea of having people listening to him was enough to make him highly anxious.

Interestingly, some people cope with their anxiety by looking away or minimizing what they can see (P1, P5, P18). P1 mentioned that for online talks, he likes to look directly at his camera rather than the people on the screen. P18 stated he normally looks at his presentation slides on his computer, instead of his audience. While P5 mentioned that she likes to make “windows super tiny... to like, avoid the audience...” she wondered, “maybe with the filter, I'd be more incentivized to look at the screen...” These kinds of coping strategies might diminish the impact of on-screen interventions. However, AR filters could potentially draw people's eyes back to looking at the screen and audience.

5.3.7 System Design Factors. Besides the factors that are directly related to the task of public speaking, independent technical, personal, and social factors must also be considered.

Alignment with Personal Identity and Interests . People's sense of identity, and its reflection in the filter options also played a role in their choice. P3 selected the lion filter stating: “I really like animals in general... I'm a biology major.” Conversely, P7 pointed to the wigs as something he couldn't understand: “... I guess if you had more of an affinity for comics or anime [...], I just didn't relate to it at all.” P6 self identified as non-binary or third-gender, and did not feel drawn to any filter, because they felt all of them did not represent or match their sense of self and identity. They would have preferred that people can customize their filters, as people do in games or virtual reality by customizing their avatars. Furthermore, P6 mentioned that people who may be transitioning genders but are not ready to come out would most probably appreciate the private, on-self filter ability.

Uncanniness . On occasion, participants mentioned that the visual qualities of the filters affected their decisions. P9 found the Lion and Koala were “too realistic” and therefore weird. Similarly, P1, P3, P5, P8, and P13 turned away from the Smile filter due to its uncanniness, and the sense that it would never be real (P1, P3) or natural (P3) enough. However, this was not always a disqualifying factor, with P14 embracing the Koala filter and the humor that came with its uncanniness.

Information, Consent & Social Acceptability . Generally, people had very little concern about needing to know about a person's use of filters in a private sense, if the person was their friend (P4, P13), but may be more concerned with strangers (P4). Also, many people expressed empathy (P5, P9, P15), saying they wouldn't mind if someone used private AR filters on them if they found it helpful. Some cared about what filters would be available, but were accepting if the filters are “silly” (P3) or aren't “weird or malicious” (P9). P3 also recognized equality and reciprocity as contributing to her acceptance: “I would definitely be okay with it, because I know that it would be nice for me to use it on other people, so I'd be okay that they'd be doing it on me.” Both P15 and P16 raised the concern of people using private filters to make others look ridiculous. However, while P15 didn't care since anyone can do anything to a picture anyways, P16 strongly believed in consent: “’I think we should always ask for, you know, their opinion. They should always approve. I think the audience should be informed always.”

5.3.8 Interview Insights: Variation in Strategies Based on Anxiety Levels. This exploratory user research via prototype testing was designed to elicit central themes that must be considered in the use of private AR filters, as well as to identify core opportunities. The different themes derived from the interviews show that even in a group of people who share anxiety about public speaking, there are various requirements due to different coping strategies for a private filter intervention. Despite their uneasiness with the situation, some people value to stay in control by being able to read the audience and check on their own self image. Hence, these people might see private AR filters as a tool to ease their anxieties and make them more comfortable, but do not want filters to hinder their abilities to observe the situation. Others, seem only able to cope with the situation of public speaking by straight-out ignoring the audience, for instance by minimizing their videos. For this group, private filters on others could be an option to let the audience back on their screen and gradually enable a bit of interaction. Filters on oneself can provide an option for self-disguise, leveraging a sense of anonymity or being somebody else to counter personal anxiety. In addition, we also observed a group with other personal priorities, ranging from the search for humorous to comforting options that align with the personal identity, which requires more subtle, nuanced, and customizable filter options. Interestingly, we noticed that the strategies people employ with the use of AR face filters may scale with their level of anxiety. Those who indicated a preference for blocking out the audience (P1, P2, P5, P14, P18) had higher levels of pre-speech state anxiety (SUDS) on average (M = 67.4, SD = 13.9) compared to those who preferred vigilance (P7, P8, P9, P12, P13) (M = 41.1, SD = 15.0). Perhaps, as people learn to better manage their anxiety, the intrusiveness or intensity of the effects they choose to use would gradually subside. Given the interview situation with a researcher, positive reviews from participants may need to be taken with a grain of salt. Nevertheless, there was overall less push back to the idea than expected after trying the prototype. Overall, it was surprising to see such a wide variety of different motives and needs. Identifying them is crucial to inform future applications as well as study designs to further investigate the capabilities of private AR filters to tackle public speaking anxiety.

6 DISCUSSION

Popular culture suggests that imagining your audience as looking different can help reduce public speaking anxiety. However, to our knowledge, this has never been formally researched. In this paper, we looked at the possible utility of AR for this purpose. In a nutshell, we discovered that privately applying AR Face filters on oneself and others during video conferencing can be a helpful intervention for people with a fear of public speaking (FOPS). From our pilot-study, we learned that there is not just one but a variety of strategies people use when employing AR Face filters to mitigate public speaking anxiety. We learned that how accepting and comfortable people are with Private AR Filters on others, depends on what filter types are enabled, as well as how the aspects of respect, agency, and consent are handled in the system. In this section, we elaborate on the results of the survey and pilot-study, and discuss the contribution that our findings make beyond previous research in the area of serious AR applications and managing public speaking anxiety.

Prior technological interventions to combat fear and anxiety have focused on creating training experiences based on exposure therapy [ 7 , 29 , 45 , 47 , 55 , 59 , 64 ], sometimes in combination with cognitive behavioral therapy [ 2 , 29 ]. Others introduced a supportive virtual agent as a coach [ 60 ], co-presenter [ 57 ], or audience member [ 32 ]. Our work introduces a new strategy to the suite of existing tech-based strategies to combat public-speaking anxiety using private AR face filters to enable speakers to modify their perception of the given situation in real-time. This allows for an intervention that is gradational and personalizable. More deeply, our research also contributes insights on specific strategies people may take to using AR face filters to tackle their fear of public speaking. These can be grouped into three main categories: audience, self, and situation .

Firstly, a speaker may use AR to alter the perceived “approachability” of the audience . Prior works on virtual agents for social support provide evidence that making the audience appear friendlier [ 32 , 45 ] can help to reduce stress. Our findings reinforce this concept, but also unveil that others may instead prefer to use AR filters to create a virtual shield that distances them from their audience in order to reduce stress. This adds nuance to earlier findings that an inability to perceive an audience's non-verbal cues induces anxiety and stress in speakers [ 5 , 26 ], and highlights that highly anxious individuals, in contrast to a more generalized population, may instead favor occlusion. Our work suggests that a speaker's desire for seeing the audience clearly may be modulated by the speaker's level of anxiety. Participants with very high anxiety tended to not want to see the audience, while those with less severe anxiety felt that not seeing their audience clearly actually increased their stress.

Secondly, with regards to the self , prior works have shown that AR beauty filters (while having their downsides [ 52 ]) can boost people's self-esteem [ 20 ]. Investigations into the use of attractive virtual avatars [ 30 ] corroborate this idea. While we found that some people may wish to enhance their appearance to minimize anxiety during online public speaking, an opposite strategy to disguise or render oneself unrecognizable was taken by highly anxious people. This echoes Nitschinsk et al.’s [ 36 ] findings that people who are socially anxious prefer anonymity in online spaces. Our results also agree with the findings from Aymerich-Franch et al. [ 3 ], that socially anxious individuals prefer embodying dissimilar avatars in VR for public speaking tasks. As such, people with severe anxiety who are given the opportunity to privately change their looks with AR face filters would likely favor obscuration over enhancement, but this can shift as their anxiety eases.

Feelings of anxiety during online public speaking may also be modulated by a person's needs for authentic self-expression and the uncanniness of the AR filter effects. Many people value the ability to express themselves authentically [ 19 , 20 ], which was a point stressed by one of our interviewees. Providing a sufficient number of filter options and customization possibilities would enable people to maintain a consistent self-concept, which has been known to bolster people's sense of confidence and control as part of self-verification theory [ 53 , 54 ]. It is a known issue that people can find AR face filter effects uncanny, and that people are particularly sensitive to distortions made to their own faces [ 15 ]. Our findings suggest that even if the private AR face filter effects are unrealistic and cartoonish (such as the dog and poster filters) they can still be helpful for people with FOPS to combat anxiety, and are also better with reducing uncanny valley effects [ 51 ].

Thirdly, we found that AR can be used to modify one's perception of the situation . Some subjects emphasized a preference to minimize distractions, fitting into the concept of diminished reality [ 8 , 27 ], to reduce feelings of overwhelm or improve focus. Others sought to make the situation more humorous. This aligns with some prior work that suggests that humor can be helpful to reduce stress and state anxiety [ 14 , 28 , 65 ]. This highlights that even within a subpopulation of anxious speakers, people can have vastly different personal preferences for how to apply private AR face filters, which should be considered in the design of future systems and studies.

7 ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS

Overall, ethical considerations regarding respect, transparency, and consent should be taken into account when using private AR filters. Heimo et al. [ 17 ] cautioned that AR filters could be leveraged for bullying and harassment online, through the non-consensual sharing of images or videos created using disrespectful effects. These concerns were similarly raised over the course of our investigation. Given the sensitivity around this topic, designers of private AR applications should strive to minimize the potential for harm.

We would recommend video-calling platforms to be transparent about whether or not they enable private AR filters, since it is a non-standard feature . If they do allow for this, they should disclose what AR filter effects are available for use. We would also recommend for people to be asked to acknowledge and consent to these terms prior to engaging with the platform. This is because we learned that for some people, the prospect of having an AR filter applied to them by someone else, even privately, is something they would want to be able to give their consent to first. We recommend asking for consent overall rather than on a per-filter-use basis to alleviate fear of judgement for speakers in the moment. For finer-grained control, platforms could consider giving users the choice to opt in or out of filter categories or specific filters within their user preferences, and session hosts could create invitations with specific requests for which filters they would like to allow for the session, as part of a negotiation between users.

Providers should make efforts to limit what filter types are available by excluding those with an explicit intent to harm (e.g. those containing derogatory images and text), similar to how platforms for AI-generated content restrict content creation [ 43 ]. However, in many cases, making such judgements is not straightforward. Similar to social media platforms, it may be necessary for platforms with this feature to have an internal review board as well as mechanisms for users to be a part of an active and ever-evolving discourse. For instance, users should be able to flag concerns with existing filters as well as make suggestions for what new filters to introduce.

Furthermore, technical efforts should be made to block the non-consensual capturing or sharing of screenshots or recordings on the platform. While there will always be the potential for misuse, we believe that the risks can be managed to help nurture a professional and respectful online environment that can empower people to manage their fear of public speaking and communicate their ideas. Given this, the concept of applying private AR filters can make the life of people with fear of public speaking a little less stressful.

8 Limitations & Future Work

The survey and the experience-based interviews represent an initial exploration of the applicability of private AR face filters in the context of online public speaking. Building upon this work, structured lab experiments could be conducted to quantify its impact on public speaking anxiety levels. While we gathered information in the form of self-reports and reflections, future work could employ the use of physiological readings such as heart rate, heart-rate variability, or electrodermal activity to measure stress and anxiety. Furthermore, while we exposed people to an impromptu speaking task, this type of intervention could be studied longitudinally, such as over repeated practice sessions. As we discovered that people with greater anxiety preferred more intrusive effects, it would be interesting to explore interventions that gradually fade away as people's public speaking abilities improve. Private AR face filters may also be applicable to other situations. For instance, it may be useful to privately mitigate Zoom fatigue, assist with skill-development (e.g., acting and improv), or nurture social empathy. Finally, while we explored screen-based AR, it would be exciting to explore the nuances that could exist when the experience is ported over to work on head-mounted displays (HMDs).

9 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we conducted an early exploration into the potential for AR face filters, applied privately either to oneself or to others, to serve as an aid to mitigate anxiety in online public speaking scenarios. A survey with 100 respondents captured a snapshot of a general public's thoughts and concerns surrounding the private use of AR in this context. It revealed that issues around respect, distraction, privacy, and consent must be considered. It furthermore highlighted which types of filters would be considered acceptable and promising candidates for this use case. In order to better understand the interplay of possible influential factors governing this space and technology, we prototyped a web-based private AR video conferencing application, and asked people with a fear of public speaking to try the app in the context of a speaking task. Based on an analysis of data elicited through surveys and interviews with these individuals, we presented a collection of insights on how they perceived the use of these filters in both on-self and on-others configurations. Our results revealed that people may choose to leverage private AR face filters in different ways to help them cope, depending on the intensity of their public speaking anxiety, and their existing public speaking habits as well as additional personal and social factors. As such, AR may have practical applications in this context and is a promising avenue for further investigation.
