CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Play-to-Earn (P2E) crypto-games recently emerged as a gig opportunity despite the absence of regulations. As these platforms continue to grow, there is a need to understand the interactions involved to protect vulnerable stakeholders. This paper describes how an unintended social dynamic became a strategy guiding players to navigate an unregulated space. First, we inquired through surveys ( N = 69) and interviews ( N = 9) to understand stakeholder motivations and practices in this space. Second, we analyzed data and then conceptualized eight themes (e.g., Management, Social, Gaming). Then, we uncovered four types of relationships (e.g., Manager-Scholar, Manager-Investor-Scholar, Coach-Mentee, Scholar-Turned-Manager) that shaped the behaviours of the different users on the platform. Lastly, we present design implications and recommendations to guide the design of P2E crypto games and the gig-focused communities that thrive around them. Our results contribute to ongoing discussions in designing digital gig economies and crypto-based games.

1 INTRODUCTION

Crypto-games are video games that run on top of a cryptocurrency network. Unlike traditional video games, they allow users to own, earn, and trade their virtual assets without relying on a central authority. This is enabled through decentralized applications (DApps)  [ 37 ] found on distributed ledgers such as Ethereum  [ 41 ]. Recently, crypto-games began including Non-Fungible Tokens (NFT) as part of their gameplay and economy [ 51 ]. NFTs are unique, blockchain-based tokens that certify that an individual owns a single digital asset. This technology introduced new interactions and mechanics in digital artworks, events ticketing [ 15 , 29 ], and many others  [ 35 , 38 ]. While governments are still studying how to regulate the crypto ecosystem best  [ 11 , 13 , 22 , 34 ], the space of crypto-gaming continues to grow, with the reported market capitalization of the top crypto-games’ tokens totaling 6 billion USD and, a 24-hour trading volume of 1 billion USD [ 9 ].

Through NFTs, crypto-games can reward gamers with rare in-game assets and objects, such as limited-time collectibles or items with unique characteristics that players can trade or sell for fiat currency  [ 16 ]. This potential to earn money from assets owned in a game garnered a lot of income and traction in certain countries and territories in South East Asia (such as the Philippines, Vietnam, and Hong Kong), a region that is projected to have 139 million mobile gamers by 2027  [ 30 , 44 ]. Thus, waves were made in local game industries and led to several game developers exploring crypto-games and NFTs. One of these games is Axie Infinity, designed to allow players to "Play-to-Earn" (P2E) by competing in battles, creating new NFTs, collecting and speculating on rare NFTs in the market, and many others. In this specific game, the NFTs are called “Axies” and represent in-game characters with unique art, skills, and traits  [ 31 ]. To play the game, interested players must purchase or own a team of three Axies that they use to battle within the platform. Winning ranked matches in the game's Battle mode and ranking high in the seasonal leaderboard rewards players with cryptocurrency tokens called Smooth Love Potion (SLP) and Axie Infinity Shards (AXS)  [ 31 ]. Players can use these rewards to either improve their teams, buy new Axies from the marketplace, or convert into the fiat currency of their choice through a cryptocurrency exchange.

An NFT-based game such as Axie Infinity proved to be an alternative income-generating tool for around 700,000 players from the global south affected by the limitations caused by the pandemic  [ 5 ]. At its height in 2021, reports on the possibility of earning as much as 600 USD while playing Axie led both employed and unemployed individuals to gravitate toward the game. Earning up to 600 USD per month is considered above average, especially in countries in the global south  [ 6 , 10 ]. Some people would play for as little as 2 hours a day using Axies they own or have rented. At the same time, some would invest money and rent out teams of Axies to other people under their own profit-sharing agreement  [ 1 ]. Some see Axie Infinity and similar games as the future of work, with Play-to-Earn as the evolution of the gig economy where willing players would render services for other players willing to pay  [ 40 ].

Axie Infinity suffered a major hack in March 2022. At that time, the value of their AXS token reportedly dropped by 98% from its August 2021 peak in July 2022. From there, the number of users interacting with Axie Infinity's smart contracts has dropped to approx. 23,000  [ 12 , 19 ]. Without the assistance of third parties, Axie Infinity's smart contracts enable the reliable verification, enforcement, and execution of digital transactions inside the Axie ecosystem. Some players even told others to stop playing the game [ 6 ]. This story of Axie Infinity tells us that for future Play-to-Earn games to succeed and deliver their vision, there is a need to gain a better and more structured understanding of the space and the relationships within them.

Our paper investigates the perceptions and relationship dynamics between Managers and Scholars in the Play-to-Earn gaming space. This novel organizational structure shows a glimpse into the future of work in a post-coronavirus society where labor is increasingly being done online. We also set out to understand how Play-to-Earn systems and cryptocurrency incentives affected their motivation and strategies in playing Axie Infinity and similar games. In a mixed-method study, we first surveyed 30 Managers and 39 Scholars ( N = 69 in total) and then did semi-structured interviews with nine participants. From here, we gained a deeper understanding of this phenomenon that is crucial to the design of digital societies, economies, and games. We summarize our contributions into the following points:

  1. Empirical data from the mixed method techniques describing the partial landscape of NFT-based P2E games that exists in the global south.
  2. A critical analysis bearing findings on the perceptions and relationship dynamics of Play-to-Earn Scholars and Managers, particularly in the Philippines.
  3. Design implications and recommendations in NFT-based P2E games scenarios design space from these data (key features and design recommendations).

2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK

Our work inquires into a discipline (e.g., cryptocurrency, NFT) with potential and technological significance. However, the phenomena we investigate overlap with systems of similar mechanics and relations, such as gig economies, cryptocurrencies, and game design. Thus, we position our contribution according to this specific landscape.

2.1 Axie Infinity

Axie Infinity is a cryptocurrency-based game where players battle their team of Axies, which are axolotl-inspired NFTs, against other players in exchange for Smooth Love Potions (SLP)  [ 48 ]. Smooth Love Potion is a digital token used for trading and breeding inside the Axie Infinity Ecosystem. Axie Infinity Shards (AXS), on the other hand, are governance tokens earned during competitive mode and are used for breeding and voting on proposals for the game itself  [ 32 ]. While its game mechanics are similar to other turn-based RPG games, its primary selling point is the ability to directly convert in-game SLPs into real currencies. Play-to-Earn games employ blockchain technology, enabling players to earn rewards or money when playing them  [ 50 ].

Since Axies are NFTs, players have to buy them to play the game, wherein a team of 3 Axies can cost around USD 1,000. This can be a challenge for casual players when expanding their teams. This seeming limitation led to an operational setup comprising two more roles beyond that of a player: a Manager and a Scholar  [ 1 ]. A Manager is an individual who buys and rents out teams of Axies to other people under a profit-sharing agreement. Meanwhile, a Scholar is an individual who plays the game using a rented team of Axies without upfront payment for a percentage of their earnings. This study investigates the dynamic relationship between managers and scholars and their role in the rapid growth of the game.

2.2 NFT and Cryptocurrencies

While NFT and cryptocurrencies are relatively recent, transactions and exchanges have been occurring on a large scale. This has motivated the emergence of non-fungible tokens (NFTs) in game development. Literature suggests eight (8) known challenges in this domain  [ 17 ]. These are (1) regulation, (2) power consumption, (3) security, (4) blockchain risks, (5) marginalized communities, (6) ownership and intellectual rights, (7) actual currencies, and (8) exchange. Since NFTs use a cryptocurrency that is decentralized in nature, no central banks or departments are overseeing their transactions. The lack of oversight from authorities has risks that can be used to commit crimes. As of the time of writing, the process of Ethereum's verification is synonymous with using substantial power consumption. The environmental impacts of cryptocurrencies have taken the attention of climate activists, especially concerning their global warming impact. NFT owners will be left with a useless token if the services that host the NFT become defunct or close down.

Furthermore, hackers are a significant security concern because owners will lose their tokens if their device is hacked/stolen. As robust as blockchain might seem, it is still prone to code bugs that can affect Ethereum's functionality. This kind of vulnerability has the potential to be exploited by hackers. As NFTs are composed mainly of digital goods exchange, there is also a risk of marginalizing the artists’ community whose creative approaches and outputs are better equipped in a digital format. Digital replicas of physical objects present another legal challenge. While the owner of a digital photo of a physical object can be tracked by an NFT, the copyright owner of the physical object cannot be determined. Players who want to swap, for instance, an NFT with another NFT (such as Cryptokitty and Axie) face an element of risk because there is no central structure for setting the exchange rate or supervising these transactions. A method of exchanging one currency for another exists using physical money. Exchanging one coin for another, nevertheless, is more difficult with cryptocurrencies. Cryptocurrencies can be bought quickly, but converting them into cash can take more work.

Among the eight (8) challenges presented above, four (4) persist in the context of NFT-based P2E games. First is their challenges in regulation because the in-game economy of P2E games relies on digital tokens such as SLP. Security is another problem because of the recent hacks on Axie. A notable hack in 2022 concerns 623 million USD through their discord bot  [ 43 ]. Axie's currency of SLP relies on Ethereum, and its blockchain is not resistant to bugs and vulnerabilities, which is a massive threat to Axie. Since Axies are NFTs, it also has problems when exchanged with another NFT. Lastly, the nature of cryptocurrency is volatile. As long as the value of SLP is down, it leaves no demand to be exchanged for cash. Axie's SLP also has difficulties being exchanged for another cryptocurrency. Despite cryptocurrency's growing adoption and popularity, these have introduced several challenges and issues in the domain. In prior works, there have been instances such as perceived capital losses and the current design of some mobile cryptocurrency wallets. In this research, we revisit these challenges and investigate how Manager-Scholar relationships helped players navigate the Axie platform.

2.3 Gig Economies and Gold Farming

Individuals who engage in gig economies are usually referred to as gig workers  [ 49 ]. Such individuals enter formal relationships with agreements and projects typically on-demand or by need. The type of job is usually in the form of a service-rendered that a client or contractor compensates. Gig workers distinguish themselves from freelancers because they leverage specific platforms to improve their skills (whereas freelancers sell their skills to maximize their flexibility and freedom) [ 39 ]. Another distinct qualifier of a gig worker is the notion of zero-hour contracts. As most frequently observed, especially in the UK, a worker is not obliged to provide any minimum number of working hours to engage in their service [ 27 ]. In the context of massively multiplayer online role-playing games (MMORPG) that emerged as early as the 2000s, gig economies formed around players providing services and in-game items to other players in exchange for real-world money. This form of real-money trading called “gold farming” was often led by groups and entities based in “developing nations”  [ 20 , 45 , 47 ].

Gold farming is a form of digital labor primarily observed in China, where individuals and organizations provide services and trade virtual goods, items, and currency in exchange for real money as a form of alternative employment  [ 23 , 24 , 46 ]. While the notion of a gig worker and gig economies have other advantages and disadvantages, several recommendations and guidelines have been introduced in existing research across other domains  [ 2 , 21 , 39 ]. Building upon this previous literature, we relate P2E games and their players to be similar to gig economies and gig workers. They leverage a platform to improve their skills and work within a flexible number of hours. In this research, players of the game Axie Infinity especially individuals referred to as scholars whom their managers employ, are discussed similarly within the context of gig workers. As such, this research aims to understand how players, especially scholars, are similar or different from gig workers and what recommendations and guidelines could be introduced for them to thrive in a predominantly unregulated space such as NFT-based crypto-games.

3 METHOD

We used a mixed-method approach to collect and analyze our data. First, we did an exploration survey to capture a glimpse of the landscape of Axie players, managers, and scholars. Second, we did in-depth interviews with managers to understand their different motivations and experiences in the Axie journey. Finally, we synthesised all these data to develop recommendations based on the insights that we have uncovered.

3.1 Exploration Survey

The surveys were disseminated using convenience sampling through our networks. We recruited 30 managers and 39 scholars to answer this survey. Individuals were recruited as managers if they were managing or had managed at least one scholar for the Play-to-Earn crypto-game Axie Infinity. On the other hand, individuals were recruited as scholars if they worked or had worked with at least one manager for the Play-to-Earn crypto-game Axie Infinity. 15 USD worth of either cash or cryptocurrency was raffled off to five random participants. In the exploration survey, we asked several questions intended to having a broader understanding of the landscape of Axie:

  1. What was the level of experience of managers and scholars in Axie Infinity?
  2. What was the level of knowledge of managers and scholars with cryptocurrency before and after playing Axie Infinity?
  3. What did the financial situation of managers and scholars look like before and after playing Axie Infinity?
  4. What motivated managers and scholars to go into Axie Infinity?
  5. How much time did managers and scholars spend on Axie-related activities?
  6. What did a typical day look like for these managers and scholars?
  7. What additional tools did managers and scholars use while playing the game?
  8. What was the relationship like between managers and scholars?
  9. What did the profit sharing look like between managers and scholars?
  10. What did the family and friends of these managers and scholars feel about their involvement with Axie Infinity?
  11. What was the level of satisfaction of managers and scholars with the gameplay and their earnings?
  12. How and where did managers and scholars use their earnings from the game?
  13. What opinions did managers and scholars have on Axie's economy, roadmap, and the game itself?

The survey questionnaires captured high-level demographic information about the landscape of Axie Infinity players. However, capturing in-depth perceptions and opinions on these answers was challenging using survey questionnaires alone. Thus, we proceeded to recruit participants for interviews to better understand their motivations, managerial activities, best practices, and other manager-related activities within the platform. To do this, we decided to proceed with recruiting participants diving deep into these matters mentioned.

At the time of our interviews, the value of SLP was already down; thus, there were few active managers and scholars anymore. People were not so enthusiastic about doing the interviews, which begs us to uncover why they decided to leave the platform so we could provide recommendations in the future. Thus, we recruited up to 9 interview participants from the pool of 30 participants in the survey to share their insights. Our interview participants were either (1) small-level managers having up to 10 scholars, (2) Medium-level managers having 40 scholars, or (3) Enterprise-level managers having up to 300 scholars. Interview participants were invited formally through email. A copy of the consent forms and the proposed interview structure were sent to them. In the forms, specific data storage policies and anonymisation policies were included. Participants were allowed to ask questions and clarify some matters before the recording commenced. The interviews were done virtually and recorded through a Zoom session. The interviews lasted between 45 minutes to 1 hour and 50 minutes.

The questions were structured into four topic groups, namely (1) Starting as an Axie Manager, (2) Your experiences, best practices, and learnings from being an Axie Manager, (3) Your opinion on Axie, and (4) Open Discussion. During the interviews, we wanted to find out some other things that were not covered in the survey, such as their motivations, best experiences, and many others. We also wanted to inquire about their thought processes regarding their answers to the survey. These questions were derived after our analysis of their survey answers since some required more depth and further inquiry.

3.3 Quantitative Data Analysis

The survey responses were collected and organized into.csv format. Then, we built two Jupyter notebooks for scholars and managers so we could manage the data points per category. Due to the variations in columns for each participant type, merging all survey data was not feasible. The data for each group might be seen more efficiently as a result. The columns were then given new names that were easier to remember, such as age, region , and educational_attainment before we set up the Jupyter notebooks.

Next, we performed a data cleansing procedure (such as converting the NaN values to None ). For easier data preparation, we then standardised some scale values (for example, if contentment is being measured, the extreme points will be changed to 1 (Dissatisfied) and 5 (Satisfied)). Afterward, we eliminated several data pieces that, in our opinion, were false or out of context. Finally, we sorted the responses to the open-ended questions into categories. We tabulated the number of terms that belonged to each group.

We built the visualizations from managers and scholars independently after cleaning the data. Horizontal bar charts were used to display the count data. Then, we used 100% stacked bar charts for questions and time measures. Additionally, we compared percentages with only a few data points, such as yes or no responses, using pie charts. After that, we combined the data from the managers and scholars into a single Jupyter notebook to create comparative visuals. We accomplished this by contrasting the data of scholars and managers.

3.4 Thematic (Qualitative) Analysis

In utilizing this reflexive thematic analysis process, we transcribed nine managers’ interviews. We uploaded the combined transcriptions into MAXQDA2022 tool [ 28 ]. Then the transcriptions were read twice before coding to familiarise the data. After reading, the group collectively coded the data. As the coding process was done, memos and comments were created into the document.

In the first round of analysis, we began with 472 codes identified. These 472 codes were organized into ten (10) initial categories. The ten initial themes were (1) Gaming Experience, (2) Environmental, (3) Emotional, (4) Social, (5) Management, (6) Communication, (7) Onboarding, (8) Financial, (9) Entrepreneurial, (10) P2E or Play-to-Earn. Another coding round was done to understand the trends in the data further. After the second coding, we convened together. We discussed where we aligned all the codes, ensuring that we agreed on them. This discussion, among other changes, has led to merging some themes, such as Gaming Experience and P2E, into one category simply-named Gaming. The codes of the “Emotional” theme were merged into the Management theme because it relates to the emotional experience of the managers handling their scholars.

4 RESULTS

We organise our results based on two major areas, namely 1) findings from the survey among managers and scholars, and 2) details unpacked in our in-depth interviews with managers.

4.1 Survey Findings

4.1.1 Time spent for activities. - Both managers and scholars spent their time on the platform doing several activities, such as (i) studying the meta of the game, (ii) playing the Axie game itself, and (iii) learning and discovering new strategies to help them in their competitive matches. Scholars spend some time too learning more about cryptocurrencies. At the same time, managers also allocate a portion of time to managing their scholars. Scholars did not necessarily spend a lot of time studying meta (12/39 use <1 hour per week while 11/39 spend 1–5 hours per week). On the other hand, 12/30 managers spend <1 hour per week, while 10/30 did not specify exactly how many hours they dedicate to studying the game's meta. Also, 10/39 scholars did not specify as well if they spent time studying the meta of the game. Scholars spend a significant amount of time playing the game since this is the nature of their engagement (7/39 spend <1 hour per week playing, 24/39 spend 1–5 hours per week). Interestingly, managers also play Axie even if they have their scholars in their team (6/30 spend <1 hour per week while 17/30 spend 1–5 hours per week). Learning strategies are a joint activity between scholars and managers (12/39 scholars and 9/30 managers spend <1 hour per week, whereas 13/39 scholars and 7/30 managers spend 1–5 hours per week). However, 10/30 managers did not specify whether they spent some time learning strategies for their games with their scholars. Scholars spend <1 hour (13/39) per week and 1–5 hours per week (9/39) learning about crypto as part of their jobs. 17/30 managers spend <1 hour and 10/30 spend 1–5 hours managing their scholars during a week.

4.1.2 Participants that tried breeding Axies. - Breeding is a burn mechanism in Axie Infinity that uses the game's reward tokens, Smooth Love Potion (SLP) and Axie Infinity Shards (AXS), to create new offspring from two of the player's Axies. This allows players to get a chance to develop new Axies with specific traits  [ 32 ]. According to our data, only 1 out of 39 scholars and 17/30 managers have tried to breed Axies. Since engagements with managers and scholars depend on the results and winnings, we believe this is one of the primary reasons why very few of our scholar participants engaged in breeding. Compared with the results in the previous section, most of their activities are spent learning the game, strategies, and many others. In addition, the task of breeding also involved some financial implications (giving up your reward tokens to roll out a “better” axie, thus burning some of your rewards in turn). Scholars using the Axie monsters (on loan from their managers) tend to avoid involvement. They would leave this activity to the managers themselves.

4.1.3 Challenges encountered. - Several financial factors challenged 18 out of 30 managers and ten of 39 scholars. Furthermore, scholars (16 out of 39) and managers (15 of 39) experienced and shared that they felt anxiety over the volatility of the market or the cryptocurrency market alone. 16 of 39 scholars also encountered challenges, such as time management, reaching the quota, and lack of gameplay knowledge. On the other hand, 7 of 30 managers encountered difficulties such as game strategy, finding scholars, and expanding their current investment.

4.1.4 Usage of Earnings. - Managers (21 out of 30) use their revenues to reinvest in Axie. In contrast, the scholars (24 out of 39) use their earnings to cover regular living expenditures. Intriguingly, leisure is the second most common way for managers (13 out of 30) and scholars (22 out of 39) to spend their money. Another usage is that (7 out of 30) managers’ earnings have also been invested in other NFT games or other cryptocurrencies. As for the scholars (7 out of 39), even though some have not yet received any compensation from Axie, they use their earnings to include investments in other assets.

4.1.5 SLP/AXS Expenditure. - The scholars (32 out of 39) and managers (20 out of 30) convert their SLP/AXS revenues into Philippine Peso (PHP) cash. Building special Axie teams and holding on to earnings are two other ways scholars spend their SLP and AXS. Managers’ SLP/AXS expenditure is more diversified than scholars’ because they invest their SLP/AXS in NFT games or other cryptocurrency investments. It is diversified through the following: Managers (13 out of 30) breeding new Axies, Managers (19 out of 30) changing their SLP/AXS into stablecoins (USDT/BUSD/USDC), while other managers (14 of 30) swapping their SLP/AXS into different cryptocurrencies. We see that scholars are more cash-motivated, arising from various needs and financial challenges which may have brought them to engage in such a gig in the first place.

4.1.6 Tools Used. - Both managers and scholars use tools to manage their earnings and track game activities. Almost all (38 out of 39 scholars) use cryptocurrency wallets, such as Ronin wallet, Binance, Coins.ph, etc., to store and manage their earnings. They also use fiat storage (11 out 39) and other tools (4 from 39), including spreadsheets and Axie-related tools in managing their payments or tracking game activities. On the other hand, 22 of 30 managers use Axie-related tools like Axie tracker, Axie management, Axie marketplace, and others to manage their scholars’ earnings. Spreadsheets (6 of 30) and other tools (2 out of 30), such as Binance and Facebook Messenger, were used in managing their scholars’ earnings.

4.1.7 Satisfaction with Axie Infinity. - We found no observable trend in the satisfaction of scholars towards Axie Infinity's roadmap (17 were generally displeased, 17 were also generally pleased, leaving 11 feeling neutral about it). This calls for more accurate metrics for measuring general satisfaction in such games. In contrast, 14 out of 30 managers are satisfied with Axie Infinity's roadmap. Moreover, 20 of 39 scholars and 14 of 30 managers are satisfied with Axie Infinity's gameplay. Lastly, 23 of 39 scholars and 19 of 30 managers are dissatisfied with Axie Infinity's economy. Since the survey was done at a time when the value of SLP was meager, we argue that this factor contributed to their dissatisfaction with the economy.

In summary, some quantitative findings shed light on some interesting insights. The results show that scholars generally spent more time with Axie Infinity than managers. When using the game's token-burning mechanic, managers are more likely to try it out than scholars. Both managers and scholars expressed anxiety over market volatility, but managers were more likely to be challenged by financial factors. Concerning earnings, managers tend to reinvest them back into Axie, while scholars tend to use their earnings for everyday expenses. We also noticed that managers (20/30) and scholars (32/39) converted their SLP/AXS earnings into PHP.

Interestingly, more managers were likely to keep their earnings within the crypto ecosystem by trading into other cryptocurrencies/stablecoins, breeding new Axies, and exploring other NFT games compared to scholars. Regarding the tools they use, scholars mostly cited using crypto wallets, while managers mostly cited Axie-related tools. Lastly, we can observe that managers were satisfied with the roadmap of Axie infinity. This gives us the impression that managers are concerned with long-term improvements concerning the economy in the game. Scholars spent more time playing the game itself, which led to more of them being generally pleased with the gameplay of Axie itself.

4.2.1 Manager Motivations. The manager's motivations are a huge part of the insights under the Management theme. One of the key motivators for managers creating their Axie teams is wanting to help those in need, which M6, an enterprise-level Manager, especially stressed, “This was our way of helping guys who needed it way more than”. M6 and M7 were even exclusively recruiting students as scholars so the students could have an income while staying in their homes. Even when earnings were low, all managers M1-M9 did not forcibly stop their scholars from playing the game (whether that was for extra money or just out of a desire to play). However, managers did not want to invest additional funds into Axie during the market downturn. The second motivator is for the managers to gain at least what they have invested in creating the Axie team and earn some profits after gaining their return on investment. But not all managers could get their ROI. There was even an instance where M4 stated, “We didn't get our ROI (Return of Investment).” All managers want the scholars to treat Axie as a fun game, not a part-time job. If players have fun while playing it, earning from it will naturally come along. M2, a gamer, stressed this during the onboarding to scholars, “Axie is a game first, business is second.”.

4.2.2 Profit Sharing. The codes of profit Sharing scheme falls under the financial theme. The managers that we interviewed opted for profit-sharing schemes that are grouped into the following: (A) Favoring the Manager (60–40 or 70–30), (B) Balanced 50–50,(C) Favoring the Scholars (60–40 or 70–30). The scholars agreed to their respecting sharing schemes above during their onboarding. Most of them did not try to negotiate the rates. For Profit Sharing A, the manager's intention here is to get the return on investment first. When they have already gotten their investment return, they will increase the rates to either have a balance or a rate favoring the scholars. However, these profit-sharing schemes are not strictly fixed, like in the case of M8 through the given statement, “Since we're small time managers, our deal with our scholars was 70–30 in favor of us. We agree that after we get the investment amount from the team they're playing, we will modify the profit sharing to 50–50”. There were many instances where managers gave scholars incentives when they gained an excellent MMR and did volunteer work for their Axie team. M3 gave the most incentives among the nine managers, as proved by a statement saying, “If a scholar has a birthday, or it's Christmas, we give incentives for morale.”

4.2.3 Relationships and Perceptions within their Social Circle. Most managers cited having a friendly or casual relationship with their scholars even as they scaled their operations, which was evident in the codes of the Social theme. Just like with M1’s case, a medium-level manager, “I want to foster an environment that we're all close buddies in the team ”. This casual relationship has resulted from most managers recruiting their close friends or friends from their familiar networks. Nevertheless, some managers have said that since they helped their scholars gain money, the scholars saw this as a sign of respect. In turn, this respect turned into a more formal to their relationship.

4.2.4 Starting a Scholarship Program. All Managers M1-M9 believed that their prior competitive gaming experience helped them better make strategies for the game. Then these strategies were adopted by their scholars in the game. It is also an essential factor that managers have experience playing as a scholar or a player in Axie because the first-hand experience of being a scholar and player has helped them see their perspective and manage expectations. This was further argued by M9, “Managers definitely should have experience in the game before becoming a manager. A non-player manager will never know what team to build and will rely on others for input. A month of playing the Axie should be enough to be a manager.” Additionally, the Management and Gaming themes codes were relevant to getting insights into creating a scholarship program.

4.2.5 Crypto/NFT. This section discovered insightful information on the interviews’ financial, gaming, and social themes. Not all managers had ample knowledge about cryptocurrency when they created their Axie teams. Nevertheless, their time playing Axie and running a scholarship program led them to learn more about the cryptocurrency market and the blockchain/crypto ecosystem. Some managers created guides and tutorials for their scholars on how to cash out their crypto rewards into Fiat. M3 even shared his experience teaching scholars about cryptocurrency and not just about Axie, “As a manager, I teach my scholars tips and tricks about investing/trading cryptocurrencies.”

4.2.6 Game Related Insights. As frequently seen in the gaming theme's codes of all managers’ interviews, M1-M9 all believed that the Play-to-Earn aspect in Axie Infinity's context had focused too much on the “earning” part rather than the “gameplay.” This phenomenon resulted in the flooding of “low-quality”
players/managers/scholars who were only interested in earning money. M6 attested to this by saying, “A lot of the people who are just there for the money aren't even active anymore.” According to the managers, the boom in players who were only primarily interested in earning money has led to the actions of Sky Mavis, Axie's developers focusing only on the game/crypto economy rather than improving the core gameplay loop/meta.

Each manager has acknowledged that having a “good game” is crucial in Play-to-Earn/crypto-games. As stated by M7, “Until they make a fun and viable game that would keep the users interested for months at a time, I don't see P2E going anywhere right now.” M7’s statements were further echoed as well by all managers. Play-to-Earn games should strive to be games first rather than solely focusing on the games as a way to earn. All of them felt Axie was being too “gritty” instead of a fun game.

4.3 Four Dynamics among Axie Players

We uncovered that Axie Players go beyond the typical Manager-Scholar setup. The relationships between the Managers and Scholars have been one of the key themes in our data. Specifically, we found four dynamics that operated differently within the space. See below:

4.3.1 Direct Manager–Scholar. This dynamic is the most common one, especially for small-level managers. Since most managers recruit their scholars from gaming or familiar networks, the dynamics between them are very friendly. This dynamic emphasizes the value of quality over quantity. This dynamic's profit sharing is composed only of the Manager and Scholar. The difference between this dynamic with the Investor–Manager–Scholar one is that the Manager was the one responsible for the initial investment of creating an Axie team.

4.3.2 Investor–Manager–Scholar. Since creating an Axie team requires considerable effort and money, investors were invited to provide partial or complete capital to ease starting an Axie team. Investment is beneficial for the managers because they can have their undivided attention to creating Axie teams, recruiting players, and training scholars for their team. This kind of dynamic is most prevalent for medium-level and enterprise-level managers. This dynamic also divides profit sharing into three groups of people Investors, Managers, and Scholars.

4.3.3 Coach–Mentee. As a way for the managers to help the scholars earn more, they mentor them on other things that can be another source of livelihood, such as crypto trading, social media management, and NFTs. This dynamic solidifies the motivation of the managers to help their scholars earn extra money through various sources outside the Axie Community. This dynamic emerged mainly when the value of SLP started to drop continuously at the start of 2022. The same people under the Direct Manager–Scholars dynamic are the same people doing this Coach–Mentee dynamic.

4.3.4 Scholars–turned Managers. In this dynamic, we have talked with managers who decided to be scholars before going through the manager path at Axie. They have agreed to be a scholar first to test the waters, gameplay, and economy of Axie's work, especially its unique Play-to-Earn mechanism. Making an Axie team also requires a considerable investment, so their time playing as a scholar was one of their ways to earn the necessary capital or initial investment without dipping from their savings. Also, their time spent as a scholar for someone else's team became references and pointers for them on how they will create and manage their Axie team.

5 DISCUSSION

5.1 Motivational Factors of Managers and Scholars

Due to the adverse financial effects of the COVID-19 lockdowns, many people lost their source of livelihood. People were mainly stuck inside their homes, so finding a new job proved unfruitful. The managers saw Axie as an opportunity of helping people earn money in the comfort of their homes while playing and having fun with a game. The scholars, too, on their part, did not need to shell out any investment and could earn, so this manager-scholar feature of Axie seemed like a win-win situation for both parties during that time.

Starting an Axie team requires a considerable amount of effort and investment. With this huge investment, managers are understandably only comfortable giving an Axie team to a reliable scholar. To recruit a dedicated scholar, most managers recruit from their known familiar network of friends and gamer-friends. Scholars who are gamers from the start are preferred because gamers can quickly grasp the gameplay of Axie, and they do not need to learn everything from scratch. M3 also shared insights on who are reliable scholars, “[Those who already are] friends are preferred because my friends are heavy gamers and can process the gameplay easily. Family members weren't preferred to avoid conflicts” Even if Axie is not an e-sports, managers treated it as such by creating an organized onboarding model with initial training. M2 even had an organised way of onboarding similar to a company's, “We pool together recruits for scheduled onboarding.” The onboarding explains the rules and regulations of the team. If players are found to repetitively not following the agreed rules, they will be kicked out, and if a player wants to leave the team, they are free to do so. For both situations, an off-boarding process commences by turning over the Axie account credentials given to the manager when they joined the team.

5.2 Design Implications and Recommendations

The design space of Play-to-Earn crypto-games brings new forms of labor and social relationships for future digital gig economies. While traditional game design serves as a baseline for these games, we have reported the four relationship dynamics that emerged during interviews with how managers operate beyond these platforms’ original design and capabilities. This opened up potential security risks, exploitation, and abuse. Thus, we extend the insights in the studies of  [ 23 , 24 , 46 ]. We echo some of the recommendations of our interview participants (as seen in  section 4.2 ) when coming up with recommendations for improving the current design space. We recommend that designing P2E crypto-games with these relationship dynamics can address these concerns. Thus, we present recommendations that provide crypto-game developers and digital gig economy designers with a guide on navigating the social structures on these platforms. In deriving these recommendations, we borrow approaches and techniques such as Actor-Network Theory (ANT) [ 18 , 33 ] used in addressing relationship issues in gig economies [ 26 ]. Since gameplay in Axie Infinity involves competitive gaming and regular seasons, we can also treat them as “live service games” or, more commonly known as “Games as a Service” (GaaS) [ 4 , 8 ]. Additionally, since this gaming platform involves a large number of users active all at the same time, we can also look at recommendations on Massively Multiplayer Online Role-Playing Games (MMORPG) [ 7 ]. Lastly, since these platforms thrive on social relationships, we borrow recommendations from computer-supported cooperative work involving blockchain  [ 36 ].

5.2.1 Enabling Smoother Manager-Scholar Dynamics. Manager-scholar relationships occur over third-party communication platforms such as Discord and Facebook Messenger. Data from the surveys and interviews suggest that all scholars and managers use third-party tools such as Google Sheets, Excel, Facebook Groups, Twitter Direct Messaging, and Line messaging apps for their management tasks and financial transactions. Moreover, managers use custom software to facilitate management processes between managers and scholars, such as creating dashboards, bots for information on demand, and coaching sessions. While our managers (M8, M9) reported no issues using these platforms, leaving a large chunk of the game's community to spend significant activity time on these third-party platforms poses added risks such as abuse, hacks, and exploits. This is most true in a space where a significant amount of money is involved  [ 43 ]. We recommend that crypto-game developers implement their first-party interfaces to enable managers and scholars to conduct important transactions. This setup minimizes risks and facilitates trust [ 3 , 52 , 53 ] in handling this information and transactions compared to doing them outside the game's platform. This also pushes for smoother manager-scholar dynamics within their own spaces and communities  [ 42 ].

5.2.2 Game-Related Recommendations. Aside from enabling smoother relationships with the various roles in the gaming space, the underlying technology must also be considered in the future design of crypto-games. We relate blockchain applications with data-driven game-related recommendations to make sense of these crypto-enabled platforms  [ 14 ]. Managers (M4, M7) claim that when it witnessed a sudden increase in players and investments, they neglected to strengthen the gameplay mechanics to discover solutions to deal with the game's economic instability. During our interviews, some managers of M6 believed that Axie Infinity's excessive promotion of scholarship programs led to its downfall. This attracted more people to focus on making money solely, thus lowering the quality of competitive matchmaking. While steps are now being taken to address issues with gameplay and economy [ 25 ], we recommend that crypto-game developers establish a solid gameplay foundation before implementing Play-to-Earn mechanics. The managers we interviewed (M1, M2, M7) reported too much focus on Play-to-Earn mechanics, stating that these mechanics attract individuals who are primarily interested in earning money first rather than playing the game itself. Thus, game developers must be able to make sense of the technologies, the relationships, and the reactions to these interactions. All managers felt Axie was too “gritty” instead of a fun-to-play game. This statement matches our survey data, showing that 12 of 39 scholars spend 1-5 hours studying meta, 7 of 39 playing Axie, and 12 of 39 learning strategies. This adds up to roughly 5–15 hours in a week. The impact of this total number of hours would differ for a full-time student or someone employed in a 40-hour job. In this context, it is also essential to understand what drives this particular gaming behaviour, whose impact may vary depending on the status and other attributes of a given player.

5.2.3 On Axie-related and other relevant topics. The token rewards and player base for Axie Infinity have steadily dropped in recent months. Managers cite the lack of utility as one of the contributing factors to this event. Additionally, data shows that 23 of 39 scholars and 19 of 30 managers were dissatisfied with how Axie Infinity's economy was handled. We argue that this is caused by Axie Infinity's reward token Smooth Love Potion (SLP), having little utility and an unlimited supply. This resulted in the continuous loss of value every time players decided to cash out. Introducing an additional utility by designing game features that burn the token rewards in exchange for better items within a game should be explored to keep the game economy healthy. Moreover, crypto-games should intentionally design token rewards with limited supply [ 36 ] to encourage usage within the platform, thus maintaining or increasing its value even as the game's player base increases.

6 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

This study recruited participants mainly from the P2E community in the Philippines. It may not represent the P2E communities in other regions, such as South America and South East Asia. Most of our interview participants were small-scale managers handling up to 10 scholars in their Axie team. The limited number of participants is due to managers and players that have already stopped operations and playing. It would be advantageous to interview and get insights from more medium- and enterprise-level managers and full-time players/scholars. Interviewing scholars would help understand the difference in experiences, motivation, and perspectives between scholars, managers, and other stakeholders. It would also be beneficial to interview participants from other P2E communities and compare the difference in management structures and scholarship systems. As more P2E games gain popularity globally, it would be interesting to interview other gamers. This will help us gain insights into game design, mechanics, and strategies to grow their user base. While such games provide new opportunities to earn a decent living, it is worth investigating the precarious conditions of players/scholars and other externalities, especially in developing countries.

7 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we conducted a mixed-method study investigating the relationship dynamics between managers and scholars in the Play-to-Earn gaming space. We uncovered eight themes that were conceptualized during interviews with managers: (1) Management, (2) Financial, (3) Entrepreneurial, (4) Gaming, (5) Environmental, (6) Social, (7) Platforms, and (8) Recruitment. Based on the data collected from the survey and interviews, we gained insights into how managers and scholars operated within unregulated Play-to-Earn systems. We found four relationship dynamics (Manager-Scholar, Investor-Manager-Scholar, Coach-Mentee, Scholars-turned Managers). These relationships showed us how some players were motivated despite an unstable environment. We lay out a set of design implications and recommendations to guide future researchers in designing such platforms. We call for more studies involving larger-scale samples to further understand digital gig economies and crypto-based games.
