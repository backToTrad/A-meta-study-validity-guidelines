This interest has persisted as scholars highlight the need for more situated understandings and deeper integration of ethics into HCI. In parallel, HCI scholars and students have become increasingly involved in teaching computing ethics across many different university contexts, bringing in valuable perspectives informed by the connections between HCI and the socio-technical subject matter of computing ethics. Yet explicitly bringing these two threads together – examining the teaching of ethics through an HCI research lens – remains nascent. This paper integrates work in HCI and computing education to focus on the role and experience of computing ethics teaching assistants (CETAs), who are increasingly involved in ethics instruction and whose perspectives are predominantly missing in existing literature spanning HCI and computing education. Drawing on HCI theories and methods, our qualitative study of eleven CETAs at two American universities makes three contributions to the HCI literature. First, we build an understanding of who these TAs are with respect to the unique position of teaching computing ethics. Second, we characterize how CETAs’ teaching and learning is situated and shaped within different communities and institutional contexts. Finally, we suggest several implications for the design of ethics instruction within undergraduate computing programs. More broadly, our work can be viewed as a call to action, encouraging HCI scholars to play a more significant role in studying and designing the teaching and learning of computing ethics.

1 INTRODUCTION

The HCI research community has long centered ethics in HCI research and practice [ 15 , 16 , 23 , 25 , 37 , 57 ]. This interest has persisted as scholars highlight the need for more situated understandings and deeper integration of ethics into HCI. For example, HCI scholars have recently proposed the notion of situational ethics [ 41 ]. Whereas traditional understandings of ethics in HCI historically have drawn from perspectives that focus on individual decision making, situational ethics encompasses a more dynamic and relational view of ethics these scholars argue is better able to guide the ever-changing needs of HCI. Furthermore, HCI scholars continue to study and develop other frameworks such as ethical mediation to inform ethical design practice and design education [ 26 ]. Ethical mediation characterizes the multiple mediating and situational factors that impact ethical awareness and decision making in UX practice [ 11 , 27 ].

In parallel, HCI scholars and students have become increasingly involved in teaching computing ethics across many different university contexts. HCI scholars have much to bring to the design and delivery of ethics education given the connections between the discipline of HCI and the socio-technical subject matter of computing ethics. Notably, supported by initiatives such as Mozilla's Responsible Computer Science Challenge [ 1 , 2 ], HCI researchers are developing new approaches to teaching ethics that draw from a variety of disciplinary perspectives and pedagogies [ 21 , 22 , 29 ]. Others are contributing to more participatory and democratic-centered approaches to teaching ethics in order to foster participation in computing education [ 7 , 17 , 22 , 56 ].

Yet bringing these two threads together – examining the teaching of ethics through an HCI research lens – remains in its infancy. Building on calls for more situated understandings and deeper integration of ethics in HCI research and practice, this paper synthesizes work in HCI and computing education to focus on the role and experience of a specific group that is central to ethics teaching: computing ethics teaching assistants (CETAs). CETAs offer a compelling place to begin using HCI methodologies to study the teaching of computing ethics. Despite being a crucial part of the TA labor force, especially for universities that rely on TAs to keep pace with booming undergraduate enrollment, CETAs’ perspectives and experiences are largely missing in the existing literature spanning HCI and computing education (see for exceptions: [ 13 , 38 , 40 ]). There is also reason to believe that the experience of CETAs differs from that of TAs in technical courses for reasons ranging from content to institutional support to social dynamics. In content, especially at the undergraduate level, technical material tends to have clear right and wrong answers. These answers are easy for TAs to grasp, and abundant resources are available in textbooks and online for TAs and students to verify answers and/or develop understanding. Students quickly develop familiarity with how to learn technical content and how to use TAs as a resource. While technical TAs may interact with students interpersonally during office hours, debugging code or correcting technical misunderstandings are different tasks than navigating group or one-on-one discussions of complicated ethical topics. Likewise, it may also be easier for TAs to establish their expertise regarding technical content, and some students may place higher value on learning technical content.

Informed by this context, we describe a qualitative study of eleven CETAs, four of whom are HCI graduate students, at two American universities. This study sought to answer the following research questions:

RQ1 : What is the experience of those on the front lines of teaching ethics in computer science?

RQ2 : How do institutional contexts shape opportunity, constraints, and challenges for CETAs? The two universities in our study are decidedly different in their size, disciplinary scope, student body, and in their approach to structuring ethics education and CETA roles.

RQ3 : What unique perspectives do CETAs offer by their positioning between the role of student and experienced instructor?

We begin by reviewing relevant work spanning approaches to teaching ethics in computer science, CETAs, and situated perspectives that guide our analysis and efforts. Subsequently, we synthesize a thematic analysis of interviews with eleven CETAs at two American universities that have taken very different approaches to computing ethics education, and subsequently use CETAs differently. Through our analysis we make three contributions. First, we build an understanding of who these TAs are within their unique position of teaching computing ethics. Second, we characterize how CETAs’ teaching and learning is situated and shaped within different communities and institutional contexts. Finally, we suggest several implications for the design of computing ethics instruction within undergraduate computing programs. More broadly, our work can be viewed as a call to action, encouraging HCI scholars to play a more significant role in studying and designing the teaching and learning of computing ethics.

2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK

2.1 Models for Teaching Ethics in Computer Science

Our work is informed by a growing body of research spanning computing education, HCI, and related disciplines focusing on computer ethics. The attention on ethics education is motivated by widespread calls for computer scientists to better engage with the ethical dimensions of their work and to incorporate more expansive, participatory, and democratic centered approaches to teaching ethics [ 33 ]. Such calls have resulted in new ethics-focused activities such as the ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability and Transparency as well as educational initiatives that span academia and industry such as the Mozilla Foundation's Responsible Computer Science Challenge, an effort to integrate ethics with undergraduate computer science training in innovative ways [ 1 ]. Notably, HCI researchers have played central roles in building and scaling these activities and initiatives [ 2 , 6 , 18 , 20 , 21 , 47 , 50 , 51 , 53 ].

In particular, we draw from researchers and instructors who are developing different kinds of teaching models in various institutional contexts. These include standalone models that concentrate computing ethics and societal implications into a single course often taken at a late stage of a computing program. To offset the concern that including ethics only in a single course sends the message that ethical concerns can be bracketed off from technical material, integrated or embedded models have recently gained traction. These focus on the development and use of case studies, modules, and game-based activities designed to fit specific CS content areas (e.g., computer vision, machine learning) [ 4 , 14 , 49 ] and creative approaches to teaching computer ethics that span multiple disciplines, for example, through science fiction, autobiographies, embodied activities, or project-based learning approaches [ 7 , 52 ]. A third model leverages experts on ethics within other units of the university, such as a philosophy department, to teach computing ethics. Hybrid versions of the second and third models – embedding ethics as a partnership between computing and ethics experts — are also possible [ 29 ].

These models can be informed by approaches and new developments around teaching ethics in other disciplines such as engineering, especially at the undergraduate level [ 42 , 43 ]. For example, engineering ethics education has traditionally focused on issues confronted by the individual engineering practitioner, such as the obligation to work only within the realm of one's expertise, rather than broader societal concerns that the profession faces [ 30 ]. Along related lines, engineering ethics often examines an engineer's obligation to avoid harm, or what is sometimes referred to as preventive ethics, and to comply with engineering codes of ethics or the law. Yet scholars within the realm of engineering ethics are starting to more consistently draw attention to the issue of social responsibility [ 8 ]. There are many different learning objectives embedded in approaches to engineering ethics, including the aim of having students learn about professional codes of ethics or conduct [ 39 ] or role playing scenarios have been implemented in engineering ethics courses [ 31 ].

Yet, scholars and practitioners developing models to teach ethics in CS are increasingly recognizing how and why teaching ethics in CS is both similar and different from teaching ethics in other disciplines such as engineering.

2.2 The Role of Teaching Assistants in Computer Science

The role of TAs in computer science programs has received some attention in the computer science education literature. For example, Mirza et al. reports on a systematic review of publications on undergraduate TAs dating to the late 1980s, finding that experience reports are the dominant form in the literature, representing about 30 papers [ 40 ]. About half of the experience reports in the survey conduct some analysis of their data. Less common are research papers that identify research questions and follow an accepted methodology to pursue answers. Among the seven unredacted research papers included in the review, several focus on the impact of undergraduate teaching assistants (UTAs) on student learning [ 3 , 19 , 48 ] and one on the evaluation of UTAs [ 46 ]. The two papers that are closest to our interests focus on what makes the UTA experience satisfying, finding that environmental and social factors matter, including the presence of a social support system [ 44 ] and, related, a team teaching setup [ 45 ]. Among areas called out for future research are “[u]nderstanding the benefits and challenges of the various roles [of UTAs]” which is viewed as “essential to assess their impact on the professional development, learning, and sense of belonging of UTAs, especially those from underrepresented groups” [ 40 ]. Further, papers that study benefits at the level of UTAs, students, instructors, and institutions are almost entirely categorized in the survey as anecdotal, indicating a large gap in the research literature.

In the CHI community, relatively few papers have considered the role of teaching assistants, reflective of the overall paucity of research studies with this focus. A recent exception is Malysheva and Kelleher [ 38 ] who examine the use of automatic code corrections for assisting TAs as they are helping students debug code. Echoing Mirza et al., Malysheva and Kelleher are also interested in the challenges that TAs face and how they interact with students. For example, they note “our understanding of TAs is sparse and largely informed by studies of programs” [ 38 ].

We seek to add to this small body of work on the role of TAs in computer science programs, focusing on how CETAs are embedded within particular communities and institutional contexts that shape how CETAs teach ethics.

2.3 Examining Ethics TAs from a Situated Perspective

Situated understandings of ethics focus on relational and contextual dimensions between students, technologies, and the environment in ways different from traditional views of ethics, which emphasize notions of individual responsibility, professionalism, and codes of ethics. Yet, teaching ethics in CS is primarily understood at an individual level, focusing on particular pedagogies or content individual teachers can use to teach ethics in CS. Both perspectives have value.

Specifically, we draw on an established body of research that illustrates how teaching is a culturally and historically situated activity, constituted in relation to learners and communities [ 9 , 32 , 34 , 35 ]. This literature emphasizes the social dimensions of teaching, viewing student learning as an interactional accomplishment between teachers, students, and the institutional settings in which they work [ 12 , 28 ]. For example, such work understands teachers and teaching as situated within particular institutional settings that shape and are shaped by teachers and students. Likewise, such work draws from ideas central to the CHI community such as notions of communities of practice and legitimate peripheral participation [ 36 ], which have been used in HCI to understand Wikipedia editors [ 5 ] or to grow the global HCI practice [ 54 ].

Like others in HCI who use a situated perspective to analyze technologies and practices in context, our work is informed by a situated perspective on teaching in two ways. First, such a perspective guides our analytic focus on how CETAs are situated in different institutional settings; draw from their own personal and cultural resources to engage with ethics teaching; and how the kinds of social relationships they develop, maintain, and build in and outside of the classroom influence their identities and approaches to teaching and learning ethics. Second, we consider what kinds of communities of practice shape each CETA's views of ethics and how they have become more or less central participants in a community of practice. In doing so our work complements existing work on teaching ethics in CS to highlight the value of a situated perspective 1) to understand how CETAs teach, and 2) to better support CETAs teaching in different kinds of university contexts.

In summary, universities are responding to widespread calls for great emphasis on computer science ethics by developing different models for inclusion in computing curriculum and involving CETAs in different ways. Yet the research literature on the role and experience of teaching assistants in computer science is sparse. We draw upon the theoretical and methodological tools of HCI to examine complex questions about the teaching of ethics in computer science.

3 METHODS

3.1 Recruitment and Participants

To answer our research questions, we recruited and interviewed CETAs from two different institutional settings. University A is a large public institution with a focus on STEM. University B is a small private liberal arts university. Both universities are classified R1 (very high research activity) by the Carnegie Classification. University A requires computer science majors to take one of a small number of standalone courses within the department to fulfill the major's ethics requirement. University A uses TAs within these courses to distribute the grading workload and, in larger sections of the course, to allow students to engage with material in small group settings through TA-led discussion sections. University B embeds computing ethics content into existing technical courses. It hires teaching assistants to spearhead this integration, either by developing new assignments and content or by revising existing materials.

We began recruiting participants in the summer of 2021. At University A, we began by reaching out to professors of one of the computing ethics courses to ask if they would put us in contact with their current or recent teaching assistants. At University B, we began recruiting by contacting former students who had been involved with developing that institution's ethics program. From there, we were able to correspond with the faculty members in charge of the program, who we asked to connect us with current CETAs. Because of the structure of their TA programs, participants from University A were all graduate students, and participants from University B were all undergraduate students, though University A makes use of some UTAs as well. This is one of several significant differences in the two institutions that allow us to draw contrasts and understand cross-institution and cross-approach commonalities.

In total, we recruited five participants from University A and six from University B. University A participants were TAs for the same computing ethics course, though at different times and for different class sizes, which resulted in differences in course structure. Large course offerings had around 200 students per class and were formatted around a biweekly lecture and a two-hour long discussion section in groups of 10. Small course offerings were lecture-only and had around 30 students. University B participants were CETAs for different large technical courses (roughly 150 to 200 students). Some also helped develop the program when it was first introduced (B.1 and B.2), while all others came to University B after the program was piloted. Table 1 summarizes the participants.

3.2 Data and Analysis

We conducted semi-structured interviews with each participant to elicit their perspectives on computing ethics, its instruction, and their role as CETAs. For example, we asked about their background, their general TA experience, and their specific CETA experiences. In talking about being a CETA, we asked questions related to course logistics (e.g., how many students, what were CETAs’ responsibilities, what training did they receive) and to their reflections on the course (e.g., what motivated them to be a CETA, what did they expect from the position, and what challenges did they encounter). We also asked what experience participants had with computing ethics before becoming a CETA and how they understood computing ethics instruction, and its significance, more broadly. See Appendix A for the full interview protocol. Following approval by our university IRB, two of the co-authors split the interviews, which were conducted in the summer and fall of 2021 via video conference. These interviews were audio recorded, lasted approximately 1 hour, and transcripts were produced by the research team.

Under the supervision of the other authors, Author 1 analyzed this data using thematic analysis in the grounded theory tradition [ 10 , 24 ]. They began their analysis by conducting an action-oriented open coding process. This approach was chosen to preserve the individual voices of participants and to emphasize the processes of teaching, learning, reflecting, and identity-making over mere description. With the help of the research team, these codes were then organized into thematic categories as we became more familiar with the dataset through coding, analytical memos, and collaborative discussions amongst our research team, which established agreement and iteratively refined themes amongst our research team. Notably, we iteratively refined these categories by testing and comparing them across universities. Thematic analysis was initially done with University B data. These themes were then used to analyze University A data to see which categories held up and which ones needed to be refined and discarded. Subsequently, we continued to discuss and iteratively develop themes over a five-month period as we moved toward structured analysis, again allowing themes and their organization to emerge and be tested for applicability across universities.

Prior to sharing our analysis, we acknowledge the positionality of our research team, all but one of whom are from the United States. The non-US author is from Australia. Author 1, who led interviews with CETAs and analysis on the project, is also a student with considerable TA experience, providing them with a unique perspective and positionality related to the goals of this project. Their instincts as a former TA for classes spanning coding, technical writing, and digital media, as both an undergraduate and graduate student, guided creating the interview protocol. These experiences also acted as an entry point into how they conducted interviews and their analysis, informing how they have constructed meaning from the wealth of data provided by participants. Other senior team members have a long history of working at the intersection of ethics and education and have varying expertise spanning computer science, learning sciences, ethics, and critical data literacy.

4 FINDINGS

We organize our findings thematically, with two ways to follow the narrative thread. The first is along a temporal dimension, starting with who CETAs are as they come into the TA experience and why they gravitate to the TA role (Section 4.1); then what they experience in the day-to-day work of being a CETA (Section 4.2), from formal preparation (Section 4.2.1) to specific responsibilities as discussion leaders (Section 4.2.2) or as primary designers of assignments (Section 4.2.3); and finally how they reflect on ethics education with the benefit of having served as a CETA (Section 4.3). Alternatively, one can read the narrative thread along a scale dimension, moving from the personal experiences with ethics (Section 4.1) to the small group and local experiences as a CETA in courses (Section 4.2) to the bigger picture, broad perspectives on ethics at the level of program structure, department, industry, and society (Section 4.3).

4.1 Who TAs Ethics Courses and Why (RQ1)

We begin by considering how our participants have come to understand ethics, how that shapes their role as CETAs, and why they were motivated to become CETAs. Their understanding of ethics is informed by contexts that include courses, but also go beyond courses. This in turn shapes how participants act as CETAs. That is, CETAs are themselves oriented toward computing ethics by prior experiences, such as taking computing ethics classes as students, doing HCI research, and working in the industry, which then informs how they fulfill their responsibilities. This orientation is an important part of answering RQ1: What is the experience of those on the front lines of teaching ethics in computer science?

4.1.1 Encounters with Computing Ethics through Formal Coursework. The most cited experience with computing ethics prior to becoming a CETA was through coursework as an undergraduate student. With the exception of TA A.3, all participants reported either taking a computing ethics course (TAs A.1, A.2, A.4, A.5), taking a non-computing course with ethics and social responsibility content (TAs B.1, B.2, B.5), or taking a technical course with embedded ethics content (TAs B.3, B.4, B.5, B.6). Additionally, only TAs A.1 and A.3 had not taken the course that they would eventually TA as students. Unlike the other three University A participants, they received their undergraduate CS education elsewhere, though while A.1 took a computing ethics course as part of that education, A.3 did not. A.3 completed their undergraduate degree in computer science outside of the United States and did not encounter any ethics content as part of that curriculum. As a result, TAing computing ethics was A.3’s first exposure to the subject. They described this as “shooting [themself] in the foot” since they lacked any expectation for the course and had the additional responsibility of learning the course material alongside the students.

For the other CETAs from University A, taking the computing ethics course as undergraduate students was foundational in developing their own understanding of the subject and greatly informed their experience as a TA. A.4 described it as one of “the two most formative classes that [they] took in undergrad.” A.5 was even more complimentary, remarking that “I had fun while I was taking this class, so I didn't want that fun to stop. So, I retook that class as the TA.” As they described it, the class also helped them realize the ubiquity of prejudice and bias.

University B CETAs had different initial encounters with computing ethics depending on their year. As some of the first CETAs, B.1 and B.2 could not encounter embedded ethics content in their technical classes since it did not yet exist. However, they and B.5 mentioned learning about the intersections of computing, ethics, and politics in other departments. They described this instruction as helping them develop a broad critical approach to computing technologies that contrasted with the more classical ethics framework described by University A participants or the technical case studies described by other University B participants. For example, B.1 explained that their dual major in history “[gave them] more of a bird's eye view of the industry, right, the industry as a whole, with all of its warts, with all of its inequalities, with all of its imperfections.” B.2 explained that in other classes, such as a course on the history of state surveillance, they “got way more into tech ethics and radical or critical or anti-oppressive perspectives in tech.” Finally, B.5 described taking a course on Silicon Valley and techno-dystopias which broadened their horizons: “I think that was really the setting in which [...] a lot of my perspectives were kind of changed and I really became aware of broader issues in tech not just related to algorithms themselves.” For all three, these encounters with computing ethics were part of their motivation for becoming a CETA, either to develop their perspectives further or to apply and teach them.

University B TAs later all encountered computing ethics embedded in their technical courses from the beginning of their time in the department. B.3, for example, described being profoundly impacted by an early project in their introductory course:

“I think the second ever ethics assignment I did genuinely changed the way that I saw coding in itself. And so, I think having those really just thought-provoking assignments early on in intro classes, I think is something [University B] has done really well.”

TAs B.5 and B.6 were more critical of the computing ethics content that they had encountered, but this was what motivated them to become CETAs. A B.5 explained, they were at times unimpressed with the ethics questions in their homeworks and projects, despite perceiving a lot of potential space for computing ethics content in deep learning. This in turn made them eager to be a CETA in order to improve the content and capitalize on that potential. B.6 made similar remarks, feeling that, beyond individual questions on individual assignments, “there was something, like, systematically wrong or inefficient about the way that particular classes were handling the [computing ethics] content.” For them, this systemic issue was one of prioritization, so they sought out a course that would give them wide latitude to shift those priorities:

“[It's] clear that many classes are unwilling to sacrifice the time and effort that you spend on technical coding assignments with what you would need for an actually impactful [computing ethics] assignment. And I figured if I joined a class that gave me as much freedom as possible in terms of designing this content, then, like, maybe I could figure out a way to make it even more integrated so that it feels more – like, you can't do the class without also doing the [computing ethics] content.”

4.1.2 Encounters with Computing Ethics beyond the Classroom: Research and Industry. Participants also interacted with computing ethics beyond the classroom, as part of an ongoing investment in their research, careers, and tech culture more broadly. As graduate students, University A participants had conducted academic research before becoming CETAs. The five University A TAs who discussed their work described diverse projects under the HCI umbrella, with computing ethics appearing as a core tenet of this research, including human-centered networking (A.1), artificial intelligence, simulation, and cybersecurity (A.2), wearables and wellness research (A.3), and user experience research (A.4). A.2 put it the most bluntly, stating “I essentially encounter [CS ethics] everywhere I go because mainly I study CS” and articulating computing ethics as an inherent, integral part of their research and studying computer science more broadly.

Some participants had also interacted with computing ethics from within industry. The two TAs who spoke most extensively about internship experiences in relation to computing ethics were A.4 and B.2, who had vastly different experiences. A.4 had a positive experience working in the ethics office of a major tech company during what was ostensibly a technical internship. That they were given this opportunity led them to believe that computing ethics mattered to the tech industry as a whole and, furthermore, that industry's approach to ethical problems aligned with the approach they had learned in class. By contrast, B.2 was thoroughly discouraged by their internship experiences and what they perceived to be a fundamental incompatibility between the values of social responsibility and technical problem solving:

“[W]hen you're offering technical skills to solve the problem, it's going to be a problem with some supposedly technical solution [...] I felt sort of like studying computer science funneled me into starting jobs where I didn't necessarily love the approach to problem solving. But like that was what makes sense once you have those skills.”

In their mind, beginning with technical capacity and then moving toward problem solving distorts the problem trying to be solved, forcing it to conform with what was possible given the technical skills at hand.

Finally, prior to being CETAs, participants encountered computing ethics within the broader milieus of culture and society. TA A.1, for example, explained that their interest in human-centered networking was derived from their firsthand experience with and personal concern for the communities that their research seeks to help. Computing ethics is, for them, a personal and social investment in bettering the lives of people they care about. TA B.4 mentioned encountering computing ethics content through pop media such as podcasts. Other investments and encounters were more political. TA A.2 decided to become a CETA specifically because they were frustrated with the privacy practices of a large social media company. TA A.3 complained about social media companies absolving themselves of the responsibility for the spread of misinformation around the COVID-19 vaccines. TA B.1 talked extensively about their personal leftist politics and how their decision to become a CETA was informed by their campus activism in 2018 against tech companies with ties to the US Immigration and Customs Enforcement agency.

After another internship experience, this one with a green energy startup, TA B.2 positively reoriented themself toward computer science. Not only were they energized by the possibility of building helpful, socially responsible technology, but they connected this work to a much more ambitious goal for themself and their university's integrated ethic program:

“I feel like it's where my relative skills are so it makes sense just to be in this growing field to try to be like a positive part of it and through [the embedded ethics program], for example, hopefully change the culture of tech for the better in whatever small ways I can.”

Summary of who TAs ethics courses and why: CETAs are a diverse lot who come into their position with a variety of prior experiences with computing ethics, from within the classroom and beyond. Such experiences include taking a computing ethics course, learning about tech and society in non-CS courses, interacting with embedded ethics content in technical CS courses, doing ethically minded CS research, and working in the tech industry. CETAs’ own learning about computing ethics is situated, dependent on the contexts in which the topic is encountered, which then often motivates becoming a CETA. Our participants’ motivations were tightly coupled with these prior experiences, informing their personal understanding of computing ethics, its importance, and how it should be taught.

4.2 What is the CETA Experience (RQ1, RQ2)

In this section, we synthesize specific insights participants offered with regards to their role as CETAs within specific courses. We begin with how our participants were—or, in many instances, were not—prepared for the position. We then focus on those elements of their practice that most directly contributed to students’ development: discussion sections, in the case of University A CETAs, and assignment design, in the case of University B CETAs. In so doing, we emphasize that our participants are not merely teaching assistants, tasked with grading, updating course websites, and debugging code, but are, in many instances, teachers themselves. The experiences speak directly to RQ1: What is the experience of CETAs? and to RQ2: How do institutional contexts shape opportunity, constraints, and challenges for CETAs? The day-to-day experience of CETAs is strongly coupled to the institutional context in which they work thus fully detangling the interview data to answer each of RQ1 and RQ2 separately is not practical.

4.2.1 Preparing to Teach Computing Ethics. Participants came into their position with varying levels of preparation from their department and their professor. On one end, some participants at both universities received no training at all. The least-prepared participants were from University A, where three participants did not even receive their department's general TA training. Two such CETAs (A.1 and A.2) were supposed to take the training course concurrent with being a CETA, but the training course was not offered during the summer semester when they taught. A third (A.4) simply missed the training entirely as a master's student who did not need to complete the training course as part of their degree requirement. Two University B participants (B.1 and B.2) received some general TA training but became CETAs before the department had developed a specific training for the CETA program. Subsequently, they did not receive any preparation for their unique pedagogical responsibilities.

At a middling level of preparation, two University A TAs (A.3 and A.5) reported receiving the department's generic TA training. However, both described this training as too broad, with A.3 going as far as to say that it did not add any value to their experience as either a technical or a computing ethics TA. Another University A TA (A.4) described receiving some course-specific preparation from their professor, who gave them guidance on how to conduct discussion sections:

“[The professor] gave us a lot of guidance, like any political persuasion is welcome [...] let two other people talk before you talk again, so sort of basics like that [...] she did really emphasize how significant the discussion groups are and how [attentive you] need to be as a TA, like making sure people have a good experience, that you're calling on people if they're not talking, that you're [making] sure you are giving everyone an equal opportunity to participate, things like that.”

However, it was unclear to what extent other University A TAs received this preparation. More frequently, these participants relied on their experiences from taking the course as a student, rather than formal preparation.

At the highest level of preparation, University B TAs participating after the ethics program was established described attending a special week-long training and course development session that occurred before the start of the semester, called TA camp. TA camp served multiple functions, beyond mere training: it taught students pedagogical skills, gave them a space to build assignments and workshop them with other CETAs, fostered community amongst CETAs, and acted as a site of theoretical onboarding. That is, as an explicit part of their formal preparation, University B CETAs learned about the program, its goals, and its values. As B.2 explained it, the intention was to get new CETAs “up to speed,” establishing a baseline understanding of the program and its visioning across all CETAs.

Preparation continued throughout the course, through course staff meetings (A.5, A.6, B.1, B.6), through program meetings (B.1, B.3), and by working with fellow TAs. For University B CETAs, working with fellow TAs was a given, arising from participants having a co-CETA. This was a particularly invaluable form of support, with B.5 saying:

“If I had been like the sole STA for this course [...] I think it would've been like a completely different experience And I know that some courses [...] only have one [CETA], which I think [...] would be somewhat like isolating because, I mean, at the end of the day it's kind of like up to you what you want to do and how you want to iterate on things [...] that back and forth with the other [CETA] was a really good like balance and also like really helped bring out like different ideas when you are able to collaborate.”

Having a co-CETA made the position a less isolating experience and allowed the pair to iterate on content together, working off of one another, rather than one CETA shouldering all the burden of content design. By contrast, it was not a given that University A CETAs would have co-TAs, let alone work closely with them. Only A.4 mentioned having a significant interpersonal relationship with another CETA, with whom they could discuss course trends and sensitive conversations that had come up in discussion section. They valued this friendship, while A.2 described feeling isolated from the rest of the course staff. Their preparation was limited because they did not know what other TAs were doing in their discussion sections.

Finally, throughout the semester, whenever their preparation failed them, participants at both universities were able to rely on professors, head TAs, and, in the case of University B participants, mentors to fill in the gaps. This support was at times inconsistent. A.5 described having an unreliable HTA that caused major grading problems. TAs B.2, B.4, and B.6 mentioned that technical professors were highly variable in how much space and time they wanted to give CETA content— and, by extension, sacrifice from technical content. At the same time, other technical professors were described as exceptionally supportive (B.1, B.3, B.6). Later University B CETAs (B.3, B.4, B.5, B.6) were also able to rely on the unique support structures afforded to them by the program, primarily in the form of two program mentors. Participants described learning from them during TA camp and receiving help from them throughout the semester. They spoke highly of this specific support for computing ethics pedagogy, in contrast with early University B CETAs. As B.1 put it, “it was like you get pushed into the deep end and you sink or swim that first semester” as CETAs struggled to develop computing ethics content without any prior experience and without any training.

4.2.2 Facilitating Classroom Discussion. Here, we consider the main teaching responsibility of three of our five University A participants. The other two, A.1 and A.3, were CETAs for a smaller version of the course, which did not have TA-led discussion sections. Instead, the professor directed class-wide discussions during lecture time, which the TAs observed but rarely participated in. Thus, A.1 and A.3 contributed to student learning almost exclusively through grading.

By contrast, TAs A.2, A.4, and A.5 each ran two discussion sections consisting of ten students each which each met once a week for two hours. This sustained, intimate contact with students throughout the semester allowed students to reflect on the course readings and lecture content, as well as build their own perspectives through dialogue with their peers. TAs followed a discussion guide prepared by the professor, which had open-ended questions about each of that week's readings and which they often lightly annotated in advance of the discussion.

As our participants acknowledged, however, the purpose of the discussion section was not to strictly follow the guide, but rather to facilitate conversation. Participants differed in how they viewed this responsibility. A.2 saw themself as fundamentally on the same level as students, rather than acting as a facilitator: “[it] was just me contributing ideas rather than being a leader. So, it just seemed like a healthy discussion rather than a teacher-student relationship.” A.5 acknowledged that managing a discussion section did require some specific social skills to encourage students to participate but stopped short of understanding that role as teaching in and of itself.

By contrast, A.4 felt that leading discussion sections not only was teaching but was the most important instance of teaching in the entire course because students were able to think through and develop their own opinions in real-time, at the “moment-to-moment basis of learning.” As they explained,

“I wouldn't say debate, it's more like trying to – come to an understanding of different perspectives and integrating them together [...] if you enter with the primary goal of understanding your own perspective and other people's perspectives a little bit better, you could probably leave with a good experience and have learned something.”

They articulate here the significance of discussion sections as a way to synthesize others’ perspectives and, in so doing, come to understand one's own perspectives better. Furthermore, as A.4 explained, facilitating such synthesis and open conversation as discussion leader required unique skills. Though the professor gave them some guidance, such as being politically neutral, in the moment of sensitive and charged conversations around technology like explicit deepfakes, A.4 described struggling:

“You know, sometimes there's sensitive issues that come up and it's like making sure that you're talking about them in a respectful way, but still talking about can be tough, because you don't want to shame someone for saying something that could be interpreted offensively, but you also don't want to permit them to be offensive.”

In other words, CETAs characterized how they must strike the balance between facilitating an open conversation and an equitable conversation, allowing students to learn from one another and learn more about themselves while also minimizing interpersonal harm.

4.2.3 Negotiating Assignment Design. Our University B participants did not interact with students individually like University A participants did. However, their work is still, in the words of TA B.5, “the main interface through which students get the ethics content” as CETAs build such content into their course's existing technical assignments. Participants identified two approaches to achieving this goal of integrating ethics at the assignment-level: a “tacking on” approach in which CETAs develop a few questions that are related to but largely separate from the technical requirements of an assignment, and an elusive “deeper” approach which would make computing ethics a fundamental part of the homework or projects. “Tacking on” might involve students commenting on a short paper or a real-world case study, or responding to a hypothetical situation that CETAs describe, or writing a few sentences about the ethical implications of the technical project itself. Such content appeared either before the technical project, as part of a broader written assignment that checked students’ conceptual understanding of the assignment, or after, as an additional part of the final submission. In any case, regardless of specific implementation, this content was separate from the technical project itself. A deeper approach, such as that described below by B.6, would seek to make the ethical requirements inextricable from the technical requirements.

Participants were often vocal about the pedagogical inferiority of the former approach. In fact, every University B TA except B.5 was highly critical of it for keeping technical and ethical content separate, with B.3 calling it the “bare minimum” approach to computing ethics pedagogy. Speaking from their experience encountering computing ethics content as a student, B.6 complained:

“I would put time into the assignments because I thought they were interesting [...] and I wanted to care. But the output that I would receive after putting in all that work was just it didn't really match. Right? Especially after I was spending so much time stressing out about these coding assignments and I had three other classes to worry about.”

At the same time, they and other participants acknowledged the time constraints and institutional limitations which produced these lesser results. TA B.1, for example, said that they and their co-CETA deliberately simplified the ethics content to make it easier to grade, since they were solely responsible for grading hundreds of weekly student responses. TAs B.2 and B.5 described coming up against course deadlines as they scrambled to finish designing assignments throughout the semester. TAs B.2 and B.6 also commented that, as undergraduate students, CETAs are already busy and might default to tacking on to simplify their workload. B.6 also put some of the blame on professors themselves, saying:

“[When] you're a sophomore or junior in charge of an entire ethics curriculum for this course [...] and the professor seems to, you know, just want a [computing ethics] curriculum without [...] negotiating, you know, class time to require the students to [do computing ethics work] then it's like, you know, what more can you do than just assign an article, assign a video and have them answer some questions?”

Even given these limitations, participants sought to move beyond the tacking on approach, which required thinking deeply about the pedagogy of computing ethics. This required both being able to assess the learning needs of students and being more deliberate with assignment design. To the first point, TA B.4 and their co-CETA solicited mid-semester feedback from students via survey. They described the response as generally positive, though some students felt that the material was an add-on. This inspired them to make the computing ethics content as relevant to the assignment as possible, often by de-emphasizing reflexivity and encouraging students to be more creative with their responses.

To the second point, TA B.5 spoke with particular depth about approaching pedagogical design intentionally. They described the process of moving from a learning goal to a fully realized assignment to, finally and most importantly, student learning:

“[O]ne of my favorite aspects of [teaching is] having [...] something I really wanted [students] to get out of [an assignment] that I feel I've realized about the field [...] putting that into the question and then reading student's responses [and] like seeing them really get the gist of what you intended without having explicitly told them “this is what you're supposed to get out of it” [...] I think that was really the most rewarding part for me.”

With these additional two responsibilities in mind—assessing student needs and intentional pedagogical design—some participants were able to deeply integrate computing ethics within their assignments. The most creative solution came from TA B.6, who explained that they added a preliminary “requirements gathering” phase to all their course's projects. Other course staff posed as stakeholders on the course messaging platforms, and students had to find out what these faux stakeholders wanted from their project. As TA B.6 pointed out, because each stakeholder has different needs, students had to balance these requirements themselves, and each project looked a little bit different depending on who students chose to interview and prioritize. This set-up encouraged deep engagement with competing design values and goals, as well as required students to think about computing ethics before coding, rather than as an afterthought to an intensive technical assignment.

Summary of the CETA Experience: Informed by training, formal hierarchical support, and informal interactions with fellow CETAs, participants were an essential part of teaching computing ethics to their students. These interactions, whether through small-group discussion sections or written assignments, allowed students to develop their own sense of ethical sensitivity around computing technologies. What's more, because these were institutionally contextualized interactions, CETAs were themselves influenced by these experiences. Their own sense of computing ethics continued to develop as they refined their social and pedagogical skills within a multi-tiered community of students, fellow TAs, and professors.

4.3 What Unique Perspectives do CETAs Bring (RQ3)

Extending the notion that CETAs approached their teaching from a situated perspective, our participants further recognized that computing ethics instruction is not confined to individual courses or even individual people, but is rather contextualized within the broader goals of the department and university, as well as how this instruction then links to tech as an industry and becomes an integral part of modern society. These reflections contribute to RQ3: What unique perspectives do CETAs offer by their positioning between the role of student and experienced instructor?

4.3.1 Perspectives on Standalone and Integrated Approaches to Teaching Ethics. In reflecting on how their program approached computing ethics instruction, CETAs across both universities described wanting to combine the standalone and integration approach. University B participants were the strongest proponents of this, with every participant except B.3 advocating for introducing additional standalone courses into the curriculum. However, TA A.1 also spoke at length about wanting University A to add the integration model. They worried that the standalone approach treats ethics as an afterthought, whereas embedding relevant computing ethics content into technical courses would encourage students to think more critically from the beginning of their computer science career.

As University B participants acknowledged, however, the integration approach on its own has some fundamental limits to its efficacy. TAs B.2, B.5, and B.6 all discussed in depth how the material that they can implement as CETAs is limited by the scope of the class that they are TAing. As B.2 argued, tying ethics content to technical content might mean that students miss learning about the larger systems that shape technology:

“[I]f students take classes about racism, or capitalism, or colonialism in other departments, I think that would honestly do more for their socially responsible computing than an attempt to expose one interaction between one of those systems in a technical system in a computer science class.”

TA B.3 pointed out that such an approach, absent a department-level requirement, depends on students to self-select into computing ethics content. They ultimately concluded that important ethical considerations, such as the relationship between race and facial recognition technology, “are just best addressed in terms of, how well can you connect the ethics issue you're talking about to the class that it's being taught in?” Still, other University B TAs felt that combining the approaches would be the best way to go, with B.4 saying that while the integration approach emphasized the everydayness of computing ethics in a technical career, standalone classes would allow a deep dive for students who are more interested in the subject.

As these reflections make clear, there is no consensus among University B's CETAs about how to best approach computing ethics instruction, but as active instructors themselves, they have many ideas to share.

4.3.2 Orientations to Computing Ethics at Department Level. Of every participant, TAs B.1 and B.2 thought the most about how the embedded ethics program operated within the department as a whole, perhaps because they were both students before the program started. B.2 described the program as a signifier for the department, marking its values and providing keen students a way to get involved with advocacy and change work, though they were unsure whether this change extended to the whole culture of the department. B.1 argued, however, that the department merely touted these values. In their mind, the real reason for developing the program at all was to respond to activist backlash in 2018. As former U.S. President Donald Trump escalated the incarceration and deportation of immigrants, students protested recruiting tech companies who contracted with the US Immigration and Customs Enforcement agency. B.1 argued that creating the integrated ethics program offered a veneer of responsibility for the department, while dodging the political issue of allowing such companies to recruit at tech career fairs.

It is not clear to what extent the political consciousness evinced by these two participants extends to the department as a whole. Both B.1 and B.2 felt that some professors wanted computing ethics to be taught apolitically, in contrast to their firm belief that the subject was always already politicized. As B.2 argued, the very framing of the subject is a political question:

“It's not just those hot button issues but it's also, how do we frame these issues? Are we talking about instances where people in marginalized groups were harmed by technology and taking a stance on it and calling things out as harmful? Or are we bringing up interesting questions and ethical dilemmas that we want students to ruminate about?”

Is computing ethics education grounded in practice and advocating for radical change, or is it largely theoretical? B.2 felt that these questions were not answered by the CETA program while they were a student, and that this lack of clarity in the program's mission made their job harder. In a similar vein, B.1 went on to argue that what they wanted from the program, and from the department, was more moral directness, rather than nuance: “The issue is, you know, when is it OK to stand in front of a class as a professor or stand in front of a group of students as a TA and say, this thing is wrong, it is wrong to do X, whatever X may be?”

4.3.3 Ethics in Relation to Tech Industry. Participants’ discussions of computing ethics frequently orbited around the tech industry, as a place of work that they were preparing students for, a facet of their own futures that they wanted to improve, and a force within society that they wanted to critique. University A CETAs were more likely to bring up industry in the first context. TAs A.2, A.3, A.4, and A.5 all felt that one of the goals of computing ethics education was to make students aware of the responsibilities of computing professionals. As TA A.2 put it, “I wanted to ensure that if I were to enter the field of CS [...] I wouldn't be violating the rights of our clients or the clients of whatever software I develop. So, I feel like this course is essentially an extension of my passion for protecting client rights.” A.4 further argued that individual professional responsibility was essential in lieu of broader corporate or legislative standards for how tech workers should ethically conduct themselves.

By contrast, University B's computing ethics curriculum has few places to discuss professionalization. When these participants made similar remarks, their emphasis was less professional responsibility and more personal responsibility within what they often viewed as a fundamentally unethical system. Both B.1 and B.5 argued that evaluating industry ethics should be considered when pursuing a tech career. B.5 said:

“I think all students who are going into like engineering careers after this should know about, for example, how technology companies influence the geography around them [...] like in California; like the cost of housing and the cost of living, for example, or how venture capital plays into tech innovation and the inequities there.”

B.1 was more strident still, naming specific companies, such as Palantir and Amazon, that they felt the department should discourage students from joining. As B.6 acknowledged, however, this would put the goals of the CETA program in conflict with those of the department writ large, since many students seek CS out for employability. As they mused, “how do you fit ethics content into the curriculum in a way that, you know, satisfies that financial stability urge while also [...] not contributing to this toxic work culture that CS is so known for?”

4.3.4 Computing, Ethics, and Society. At times, participants set their understanding of computing ethics even further still from the classroom. In framing the scope of the problems posed by computing technologies, A.3 explained: “computing technologies have basically taken over everything that we know [so] what you build has consequences, even if it's very small.” For them, the societal ubiquity of this tech is part of what necessitates computing ethics education in the first place. A.2 described the significance of this education in similarly grandiose terms: “I think ethics and computer science is extremely important because I believe the goal of computing is to help rather than harm.” This statement points to the idealized function of computing technologies as tools for social good, underscoring the significance of computing ethics education in achieving that goal.

Some University B TAs were similarly far reaching. B.1 and B.6 described computing ethics education as an antidote to what they respectively identified as “the techno-optimism of the 1990s and 2010s” and the “tech savior complex”. In both instances, the participants articulated a cultural narrative around computing technology's capacity to solve societal problems. They positioned computing ethics education as part of shifting such broad cultural understandings in a more critical direction. By contrast, B.2 was skeptical of computing ethics education's capacity to effect change at such a large scale, saying “I also just think that it would be impossible to meaningfully shift the culture of tech primarily within tech classes.”

Even so, all participants recognized that, whatever form computing ethics education took at their university, it was better than nothing. Most optimistic and far-looking of all was TA A.3: “[j]ust getting a head start on [computing ethics material] kind of makes you a good engineer, maybe a good – definitely a good researcher, and also a good person. I think the last one matters the most.”

Summary of Broader Perspectives of CETAs: Our participants felt that the teaching and learning of computing ethics was not limited to discrete, often interpersonal interactions with students but rather encompassed broader institutional and structural contexts. This includes how computing ethics education is itself implemented, either as a standalone course or through an embedded program, as well as what it means for the department or university: as itself an ethical responsibility to the future of computer science, as a signifier of values and commitments, as a superficial engagement with profound sociopolitical issues within computer science as a field. This further points to why computing ethics education is important at all, as participants reflected on the role it plays in industry, where many of their students will end up, and in society, where their students’ work will have impact. Recalling how industry and society also influenced our participants’ understanding of computing ethics, we see here the reciprocal nature of situated teaching and learning as CETAs learn, teach, grow, and change the future.

5 DISCUSSION

Our analysis presented a situated view of CETAs. In our discussion, we further develop this perspective and its key features for understanding CETAs. First, we describe CETAs’ identities as they develop in relation to their own practice of teaching computing ethics. Second, we describe the institutional contexts within which CETAs operate, understanding these contexts as communities of practice that not only influence CETAs teaching and development of ethical sensibility but which CETAs influence in turn as active and passionate community members. Third, we describe how CETAs and their perspectives should be valued because of their role in computing ethics education as a community of practice. We offer some concrete suggestions on how CETAs can be prioritized in computing ethics education going forward.

Our discussion continues in Section 6: IMPLICATIONS, in which we consider this work's meaning for educators and researchers alike of undergraduate computing ethics education. We end with a broader call to action within HCI to participate in designing and researching computing ethics education, as well as studying the CETAs who will also (and already are) involved in this work.

5.1 Negotiating Identities as Ethics Teachers

Across our participants, we encountered a variety of experiences and personal histories that shaped their relationship to computing ethics not only as a topic they had learned but also as a subject they negotiated to support student learning and use as future computing professionals. Any understanding of a CETA identity, then, is necessarily situative—informed by the multivalent contexts in which these TAs teach, learn, and live computing ethics.

Some of these experiences and personal histories were similar across these contexts. For example, all of our participants self-selected into being a CETA and were motivated to do so by an existing interest in computing ethics. This may speak to a broader, latent passion for computing ethics in the student body, contrary to predominant narratives that computer science students are uninterested in the ethical and political dimensions of their field. Furthermore, all of our participants demonstrated a nuanced and varied understanding of computing ethics and its significance, both for computing generally and as part of an undergraduate computer science education.

Yet how these understandings developed, and in turn informed our participants’ teaching practices and views of ethics, was deeply contextual, influenced by their prior exposure to computing ethics both in the classroom and beyond, their training, the specific course they were a CETA for, and the broader department and university within which they worked.

Particularly, our analysis highlights how CETAs’ learning about computing ethics is itself not bound to the classroom. Rather, their encounters across contexts contributed to their developing identities and a highly personal ethical sensitivity, as a form of awareness of and receptivity toward the ethical complexity of computing technologies, as well as the industries that produce them. We demonstrated that CETAs often articulated their position and identity contextually and in relation to different kinds of communities in and outside of the classroom. That is, the variety of situations and places within which they interacted with computing ethics—as a student, as a TA, as a future tech worker, as a member of society in the digital age—strongly influenced their experiences and perspectives.

Moreover, CETAs played an integral role in constructing that “moment-to-moment” space for learning computing ethics in the classroom, whether through running discussion sections or designing computing ethics assignments, and it was in this moment-to-moment interactional space where many CETAs saw possibilities for meaningful learning. Even within the diminished space for interaction with students for CETAs who primarily designed assignments, these CETAs often shared aspects of how they supported student learning through interaction in the forms of feedback to students.

A key tenet of a situated perspective on teaching is that teaching is an interactional accomplishment between teachers, students, and the myriad of contexts and settings in which they work. Our analysis highlights the value of understanding CETAs and ethics teaching more generally from such a perspective.

5.2 Teaching Ethics within Different Communities of Practice

Our analysis illustrated the value of conceptualizing ethics teaching from a communities of practice lens, focusing on how CETAs are embedded within particular institutional contexts that support and constrain the kinds of relationships, pedagogies, and experiences they can have. Here, we expand upon different ways such communities shaped CETAs a) experiences and desires to become more central participants in teaching ethics, b) their own personal understandings of what ethics means, and c) their openness to evolving their understandings of ethics.

First, university and education level affected why participants wanted to become more central participants in teaching ethics. While all of our participants were passionate about the subject, University A participants as graduate students were more influenced by their own research and career trajectory, seeing teaching computing ethics as a natural extension of their broader computing work. Similarly, early University B participants, though undergraduate students, were motivated to become involved with the nascent embedded ethics program because of how they felt about the tech industry, whether through internships or as a facet of their everyday lives in the digital age.

Later University B CETAs, and many University A CETAs, also wanted to more actively participate in computing ethics instruction because of their experiences taking the classes they would later TA. Throughout the course of their own learning, participants discovered personally relevant computing ethics issues, teaching practices, and parts of the curriculum that they thought could be improved. Already being a part of their university's community of practice around computing ethics, participants were inspired to become more central participants in this community by carrying forward their lessons to the next cohort of students.

This leads to our second point: that these communities of practice shaped our participants’ personal understanding of what ethics means. For participants at both universities, the same community within which they would later teach was instrumental for framing the importance of computing ethics early in their academic careers. At the same time, institutional and curricular differences explain variations in how participants conceptualized ethics. Many of our University A CETAs received stronger training in classical ethical models and argumentation as a core part of the standalone course. University B CETAs never referenced specific philosophers or theories. Rather, coming from a university that emphasizes breadth of intellectual training and studying outside of one's department, University B participants developed a broader ethical sensibility informed by the liberal arts and humanities.

Finally, by reentering their communities of practice as TAs–effectively instructors of computing ethics–our participants continued to learn and grow in their understanding of both computing ethics and its pedagogy. The latter was most present in our University A participants, who were able to engage with their students intimately through discussion sections and in smaller classes. Such opportunities allowed these CETAs to revisit and strengthen their understanding of the class material, but it also allowed them to learn from their students, who were often more knowledgeable about technical areas outside of a CETA's field of expertise. By listening to students and grading writing on issues important to them, CETAs deepened their own understanding of how computing ethics plays out across subdisciplines.

For University B CETAs, their evolving understanding of computing ethics focused more on its pedagogy. By trying to design course materials and collecting feedback from students, our participants realized the insufficiencies of the tacking-on approach but also grappled with the material conditions that prevented deeper integration. They had to navigate limited training and resources at the beginning of the program, uncooperative professors, and personal availability and capacity as undergraduate students, our participants. Drawing from their experiences in this community of practice, many further concluded that the embedded ethics approach was not enough on its own and needed to be complimented by a standalone course to dive deeper into the material and facilitate more critical engagement.

Such communities of practice are strongly defined, and distinguished from one another, by institutional context. University A is a large public institution with a focus on STEM, its computer science department has adopted a standalone ethics course requirement (fulfilled within the department itself), and its CETAs are primarily graduate students who do not receive special training for the position. University B is a small private liberal arts university, its computer science department has adopted an embedded ethics approach, and its CETAs are primarily undergraduate students who do receive special training. Such contexts influenced CETAs’ responsibilities, experiences, struggles, support needs, learning, and teaching practices.

Importantly, there are many different kinds of institutional approaches to teaching ethics. For example, even standalone computing ethics courses within computer science departments take myriad forms, sometimes offering students the chance to focus on ethics in relation to specific areas of technical interest, like robotics or artificial intelligence or alternatively, fulfilled outside of a computer science department. Regardless of approach, our analysis emphasizes the value of conceptualizing ethics teaching from a communities of practice lens, focusing on how teachers or CETAs are embedded within particular institutional contexts that support and constrain the kinds of relationships, pedagogies, and experiences they can have.

5.3 Valuing the Perspectives of CETAs

In making decisions about teaching ethics, CETAs deserve first-class consideration. More so than TAs of many technical computing courses, our analysis highlights how CETAs are involved on the front lines of course design and delivery. Yet, limited attention has thus far been paid to soliciting their perspectives in the design and evaluation of ethics teaching. For example, our analysis repeatedly demonstrated how CETAs spoke candidly about the importance of ethics education for CS students, what their undergraduate program was doing well, and where their department had room for improvement. Put differently, they are well-positioned to give such evaluations and we suggest poised to drive curricular changes.

Moreover, we suggest the perspectives of CETAs have much to offer the kinds of training and support they (should) receive. Our analysis highlights how CETAs have unique responsibilities whether they are operating in a standalone computing ethics course or designing and embedding ethics content– responsibilities which are not adequately addressed by their general training, as both our University A and early University B participants reported. Ideally, computer science departments would allocate time and resources to give CETAs specialized training to prepare them for grading subjective writing, leading balanced and unbiased discussion sections, and effectively building and integrating computing ethics content. In this regard, University B's ‘CETA Camp’ is a useful model.

Alternatively, universities might instead incorporate such preparations into more general TA training. This is consistent with one of our participants’ (A.3) suggestion that there are certain classes all students should be equipped to teach– among them computing ethics. Indeed, technical TAs may find themselves tasked with CETA responsibilities under an embedded ethics model. As some of our University B participants mentioned, as there were only two of them per course, they had to rely on a technical TA to help with grading and, in one instance, running integration activities with students. Exposure to CETA training would allow them to better interface with the CETAs in their teaching staff. Furthermore, departments with limited capacity to modify TA training programs could also consider allowing CETAs to train one another. A more formalized peer-to-peer system of training would leverage two of the most common forms of support we saw amongst our participants: other CETAs and their own prior experience. When available, our participants spoke strongly about being able to rely on other CETAs, especially those from University B, who worked in close concert with a co-CETA. For University A CETAs, however, such opportunities for amiable partnership and support were opportunistic, dependent upon forming relationships separate from the structure of the course staff.

6 IMPLICATIONS

6.1 Turning to Computing Ethics Education

There is tremendous opportunity for the CHI community to contribute additional scholarly work to understanding the teaching and development of ethical practice by computer scientists, whether they pursue research, development, startup, or design careers. Formal exposure to ethics begins in undergraduate programs, where the seeds for attitudes, priorities, mental tools and relationships with people and the environment are established. Among all undergraduate topics, ethics stands out for its emphasis on critical thinking and broad consideration of socio-technical interplay. CHI researchers are ideally positioned to ask and answer research questions in the space of ethical practice development. As our work has indicated, using CHI research lenses allows an examination of teaching and learning that complements prior work.

6.2 Deepening Scholarly Perspectives on Computing TAs

CETAs and TAs more broadly are invaluable sources of labor for any computer science department, allowing larger departments in particular to keep pace with booming student populations and replicate some of the benefits of a smaller class, like more personalized interaction time with an instructor. At the same time, CETAs are particularly understudied compared to other TA populations. Across all types of TAs, universities are facing increasing scrutiny for the critical role TAs play in course delivery contrasted with the relatively low pay that TAs receive. The Fall 2022 strike by teaching assistants across the University of California system has placed in sharp relief the dependence on TA labor, as noted in a December 8, 2022 article in the LA Times, “The striking workers’ absence from classrooms and offices this week has limited the scope of final assessments, providing the greatest show yet of the crucial contributions they make to UC's reputation for excellence. These are the workers who conduct study sessions, offer crunch-time tutoring, read term papers, and proctor and grade exams” [ 55 ]. The issues in play are, of course, complex and span well beyond the scope of CHI. However scholarly work that deeply examines the experience of computing TAs has the potential to influence efforts to highlight and better support their critical work.

7 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

Our work has several limitations that may affect the generality and robustness of our results. Our sample size of CETAs was relatively small (11 total) and limited to two institutions. The substantial differences in the institutions may offset some of the limitations of just two institutions. Our methods for recruiting TAs were skewed by relying on professors for recommendations of students; if professors were biased to suggest certain types of students, that would affect our data and analysis. Our interviews and their analysis may contain confirmation bias on the part of the researchers or desire to meet the researchers’ expectations on the part of the interviewees. The theoretical aspects of our analysis, while firmly grounded in our data, have been constructed and should be tested further in new settings and with new participants.

Two lines of future work seem especially promising. First, given the diversity of approaches to ethics curricula and departmental investment in foregrounding responsibility, additional research about the roles, responsibilities, and perspectives of CETAs across more institutions would help confirm, refine, and/or expand the theory developed here. Experiments with variations in approach or further kinds of grounded theory studies within the same university would help in teasing out the contribution of university effects from course/TA/approach effects. Second, the question of how to best support CETAs in their unique role remains open. Our participants reflected on support mechanisms that they found more and less helpful, providing a starting point for future design.

8 CONCLUSION

In this paper we sought to examine the teaching of ethics through an HCI research lens. We focused on a relatively unstudied group that is increasingly central to computing education and specifically to computing ethics education, namely computing ethics teaching assistants or CETAs. We used theoretical perspectives and methods central to the CHI community to conduct a situated analysis of CETAs, highlighting the value of a situated perspective to understand CETAs and ethics teaching more generally. We also suggested this work further highlights that the perspectives of CETAs should be a more central focus for HCI researchers, who are uniquely positioned to research, understand and support CETAs’ teaching and learning. More broadly, our work can be viewed as a call to action, encouraging HCI scholars to play a more significant role in studying and designing the teaching and learning of computing ethics.

A.1 Semi-Structured Interview Protocol

We designed the interview protocol to be broadly applicable to all our participants. Where helpful, we included variations in the questions based on whether computing ethics was a standalone course (University A) or a component of a technical course (University B).

These are sample questions for a semi-structured interview. The interviewer might expand upon an area if they feel it will lead to a deeper understanding of the participants.

Background

  1. Where do you go to school?
  2. What do you study? What year are you?
  3. How long have you been a Teaching Assistant?
    1. (if applicable) Ask about past degree programs too

TA Experience (general)

  1. For what courses have you been a TA?
    1. If participants have experience TAing in other departments, ask them to talk about those experiences as well. Are there any notable differences?
  2. What has been your experience as a TA in general?
    1. What have been some of your responsibilities as a TA?
  3. What are some of your favorite parts of being a TA? Least favorite?
  4. What kind of training did you receive for being a TA? Do you think this training was valuable, and if so, what parts?
  5. What challenges have you encountered as a TA?
  6. How do you personally understand your role as a TA in undergraduate computer science education?

Computing Ethics TA Experience

  1. What has been your experience with computing ethics as a student (coursework, classes, lectures; any degree program)? Were these experiences valuable, and if so, how?
  2. What courses have you TA'ed that have touched on ethics?
  3. Ask to elaborate on the course (what did it cover? How was ethics incorporated?) & give some course logistics (i.e., # of students, # of TAs, TA hierarchies)
    1. What did it cover? How was ethics incorporated?
    2. How many students were there? TAs? How were the TAs organized?
    3. (ethics course) What kinds of assignments did students have? Please give one or two specific examples or (ethics component) What assignments did students have related to ethics?
  4. What motivated you to TA these courses in particular?
  5. How did you feel going into the course for the first time? What were your expectations for it?
    1. If asked, clarify expectations regarding the experience of teaching ethics, though general expectations are also valuable.
  6. How do you understand your role as computing ethics TA? Compared to other courses you have TA'd, has the ethics component affected your (and if so, how?):
    1. Responsibilities?
    2. Goals?
    3. Training?
    4. Challenges?
  7. What are your thoughts on ethics in computer science education generally? What ethics content should be taught to which undergraduate computer science students, and how?

Support

  1. As a TA for these courses specifically, what challenges have you encountered?
  2. Are any of these challenges different from challenges you've encountered as a TA for other courses? Which ones? Why do you think they're different?
  3. Where would you seek help for dealing with these problems?
    1. Think about both formal support systems, like your supervisors, and informal ones, like your friends or other TAs.
  4. What kinds of support do you think ethics TAs need? Would you want/have wanted more support, and if so, in what form?
