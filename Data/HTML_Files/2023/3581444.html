Collaborative Mixed Reality (MR) systems that help extend expertise for physical tasks to remote environments often situate experts in an immersive view of the task environment to bring the collaboration closer to collocated settings. In this paper, we design UnMapped, an alternative interface for remote experts that combines a live 3D view of the active space within the novice's environment with a static 3D recreation of the expert's own workspace to leverage their existing spatial memories within it. We evaluate the impact of this approach on single and repeated use of collaborative MR systems for remote guidance through a comparative study. Our results indicate that despite having a limited understanding of the novice's environment, using an UnMapped interface increased performance and communication efficiency while reducing experts’ task load. We also outline the various affordances of providing remote experts with a familiar and spatially-stable environment to assist novices.

1 INTRODUCTION

Advances in telecommunications and collaborative technology over the past few decades have made working with each other over distances an easier and richer experience, enabling more distributed teams and shifting how we structure and leverage expertise across various collaborative scenarios. Mixed Reality (MR)—a collection of display, input, and tracking technologies that blend physical and digital worlds [ 85 ]—has grown in popularity as a collaborative medium, especially for collaborative physical tasks [ 30 , 119 ] where remote individuals work together and communicate to perform actions on objects in the real world [ 37 ].

As expertise and skilled professionals are increasingly concentrated in urban centers across a variety of domains like manufacturing and healthcare  [ 83 ], the specialized knowledge needed for physical tasks like surgery [ 100 ] or aircraft repair [ 76 ] is not always available where needed. In such scenarios, MR-mediated collaborative systems offer a way for geographically separated experts to help novices complete tasks by guiding them through the required steps. MR's spatial and immersive nature enables experts to provide novices with cues that can be directly overlaid onto the task environment and allows for communication behaviors similar to colocated collaborations, especially when compared to traditional screen-bound experiences [ 7 , 111 ]. MR-enabled real-time guidance has been put to use in a variety of task scenarios from mining [ 5 ] and manufacturing [ 98 ] to healthcare [ 106 ] and crime scene investigations [ 95 ].

Remote experts use the skills and tacit knowledge they've developed through practicing their craft to make completing physical tasks easier, less error-prone, and more efficient for novices. However, this takes extensive coordination and requires experts to constantly understand and make inferences about the task environment and the objects within it [ 36 ]. Immersive interfaces that provide a three-dimensional (3D) view of the task environment can make it easier for experts to understand remote environments as it lets them engage with it more naturally [ 109 ] and allows for a spatial experience that can be especially beneficial for complex tasks [ 53 ]. However, even a high-fidelity environment that mimics the real world isn't perceptually the same as being physically present in it [ 107 ]. In addition, the seams and discontinuities introduced by both the technology and the distance between collaborators, alter the nature of the communication and fundamentally changes how experts experience the remote environment [ 23 , 56 ]. Finally, the sustained mental and cognitive exertion required to understand remote locations can become extremely taxing on experts [ 96 ], especially when they routinely use these collaborative systems to provide guidance to multiple novices located in different distant and unfamiliar environments [ 24 , 61 ].

A key characteristic of physical tasks is that it is an extremely situated and embodied activity that is strongly tied to the environment it occurs in [ 11 , 81 ]. Consequently, in addition to being functional entities required to complete a physical task, the environment and objects within it are an integral part of how we think and behave [ 122 ]. Experts, in particular, build spatial memories and structure the environments they routinely perform physical tasks in [ 63 ]. Tapping into these situated experiences could provide new research approaches that have major implications for practitioners building MR-mediated remote guidance systems [ 64 ] and have the potential to be a key component of creating effective and enjoyable technology-mediated experiences that go beyond just being replacements for collocated communication [ 51 ].

In this paper, we compare experts working in a fully immersive interface that leverages their spatial memories in a familiar workspace (i.e. their own space), with an alternative interface that places experts in a full reconstruction of the novice's environment. Results show how having experts working in a familiar environment reduces the overall effort required to provide effective guidance and eases repeated use of the system across different novice environments. More specifically, our work contributes the following key aspects to the current research on collaborative MR systems for physical tasks:

  1. The introduction of a design approach dividing the task environment into two subspaces that combines a live 3D view for the active task space and a static 3D view for the peripheral task space , within a context-aware collaborative MR system that provides system-generated visual guidance to task objects.
  2. The implementation of two collaborative MR prototypes that use this approach: One that immerses the remote experts in a static 3D recreation of a familiar environment where they have established spatial memories ( UnMapped ), and one that uses a static 3D recreation of the novice's environment instead ( Mapped ).
  3. A comparative evaluation that studies the impact of these prototypes on remote guidance for physical tasks over two sessions and outlines the implications that immersing remote experts in a familiar environment has on (a) the remote guidance experience, and (b) on repeated use of collaborative MR systems.

2 BACKGROUND AND MOTIVATION

In this paper, we focus on collaborative physical tasks where an expert who is geographically separated from the task environment is required to draw on their knowledge and experiences to provide guidance and assist a novice. Remote guidance or mentoring for physical tasks demands extensive coordination, and observational studies of communication during these activities have shown that experts use their understanding of the environment to (1) direct the novice's attention to objects or artifacts in the environment ( Referencing ) and (2) explain manipulations to carry out on or with these objects ( Communicating Action ) [ 14 , 37 , 81 ]. Being able to partake in these communication processes and coordinate action requires remote experts to constantly understand and make multiple inferences about the task environment and the people and objects in it [ 36 , 68 , 89 ].

When removed from the physical task environment, the collaborative system becomes the sole source of information that experts use to gain situational awareness [ 26 , 31 ] and build the common ground required for object-focused communication [ 16 , 18 ]. Therefore, the system and how it represents relevant information dictates the ease with which users can provide, understand, and act upon remote guidance. By extension, the communication medium employed heavily impacts the support that a system can provide for a remote collaborative task.

Compared to traditional screen-bound experiences, immersive and spatial technologies like Mixed Reality (MR) and Virtual Reality (VR) pair especially well with the spatial nature of physical tasks and facilitate more natural interaction and communication behaviors [ 7 ]. MR's ability to understand and present information within the environment grants users instructional and gestural cues from remote experts while maintaining environment context. This makes it an effective communication medium for novices and has been shown to allow for efficient communication, increased feelings of presence, and decreased cognitive loads among both local and remote collaborators [ 4 , 119 ].

Since the remote expert's environment is rarely relevant to the task at hand, collaborative MR systems have freedom of choice with the technology they use. For example, remote interfaces are routinely experienced through desktops [ 112 ], tablets [ 2 ], and VR headsets [ 38 ] while the local novice uses an MR device. The advancements in immersive technology and affordances of VR headsets have made it an increasingly popular choice for remote expert interfaces, especially because it allows experts to experience and interact with a virtual task environment that mimics the physical space [ 42 , 96 , 119 ].

2.1 Supporting Situational Awareness with Shared Views

Regardless of the technology used, the most common way collaborative systems support an expert with building common ground and situational awareness is through a (shared) view of the environment [ 35 ]. Prior research shows that for physical tasks, remote experts benefit most from a view of the workspace, task tools, and the novice's hands as opposed to one that focused on a view of the novice and/or their face [ 43 , 44 ]. This shared view supports environmental awareness and improves communication efficiency by allowing for less reliance on verbal verification [ 68 , 84 ]. Collaborative MR systems typically provide a view of the task environment through video streams, 360 videos, 3D reconstructions, or a combination of these features [ 21 ].

While 2D video streams of the task space provide a good overview [ 70 , 97 ], they typically offer a partial view which makes it hard to identify objects and other details in the task environment [ 35 ]. On the other hand, a First-Person View (FPV) that follows the novice in the task space can decrease experts’ cognitive load [ 72 ] but the view can be disorienting for the expert [ 52 , 112 ]. A few systems use multiple video feeds [ 99 , 101 ] but switching between them leads to a sub-optimal experience [ 43 ] and doesn't enhance the expert's ability to understand the task environment [ 36 ].

The capability to situate a user in a computer-generated 3D environment is a key component of how immersive technology brings collaborative settings closer to face-to-face collaboration [ 7 , 22 ]. Collaborative systems like RemoteFusion [ 1 ], OpenIMPRESS [ 67 ], and BeThere [ 110 ] use this capability to immerse remote experts in a 3D reconstruction of the local task environment, enabling them to move around the space independently like they would in the real world. These reconstructions are most commonly created using information from depth sensors in the task environment [ 39 , 41 ] and have been shown to improve spatial awareness and social presence among remote experts while allowing for greater freedom of movement and independence of perspective [ 4 , 74 ]. However, in practice, 3D reconstructions require specialized hardware and high network bandwidth as they are computationally expensive and difficult to update in real-time [ 57 , 90 ]. In addition, real-time 3D views have a lot of noise due to systemic errors [ 62 , 71 ] and more robust depth sensing and computer vision techniques are dependent on the texture and materials of objects in the task environment. For example, shiny objects or thin artifacts are hard to capture [ 86 ]. These factors compound and make real-time 3D views difficult to implement and challenging for experts’ to glean details from realistic scenarios [ 41 ].

2.2 Designing Around the Limitations of Dynamic 3D Views

The task environment for a physical task can typically be divided into two broad subspaces: the active task space —where a bulk of the action or object manipulations that drive the task forward occurs, and the peripheral task space —or the rest of the environment that provides additional context and holds objects of interest required to complete the task (like tools). For instance, the surgical field and the car engine are the active task spaces for surgery and car repair respectively, and the tools or instruments needed are typically spread out in the novice's operating space or workshop.

One design choice that collaborative MR systems like 3D Helping Hands [ 113 ] and Vishnu [ 73 ] make to reduce the computational burden and overcome bandwidth limitations, is to reconstruct just the active space. This ensures that experts can experience the benefits of a 3D view for the part of the environment they need the most dynamic updates. However, the reduction in coverage of the task area hinders the overall effectiveness [ 75 , 114 ] of the collaborative MR system since it's challenging for experts to guide the novice's attention to objects or tools in the peripheral space that needs to subsequently be brought into the active space. Needless to say, a recreation of a portion of the environment also wouldn't suffice for room-scale tasks like engine room maintenance or crime scene investigation where the active space is the whole task environment.

With referencing being the primary communication need within the peripheral space, collaborative MR systems need to support the acquisition of spatial knowledge with respect to the region [ 14 , 58 ] since experts typically need to know where an object is to be able to guide novices to it [ 15 , 117 ]. This is especially crucial for remote guidance for physical tasks where objects in the environment play a crucial communicative role [ 18 , 87 ] as their physical features and spatial relationships are directly used in the conversation. Moreover, remote guidance is most impactful for complex procedures that require specialized knowledge which often use customized tools, or those that have non-descriptive eponymous names, or both. For example, surgical sub-specialties tend to use obscure instruments that can be hard to recognize from just their names or descriptions without extensive training [ 12 , 29 ]. The lack of domain knowledge among novices, therefore, means that they might not know what to look for or be able to identify a tool even if they visually register it without additional support from experts.

Researchers have also explored combining 3D reconstructions with non-3D views of the task environment to provide experts with the benefits of a real-time updated 3D view as well as spatial information from the peripheral space while conserving computational resources. For example, ARTEMIS [ 41 ] provided remote experts with a 3D view of the surgical site on the patient and opted to use 2D video feeds to provide information about the rest of the environment; other systems combined 3D reconstructions with 360 videos [ 75 , 94 , 114 ]. However, the limited depth perception offered by these views [ 115 ] makes it difficult for experts to comprehend details and object locations without extensive verbal communication and interactions with the novice.

Room-scale immersive experiences create a compelling sense of "being there" and this enhanced perceptual richness increases users’ comprehension of the virtual environment [ 108 ]. Users are, therefore, more likely to engage in more natural behaviors [ 109 ] and show higher levels of presence [ 9 ] and engagement [ 60 ] which can be especially beneficial for complex tasks [ 53 ]. Combining pre-scanned 3D models of environments with live 3D reconstructions of smaller areas is another approach that reduces computational complexity without making compromises on the extent of immersion experienced. For example, Dou and Fuchs [ 25 ] scanned static portions of a room and aligned live data of moving artifacts in the space to create a noise-free 3D reconstruction of the whole environment. Lee et al. [ 77 ] combined high-fidelity static objects that are actively worked on during the task with a lower-fidelity real-time updated point cloud of the peripheral space that experts could interact with. The main advantage of this approach is that the static portions can be processed in advance to increase the visual fidelity of the final reconstruction, leading to higher levels of realism and perceived presence in the virtual environment [ 120 ]. While prior research has shown that such a static 3D view is sufficient for remote experts to understand the spatial relationships between objects [ 40 ], the disadvantage is that any information represented by the static portions becomes outdated as the task goes on, especially when the locations of objects within it change.

2.3 Leveraging Spatial Capabilities in Collaborative MR

One way to overcome the difficulty of acquiring spatial awareness in prior work has been to leverage the MR's spatial and environmental understanding so experts don't have to rely on just a view of the environment. For instance, SceneCam [ 99 ] uses the local user's proximity to task areas to automatically choose the best view for remote collaborators, making it easier to keep track of relevant actions for the current stage of the task. It also provides remote users with a map of the local user's position with respect to the different spaces in the task environment. Johnson et al. [ 58 ] also provide a similar 2D map that shows the positions of both relevant task objects and the novice in the local environment that reduced the expert's reliance on a view of the environment to form spatial awareness of the task space.

Despite the success of these efforts, having information about the environment alone is often not enough, since experts still need to rely on verbal communication to guide the novice's attention to relevant task areas or artifacts. When physically present, experts use non-verbal gestures and communication mechanisms like deixis that reduce the effort it takes for them to refer to objects and for that reference to be understood by novices [ 18 ]. Today's collaborative MR systems take advantage of the spatial capabilities of the medium to help remote experts use these mechanisms for referencing. This includes representing their hands in the novice environment [ 53 , 54 ], annotation tools [ 41 ], virtual pointers [ 116 ], head pointers [ 124 ], and sharing gaze cues [ 92 , 93 ]. However, by imitating how we make references in the real world, these features suffer from the same drawbacks, especially in cluttered or complex spaces—references made can be ambiguous and occluded objects can't be referred to at all. Moreover, the use of these features still relies on experts’ situational awareness which is bottlenecked by the view of the environment. For example, experts cannot use virtual pointers to refer to objects without already being aware of where they are [ 14 , 18 , 58 ].

Advances in object detection and tracking have helped enhance the awareness of collaborative MR systems and enabled features that both reduce referential ambiguity and the need for experts to constantly keep track of objects in the environment. The TAC system [ 8 ] highlighted objects in the task space when experts clicked on a region of their video feed, allowing experts to highlight specific objects and regions even if they clicked on the approximate area of interest. AlphaRead [ 13 ] allowed experts to label objects in the environment, and tracking allowed for labeled objects to be referred to even when they were moved or out of the experts’ view. Johnson et al. [ 58 ] allowed remote users to click on the icons of objects on a separate interface that initiated visual cues that guided the novice to it. Experts could therefore make unambiguous references to objects without having to know where they were.

2.4 Our Design Strategy

In this paper, we build on the prior work discussed so far to design a fully immersive room-scale interface for remote experts providing MR-mediated guidance during physical tasks. A key differentiating factor of our design approach (Fig. 1 ) is that it supports the inclusion of distinct features within the active and peripheral spaces of the task environment that cater to the differing expert needs in them. We provide a live 3D view through a real-time updated point cloud within the active space, where experts need dynamic updates of the novice actions and support for both referencing and communicating action; experts can also refer to artifacts within this view using a 3D "finger pointer" object and communicate any necessary object manipulations using shared virtual replicas of the task objects. For the peripheral space, where experts primarily need referencing support, we immerse them in a static 3D mesh of the task environment and augment it with the ability to initiate visual cues that guide novices to task objects within it. This allows us to have a life-sized relatively high-fidelity view of the space that preserves the experts’ ability to unambiguously refer to objects, without the need for real-time updates to the mesh.

Any view of the environment—live or static—requires experts to expend effort towards gathering and synthesizing information about the task space from it. Experts still need to perform a visual search [ 28 ] within the virtual environment to find the tools or objects they would like to refer to or guide novice's attention towards. And while this may be trivial in unmediated collocated collaborations, technology-mediated views of spaces change the nature of how experts experience and prioritize their attention, even without the added seams introduced by the communication medium [ 50 ]. In their paper, Johnson et al. [ 58 ] found that experts performed better when the buttons corresponding to task objects were presented as a list than when it mapped to the novice's task environment, and having spatial awareness in addition to system guidance to objects hindered the communication between experts and novices. While a list would be difficult to parse for complex tasks with a lot of objects in the space, one implication is that experts might not need the information presented to resemble or map to the spatial layout of the novice's task environment.

The inclusion of system guidance potentially reduces experts’ need to acquire the spatial information needed for referencing within the peripheral space. Moreover, the disconnected views for the two subspaces in our design approach allow us to independently change one without impacting the other. There is, therefore, an added opportunity to immerse experts in a different peripheral space without impeding their access to a dynamic view of the active space. In this paper, we explore and evaluate an alternative to recreating the novice's peripheral task space that preserves the benefits of an immersive environment while reducing the need for experts to understand unfamiliar environments by leveraging their existing spatial memories.

3 RESEARCH APPROACH: LEVERAGING SITUATED EXPERIENCES

Our presence in the physical world and the intimate relationship between our cognition, behavior, and external artifacts has been extensively studied under an umbrella of theories including embodied, situated, embedded, and enacted cognition [ 88 , 104 , 122 ]. In moving beyond screen-bound experiences, immersive and spatial interfaces have inspired HCI practitioners to integrate how we interact with the physical world to create new design paradigms [ 64 , 121 ].

Collaborative MR systems have features that build on these theories to reduce the effort it takes to communicate. Virtual pointers, gaze cues, and reconstructed hands are all technology-mediated equivalents of how we communicate with our bodies in the real world. The ability for collaborators to annotate in remote environments also allows for cognitive offloading [ 104 ] that minimizes working memory demands, and virtual replicas that can be manipulated to communicate complex actions enable experts to use epistemic actions that reduce their cognitive load [ 65 ].

A key consideration under these theories is also how we manage and adapt to the space around us. Physical task experts, in particular, have their own workspaces where they practice their craft and accrue specialized and tacit knowledge over time that is tied to their situated experiences in these spaces [ 11 , 81 ]. More specifically, experts form extensive spatial memories in spaces that they are familiar with [ 48 , 102 ]. This memory of artifact locations and the spatial relations between them makes it easy to retrieve objects in their environments with little to no effort [ 32 , 102 ]. Experts have also been known for "jigging" or structuring their environments to simplify perception and reduce the cognitive and physical effort it takes for them to complete tasks [ 63 ]. Even when there are standard spatial layouts of tools or instruments for a particular task, experts will modify the layout of their workspaces where object locations are personally meaningful to optimize their own performance.

When it comes to the practical use of collaborative MR systems, experts will realistically use them multiple times with novices in different environments. For example, over time a specialist trauma surgeon will help medical personnel with emergency care in rural hospitals, in ambulances, or during field surgeries. However, remote collaborations in general require sustained focus and effort that inevitably introduces a form of techno-stress and fatigue. The rise in remote work during the COVID-19 pandemic caused a phenomenon researchers now term "videoconferencing fatigue" [ 6 ] that, among many other things, is caused by the integration of frequent remote collaboration sessions to one's work practice [ 24 ]. We can be fairly certain that the added complexities of understanding remote environments and assisting with spatial and physical tasks from afar on a regular basis will only compound this effect, especially in critical and fast-paced settings. It is therefore crucial that we design collaborative MR systems to both reduce the effort it takes to communicate in individual sessions and simplify repeated use.

Computer interfaces have long been designed to support spatial memory and enable recall and recognition of different interface elements [ 105 ]. In general, the usability of an interface increases when users are familiar with it and a spatially-stable interface allows users to build spatial memory within it, eliminating the need for visual search. 3D interfaces support this spatial memory in an extremely intuitive way allowing users to accurately recall object locations in a spatial arrangement [ 19 , 78 ].

For these reasons, our work leverages remote experts’ situated experiences to make both single and repeated use of collaborative MR systems easier. More specifically, we hypothesize that immersing remote experts in a familiar and spatially-stable environment in which they already have established spatial memories will ease communication and reduce the overall effort it takes to provide remote guidance for physical tasks and make repeated use of collaborative MR systems easier. To test our hypothesis we designed and built two prototype systems based on our design approach— Mapped and UnMapped . These systems are identical except for one key difference in the peripheral space: UnMapped immersed remote experts in a static 3D recreation of their own familiar workspace where they have preexisting spatial awareness, and Mapped immersed them in a static 3D recreation of the novice's environment.

4 PROTOTYPE DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION

In this section, we provide details of the features that make up the Mapped and UnMapped prototype systems and outline the implementation details for them.

4.1 System Overview and User Features

Our prototype collaborative MR system provides an immersive VR interface for remote experts using an Oculus Quest 2 headset 1 , and an MR interface for local novices on a Microsoft HoloLens 2 headset 2 . The audio channel for our MR system was enabled via TeamSpeak 3 .

4.1.1 Static 3D Peripheral Space: Once in VR, the expert is immersed in a room-scale static 3D recreation of either the novices task environment ( Mapped , Fig. 2 -Left) or their own workspace ( UnMapped , Fig. 2 -Right). The static 3D scans of the experts’ environments were made using a Matterport camera 4 . Experts could move around freely in VR within this 3D environment. The environment was also equipped with virtual objects that corresponded to task tools and a finger pointer object (Fig. 2 -3). The virtual task tools could be present either on surfaces (Fig. 2 -4) or within cabinets in the peripheral space (Fig. 2 -5).

4.1.2 Shared Active Space: The novices’ task environment was equipped with an Azure Kinect 5 (RGB+D camera) positioned over the active task space (Fig. 4 ). This camera captured and streamed a 3D color point cloud to the VR interface in real-time and allowed experts to have a view of artifacts and novice actions within the active space. The shared point cloud was embedded on top of the experts’ static 3D VR environment and housed within yellow borders so users could distinguish the live regions from the static parts in VR (Fig. 2 -1). Importantly, experts could not see live updates for any part of the novice's environment outside of this point cloud.

4.1.3 Using Virtual Replicas and Finger Pointer: Our prototype system allowed experts to move the virtual finger pointer and create replicas of the virtual task objects placed in the peripheral space. To create a virtual replica, experts could "grab" the corresponding virtual tool with their VR controllers or "click" on it with a ray cast (laser) pointer. Experts could also click on cabinets to reveal virtual objects within them on a 2D menu that showed icons corresponding to the objects, allowing the expert to select any of them (cabinets could be "closed" by clicking anywhere outside the menu). Clicked objects would spawn a replica in a designated Replica Space for easy reach and subsequent manipulation (Fig. 2 -2). The original virtual object would stay in its position but experts could now bring either the virtual finger or a replica (or both) into the active space to communicate actions or tool manipulations to the novice. When these objects were in the expert's active space, novices would see them as 3D holograms in their space as well (Fig. 3 -1). Experts were free to move these objects outside the active space as needed, at which point the novice would no longer see the corresponding hologram in their space.

4.1.4 System Guidance cues in MR: When experts interacted with a virtual replica of tools in the peripheral space, our prototype system automatically guided novices to the same or similar tool in their own environment if it existed. The visual cues for this guidance included 3D arrows (Fig. 3 -2), and a circular indicator around the tool (Fig. 3 -3) inspired by similar cues designed for the HoloLens’ small field of view [ 59 ].

4.2 Study-Specific Features

We added additional features to our prototype that would facilitate our study. First, the VR interface for the remote expert also included a button (Fig. 2 -6) that would expand to show the broad goals of the task. This was done so that experts had an easy way to view their goals when in VR and ensured that our study measures were not reflective of individual differences in their retention of the task design.

Second, we did not implement real-time object detection and tracking in our prototype system and instead opted to mock this in a Wizard-of-Oz (WoZ) fashion [ 20 , 27 ] that included a VR setup, MR set up, and tablet WoZ interface. Since we needed to scan multiple environments for the study, the VR setup allowed researchers to choose from a library of 3D scans to select the static peripheral space for individual participants. It also allowed researchers to customize locations of the point cloud, Replica Space, and virtual task objects—this reflected where things were in the novice environment or expert's workspace for Mapped and UnMapped users respectively. The MR setup allowed researchers to manually set the location of physical tools in the novice's environment prior to a study session which the HoloLens used for the guidance feature; and the tablet WoZ interface allowed a "Wizard" to "de-highlight" objects and remove visual cues to it from a list of currently highlighted objects.

Lastly, the VR and MR setup included a calibration step to ensure that experts’ demonstrations within their active space were in the right position relative to the region captured by the point cloud. We were unable to use marker tracking since the task objects and novice actions could obscure any markers we placed. Instead, we did a simple point-to-point calibration that created a reference pose similar to one retrieved from a fiducial marker where the researcher manually selected 3 set points on the surface under the Kinect and then selected those same 3 points within the point cloud in the VR interface before a session began.

4.3 Implementation Details

Our prototype system was implemented in three modules: the VR system, the MR system, and the WoZ system which are connected by a web server through a series of web sockets, HTTP GET and POST requests to the server and several custom TCP sockets. Both the VR and MR systems were developed using the Unity3D game engine 6 with the Oculus Integration 7 and MRTK-v2 8 packages, respectively. The web server is hosted on AWS 9 , and runs on NodeJs 10 . The server also hosts the WoZ interface as a Progressive Web App, which the WoZ tablet accesses via the internet.

The VR system consists of a VR headset connected to a desktop PC. The PC handles rendering and socket connections to both the MR system and web server, while the headset simply displays content and facilitates interaction via the built-in Oculus Link software 11 . When experts interact with a tool, an HTTP POST message is sent to the web server and a TCP message is sent to the MR system. The MR system consists of a HoloLens application and an Intel NUC 12 computer connected to the Kinect. Visual guidance cues are initiated when the MR system receives tool selection messages from the VR system and hidden when it receives dehighlighting messages from the WoZ system.

5 EXPERIMENT

Our study compared the effectiveness of the Mapped and UnMapped expert interfaces with repeated use through a 2x2 mixed factorial design. The between-subjects factor— Interface Type —had two levels: Mapped (I M ) and UnMapped (I U ). The within-subjects factor— Session —had two levels: Session 1 (S 1 ) and Session 2 (S 2 ) to study the impact of repeated use.

5.1 Experiment Task

Experts used either Mapped or UnMapped to guide novices to collaboratively perform a cake decorating task. This task involved creating a 2-layered cake that was frosted and then decorated using icing and fondant. We chose this task primarily because, much like a variety of tasks from surgery to car repair, it included an active and peripheral task space: the decorating counter where the cake is (Fig. 4 -Left) as the central focus for task actions, and the rest of kitchen holding the baking instruments required to complete the task as peripheral space. Cake decorating experts also spend a non-trivial amount of time perfecting their craft through formal training or in specific workspaces, and accrue concrete and tacit knowledge and decision-making skills based on what they are seeing, hearing, or feeling in the moment. Importantly, the task involves specialized tools for different operations that are typically difficult to distinguish (like different piping tips) and involves specific techniques that must be used to achieve the desired result (the ability to pipe designs with icing needs more knowledge than just knowing what a piping bag is). Lastly, this task made recruitment logistically easier during the COVID-19 pandemic when access to experts and their professional spaces was extremely difficult. Many home and professional bakers use (and jig) their private kitchens to practice and experiment with their skills, which eased our access to create 3D scans of expert workspaces. Additionally, the criteria for finding a true novice was simplified since a few questions could reliably predict their comfort level with baking tools.

In our task, experts were given a specific design that they needed to collaboratively achieve with novices. This included stacking two vanilla cake layers, using chocolate buttercream as both the filling and outer frosting (achieved with a knife, a regular spatula, and an offset spatula). They also used fondant, a rolling pin, and a cookie cutter to create decorations to be placed on the cake, after which a piping bag and different piping tips were used to achieve icing decorations with two piping techniques. This cake design was chosen based on a series of pilot rounds and was considered to be complex enough that a novice would find it hard to complete on their own, but simple enough that it could be finished within 20-30 minutes with guidance. The tools used were chosen to include objects that would be well known (knife, spatula, cookie cutter) and objects that novices either wouldn't know how to use (piping bag, coupler) or wouldn't know what they looked like (fondant extruder, specific piping tips).

5.2 Recruitment

We recruited a total of 60 participants of which there were 20 expert bakers and 40 novice bakers. In our experiment, each expert baker used either the Mapped or UnMapped interface (I M or I U ) and worked with two different novices to complete the same task across two sessions (S 1 or S 2 ). This resulted in a total of 40 sessions.

A baker was considered an expert if they baked on a regular basis and were comfortable frosting and decorating a 2-layered cake, and a novice if they don't bake at all and/or had never baked a cake or frosted anything before. The average age of the experts in our study was 27.45 (SD=3.28), 8 of the 20 were male, and 12 were female. 14 experts had used a VR headset at least once before the study. 2 of the 20 experts were professional bakers and 18 were skilled home bakers. The average age of the novices in our study was 25.53 (SD=5.16), 26 of the 40 were male, and 14 were female. 11 novices had used a HoloLens at least once before the study.

Novice bakers were assigned to expert bakers based on their available schedules and randomized when possible. Two of the experts were familiar with both their novices from before the study, and three experts were familiar with one novice before the study (counterbalanced across conditions). Two expert participants communicated that they were not comfortable with us scanning their kitchen and were directly assigned the Mapped condition. The rest were randomly assigned to Mapped or UnMapped before the study began to give us time to scan UnMapped experts’ kitchens.

5.3 Experiment Setup

5.3.1 Scanning Kitchens: Prior to the start of the study, one researcher scanned the personal kitchens of the 10 UnMapped experts with the Matterport camera to create their peripheral environment in VR. The researcher also made note of where experts kept their baking tools (including those that wouldn't be used in the task) and the counter they typically used for decorating—this information was used when setting up the interface prior to their session to ensure the tools would be where they are in their actual kitchen.

5.3.2 Expert Training: Experts were trained on the VR interface before their first session to bring them to similar comfort levels with VR and to familiarize them with features common to Mapped and UnMapped—how to use VR controllers, initiate guidance to objects, use virtual replicas, and view the task design goals should they need it. This training lasted around 7-10 minutes, was standardized across all 20 participants, and was conducted by the same researcher regardless of the study condition. The virtual environment of the training session was a scan of a random kitchen. Experts were not shown the novice interface or the exact guidance cues but they were aware that initiating guidance meant novices would see visual cues to the same or similar object in their space. This training also included going over the cake design of the experiment task.

5.3.3 Novice Environment Set Up: To study the impact of changing novice environments, novices used one of two kitchens (seen in Fig. 2 and 5 ) and the order of which kitchen was used for Session 1 and 2 was counterbalanced. Each kitchen had a different layout but contained the same tools (which also included objects regularly found in kitchens that wouldn't be used in the task). However, the location of the tools was changed between spaces. The ease or difficulty of finding objects was kept similar (similar number of items within reach, on counters further away, and within cabinets). Researchers also coded these object positions within the environment for the system using the MR setup interface prior to a session.

5.3.4 Expert Environment Set Up: Experts used the VR interface in a separate room on the same floor as the two kitchens (Fig. 5 ). These spaces were far enough from each other that participants in one couldn't look into or hear the other. Before the start of a session, researchers chose the appropriate 3D scan for the peripheral space and used either the locations of objects in the novice kitchens or the expert's kitchen to place objects on surfaces and within cabinets in VR through the VR setup interface. The expert's space was also where the Wizard researcher for the experiment operated the WoZ system from.

5.4 Study Procedure

When the participants arrived, two researchers introduced the study and explained the consent forms to the novice and expert separately outside of their respective spaces. While experts were asked to use the appropriate tools for each step if they could, their main goal was to complete the cake design. Once they reached that goal, they were free to continue decorating the cake more if they felt like it. Participants were then taken into their spaces where the researchers helped put their headsets on and started the session.

Novices were given no information about the cake design or environment or tools prior to the session. This ensured that their skill gap with the expert and familiarity with the task environment mimicked real-world scenarios (like emergency repairs and trauma surgeries) where remote guidance is used in the absence of local expertise—novices tend to be untrained in the task procedure, don't know or easily recognize specialized tools, and are often in unfamiliar environments.

While there was no formal training required for the novice, once a session started, experts were asked to guide novices to a spoon that was placed in the novice environment, and use the replica of the spoon as a system test and to help them get acquainted with each other and the interface. This was also the first time the novice saw the exact visual cues for guidance. Once this was complete, participants were free to complete the task however they chose. During the task, the Wizard researcher used the WoZ interface and a live feed of the novice's FPV to de-highlight objects when the novice picked them up. If it was obvious that guidance was initiated by mistake, the researcher removed those cues too.

After the session, experts and novices were given individual post-task questionnaires to complete. Once these were completed, they met each other and talked about the task. Researchers used this time to conduct a post-task interview. The decorated cake was divided into two and given to both participants to take home.

5.5 Experiment Measures and Data Analysis

We recorded the novice's FPV from the HoloLens and actions in their environment using two external GoPro cameras 13 . We also recorded the expert's VR view and the audio channel between participants. These were synced and used for video coding using BORIS [ 34 ]. Two researchers independently coded interaction and communication behaviors for 40 sessions (double-coded 4 sessions and resolved any inconsistencies to create a final coding scheme). Qualitative observations from the recordings and participant feedback from open-ended questions on the survey and post-task interviews were also analyzed using an open coding approach and then clustered using axial codes that resulted in the various themes of our qualitative insights.

Communication behaviors were coded using a conversation analysis approach that included coding utterances (considered as the smallest unit of a conversation—this could be a full sentence or a phrase that's part of a longer sentence) and their intent, as well as references to objects and their type. These codes were created using a deductive approach influenced by prior work outlining collaborative communication [ 17 , 44 , 69 ] and refined for our study based on the double coded sessions. We only coded communication behaviors that corresponded to the peripheral space, because both Mapped and UnMapped experts were provided the same features within the active space and any communication within it would not reflect behavior changes due to the familiar or unfamiliar peripheral space. Additionally, this allowed us to reduce the impact of individual teaching styles in our measures (which primarily happened in the active space).

Our measures also included interface interaction counts, total task completion time, search time (time interval from when the expert first called for a tool to when the novice picked up the same tool), NASA TLX Task Load Index [ 47 ], Co-Presence and Perceived Message Understanding scores for collaborators’ Social Presence [ 46 ], Self-Location and Possible Actions scores for experts’ Spatial Presence [ 118 ], and a measure of experts’ cognitive representation of the novice environment using the Spatial Situation Model subscale [ 118 ]. We also included two Likert-scale questions in the post-task questionnaires ("I could tell when my partner needed assistance" and "I was able to assist my partner") to measure experts’ perceived ability to provide guidance and modified the same questions to measure the perceived quality of guidance from the novices point of view as well.

6 RESULTS

The data collected in our experiment were analyzed using a two-way mixed-model ANOVA. Survey responses were tested for internal reliability (Cronbach's Alpha > 0.7) and then analyzed by applying the nonparametric aligned rank transform [ 123 ] which enabled the use of ANOVA after alignment and ranking. We also conducted pairwise comparisons on our data when appropriate to understand the differences between Mapped and UnMapped for each Session individually. We use an alpha of 0.05 in the results below to present the main effects of Interface Type and Session as well as the results of any pairwise comparisons. We saw no interaction effects in any of our measures and they are thus not mentioned below. Additionally, we only report the main effect of repeated use if it was significant in the interest of brevity. In the remainder of this section, F represents the F-value, p the p-value, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ the effect size, M the mean, and SD the standard deviation. The error bars in the figures depict standard errors.

6.1 Task Completion and Search Time

Participants completed the task significantly faster in Session 2 when compared to Session 1 (F(1,18)=4.631, p=0.045, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ =0.205) and when the expert used UnMapped compared to when they used Mapped (F(1,18)=5.4, p=0.032, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ =0.231). Pairwise comparison showed that this difference was not significant in Session 1, but using UnMapped led to significantly faster completion times in Session 2 (p=0.044) (Fig. 6 -Left).

Isolating the time it took for participants to collaboratively search for and find the tools required for the task revealed that UnMapped led to significantly faster search times overall (F(1,18)=24.763, p=<.001, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ =0.579) as well as in Session 1 and 2 independently (p=<.001 for both) (Fig. 6 -Right).

6.2 Collaborator Task Load

Repeated use significantly decreased experts’ task load (F(1,18)= 10.472, p=0.005, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ =0.368). Experts also reported a significantly lower task load when they used UnMapped compared to when they used Mapped (F(1,18)=5.569, p=0.03, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ =0.236). Pairwise comparisons showed that this difference was significant in both Session 1 (p=0.042) and Session 2 (p=0.04). No significant differences were found in the novices’ task load regardless of the Session or experts’ interface (Fig. 7 ).

6.3 VR Interface Interactions

The total number of interactions that experts made in VR during the task was significantly lower when experts used UnMapped (F(1,18)=8.502, p=0.009, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ =0.321) overall and in Session 1 (p=0.005). Experts tended to open the virtual cabinets when searching for task tools in their environment and Mapped experts opened a significantly higher number of cabinets overall (F(1,18)=16.783, p=<.001, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ =0.483) and in both Session 1 (p=0.001) and 2 (p=0.05). Mapped experts also made significantly fewer initiations for guidance cues (F(1,18)=4.438, p=0.049, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ =0.198) and this difference was significant in Session 2 (p=0.023) (Fig. 8 ).

6.4 Communication Efficiency and Patterns

Both experts and novices used fewer utterances regarding the peripheral space when the expert used UnMapped compared to when they used Mapped (F(1,18)=9.327, p=0.007, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ =0.341 for experts, and F(1,18)=15.683, p=<.001, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ =0.466 for novices). Pairwise comparison showed that this significant effect held true for both Session 1 (Expert utterance, p=0.017; Novice utterance, p=0.003) and Session 2 (Expert utterance, p=0.022; Novice utterance, p=0.009).

We also analyzed the intent for each expert utterance and the resulting communication pattern variations between Mapped and UnMapped are visualized in Fig. 9 -Top. An utterance could have one or more of the following intents, and the measures below are the percentage of the total number of utterances of a given intent:

  1. Referring to Objects : This was when experts made a direct reference (whether a noun or deixis) to any object (relevant to the task or not) in the passive space (Example: "Now we're going to grab the knife"). UnMapped experts made significantly more direct object references overall (F(1,18)=21.957, p=<.001, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ =0.55), and in both Session 1 (p=0.005) & 2 (p=0.003).
  2. Describing Objects : These utterances served to provide novices with additional details and descriptions of the object the expert was trying to guide their attention towards (Example: "It should look like a metal tube with a T on top"). UnMapped utterances to describe objects were significantly lower when compared to Mapped overall (F(1,18)=4.822, p=0.041, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ =0.211) and in Session 2 (p=0.033).
  3. Providing Context : These included utterances where the intent was to provide additional explanations or context around the task and why they were doing what they were doing (Example: "Having that coupler there makes it really easy to change piping tips"). There were no significant differences in how much context was provided between UnMapped and Mapped sessions.
  4. Priming for guidance cues : Experts also primed novices, essentially giving them a heads up when they initiated guidance to objects (Example: "you should see an arrow to it now"). UnMapped experts primed for guidance significantly more than Mapped overall (F(1,18)=4.832, p=0.041, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ =0.212).
  5. Acknowledgements : These utterances primarily served as a confirmation that the novice understood the right thing or identified the correct object (Example: "okay" or "yeah, that one"). UnMapped expert utterances had significantly lower elements of acknowledgments in Session 2 (p<.001).

We also dove deeper into the type of references experts used when they guided novices’ attention to objects outside the active space. There were no significant differences in the number of references made by experts across either session or between those who use Mapped or UnMapped. However, there were differences in the patterns of what type of references tended to be used (Fig. 9 -Bottom). The measures below show the percentage of the total number of references of a given type:

  1. Tool Noun or Description : UnMapped experts made significantly more references that use a tool name or a description of it overall (F(1,18)=4.584, p=0.046, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ =0.203) and during Session 1 (p=0.031).
  2. Deictic Pronouns : These references included words like "that" which needed additional context to be understood. The difference in the use of deictic pronouns was not significant across interface types.
  3. Direct Spatial References : Mapped experts made significantly more references to areas in the peripheral space (like "in the corner") overall (F(1,18)=8.475, p=0.009, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ =0.32) and in Session 1 (p=0.002).
  4. Spatial Deixis : Mapped experts also made significantly more references to relative positions within the peripheral space that was deictic in nature (like "to your right") overall (F(1,18)= 8.876, p=0.008, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ =0.33) and for both Session 1 (p=0.049) and 2 (p=0.005).
  5. Non-Tool References : UnMapped experts made significantly fewer references to other objects in the task space that were not tools required for the task overall (F(1,18)=10.985, p=0.004, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ =0.379) and for both Session 1 (p=0.01) and 2 (p=0.047).

6.5 Self-Reported Questionnaire Scores

Both experts and novices reported similar social presence scores regardless of which interface the expert used (Fig. 10 ). Experts also reported no differences in their perceived ability to provide guidance, while novices reported no perceivable change in how well they were being assisted by the experts in any of our conditions (Fig. 10 ). Additionally, experts reported similar levels of spatial presence in both Mapped and UnMapped interfaces (Fig. 11 ). There were no significant effects observed for any of these measures.

A measure of experts’ mental model or cognitive representation of the novice's environment revealed significantly lower scores for UnMapped experts when compared to Mapped experts (F(1,18)=9.525, p=0.006, $\eta _{p}^{2}$ =0.346) (Fig. 11 ). There were no other significant effects.

7 OBSERVATIONAL RESULTS AND PARTICIPANT FEEDBACK

In this section, we report on common feedback and observations across all 40 sessions and discuss our qualitative results specific to the use of Mapped and UnMapped overall and with repeated use.

Experts in our study used the live point cloud of the active space to successfully sustain task status awareness. However, the noise and distortion inherent with point clouds hindered their ability to view fine details on the cake. The camera being positioned overhead also meant that the sides of the cake weren't always visible and novices often blocked the view when working on the cake. As such experts found "the task view was a bit irritating" [E16]. However, experts learned to work with the imperfections by having novices rotate the cake to accommodate parallax errors and occasionally step back for a full unblocked view. Experts also used their own experiences to "fill in the gaps" [E8] and relied on verbal communication to keep track of subtle changes within the active space. Additionally, tracking system drifts introduced calibration errors that made pointing at specific parts of the cake hard, especially for "fine motor movement [like] precise frosting movements" [E13], so experts resorted to gesturing at broad regions and providing more specific information verbally.

Experts didn't seem to be affected by the lack of dynamic updates about any "prep" actions unless novices took longer than expected outside the active space without verbal updates. When that happened, experts would ask for an update after a period of silence. Having a static view of the peripheral space meant that experts couldn't confirm whether the novice had the correct tool. Novices, therefore, "held tools in front of the [point cloud] for [the expert]"[N7] but experts still found it challenging to provide confirmation because their point cloud was "grainy"[E1] and metallic instruments wouldn't show up well.

Experts and novices both found system guidance "tremendously helpful [because] it avoided having to explain the features of tools" [E18] and mentioned that it "was the easiest part" [N4], especially for cluttered areas or when tool names didn't give novices a sense of what to look for. E16 articulated this sentiment saying "having the objects pointed out helped immensely, it made not having a great view tolerable" . In supporting the referencing that's crucial for physical tasks, system guidance allowed experts to easily direct novices’ attention to objects without extensive verbal descriptions. Even when experts knew tool locations, they preferred "not having to give [their] partner exact locations" [E20] and novices found the visual cues "much more useful than understanding [the expert's] speech" [N3].

Experts and novices also emphasized that using virtual replicas to demonstrate action was key to seamless communication when it included specialized baking tools and techniques that were alien to the novice. N7 reiterated this saying "I liked the piping demonstrations with the holograms...I would have no idea if you would have audio described it to me" .

7.1 Behavioral and Experience Differences Between Mapped and UnMapped

7.1.1 Being in a familiar environment increased experts’ confidence and decreased stress: In general, UnMapped experts had a more jovial tone and voiced feeling extremely comfortable during the task. They mentioned that "it genuinely kind of felt like I was working in my kitchen" [E8], and that this familiarity of the environment made the task less daunting. For instance, E19 mentioned "it actually put me more at ease just because of my environment and I felt more calm" . E6 also mentioned that this came as a surprise, saying "I thought it wouldn't matter at first but then it was actually really helpful because I can just be like ’oh where do I keep the offset spatula’...I feel it reduced a little bit of my mental load [which was] unexpected and I actually felt like [the novice] was in my space making a cake" .

UnMapped experts also emphasized that it wasn't just their memory of object locations that made them feel more confident—having familiar objects in their peripheral vision that they were used to while working in their own kitchens also played a part. E18 recalled this phenomenon saying "I found it very helpful [that] I could even see my plants a little bit, that actually helps a lot...obviously it was more distorted than reality but it was still helpful" .

7.1.2 Mapped allowed for collaborative searches, UnMapped removed the need for searching: When the position of a tool wasn't obvious from a quick scan of the peripheral space, Mapped experts described the tool they were looking for and engaged in a collaborative search where both participants moved around and opened cabinets in their respective environments. Some experts searched their virtual environment as they were describing the tool and only asked novices to join in if they hadn't spotted it when they were done with the description. Mapped experts found searching was especially hard when objects were occluded or in unexpected locations and mentioned the imperfections in the 3D scan made visual search harder. There were two Mapped sessions where neither participant found the relevant tool but experts were able to assist novices with using other tools as a workaround.

Experts who used UnMapped, on the other hand, found directing novices to baking instruments trivial and intuitive since they were "already familiarized [with the environment]...this is very easy on my end...I know the rolling pin is going to be where it is in my actual home, my baking tools are in these drawers...it was really nice way to navigate through the kitchen" [E11]. They were also more forgiving towards the imperfections of the 3D scan and said that "it just felt like the apartment was covered in paper" [E7] and that they stopped noticing the imperfections once they focused on the cake and didn't need a detailed view to find objects in it.

7.1.3 Mapped views required experts to attend to the full task environment, UnMapped views allowed them to focus on the active space: Having spatial awareness of the novices peripheral space allowed Mapped experts to use spatial references and other objects to ground their references even when they used guidance. However, the post-task interviews revealed that experts felt the need to constantly scan the full environment to ensure the task was moving forward smoothly. They would often visually scan the peripheral space for the next tool while novices completed the current step and primarily used verbal updates to keep track of actions in the active space. E3 mentioned that splitting their attention between the two spaces so novices had less searching to do when they reached the next step was the "hardest part" .

In contrast, UnMapped experts didn't need to expend energy towards finding objects and also experienced some downtime when novices were completing relatively long actions like frosting the cake. None of the experts realized the novice in the second session was in a different environment. Many experts mentioned noticing something different but "just assumed that was a slightly different positioning of the camera" [E11]. When asked whether they would have preferred to see the novices’ full environment, UnMapped experts said "if I was in the same space as [the novice] I could be like ’oh its on your left’...[but] honestly it has very little impact because once we are in the task, I'm just looking at the task space, I don't care about the surroundings" [E16]. E18 and E13 echoed this feedback saying "all I needed to see was the cake and ingredients, it didn't really matter where it would have been" because "none of my [instructions] were about [the] space, it was about the actual action" .

7.1.4 A Mapped or UnMapped view impacted the use of virtual replicas for acknowledgement: We observed that virtual replicas were often used as a form of acknowledgment and visual confirmation for novices to check if they got the right tool. However, Mapped experts tended to do so as a way to repair other forms of acknowledgment—like when verbal descriptions were not enough or when the point cloud view was not clear. Once UnMapped experts were confident that the guidance cues were reliable, they no longer needed verbal acknowledgments and preemptively put virtual replicas in the active space such that novices saw it on their way back from picking up the tool. For example, E8 told their novice "I'm gonna have you grab another type of spatula, an offset spatula <clicks to initiate guidance and moves replica into active space> you should see arrows to it now. I also put it in your space" . As the task progressed, experts stopped explicitly stating this and the acknowledgments became non-verbal, making the task flow more seamless.

7.1.5 Mapped experts compared their experience to being collocated, UnMapped experts felt like it was a fundamentally different experience: While all experts mentioned feeling strongly immersed in VR, Mapped experts tended to compare their experience with instructing someone when collocated and recalled feeling restricted because "figuring out how to describe objects without being physically present to gesture or show things myself was strange and tricky" . They felt that although "the environment looks real, [they] can't really do anything...even though it feels like we are in the same space" [E1]. UnMapped experts, on the other hand, voiced that this experience was fundamentally different. Some experts felt like they were working with the novice in their own kitchen, and others felt like they were in a whole new hybrid space. When reflecting on this, E17 mentioned "I didn't feel like it was really my kitchen [since] there's some distortion which cuts the reality [and] I was not feeling like I was in [the novice space]...but I was feeling like we had the connection" .

7.1.6 The Mapped system can be used in the absence of system guidance, the UnMapped system is dependent on it working: While UnMapped experts found their experience enjoyable overall, they also reflected on the fact that they were "depending completely on the technology to point [the novice] to the right direction" [E18]. In our study, the HoloLens briefly lost tracking in the novice environment in two Mapped sessions and one UnMapped session which resulted in the visual cues pointing novices in a different direction from where the object of interest was. Mapped experts were still able to use the static view to search for objects and redirect novices to them while the UnMapped expert could not do more than verbally describe the object and let the novice search for it independently.

7.2 Learning from Repeated Use

All experts felt more confident in their abilities to provide effective guidance in the second session because they got used to the interface and knew what to expect from it. They also modified their instructions because they thought "about the things [where the] first novice [got] confused and [were] trying to proactively address them" [E17]. Experts also prepped novices about the technological limitations at the start of the task and often reminded them that their additional questions were because of it. For example, E6 constantly reassured their novice when asking for more information saying things like "I just wanted to double check, it's just the limitations of the VR" .

In general, experts trusted the guidance feature more and used fewer verbal acknowledgments when they used it the second time. Reflecting on it, E18 said "I had a better sense of like...they can see [an arrow] right now, this is fine...so I had less of a feeling of [saying] ’look around for an arrow’...I trusted [the feature] more" . However, more trust in the feature didn't impact Mapped experts’ ability to use it more and the primary difference we saw in the second session compared to their first was that they looked around the peripheral space more intently during the start to preemptively find tools, but would let novices do more independent searching for tools they didn't catch in this initial scan when the time for the steps that involved it came.

8 DISCUSSION

Our results indicate that when paired with a context-aware system that can direct novices to objects of interest, a static view of the peripheral space and a live view of the active space in a task environment allows remote experts to experience the benefits of a fully immersive interface that sufficiently supports their ability to provide effective guidance during collaborative physical tasks.

8.1 The Impact of a Familiar Environment on Remote Guidance

Having remote experts immersed in a familiar environment where they have previously established spatial memories led to better performance and decreased task load. Additionally, the disconnect with the novice's task environment did not impact the experts’ spatial presence within the virtual environment. The UnMapped approach also allowed for a similar sense of social presence among collaborators, despite remote experts having a limited understanding of the novice's environment outside of the shared active space. Novices’ task load and perceived effectiveness of guidance were also not impacted by the experts being in a different environment from their own.

Being immersed in a recreation of their own workspaces where they had prior knowledge of object locations also meant that experts spent little to no time searching for task-relevant objects, making initiating system guidance for novices trivial. In contrast, when immersed in a recreation of the novice environment that was unfamiliar to them, they used this feature less as they relied on a collaborative search for objects with the novices if it wasn't readily visible to them. In fundamentally changing how effectively and frequently system guidance can be used, the UnMapped approach no longer needed experts to align or adapt how they refer to objects for it to be easily understood by the novice (a strategic process that's commonplace in object-focused communication [ 10 , 18 , 33 , 55 ]). Experts could use direct references and minimize the additional verbal information and spatial expressions they needed to disambiguate their phrases, resulting in more efficient communication.

The compounding effects of reduced visual search and communication effort when a familiar environment was paired with system guidance also led to larger experiential benefits. Experts perceived it to be fundamentally different from being collocated, and felt more at ease as it freed up their limited attentional resources. This also allowed them to focus on the actions within the active space where their expertise and tacit knowledge are most impactful. Designing for the nuances of expert needs during collaborative tasks, therefore, allowed us to leverage the strengths of an immersive environment to simplify perception and offload cognition [ 63 ]. In facilitating an experience that didn't fully imitate physical proximity, the UnMapped approach brings us a step closer to creating collaborative MR systems that allow us to go beyond being there [ 51 ].

8.2 The Implications of Having a Spatially Stable Environment on Repeated Use

The theory of least collaborative effort [ 16 ] suggests that collaborators minimize the total effort required for an interaction, and we often allocate our attention to environmental artifacts based on their ability to contribute to the overall task success [ 31 ]. This means that we often preemptively reduce the cognitive resources we make available if their perceived benefits don't substantially impact our collaborative goals. The added frustration of having to search unfamiliar environments [ 105 ] is clearly visible in our experiment. Specifically, the difficulty of understanding remote environments in the first session caused experts using the Mapped interface to only spend time comprehending the novice environment in the beginning of the task and opt to revert back to verbal communication (indicated by the increased heterogeneity in utterance patterns and the drop in significant differences of spatial references as well as qualitative insights) for objects that weren't found in this quick search. Experts who used UnMapped, on the other hand, experienced a more seamless conversation with their novices that included non-verbal acknowledgments because of their ability to use system guidance and their increased trust in it the second time.

Therefore, while the increase in overall performance and decrease in task load with repeated use indicate that all experts benefit from previous experience with the interface and the task, having a familiar environment that was spatially-stable across sessions provided additional benefits and heavily increased this effect. While it is difficult to isolate the impact of the familiar space from the effects of having just any spatially-stable interface in our study, we can reasonably predict that repeated use with varying novice environments is less disruptive with a stable peripheral space—even if it is one that doesn't initially hold any meaning to the expert. Experts can develop familiarity and spatial memory in any spatially-stable virtual environment over time, making this approach a valuable way to reduce the cognitive burden of understanding multiple remote environments when experts use collaborative MR systems regularly. Additionally, these environments can be designed to be personalizable, allowing experts to structure and progressively optimize their immersive interfaces to work better for themselves.

8.3 Designing for Practical Use and Limitations

Despite the relatively straightforward benefits of our design approach on collaborator experience, it is important to acknowledge the impact of technology on the practical implications of our work and limitations of our experiment.

First, the relatively lower-fidelity live 3D view of the active space made a non-trivial impact on user experiences. This could have impacted the self-reported questionnaire scores, especially for experts who used the Mapped interface, since they compared the experience to collocated communication more than experts who used the UnMapped interface. However, since this feature was kept constant across conditions, our results still reliably indicate that the UnMapped approach doesn't introduce additional negative effects to collaborator experiences. Moreover, our ability to provide real-time high-fidelity views will inevitably increase as technology gets better [ 66 ].

Second, the ability to provide system guidance as a feature is fundamentally dependent on technology like object tracking for tools in the novice's environment. Having an interactive static peripheral space also needs prior setup to curate the experts’ tools and environment based on the tasks they are predicted to provide guidance for, with UnMapped requiring additional personalization for experts. While we employed a WoZ approach for this, designers and system developers can choose from a plethora of techniques like computer vision, infrared markers, and RFID tags combined with machine learning or neural network architectures  [ 49 ] to detect and classify complex objects in the real world. These techniques have already been used in MR systems to track tools during physical tasks like engine components [ 80 ] and surgical instruments [ 82 , 91 ]. Similar methods can be used to detect and track domain-specific task objects in the novice's environment, and pair them with the tools in the expert's virtual task environment.

While this still requires computational resources and prior setup, a context-aware system with system guidance tied to a static 3D view allows us to provide a fully immersive environment while shifting the real-time complexity over from the network—where limited bandwidth and delays will likely remain a constraint in our near future—to individual local environments. Doing so also enables practitioners to choose the technology that best fits the needs of the task they are supporting as different techniques allow for varying levels of fidelity and accuracy [ 103 ]. For example, RFID tags provide a low-cost option that can cope well in a cluttered space [ 45 ] and real-time vision-based tracking can be modified to be computationally frugal [ 3 , 79 ]. It is, however, important to note that the Mapped interface is more conducive to recovery if technology fails with system guidance since experts can still make spatial references within the peripheral space based on the static view if objects’ locations haven't drastically changed.

Third, even if bandwidth wasn't a constraint, our design approach would allow system designers to use more computational resources to enhance and optimize the view and communication experience within the crucial active region of the task environment. Moreover, designing around experts’ spatial memories made them more tolerant towards artifact distortions in the recreated environment, and having this environment be their own workspace even introduced positive effects on their level of comfort due to the familiarity of objects in their peripheral vision. Additionally, while a VR interface allows experts to provide guidance from anywhere, the UnMapped approach could potentially be implemented as an MR interface that experts could use from within their physical workspaces as well.

Another factor to consider is that our experiment studies the impact of the peripheral space being a familiar environment where experts have established spatial memories. We believe a majority of our results primarily reflect the impact of this spatial memory where experts have preexisting knowledge of object locations in the UnMapped condition. While we did see experiential benefits of their environment being a familiar space (like lower stress and added comfort), we cannot reliably predict whether users would experience similar benefits without this prior knowledge of object locations. There is, therefore, merit in further exploring the isolated impact of familiar artifacts in the environment for remote collaborative tasks in the future.

We also acknowledge that our approach might not be suited for all types of physical tasks like room-scale tasks where the active space is the whole environment, tasks that don't use domain-specific tools, or if experts don't typically have a workspace where the spatial relationships of objects are meaningful to them. This approach would also be less impactful if novice environments don't vary, or the skill gap between collaborators is negligible as novices would already be familiar with the domain-specific tools used.

That said, remote collaborative systems are most impactful when local collaborators are true novices, especially in emergency and resource-strapped settings. Designing interfaces that ease participation for both experts and novices is thus crucial to extending our collective skills and expanding the reach of expertise. The overarching goal of our work, therefore, has been to examine the technology capabilities at our disposal to shape and explore effective design approaches that we can take when building remote collaborative MR systems. With this paper, we have added the Mapped and UnMapped approaches to the repertoire of possible design choices that practitioners can use to decide what features a specific task setting calls for.

9 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we introduced a new design approach for remote expert interfaces in context-aware collaborative MR systems, and explored an immersive environment that leveraged experts’ situated experiences in familiar workspaces as an alternative to recreating the complete task environment. We designed and built the UnMapped prototype which allowed remote experts to work out of static 3D reconstructions of their personal workspace that embedded a live 3D view of the active space within the novice's task environment. Experts could also use virtual replicas of task-relevant objects to communicate action and initiate system-generated visual guidance cues that directed novices towards tools in their peripheral space. We also built Mapped , an identical prototype that immersed remote experts in a static reconstruction of the novice's environment instead of their own workspace, and compared the effectiveness of UnMapped against it on remote guidance for physical tasks overall and with repeated use of the system through a 2x2 mixed factorial experiment.

Our results reveal multiple benefits of having a familiar and spatially-stable environment where experts have preexisting spatial memories, and showcases how this makes it more conducive for experts to use MR-mediated guidance systems as a routine part of their work practice. Despite having a limited understanding of the novice's environment, using the UnMapped interface increased performance and communication efficiency, while reducing task load for both experts and novices. UnMapped also allowed experts to focus their mental resources on the shared active space and eliminated the need for visual search within the peripheral task space. The paper also discusses the various benefits that providing remote experts with a familiar and spatially-stable environment has on collaborator experiences and sustained use.

Finally, through our work, we demonstrate the impact that considering users’ existing work practices and strengths has on creating more usable and effective collaborative MR systems. It is our hope that this inspires researchers and practitioners to explore the different design choices that technology affords to create rich collaborative experiences, even if it doesn't fully recreate reality or mimic how we collaborate with others in our physical spaces.
