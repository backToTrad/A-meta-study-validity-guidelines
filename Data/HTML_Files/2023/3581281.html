As voice assistant usage continues to grow, their homogeneity becomes even more problematic with the UNESCO report, “I'd Blush if I could” showing that designing only feminine voice assistants encourages negative behavior, both with virtual assistants and with real people [ 3 ]. While masculine text-to-speech (TTS) voices exist, ones that cover the full range of gender presentations, such as non-binary or gender-ambiguous voices are largely missing. In this paper, we present a method of creating a non-binary TTS voice and an example voice, Sam, created with input from the non-binary and transgender communities. We have open-sourced the resulting voice, along with the process and data used to create it. Finally, we present results from a large-scale survey showing that non-binary individuals are more likely to prefer a non-binary voice assistant compared to cisgendered individuals and discuss differences across age and gender.

1 INTRODUCTION

Voice assistant use in the U.S. has been growing steadily since 2017 [ 48 ] with the Covid-19 pandemic accelerating smart speaker adoption [ 6 , 36 ]. As of April 2021, an estimated 90 million users (or 35% of the US population) are already using smart speakers and the market is expected to grow to a $ 19 billion business by 2027 [ 68 ]. However, the individuals who adopt voice assistants into their daily routines consist of a diverse population including men, women, and non-binary individuals, who's gender identity is not limited to being either a man or a woman. So far, there's been very little effort put towards creating voice assistants that can represent and include non-binary individuals. But, in the U.S., LGBT self-identification has been on the rise, increasing from 3.5% in 2012 to 5.6% in 2020 [ 29 ]. Additionally, 12% of millennials identified as transgender or gender non-conforming in 2017 [ 49 ] and 56% of Gen Z'ers know someone who uses gender-neutral pronouns, such as they/them, to describe themselves [ 18 ]. All of these are examples of a growing openness to LGBTQ+ individuals more broadly and to non-binary identities specifically. U.S. devices, by contrast, have overwhelmingly feminine voices. Of the major four voice assistants on the market, all but one has a feminine name.

While there are many available masculine text-to-speech (TTS) voices, feminine voices predominantly represent TTS in media and commercial devices. Even when devices provide masculine voices as options, most users never change the default settings on their devices [ 8 ], which is why Apple has started to force users to pick Siri's voice, eliminating the default feminine voice, and has increased the number of voice options to a total of 4 without labeling them with a gender [ 44 ]. However, these voices still sound distinctly masculine or feminine with limited racial and dialectal representation. What's more, because of the role that these voice assistants play in our lives, interactions with them are seen as inherently social interactions [ 41 ]. In a study conducted by Google, 41% of people said that talking to a voice assistant felt like talking to a friend or another person [ 1 ]. But in nearly all these interactions, current U.S. voice assistant users can only “talk” to a feminine AI, which is proving problematic [ 60 ]. As a 2019 UNESCO report showed, designing only feminine voice assistants encourages negative behavior, both with the assistants and with real people by reinforcing that women should be in subservient roles [ 3 ]. Already, 5% of interactions with voice assistants are sexually explicit [ 3 ] and 30% are off-topic, abusive, romantic or sexual [ 33 ]. In sum, associating TTS voices with a single gender instead of a diverse representation creates problems.

To spark a discussion around gender stereotypes and to promote inclusion of LGBTQ+ communities in the design of conversational systems, Copenhagen Pride, Virtue, Equal AI, Koalition Interactive & Thirty Sounds Good teamed up to create Q, a genderless voice aimed at ending gender bias in AI assistants [ 4 ]. In Q's words: “I'm created for a future where we are no longer defined by gender, but rather how we define ourselves.” However, prior research has found that “an ambiguous voice is classified as strange, dislikable, dishonest and unintelligent.” Similar penalties are applied to systems where the user perceives a misalignment between the gender they assign a voice and the gender they assign a personality [ 42 ]. This prior research, however, was conducted in the 1990s and early 2000s and reported broad population preferences that did not extract individual preferences of smaller or marginalized groups. With changes in social norms, and increased acceptance of LGBTQ+ individuals, we might expect to also see some changes in user preference for these voices.

Prior research directly conflicts with the way in which non-binary individuals express themselves, mixing masculine and feminine speech patterns and intonation [ 23 , 24 , 25 ]. There is also a question of whether we should be aiming towards a “genderless” voice assistant, like Q or Siri claim to be [ 61 ]. Alternatively, to match the diversity that we as people have, we should be striving for a range of voices, that mix speech patterns and intonation in ways that are not necessarily aligned with traditional feminine and masculine norms, much like many non-binary individuals do today. Finally, when designing any system, we should be careful to include the demographic which we aim to represent in the design process. This ensures that we are properly designing for that demographic by taking their concerns and their wishes into account [ 51 ].

For each gender, including non-binary, voice characteristics can cover a broad spectrum of qualities. As there is no single man's or woman's voice, there is also not one non-binary voice and there is significant individual variability in how people express themselves. Our goal in this work was to create a process that can be leveraged by other researchers to create TTS voices that do not fit into a binary cisgender model. The process can be used to further study non-binary voices or the role of vocal traits in gender perception or used to create custom TTS voices by the non-binary community to meet their individual preferences. To that end, we present a method of creating a non-binary TTS voice that modifies the custom TTS voice creation process normally used. We use this process to create an example voice, Sam, created with input from the non-binary and transgender communities. While our non-binary TTS is geared towards Standard American English, we believe the process presented here can be repeated for other languages and dialects. As such, we have open-sourced the resulting voice, along with the process and data used to create it for use by researchers, developers and individuals interested in creating voices more representative of their preferences and themselves. Finally, we ran a large-scale survey to better understand differences between individuals across different ages and genders, and how they might perceive masculine, feminine, and non-binary voices when they do or do not conform to heteronormative expectations with respect to their speech patterns, word choices, and roles. Our results show that non-binary individuals are more likely to prefer a non-binary voice (21.4%) when compared to cisgendered individuals (1.4%). We end with an in-depth discussion of our survey results, a reflection on our design process, and the implications of open sourcing data pertaining to a marginalized community that may be misappropriated. In summary, the contributions of this work are as follows:

  • A method to create a non-binary TTS voice with input from the non-binary and transgender communities.
  • An example voice, Sam. The data for which has been open sourced, along with the voice and the process.
  • A large-scale study that aims to understand differences in how individuals perceive masculine, feminine and non-binary TTS voices.
  • A reflection on the design process, and lessons learned for researchers looking to create other variations of non-binary or non-gender-conforming TTS voices.

2 RELATED WORK

In this section, we discuss work that relates to user preference of gender in their voice assistants, human perception of gender in speech, and physical and behavioral factors that influence gender presentation. It should be noted that within this related work and throughout the paper the authors have attempted to use language that distinguishes between sex and gender, however in the related work, we retained the original researcher's language in quotes and in instances in which the meaning was otherwise ambiguous.

2.1 User Preference of Gender

Early research conducted by Clifford Nass and his colleagues found that people have an overall preference towards women's voices across cultures and genders. Nass notes that people can discern the gender of a voice within seconds [ 42 ] and that women's voices tend to be higher pitched while men's tend to be deeper, implying that pitch is the primary indicator of gender. In an interview with CNN, Nass said: “It's much easier to find a female voice that everyone likes than a male voice that everyone likes. It's a well-established phenomenon that the human brain is developed to like female voices” [ 20 ]. While, in general, users may prefer women's voices, context of use and social and cultural norms impact specific preferences. For example, Nass also notes that German drivers, on average, refused to take directions from a woman, preferring a man's voice for in-car GPS navigation [ 40 ].

Much of this research echoes the gender stereotypes and cultural norms of the time and leveraging Nass’ work to justify today's choices when designing conversational AIs will cater to the majority opinion of that time. Nass's work, although extensive and well-known, was conducted in the 1990s and early 2000s. As social norms change over time, these findings may become outdated and do not accurately represent the preferences of marginalized communities. However, more recent research has not necessarily indicated a shift in the majority of users’ preference for their voice assistant's gender. For example, Mitchell and his colleagues conducted studies in 2008 with university-aged students in the Midwestern United States and found that women's voices were perceived as “warmer.” They also found that although both genders say that they prefer women's voices, only women actually held a subconscious preference for them [ 38 ]. A recent survey of voices in human-agent interaction also found a general consensus across studies for a preference for feminine, extroverted voices across individuals [ 53 ]. However, the authors acknowledge that gender stereotypes and biases largely influence this outcome.

In another recent work, Curry et. al. surveyed 34 individuals who participated in a two-day workshop held in Edinburgh in 2020 on their preferences of gender for conversational assistants. Most of these participants identified as women. They found that 32.4% preferred a robotic voice, followed by a 26.5% gender neutral voice, 20.6% feminine voice, and finally 11.8% masculine voice [ 13 ]. Although the sample size of this study is small and the users surveyed are located in Scotland, which has different social norms and expectations than the United States, their results indicate a significant diversity in user preference. The results may also indicate that social norms and therefore user preference may be changing. This change could be further accelerated by exposure to more diversity both in human-human interaction, and diversity of choice for users as they set up their voice assistants. Given the lack of recent data, we explored user preferences for voice assistant gender both before and after a listening task in a large-scale study we conducted.

2.2 Gender Perception and Presentation in Speech

2.2.1 Pitch and Frequency. Paralinguistic qualities, qualities beyond the basic verbal message such as accent, intonation, volume, pitch, etc., influence voice gender categorization. As mentioned in the previous section, pitch, the subjective human perception of the frequency of sound, is often cited as being an important indicator of voice gender categorization, with several researchers arguing that pitch has an outsized influence in gender perception. For example, Nass notes that people can discern the gender of the voice within seconds [ 42 ] and that women's voices tend to be higher pitched while men's tend to be deeper. In general, women's voices range from 145-275 Hz with a mean frequency of 196-224 Hz, while men's voices, which are lower pitched, range from 80-165 Hz with a mean frequency of 107-132 Hz [ 9 , 16 ]. The overlapping range of 145 – 165Hz is often considered the gender neutral or gender ambiguous range [ 16 , 21 ], which is why efforts like Q have targeted that range for their voices.

Other research by Anna Jørgensen found through interviews with transgender men and women that voice plays a part in the constitution of gender identity, with pitch being a primary marker of voice gender [ 31 ]. Skuk states “the most important single parameters for gender perception are, in order, F0, FF, and SL” [ 55 ] where F0 is the fundamental frequency, the frequency at which vocal chords vibrate, FF are the formant frequencies, frequencies which correspond to resonance in the vocal tract, and SL is the spectrum level, the average of a person's vocal frequencies. It is worth noting that F0 is one of the easiest parameters to change when developing a TTS. Gelfer and Schofield also reported that subjects perceived as women have a higher speaking fundamental frequency (SFF), which is the central tendency of the frequency of vibration of the vocal folds during connected speech and correlates with the perceived pitch of a speaker's voice [ 19 ]. Additionally, Hope and Lilley found that an individual's own gender identity influences perception of other voices – with cisgender individuals primarily relying on changes in F0 to determine gender and primarily perceiving a voice as either masculine or feminine, while non-binary and transgender individuals are more likely to perceive a voice as “other.” Overall, they found that vocal tract acoustics affect gender perception most [ 24 ].

Others argue that several other factors influence gender perception beyond pitch. Zimman, for example, conducted a study in which he had listeners select what gender they perceived voices to be, manipulating 19 speakers’ recordings of a passage in 10 Hz intervals. While pitch was indeed a factor, listeners perceived a different cross over point for every speaker, showing that pitch cannot be a sole index of gender. In this study, he found that F2 (the second formant frequency of vowel sounds) and center of gravity were also significant factors [ 70 ]. This may explain why men and women that fall into the overlapping range of 145 – 165Hz considered gender ambiguous are not necessarily perceived as such. Mullennix et al. had similar results, identifying no clear transition point in study participants’ perceptions of voices from men to women. Rather, they found a steady increase in consensus between participants as voices transitioned from higher to lower pitch [ 39 ]. Sutton suggests this research may indicate that the voice boundaries that determine whether a speaker is a man or a woman are individually set by each listener based on experience and perceptions [ 61 ], as studies have shown that many gender distinguishing traits are ‘arbitrary’ and vary across language [ 27 , 28 ]. While we draw on the research around pitch to create our non-binary voice, our TTS model also considers factors such as vocal tract length, voicing energy, and prosodic style. We also look at other parameters that might influence voice perception, including the demographics of the participants in our large scale study.

2.2.2 Vocal Features Beyond Pitch. Pernet and Belin reported that pitch cannot alone be used for voice gender categorization and such categorization instead relies on multiple factors such as timbre, which is the tonal quality of sound, particularly when categorization is difficult [ 47 ]. Additional research has indicated that many other paralinguistic factors can strongly influence how the gender of a voice is perceived, such as the breathiness of the voice, a speaker's prosody, which is the rhythm, stress, and intonation of speech, where intonation is the variation in pitch that a speaker uses to express themselves [ 21 ]. Men, for example, tend to have less pitch variation and they pronounce the letters ‘s’ and ‘t’ more abruptly than women [ 31 ]. Studies involving trans individuals [ 21 ] found that intonation alone does not have a significant impact on voice gender perception. Rather, a higher percentage of utterances that use upward intonation cause listeners to perceive voices as feminine even if the actual intonation is not. Similarly Wolfe et al. reported that transwomen perceived as having feminine voices had higher F0, less extensive downward intonations, a higher percentage of upward intonations and downward shifts and a smaller percentage of level intonations and level shifts [ 69 ].

Additionally, Borsel et al. completed a study with seven participants in which breathy voice samples collected from cisgendered women were compared to natural voice samples collected from the same women and judged by panels of cisgendered men and women students. The students judged that the breathy voice samples sounded more feminine [ 65 ]. For these reasons, literature for and research on voice therapy for trans individuals show a focus on more than just changing their pitch. Rather, individuals are taught a mastery of pitch, intonation, lexicon, syntax, paralinguistic behavior, and nonverbal cues [ 11 , 32 , 37 , 59 , 67 ]. This includes aspects such as speaking in a greater range of inflections, raising the tone at the end of a sentence, and countless other changes to speech patterns. Similarly, for TTS voices, simply altering the frequency of the vocal sounds so that the pitch is in the overlapping vocal range between men's and women's voices will not necessarily change the perceived gender of the voice in any meaningful way. In our TTS, we also train separate acoustic and prosody models with data from different individuals, allowing us to ensure that prosody has a more significant impact on the resultant outcome. Finally, during the voice actor selection process, we explicitly filtered for voices that would be considered breathy, or that would otherwise result in a voice that was at either a masculine or feminine extreme.

2.2.3 Word Choice. Paralinguistics are not the only differences between perceptions of men and women's speech. Conversational style and word choice, among other linguistic properties also vary significantly. Research studies have shown conversational styles differ between genders. Although now disputed, Tannen's schema of men's versus women's speech was foundational. It laid out women's cooperative/rapport speech vs. men's competitive/report speech principles which included aspects such as: women are more polite and indirect, use tag questions and hedges, and interrupt less often and primarily to support or affirm, while men are more assertive and direct, speak more often and interrupt in order to control the conversation [ 62 ]. Research has also shown that women's speech includes more personal pronouns (I, you, she), more “cognitive” words (understand, know, think) and more “social” words and verbs, while men use more quantifiers (one, two, some more), more articles (a, an, the) and more nouns [ 42 , 45 ]. Pennebaker's analyses also showed that people perceived to have higher status use fewer I-words, and more We-, You-, He-, She-, and They-words, a speaking style that is mimicked by today's conversational assistants [ 22 ]. Not only paralinguistics but language itself makes up a critical part of gender perception whose features such as word choice need to be considered in the design of any voice assistant. These recent studies inform our approach to conducting our own study to inform the creation of our non-binary voice assistant.

2.2.4 Non-binary language. Recently, the research community has called for studies into gender and language to question the existence of the binary rather than assume it and to include non-binary and transgender participants to retheorize the existing dichotomy [ 71 ]. One such study showed that there are currently no indexes of gender-neutral presentation in English speech, but rather that non-binary individuals will mix masculine and feminine gender markers to produce a non-binary effect. This study reexamined gender markers and found a reemergence of the competitive vs cooperative dichotomy mentioned previously. Overall, feminine markers include higher pitch, greater intonation, more polite deferential and indirect speech, more emotional and descriptive words, and taking up less space in conversations both in terms of length and number of turns. In contrast, masculine speech includes lower and more atonal speech, being more assertive and direct, and taking up more space in conversations. Non-binary individuals mix these markers to produce a non-binary presentation [ 23 , 24 , 25 ] – with significant variations in how each non-binary individual chooses to mix these markers. Drawing on the results of these studies, we implement a mix of gender markers in Sam and we look at the impact of mixed gender markers in our large-scale study on user perception of TTS voices.

2.2.5 Impact of social expectations on gender perception and trust. Voice qualities, such as gender, also impact other attributes, such as perceived trustworthiness. For example, O'Connor and Barclay found that both voice pitch and context affect perceived trustworthiness. In their study they tested three contexts (general, economic, and mating related (mate poaching, infidelity)), and found that higher-pitched women's voices were more trusted in economic and mate poaching contexts, lower-pitched feminine voices were trusted in general contexts more. Higher pitched masculine voices, however, were more trusted in all contexts [ 43 ].

In another study, Tolmeijer et al. investigated the effect of gender and pitch on gender stereotyping and trust of conversational agents. They asked participants to evaluate 2 masculine synthetic voices (one pitch shifted high and the other low), 2 feminine synthetic voices (one pitch shifted high and the other low), and one gender-ambiguous synthetic voice (with pitch averaged between men and women) after completing a stereotypical ‘female’ assistance task or a stereotypical ‘male’ compliance task with a voice assistant using each voice. While they found that implicit gender stereotyping does occur, they also found that there is no significant impact on user trust of gender-ambiguous voices [ 63 ]. This finding directly contrasts the findings by Nass and Brave mentioned in the previous section, which showed that anything that is gender ambiguous is rated as dislikeable, dishonest, etc. [ 42 ]. Tolmeijer et al did, however, find that the gender ambiguous voice was given higher masculine stereotype ratings on the compliance task and higher feminine stereotype ratings on the assistance task, showing the impact of context on gender perception.

Nass also found that people tend to perceive women's voices as helpers or assistants who are helping us solve our problems, while men's voices are viewed as authority figures who tell us the answers to our problems – put a different way, women are perceived as trustworthy in “female domains”, while men are perceived as trustworthy in “male domains”. In a recent survey, Torre and White looked at the impact of voice naturalness, gender, accent, and prosody on perceived trust in voices for Human-Robot Interaction [ 64 ]. They found that naturalness, accent and prosody all have a high likelihood of impacting perceived trust. Gender, however, was found to only secondarily affect perceived trust.

Given the interplay of various vocal features and social expectations on gender perception, we incorporated all of these features into our TTS voice. To understand how word choice, context, and role impact gender perception of our non-binary voice, we also incorporate these factors into the design of our large-scale study on gender perception.

2.3 Genderless and Gender Ambiguous Conversational Assistants

Very little effort has been put towards creating voice assistants that are not clearly perceived as either men or women. When asking any of the current four major voice assistants about their gender they often reply that they do not have one. For example, Apple's Siri responds with “I'm genderless, like cacti or certain species of fish” or “Animals and French nouns have genders. I do not.” However, the name of the assistant and sound of the voice are still clearly feminine. Recently, however, several efforts have been made to create genderless conversational assistants.

One of these efforts includes the creation of Q, a genderless voice developed through a collaboration between Copenhagen Pride, Virtue, Equal AI, Koalition Interactive & Thirty Sounds Good [ 4 ]. To make the voice “genderless”, the team recorded one individual's voice that was closest to the intersection of men's and women's vocal ranges and then pitch shifted the voice to land in the middle of that range at approximately 145 Hz. As mentioned previously, men tend to pronounce the letters ‘s’ and ‘t’ more abruptly than women [ 31 ], so for Q they shortened ‘s’ and ‘t’ sounds for the voice [ 17 ]. The voice was tested by over 4,000 non-binary individuals from Denmark, the UK and Venezuela, half of which said they could not tell the gender, while the other half were evenly split between men and women. However, Q is not a TTS voice. It is simply a pre-recorded and post-processed concept to spur conversation, and therefore cannot be used in today's conversational AIs or leveraged for additional research.

Two complete voice assistants have been developed in the last few years that claim to be gender neutral. The first is Sage's conversational assistant for accounting, Pegg. Pegg does not pretend to be human, and instead presents itself as a bot with a voice that sounds child-like [ 2 ]. Pegg, however, is still perceived as a woman's name. The use of a child-like voice also raises other concerns, especially in cases where users of the technology may abuse the bot when it gets things wrong and when other power dynamics might be in play. In 2020, BBVA launched Blue, a Spanish speaking gender-neutral voice assistant that also presents itself as non-human [ 12 ]. However, since Spanish is a gendered language, the agent still refers to itself using masculine pronouns, such as “he/him.” Both examples are also only available to customers of these company's products. Another, more recent work, uses crowd-sourced data from individuals with different ages, gender, and geographies to create an open-source non-binary voice as an alternative to existing proprietary voices [ 30 ]. The voice, however, is not currently available online.

2.4 Addressing Non-binary Experiences in CHI

There is a growing body of research concerning trans and non-binary experiences within CHI, centering both on ways that it can enhance the safety and well-being of individuals and how HCI systems encode a gender binary and cause harm. Several groups have used co-design methods to specifically develop devices and mobile applications to enhance the safety of individuals [ 46 ]. Starks et al. conducted interviews and developed U-Signal, a device aimed at improving the safety of transgender women and non-binary people of color, directly examining the role HCI can play in supporting the safety needs of marginalized communities [ 58 ]. Additional researchers have examined, often using their own lived experience as non-binary individuals, deficiencies in HCI development and existing systems, highlighting how a gender binary is encoded within technology [ 26 , 52 ]. Katta Spiel conducted an autoethnography to examine infrastructure through their own experience and further explored the impacts of making key players aware that change was warranted [ 56 ]. Spiel's work asserts that, from the perspective of non-binary researchers, "non-binary people are rarely considered by technologies or technologists, and often subsumed under binary trans experiences on the rare occasions when we are discussed" [ 57 ]. Additional research has examined experiences and needs of the trans and non-binary communities specifically in voice technology. Alex Ahmed conducted a set of interviews aimed at using trans competent design to create a voice training mobile app where participants stressed the need for flexibility and non-adherence to strict cultural norms regarding speech [ 7 ]. Using interviews with members of the trans and non-binary community, Rincón developed a set of recommendations for designers and researchers creating voice-activated artificial intelligence. Most participants interviewed were positive concerning a "wide spectrum of un-gendered voice options" in voice technology but felt such voices needed to represent trans and/or non-binary people. Issues of representation, however, were secondary to other changes needed with voice technology to many participants [ 51 ].

In our work, we aim to develop a process by which a range of non-binary TTS voices can be created and integrated into new and existing voice user interfaces as another option. We create a fully usable non-binary TTS voice, called Sam, as an example of this process. We also aim to move beyond pitch as the only expression of gender and look to integrate other paralinguistic and linguistic features that impact voice gender perception.

3 PROCESS: DEVELOPING A NON-BINARY VOICE

Instead of starting with existing data, we chose to identify and work with a voice actor and record our own audio. We did this for several reasons, the first being that it would allow us to find a voice actor which was closer to the overlapping pitch range between masculine and feminine speech and who was versatile in their intonation, such that they could more likely pass as either either a man or a woman. This gave us the opportunity to explore different representations of non-binary speech, by mixing various masculine and feminine markers like we see people do. From there we processed the audio data from the voice actor and created a first round of possible synthetic voices. Those voices were evaluated through a survey with the non-binary and/or transgender community. Based on the feedback from the community survey, we modified our pre-processing and our model training phases and we created a second round of synthetic voices, which were again evaluated in the same manner as the first round. From there, we created a final TTS voice, Sam, as an example of our process, which was evaluated in a large-scale general population survey. 3.1 Selecting a Voice Actor

We recruited a voice actor through the researchers’ social media networks and through Voices.com. As the goal was to identify a voice that was close to center on key characteristics as possible and knowing that non-binary voices are potentially even more diverse in range than men and women's voices, we did not exclude participants of any gender or sexual orientation from our job posting. Instead, our post stated the following:

“This project's aim is to advance representation, conversation, and research for the empowerment of marginalized gender communities. We are only seeking candidates who support our efforts to accelerate equality for all, especially gender identity and gender expression and, ideally, who are members of trans, non-binary, gender non-conforming and/or genderqueer communities. There are no preferred voice characteristics associated with this requirement.”

Through these combined channels, 111 professionals and 5 amateurs submitted a voice sample of a short script:

“Hi. I'm Sam. I identify as nonbinary. I work as a history teacher in Hartford, Connecticut, but I am originally from Missouri. I live in Hartford with my girlfriend of five years. I went to college in Chicago, where I studied history and theatre.

The weather in Hartford today is sunny, with few clouds. The temperature is fifty-five degrees, with a high of fifty-seven and a low of forty-nine.

What a coincidence, but I don't think I know them.

I like cats, but I've always been more of a dog person.

That's very nice of you to say. Thank you.”

Many of the voice actors also included a short description of their interest in the role (See Table 1 for examples) as part of their submission, with many either identifying as part of the LGBTQ+ community or having a close friend or family member who does. From the 116 entries we received, we narrowed it down to 12 candidates (7 professionals and the 5 amateur candidates) based on their interest and on a first pass assessment of the clarity of their speech.

Next, two researchers individually scored candidates on a scale from 1-5 on 6 different features: How closely their accent matched a generic American English Accent, whether they had a natural non-binary speaking style (i.e., mixing characteristics of both feminine and masculine speech patterns and intonation), how confident, warm and authentic they sounded, and how good their pronunciation and enunciation were to ensure that we ended up with high quality recordings, and thereby a high quality synthetic voice. The top five candidates (4 professionals, 1 amateur; 2 she/her, 2 they/them, 1 he/they) were selected for an additional review by two additional subject matter experts: one in speech synthesis and one in gender presentation in speech. The speech synthesis expert ranked candidates based on criteria such as breathiness, clarity of speech and difficulty of post-processing the natural pitch of the speaker. The gender presentation in speech expert ranked the candidate based on non-binary speaking style. The candidate that ranked the highest across the two expert rankings was selected as the voice talent for our non-binary TTS – ultimately this ended up being a woman with a strong skill for emulating different intonations and a voice who's pitch was easy to manipulate. While non-binary candidates were included in the final round, the final voice was chosen exclusively based on technical merit and the ability to use the speaker as a baseline to introduce a number of shifts and modifications. As stated previously, there is no single non-binary voice and the non-binary voice talents exhibited characteristics that would not have easily been shifted as the voice talent selected. While the authors understand that selecting a voice talent that identified as a woman to create a non-binary TTS voice creates tensions with the underlying goal of including non-binary individuals in the process, we did not want to exclude any individual based on gender alone in this process. Instead, we ensured that non-binary individuals were included in other ways throughout the process: as research collaborators, through community feedback, and through voice data that was incorporated at later stages into the average and prosody models.

3.2 Generating the First Round of Synthesized Speech

We worked with the voice talent remotely to record for 6 hours in the studio, resulting in approximately 2 hours of audio data. Our recording script consisted of 5 hours of an open-source script and 1 hour of articles found on the web. Content from our script came from press releases, news articles, wikis, and books in the public domain. We looked for informational, non-emotional content that was not number heavy but that covered the range of phonemic transitions generally found in American English. We tried to incorporate words related to how we might use the voice – in our case we chose references to other voice assistants and content around gender.

We trained two sets of TTS models – one with a high quality proprietary neural TTS, called CereWave, that is not open sourced, but for which a developer can purchase a license to the SDK or web API 1 and another with Idlak-Tangle [ 50 ], an open source TTS integrated with Kaldi. We chose to use the proprietary neural TTS for the voice that we evaluated because this TTS engine allowed us to create a high-quality voice with less speaking data than typical systems and which was more aligned to the quality of the masculine and feminine voices we used. Both neural TTS models are trained similarly so we will detail our open source TTS model that is publicly available.

The model and method of training it is shown in Figure 2 . First pre-processing is done using Idlak's front end. Speech and transcriptions of the voice actor's data is preprocessed into phonemes, the smallest unit of sound that make up a word's pronunciation and meaning different from another word. The text data is first processed into individual tokens using a tokenizer. These tokens are used with a lexicon, or pronunciation dictionary, and letter-to-sound rules to give appropriate phonemes. Kaldi specifically generates quinphones, where the phoneme includes the two left phones and the two right phones. Next, Kaldi's Hidden Markov Model (HMM) is used to do forced alignment and obtain acoustic frame level phoneme alignments with the text. The merged phoneme labels and alignments give a rich phonetic and contextual presentation for each state of the HMM, called state level full context labels. These labels are given as input to the prosody-DNN.

Our neural TTS model trains two Deep Neural Networks (DNNs), the prosody-DNN and the acoustic-DNN. DNNs are a machine learning method for automatically learning functions to transform input data into a desired output. In our case, we want to transform input text into speech audio representative of members of the non-binary community. The prosody-DNN is trained to predict the duration of both the phonemes and HMM states from the input labels. A frame-level mapping between input labels and the output duration predictions of the prosody-DNN were used as input to the acoustic-DNN. The acoustic-DNN is trained to generate the acoustic features, F0 and Mel-cepstral coefficients (MCEP), present in the audio data. The output acoustic features are input into a Mel log spectrum approximation (MLSA) vocoder to synthesize speech.

Because our set of voice actor data is rather small and in a single style, we use a technique called transfer learning. Transfer learning involves taking the features of a trained DNN model on a dataset in the same domain, in our case speech data, and adapting it to another set of data. Our final DNN model is adapted from a set of pre-trained DNN models that used pitch averaged data from men and women, balanced so that the median of the pitch and vocal tract length distribution fall approximately to the average of male and female ranges. The adaptation procedure itself involves retraining specific layers of neural networks at different rates, to transfer certain characteristics of the average model to the final model, such as the ability to speak with different emotions and elocution styles. This average model was built from a database of 2 men and 5 women General American English speakers from the CSTR VCTK Corpus 2 . Each voice in the database was rated by 10 native English speakers recruited through Amazon Mechanical Turk on a sliding scale with male on one end and female on the other and an average gender score was generated (0.0 – perceived as feminine, 1.0 perceived as masculine).

We also trained the Acoustic-DNN with a gender control parameter based on perceived gender to shift the voice to be perceived as more "gender neutral". The Acoustic-DNN model uses these scores as part of its training to predict different gender values for the speech output, even if they may not have been observed in the actual data to help ensure the non-binary voice will be perceived as non-binary. It is important to note that the model learns to generate a non-binary voice beyond a simple pitch shift by learning the appropriate features that make a voice non-binary from training on the voice actor data. The model will consider factors such as vocal tract length, voicing energy, and prosodic style including emotion and elocution to generate as natural sounding a voice as possible.

From the raw audio data, we generated 4 different sample voices:

  1. Gender shifted to 0.4 (towards neutral but on the feminine side) – Voice0.4
  2. Gender shifted to 0.5 (neutral gender) – Voice0.5
  3. Gender shifted to 0.6 (towards neutral but on the masculine side) – Voice0.6
  4. As 3 but with the source audio pitch lowered by 1 semitone. – Voice0.6 pitch lowered

Pitch shifting for 4 was applied to the raw audio recorded by our voice talent as a pre-processing filter. These transformations lowered the pitch of the speaker to bring it closer to range "between" men and women speakers by 1 semitone. We used this pre-processed data to generate a neural TTS voice which reflected the character and voice style of our voice actor.

3.3 First Non-binary and Transgender Community Survey

To evaluate the first round of voice options, one of the authors who identifies as non-binary recruited participants through Facebook groups for non-binary individuals to respond to a survey about what they thought of when they thought of a non-binary voice and to listen to and rate the voice samples. 26 participants responded anonymously to the survey (18 – 45 years old, avg. age 25-35). Participants’ gender varied greatly with individuals identifying as "male", "female", "non-binary", "genderfluid", "agender", "genderfae" and "trans man", among others. All participants were native English speakers. Participants primarily lived in New England, though we had a few respondents from Texas, Washington, or outside the United States in Mexico and England.

We asked participants to listen to a sample of each voice individually and then rate the voice on a 5-point Likert scale for the following:

  • Does this voice sound non-binary to you?
  • Would you feel comfortable with this voice representing non-binary individuals?
  • How natural does this voice sound?

Of the 4 voice samples rated, participants identified Voice0.6 as most authentically non-binary (see Figure 3 for means and standard deviations). This voice was towards neutral but a bit on the masculine side. For each voice sample, they were asked for any other thoughts they had to elicit feedback that was not captured by our Likert style questions. Responses to these questions ranged from "Speech rhythm and intonation sounds very artificial" to "it's pretty good" to "it's grainy". Participants were asked two additional questions after listening to all voice samples that allowed for free form input:

  • "What else do you we need to take into account when creating a non-binary TTS voice?"
  • "Do you have any additional comments?"

Free form responses indicated a lot of enthusiasm for the project and various participants indicated that any voice can be non-binary, reinforcing that there is no one non-binary voice and that the voices that represent this community should be as varied as the individuals in it.

4 LARGE-SCALE SURVEY

4.1 Method

We conducted a large-scale (n = 1,010 people) exploratory study in the United States in February 2021 to explore the impact of role, intonation, and word choice on gender perception. Paricipants were recruited through a large market research agency. To increase the likelihood that participants would complete the survey, the survey length was kept short at approximately 15 minutes requiring us to focus in on a small number of conditions that could be incorporated in this survey.

4.1.1 Participant Demographics. Participants 18 and older from across the United States were recruited, with the number of individuals recruited from each of the 4 major geographic areas in the US consistent with US census data. Participants were 45% male-identifying, 45% female-identifying and 10% as not exclusively male- or female-identifying (i.e., non-binary, agender, genderfluid, intersex, etc.). The age ranges of the sample were also weighted to match the U.S. population, with the following breakdown: 12% age 18-24, 18% age 25-34, 17% age 35-44, 17% age 45-54, 15% age 55-64 and 21% age 65 and older. Nearly all respondents own a smartphone (92%) or a computer/laptop (86%) and more than one-third reported owning a smart speaker (36%) at the time of the survey.

4.1.2 User Preference Prior to Listening Task. Prior to hearing any audio clips, respondents were asked which gender they prefer their voice assistants to sound like. The study authors recognize that these initial questions on user preference may have influenced choices in the listening task, however as understanding perceived gender preferences was a primary objective of the study, the questions were asked prior to avoid the listening task's influence on responses. Nearly half of those surveyed prefer feminine-sounding voices (47%), one quarter prefer masculine-sounding, and 3% prefer a voice to sound non-binary. The remaining one-quarter of respondents indicated that they had no preference. There were variances among demographics however, with men significantly more likely to favor a masculine voice (48%) and non-binary individuals most likely to prefer a non-binary voice (21% compared to 1-2% for men or women) followed by not having a preference (36%, compared to 23-25% for men or women). Preference for a feminine-sounding voice was also higher among 18-34 year-olds (52%) and 35-54 year-olds (51%) than among respondents age 55 and older (38%). See Figure 5 for a full breakdown of user preference.

4.1.3 Listening Task. Participants then listened to a total of 24 audio clips, one per voice gender (masculine (masc), feminine (fem), non-binary), and across 8 conditions (see Table 3 for details on each condition). These conditions were chosen based on prior research of some of the salient features that might impact voice preference. Explicit choices were made to limit the number of conditions to decrease the likelihood of drop off. Further discussion of the limitations of this study can be found in section 5.3 . This resulted in clip combinations that would generate the masc voice with fem intonation or the non-binary voice in the masc role for example. We explicitly made the choice not to ask participants about gender, as we did not want to influence the way they responded to each voice to be solely focused on gender. Instead, each participant rated each audio clip on likeability, authenticity, authoritativeness, intelligence, and trustworthiness. Each participant listened to the clips in a randomized order to account for any order effects.

4.2 Analysis

Data analysis was performed using R v4.2.1 using the "aov" function in the base R stats package and through ordinal regression using the "polr" function in the Mass package [ 66 ]. To detect differences in ratings across likeability, authoritativeness, authenticity, intelligence, and trustworthiness across the three voices we used one-way ANOVA for each condition with Tukey post-hoc comparisons to detect differences between the voices. Assumptions of normality were tested through visual inspection of the distribution of residuals, and assumptions of homogeneity of variance across groups were tested through the Levene test. If an assumption was violated, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test was conducted with Wilcoxon pairwise comparisons. Bonferroni corrections were used to correct for multiple comparisons in the one-way ANOVA, and Benjamani and Hochberg corrections were used for Wilcoxon pairwise comparisons. To test for interaction effects, we fitted a linear model (estimated using OLS) to predict ratings with the gender of the voice and the gendered attributes across conditions. 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs) and p-values were computed using a Wald t-distribution approximation with an interaction effect specified between the two predictors. Similar to the one way ANOVA described above, assumptions were tested in the same way, and violations in assumptions of homogeneity of variance were counteracted with using robust standard errors.

We were also interested in determining whether age, sex, orientation, or race contributed to differences across each of the ratings for a particular voice in each condition. Ordinal regressions were conducted for each rating for all conditions, with a total of 120 regression models run. Bonferroni corrections were applied, resulting in an alpha level adjustment from 0.05 to 0.00042 to determine statistical significance. The predictor variables for the ordinal regression models included age group (6 levels: 18-24 years, 25-34 years, 35-44 years, 45-54 years, 55-64 years, and 65+ years), gender (4 levels: woman, man, non-binary, and prefer not to answer), LGBTQ+ identification (3 levels: LGBTQ+, Not LGBTQ+, and prefers not to answer), and race (6 levels: white, Asian, Black or African American, Hispanic or Latino, a different background, and prefers not to answer). The "emmeans" package [ 34 ] was used to investigate post-hoc differences between factor levels for significant effects. Log odds were converted into odds ratios for easier interpretation, with only significant effects reported in the following results. Model assumptions of multi-collinearity were tested through correlation plots for each variable and variance inflation factor tests. Proportional odds assumptions were tested through Brant-Wald tests [ 10 ]. Because there were only 7 participants who preferred not to answer regarding their gender identity, they were removed from subsequent analysis as their small sample size could influence accurate gender-related model estimates.

4.3 Results

4.3.1 Overall Preference for Voice Gender. Our results show that the masculine voice was overwhelmingly favored over all others and was rated highest across nearly all attributes in all four categories, regardless of whether the audio clip had masculine or feminine qualities (all ANOVAs for each attribute showed significant main effect of voice, p < .0001, with all pairwise comparisons showing ratings masc voice > fem voice > non-binary voice at p < .0001). This preference for the masculine voice did not align with the initial stated gender preferences of participants, indicating a low probability that the initial preference question significantly primed or biased participants during the listening task. Further, the feminine voice was consistently rated higher than the non-binary voice across nearly all attributes in each of the four categories for both masculine and feminine qualities. All fem and masc voices with fem intonation were rated better than with their masc intonation counterparts. However, while the masc voice with feminine intonation was rated better than a masc voice with masc intonation, this rating was not statistically significant. The masc voice with fem intonation was consistently rated significantly higher than both the fem and non-binary voice's feminine and masculine counterparts (all comparisons between these pairings revealed significant effects of at least p < 0.01), but the masc voice with fem intonation did not rate higher than the masc voice with masculine intonation (no observed differences across intelligence, likeability, authenticity, and authority, or trust, p > 0.05). Non-binary voices, regardless of feminine or masculine intonation, were always lowest rated compared to their masc and fem counterparts (across all measures all differences significant with p < 0.05). Masculine and feminine intonation patterns did not differ within the non-binary voice, with the exception of the trust and likeability attributes, where non-binary voices with feminine intonation were rated higher than non-binary voices with masculine intonation (fem-masc likeability Tukey pairwise comparison: estimate = 0.17, SE = 0.05, p = 0.006; fem-masc trust Tukey pairwise comparison: estimate = 0.15, SE = 0.05, p = 0.03). This indicates that tone may play a more significant role than any other attribute.

Interestingly, in the masculine control condition, the masc voice was consistently rated highest across all categories with the exception of perceptions on authority, where the fem voice was rated slightly higher than the masc voice (H(2) = 259.39, p < .0001, fem mean rating = 3.43, SD = 1.25; masc mean rating = 3.30, SD = 1.20; Tukey pairwise comparison: z = 2.56, p = 0.01).

4.3.2 User Preference by Gender Identity. Looking more closely at the results by participant gender however showed consistent increased favorability towards the non-binary voice by non-binary participants compared to men or women. The non-binary voice scored high among non-binary listeners for trustworthiness, shown in Figure 6 . A chi-square test of independence was performed to examine the relation between participant gender and voice preference in terms of trustworthiness. The relation between these variables was significant (X2 (4, N = 1003) = 38.93, p < .0001). A higher proportion of non-binary participants found the non-binary voice to be more trustworthy than the proportion of men or women rating non-binary voices as trustworthy. It also scored higher than the other voices when rated for intelligence, particularly among non-binary individuals who preferred the non-binary voice. A significant relationship was found between participant gender and voice preference in terms of intelligence (X2 (4, N = 1003) = 119.74, p < .0001), where proportionally non-binary individuals rated non-binary voices as more intelligent than their male and female counterparts, as seen in Figure 7 .

When looking at between-subjects effects, we found that participant gender influenced perceptions of non-binary voices when they spoke with a feminine word choice - particularly for likeability and intelligence. Non-binary individuals were more likely to have higher likeability ratings when a non-binary voices spoke with feminine word choice compared to both men and women (Tukey pairwise comparisons: [Women - Non-binary: estimate = -0.81, SE = 0.24, z = -3.44, p = 0.002]; [Men - Non-binary: estimate = -0.58, SE = 0.23, z = -2.46, p = 0.04]), with the overall effect of participant gender being marginally significant (estimate: 0.81, SE: 0.24, p = 0.0006, Wald: 5.72, 95% CI = [0.35, 1.28]), with the odds of this effect being 2.26 times greater that perceptions of likeability would increase (95% CI = [1.42, 3.59]).

Additionally, non-binary individuals were more likely to have higher intelligence ratings for a non-binary voice using feminine word choice than men or women (Tukey pairwise comparisons: [Women - Non-binary: estimate = -0.83, SE = 0.23, z = -3.58, p = 0.001]; [Men - Non-binary: estimate = -0.74, SE = 0.23, z = -3.23, p = 0.004], with a significant effect of participant gender (estimate: 0.83, SE: 0.23, p = 0.0003, Wald: 6.89, 95% CI = [0.37, 1.28]), the odds of this effect being 2.28 times greater that perceptions of intelligence would increase (95% CI = [1.45, 3.60]). We did not see similar effects for masculine word choice or for the masculine and feminine voices with feminine word choice.

4.3.3 User Preference by Age. Our investigations into participant characteristics also revealed that age significantly impacts perceptions of voice characteristics. Older participants (55+) tended to have lower ratings of likeability for both the non-binary voice speaking in a feminine role and the feminine voice speaking in a masculine role. No differences were observed for the masculine voice speaking in a feminine role or for the non-binary voice speaking in a masculine role.

The 55-64 year age group was significantly more likely to have lower likeability ratings than all younger age groups (Tukey pairwise comparison: [(18-24) - (55-64): estimate = 0.97, SE = 0.26, z = 3.75, p = 0.002)]; [(25-34) - (55-64): estimate = 0.59, SE = 0.22, z = 2.72, p = 0.05]; [(35-44) - (55-64): estimate = 0.73, SE = 0.22, z = 3.28, p = 0.009]; [(45-54) - (55-64): estimate: 0.69, SE = 0.24, z = 2.91, p = 0.03]), with the overall estimate of this effect being significant (Estimate: -0.97, SE: 0.26, p = .0002, Wald = 0.79, 95% CI[-1.48,-0.46]), the odds of moving from really dislike to neutral or really like being 0.37 times greater given that all other variables in the model are held constant (95% CI[0.23,0.63]).

The 65+ age group are more likely to have lower likeability ratings of a feminine voice in a masculine role than all age groups except the 55-64 category. The overall effect was marginal (estimate: -0.82, SE: 0.24, p = 0.0007, Wald = 4.24, 95% CI[-1.29, -0.35]), the odds of this being 0.44 times greater that perceptions of likeability would increase (95% CI[0.27, 0.71]; Tukey pairwise comparison: [(18-24) - (65+): estimate = 0.82, SE = 0.24, z = 3.40, p = 0.009], [(25-34) - (65+): estimate = 0.65, SE = 0.19, z = 3.37, p = 0.01]; [(35-44) - (65+): estimate = 0.70, SE = 0.19, z = 3.70, p = 0.003]).

5 DISCUSSION

5.1 Designing With and for the Non-binary and Transgender Communities

To successfully create a non-binary voice, we recognized early in the project that it necessitated the inclusion of the non-binary community at all stages of the project – including voice talent recruitment, community surveys at two stages of the voice development, engagement with and incorporation of audio data into TTS models from non-binary public personas and a larger than typical non-binary representation of participant responses to our large-scale evaluation. The goal was to provide some agency to the community to determine how this non-binary voice was created and its results. The in-community survey in particular, provided direct community feedback that influenced technology and design decisions, resulting in the incorporation of voice data from a non-binary individual to train the prosody model. At each stage we received feedback from involved individuals and guidance on how to create the technology to best represent and meet the needs of the community. Of note is that the willingness of the non-binary community to work with our research team and provide feedback was in part due to one of the authors identifying as non-binary themselves. For other researchers, it may be critical to partner with non-binary and/or transgender researchers, or researchers from the marginalized communities for which they seek to develop tools and resources [ 35 ].

In section 3, we showed how the community survey responses influenced the creation of the TTS voice. Here we dive more deeply into the qualitative feedback we received and discuss its criticality to the success of the project as well as its impact on the individuals in the community. Starting with the voice talent selection, 11 of our 116 candidates identified as non-binary. Unsurprisingly, and as indicated by their self-selection to apply to our posting, these individuals were all extremely supportive of the concept of a non-binary voice. In Table 1, we gave some examples of the feedback we received from candidates that identify as non-binary or outside the binary, or that are close family members of non-binary individuals. Here, we present feedback from public personas and individuals that contributed to the audio data we used for model training who were not paid for their services and who were supportive of the concept or excited at the opportunity to contribute to the work:

"That sounds absolutely fascinating! It's always so exciting for me to hear about the wonderful projects going on all around us to make the world a better place!" - public persona

"I'm fascinated by the project, and grant you the use of my publicly available videos as vocal reference. If at all possible I would like to be kept abreast of the project and when you begin to go public with it. It's something I would like to cover and talk about on my channel." – public persona

The individual who donated their voice data for the prosody model felt that inclusion in a project to create a non-binary voice validated their voice as part of the community.

“Before starting testosterone, I was pretty insecure about my voice, particularly about how it often seemed to be part of why people misgendered me. I knew intellectually that a non-binary voice can sound any sort of way – but emotionally, it was hard to reconcile that fact with how people responded to my voice. So for my voice to be a component in this project feels pretty validating!”

5.2 One Voice Does Not Fit All

We developed this non-binary voice with the aim of open sourcing it, such that developers and researchers could build on our process and our data. The goal was to catalyze additional efforts to create technology and spur research efforts that not only increased representation but provided technology directly to users within the non-binary and transgender communities and outside of them. Through interviews with the trans community, Rincón et al. found that participants indicated that community needs go beyond representation and include designing for trans-specific privacy considerations, developing features for trans-specific purposes, creating representative and gender-affirming voice AI personas and basing development of these features in a grounded and participatory design process [ 51 ]. While this work does not address all these recommendations, by supplying an open source non-binary voice and the tools and process to produce additional voices, we provide a necessary building block and process for non-binary and trans inclusive voice assistants. We discuss considerations for open sourcing data meant to represent a marginalized community in Section 5.4 .

Over the course of development, we received considerable feedback from voice talents reinforcing these key recommendations. Representation of non-binary identities has been limited in western media and it was a significant positive highlighted in the feedback received. Many bloggers and activists have highlighted the need for more non-binary representation in the media: “One next step in the media landscape becoming more accommodated to non-binary individuals is to simply be given the opportunity have our reflections in and on pre-existing forms of media. We need room to tell our own stories, and to let other people know who we are, what we do, and how we live.” [ 54 ]. Many of the responses from the in-community survey highlighted the need for diversity even within non-binary voices. As we have reiterated throughout the paper, there is no single defining non-binary voice and the community strongly reinforced that. At least 5 participants across our two in-community surveys brought up some aspect of creating diverse non-binary voices. For example, some participants wanted a non-binary voice that was representative of how they wanted to feel or present themselves (specifically focused on pitch), understanding that others might want something different:

“While the first thing that springs to mind when I think of a NB voice is an androgynous one, I think that a purely androgynous voice is something that it's very hard for a person to actually achieve. If I were choosing a voice I could use, I would definitely go for the most androgynous (to me) one I could find. However in lived experience non-binary people can have a wide range of voices that they may be satisfied with or not. I, for example, do not perceive myself to have an androgynous voice, though I deeply desire one. So I think it's wonderful and really important to have an NB computer voice that I would perceive as androgynous, and that's how I've framed my answers on this survey. And in terms of representation, I think it's important to have multiple options. To have enough options that there could be an NB voice that a listener might assume a gender for, to support the fact that one is no less NB if their voice doesn't match an androgynous or mixed voice ideal.” – P22 (S1)

Some of the feedback touched on the association of non-binary with androgyny or addressed the continuum that exists between men and women. This is also is addressed by many non-binary writers who emphasize the broadness of non-binary existence [ 54 ]. The feedback directly addresses the need to provide agency to the non-binary community in technology and voice assistant creation, specifically in creating many non-binary voices that span the spectrum of different presentations. As mentioned previously, non-binary voices include a large range of characteristics and traits and the limited literature available indicates that non-binary folks believe strongly that any voice can be non-binary. The work presented here does not attempt to define characteristics or traits of non-binary voices but instead provide tools that allow creators to generate TTS voices that do not fit within the gender binary, moving beyond pitch shifts and allowing the blending of traits considered masculine and feminine into a single voice. While the tools are specifically developed with the non-binary community, the open source software can be used to generate unique voices for any gender, allowing users to create TTS voices that better reflect their preferences and themselves.

The desire for diversity also continued beyond gender to include dialect and language, with at least three participants commenting on this. For example:

“Different accents is my only thought, as an English person I tend to use British English voices, but I understand that's a little more complicated!” – P5 (S2)

“...is it an English specific voice you are creating or are you going to create voices for other languages or will it work with other languages.” – P18 (S2)

Other participants used the feedback opportunities to voice broader concerns regarding the treatment of non-binary individuals in society, suggesting that when creating a non-binary voice, we also must be careful not to create one that might be stereotypically negative or harmful to the non-binary community. This further underscores the need for multiple non-binary voices and providing tools to create them. By showing how different non-binary individuals can sound from one another, we encourage representation more broadly. It also underscores the need to consider how the technology might be misappropriated and consider steps we can take to discourage negative behavior.

While the need for a diverse set of non-binary voices is clear, some participants though that just having one non-binary voice was a great start:

“Obviously everyone's going to want Specific Things for themselves but a generic N-B voice is a breakthrough really.” – P5 (S2)

“Please let me have a nonbinary voice soon I am so so so tired of not having one” – P9 (S1)

5.3 Large-Scale Study Insights and Limitations

The large-scale survey results provided broad feedback from a general U.S.-based population on the final non-binary voice along with characteristically masculine and feminine voices in different combinations of intonation, word choice and role. Cultural and dialectal differences were not addressed in this study. Participants rated the characteristics of likeability, authority, authenticity, intelligence, and trustworthiness for each voice and speaking combination. Before any listening task participants reported a solid preference for a women's voice; however, after the listening tasks, the male voice was strongly preferred by all gender and age groups. The survey itself included only 3 voices, 1 for each gender represented, which limits the generalizability of the results. It is possible the masculine voice could have just been more likeable due to characteristics that we were not aware of or manipulating, which overcame the original preferences and gender bias participants came in with. Further studies should explore the use of multiple voices and additional sentences for each test condition to rigorously determine any genuine gender preferences as opposed to individual voice preferences. The results do however challenge prevailing theory that users prefer female voices.

Of note were the high ratings for the masculine voice with feminine intonation, which is itself a non-binary representation. All participants rated the non-binary voice higher for intelligence than other traits. Non-binary participants also consistently rated the non-binary voice higher in most categories when compared to men and women participants. This demonstrates a level of interest and acceptance for a non-binary voice among the community. As stated earlier in the discussion, many non-binary participants in the community study emphasized that one voice does not fit all and the non-binary voice we created only exemplifies one way an individual could present as non-binary.

The results also provide evidence for the need for a diversity of voices within voice assistants, with different combinations of pitch, word choice, intonation and role all receiving high ratings from some sub-section of participants. While we never expected that a non-binary voice can or should replace the existing default voices of conversational assistants, it clearly demonstrates that a sub-set of users have the desire to have them available. As the market for voice assistants grows larger, diversifying the available voice genders, dialects and speaking styles can lead to broader adoption of the technology and greater user personalization. The objective is not to put a non-binary voice in every home, just in the home of the users who want it.

As issues of gender bias were addressed earlier, with women's voices being represented as subservient and subjected to undesirable interactions, there is a need to recognize that replacing the feminine voice with another gender, such as a non-binary one, would lead to similar conditions, shifting negative stereotypes and power dynamics from one gender to another [ 61 ]. This further reinforces the need for a diverse voice selection that includes all genders.

Finally, this work focused solely on a general American English voice. However, we believe that this process of designing a non-binary voice could be used for other languages and dialects as well. It is worth noting, however, that word choice differences in gendered languages can be challenging and there is still much to be done from a language perspective for inclusion [ 14 ]. Similarly, gender stereotypes differ by language and country [ 15 ].

5.4 Ethics of Open-Sourcing

As mentioned, our goal in open sourcing the data, models, and the method for creating a non-binary TTS voice was to encourage others to create many different voices, so that we can increase the diversity of the voice assistants we have available. However, one of our biggest concerns was misappropriation and misuse of this data and process to create material that could ridicule or further cause harm to the population we aim to empower. For this reason, we chose to open source the voice under an Apache 2.0 license, but keep the repository private at first, such that individuals must request access. We did not ask individuals that requested access to heavily justify their interest. Our aim was to provide a deterrent for those that would rather cause harm. Of course, bad actors can lie and provide a justification that is not in line with their intended actions and there is not much we can do to protect against that. A total of 45 outside collaborators have requested access, of which 35 have joined the repository. Many of those requesting access are either non-binary and interested in using the voice for their own purposes, or academics.

Inspired by the Hippocratic License [ 5 ], we also provided a disclaimer at the top of our README.md file asking individuals to comply with the culture and core values of the author's and their institution and to Human Rights Laws and Human Rights Principles. The content of this section is as follows:

In part due to the demographics of the individuals requesting access and their stated purpose for doing so, we have recently made this repository public 3 . Individuals can leverage the repository in any or all of the following ways: 1) They can use the existing trained TTS voice directly as a screen reader or through any other software that supports the use of Idlak voices. 2) They can embed the voice within new applications and conversational assistants. 3) They can leverage our data set to train new voices that use other TTS engines or apply other post-processing techniques to the data or 4) They can simply use the script and associated training process with other data to create a whole new voice. We hope that individuals will continue to use these methods for future research and for their own needs ethically.

6 CONCLUSION

This paper presented a method of creating a non-binary TTS voice. We sought input from the non-binary and transgender communities throughout the voice talent selection, training of the TTS prosody and average models, and evaluation at multiple stages of the process. We also presented a sample non-binary voice, Sam, which we used in a large-scale study to understand the differences between how individuals perceive masculine, feminine and non-binary TTS voices. Our survey demonstrated value of a non-binary voice for members of the non-binary community, despite not being preferred by most participants. Reception of the voice and process was positive among the non-binary community with clear indications that non-binary representation requires a diversity of non-binary voices and that active participation of the community in the process is vital to meeting community needs. The data, models and process have all been open sourced to foster continued research and development of additional non-binary TTS voices.
