Pick Me Up Before You Go-Go: Sociotechnical Strategies for Waste in Music Festival Campsites

Kellie Vella , Queensland University of Technology (QUT), Australia, kellie.vella@qut.edu.au

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581104
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Multi-day music festivals have a waste problem with much of it centred around patron campgrounds. Interviews and co-design workshops were conducted with festival patrons (N = 19) and professionals (N = 9). The interviews indicated the factors impacting festival campground waste including the proliferation of cheap items, the continuity of management decisions, highly social camping practices, the land, and interactions between these. Co-design workshops explored these to produce sociotechnical strategies using the festival timeline as a frame, with one of these, a patron planning tool chosen for further development. This paper contributes new insights on how information and communication technologies might enhance sustainable practices by facilitating relational change through better organisation, evaluation, and feedback. It concludes with examination of the challenges and opportunities for Sustainable HCI, including how to carve out a design response to a wicked problem by situating relations, meaning making, telling invisible stories, and finding leverage points.

1 INTRODUCTION

I think people might have even planned to leave it behind. Yeah, because like they buy something just for that weekend. But people weren't just like leaving it set up. They were like, you know, putting it in a neat pile, actually. Yeah, it was pretty good. (Patron 3)

And then the people who are camping there and leaving it and going, 'Oh, well, they have recycling here'. So, you know, they make that nice little neat pile. (Festival Professional 4)

Waste is a problem that isn't going away. While a discarded object may disappear from view, it may very well go on to kill sea life [ 14 ], produce greenhouse gases [ 27 ], and contribute to environmental injustice and poverty [ 28 , 32 ]. Yet, waking up to a football field full of rubbish blowing in the wind after an exhausting three-day event would test most people's individual resolve to ‘leave no trace’, particularly when the cheap tent and marquee one bought in the leadup is now ripped and bent. The multitude of factors – economical, practical, social, spatial, and temporal - working against the leave-no-trace ethos may make neat piles of rubbish seem like the best option available. Yet, as extraordinary spaces, multi-day festivals are also testing grounds: for artists’ creativity, for young people's emerging social practices, and for those trying to sustainably manage these events. Some argue that they are also experiments in sustainable living [ 5 ]. Regardless, designing a socio-technical intervention into the problem of campsite waste presents an opportunity to consider how the systems that produce this waste interrelate, are reinforced, and can be changed.

This research leans into the work on Sustainable HCI, particularly the call to embrace systems thinking [ 9 ] and apply a relational analysis to the understanding of practice [ 21 ], context [ 10 ], and the more-than-human [ 16 ]. Interview and co-design workshops with both festival professionals and patrons were conducted, producing knowledge of the factors influencing campsite waste. These included interactions between patron camping practices; the proliferation of breakable goods; the continuity of sustainability messaging and actions across festival management decisions, and the land itself expressed through ownership relations and weather. From this a set of five socio-technical strategies were produced that were mapped to the festival timeline, with one, a patron planning tool, to be taken further.  These strategies engage with speculated information and communication technologies (ICT) to aid organisation, and evaluation and feedback for the private sector, festival patrons, and festival management. All strategies were considered in light of other waste-reduction and festival management practices in use and reflect a relational approach to designing socio-technical sustainability interventions. While not conclusive, these strategies provide jumping off points to consider how HCI practitioners might effectively partner with other domains to meet their sustainability objectives and scale up interventions to influence broad as well as targeted change.

This paper makes three contributions to Sustainable HCI and other research grappling with systemic problems:

  1. A rich case study of a wicked problem that uncovers the opportunities for and the limits of ICT intervention.
  2. Argument for exploring and documenting multiple, relational, and expansive designs.
  3. Proposal of four different ways to approach a problem that can impact methods, partnerships, and topics of study and design.

This third contribution frames the discussion, which reflects on the research and design opportunities resulting from situating relations, meaning making, telling invisible stories, and finding leverage points within social groups and temporal rhythms. These strategies can readily be applied to other initiatives within CHI to pivot design work on complex problems in useful directions and impel new collaborations that aid Earth friendly futures. A key implication for Sustainable HCI practice is the need to better understand and situate intervention within relations, e.g., at the macro level between producers, consumers, and land, and at the micro level between different roles within social groups. Correspondingly, this research finds value in design work that engages with multiple contrasting viewpoints and agendas when the desired outcome is structural change.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 The problem of festival campsite waste

Festival campsite waste has been the subject of much media attention, with the image of discarded tents and other camping gear blowing across fields inspiring both horror and fascination. Event management has made solid inroads into the management of waste within market stall areas and around the main stages, with examples including re-wash initiatives (services that wash up plates and/or cups in return for a bond payment), ‘green teams’ (volunteers that manage waste communications and collections), container deposit schemes, and waste streams that filter out recyclables and compostables. However, campsites remain a last frontier with all the potential and jeopardy that this implies.

Research on the actual amount of waste produced by festival campsites it is underwhelming. However, to provide a sense of the scale of the problem, within Australia, The North Byron Parklands Environmental Impact statement reported waste generated by Splendour in the Grass 2017 , comprising 32,500 patrons [ 19 ]. Despite considerable efforts to reduce and recover waste, they reported a total of 262 tonnes of solid waste of which 86% was non-recyclable, general waste. Of this general waste, 133.6 tonnes were produced by the campgrounds. Efforts to counteract this at the event included the provision of waste personnel, colour-coded waste streams and waste bags provided for campers, and the recovery of abandoned camping equipment for distribution to charities.

This contrasts greatly with camping music events that are both smaller in size and actively attempt to achieve ‘zero waste’, as is illustrated in a study of three New South Wales music festivals [ 36 ]. In this study, non-recyclables were at most 20% of the total waste generated. Two of these events were supported by Resource NSW, and these events achieved zero waste by further processing their non-recyclables into reusable materials. While the study did not identify campsite waste it does show several potential factors impacting music festival waste, including scale, intention to reduce waste to zero, and the usefulness of inter-group collaboration. Laing and Frost [ 22 ] also note the importance of shared purpose across stakeholders when seeking to reduce waste, which can be fostered through roundtable communication. In Australia, some of these are being brokered by Green Music Australia, which manages the Party With The Planet Alliance , a collaboration between major music festivals, promoters, and non-profits to reduce campsite waste [ 13 ].

However, even with the best of intentions, waste reduction strategies can be enacted inconsistently, e.g., by promoting recycling but not providing enough recycling bins, which leaves festivals open to accusations of greenwashing [ 22 ]. Moore [ 29 ] suggests that campsite waste is essentially a single-use plastics problem and that festival schemes that encourage the abandonment of gear (e.g., by promoting donation to charities or compostable tents) are adding to the problem. Instead, she argues that camping gear should be kept in use for as long as possible through repair (including glamping with repairable canvas tents) and by economically incentivising green camping options such as Greenpeace's Eco-Camp at Download Festival, or the ‘Love Your Tent’ and ‘Respect’ schemes at the Isle of Wight Festival [ 29 ]. Eco-camps highlight a trend in providing special amenities to patrons who adhere to certain requirements, particularly leaving no trace upon exiting the festival. However, it could be argued that these schemes attract people who would already be less likely to abandon their gear, while approaches such as policy equivalents of Europe's Right to Repair laws could be more broadly effective.

Regardless, entire festivals can deliver upon the ‘leave no trace’ ethos, for which the Burning Man festival is perhaps the most well-known. However, camping site management at Burning Man is conducted a little differently to most festivals, as most patrons register their campsites with the organisers, with many of these campsites assembling into villages. This produces a level of oversight that larger festivals with minimally managed campsites lack. Other approaches to site management include green placemaking initiatives that seek to improve festival lands with tree-planting, e.g. The Planting Festival at Woodfordia [ 37 ], though it is unclear what impact this has on patron care for the site. Similarly, the sociomaterial qualities of land – its natural features, weather, and connections to history and culture – are not well understood in relation to campsite waste, though initiatives to embed care for Country, or indigenous perspectives on more-than-human relations [ 2 ], may hold promise.

Finally, understanding patron motivations for abandonment of campsite gear may hold some insight into how to reduce this activity. Green Music Australia [ 29 ] research on the topic, based on survey of over 800 patrons at three major festivals, found that abandonment was driven by damages to equipment, the social proof of seeing others leave their equipment, a belief that cleaning up was the festival's job, a lack of care for their equipment, and urgency to leave on the last day. Of these, damages to equipment and social proof were the most impactful across all three festivals. Further exploration may uncover why equipment was being damaged, and how social proof might be utilised to reduce waste instead of adding to it.

Overall, festival campsite waste is shown to have social, economic, political, and environmental angles with no one solution or ideal strategy. Furthermore, trying to get ahead of waste to stop it at the source shows no obvious beginning. Does waste become waste when it is made, bought, transported, breaks, or is abandoned, or when it fails to be recycled, repurposed, repaired, or returned? Each of these stages unleash a different approach to un-making waste. Some insight into how sociotechnical systems might intervene in these stages is offered by Sustainable HCI.

2.2 Sustainable HCI and design

Sustainable HCI is in flux brought on by the increasing urgency of human-induced climate change. An outline of the genres of Sustainable HCI research in 2010 showed that previously most papers targeted individual consumers, with many focused on persuasive approaches to behavioural change [ 7 ]. Reasonable critiques have been made of this, including the reliance on conceptions of rational actors and personal choice [ 8 , 34 ]. As such there has been a shift to acknowledge the ways in which social practices, with their institutional, material, spatial and temporal dimensions, reinforce certain narratives [ 21 , 38 ]. These narratives are particularly critical within the festival setting, as the predominately young patrons are still forming their social practices, and festival spaces are particularly receptive to experimentation. For example, Browne and colleagues [ 5 ] note that young festival attendees are finding ways to adapt to water scarcity through the collective sanction of different ways of living (not showering), and that this holds lessons for climate change adaptation, including the enjoyment of ‘not doing’. This openness to other ways of being may make intervention particularly effective if it matches the fluid and temporal nature of events. A sympathetic design direction is proposed by Light and colleagues [ 23 ], namely to embrace ‘unfinishedness’ and open-endedness in design in the hope of matching the shifting complexity of sustainability problems with adaptative and appropriable responses. They also propose the use of temporal tools to achieve this.

Similarly, designing with complex problems necessitates a focus not on solutions but on creating shifts in ‘culture or power’ [ 20 ]. This acts as a timely reminder that while few musicians can make a livelihood from their art, Live Nation Entertainment, Inc., the global entertainment company that operates several of the festivals approached by this study, at time of writing had a net worth of US $ 20.97 billion [ 25 ]. Some festivals owned by Live Nation are active in patron data sharing with sponsors, as well as weaving sponsorship and branding into the festival experience for patrons to replicate and share through their social media networks [ 6 ]. This commodification of experience is not actively sought by all festivals, with many taking a counter-cultural stance, however, music festival patrons are typically members of wealthy nations and sub-groups whose disposable income funds disposable items. Waste in or out of this context obviously evokes a political economy lens (on the unequal relations between those that make waste and those that dispose of it; the globalization of waste [ 11 ]), but for design, a more effective approach may be situated knowledge production [ 15 ], with a focus on social and material infrastructure [ 35 ].

This approach, implying open dialogue with diverse actors, also aligns with the call for collaboration between HCI and domains that are at the forefront of environmental work [ 23 , 33 ] and for systems thinking [ 9 ]. Cross-domain collaboration is an awkward but fertile space to work in, and with an existential crisis looming, an inclusive vision of who to design with and for seems like an antidote for the thinking that brought us to this place. Designing as if we are in it together [ 24 ], calls for consideration of the more-than-human, as well as an appreciation for the messy and deeply held feelings humans have for this world. It engages with speculation and thinking about ‘how things could be different if they generated care’ [p. 60, 31]; pertinent to the study of the unwanted, abandoned, invisible, and discarded. Designs that facilitate collaboration and support between people who care may answer this, as well as meeting the need to facilitate collective action [ 8 ] and account for the political dimension of sustainability work [ 20 ]. Such collectives, where they engage politically, can be effective in pushing for policy change that is otherwise in the hands of lobbyists. Approaches such as these complement others such as ‘Green Policy Informatics’ [ 4 ] without closing the door to the many skillsets that HCI has at its disposal or to the desires and needs of its diverse participant groups and collaborations.

Finally, it's worth considering the possibility of leverage points and the special conditions of these communities. Music festivals provide rites of passage and spaces in which young people can experiment with their identity [ 17 ]. Their openness to new experiences also means that they are generating new knowledge from these contexts and others like them, e.g., share houses. Within these social groups might there be dynamics that could be appropriated to change their culture and that of entire festivals? Better understanding their challenges and networks creates an opportunity to be of service, and potentially impact waste production beyond the festival fence and timeline [ 26 ]. Similarly, festival management are constantly experimenting with new ways of dealing with this problem. What insights and practices are working for and against its resolution? This study approaches the problem of campsite waste as an opportunity in relationship building, transition, collaboration, and design. As such, this research aims to learn:

RQ1. What factors influence the abandonment of things in music festival campgrounds?

RQ2: What sociotechnical interventions might result from engagement with these factors and their relationships?

3 METHOD

The objective of the overarching project is to co-design a socio-technical intervention that will reduce music festival campsite waste, specifically to reduce the total landfill produced by the festival campgrounds, not just reduce littering on site. The following reports the social research conducted in the first year of a multi-year project, with following years to be focused on intervention refinement, promotion, and evaluation. The social research focused on learning about festival patron and professional practices relating to campsite waste and on proposing interventions that are matched to these practices.

3.1 Context

The project involved partnership with a not-for-profit organisation advocating for better environmental practices in the music industry (Green Music Australia or GMA), a music festival ( Strawberry Fields ), a council (Byron Shire), and a North Coast of New South Wales not-for-profit focused on supporting its local community to reduce emissions and waste (Mullum Cares). Initial plans entailed partnership with North Coast music festivals, particularly Splendour in the Grass . Hence the early interviews and co-design were split between a North Coast focus, and input from Strawberry Fields , a smaller festival in the south of the state that was active in trying to reduce its environmental impacts. While the project did not secure a partnership with Splendour in the Grass , they were active in providing feedback in an industry roundtable event (described in the Procedure).

The timing of the project coincided with the Covid-19 pandemic, which impacted the project in several ways. Festivals suddenly had an uncertain future, and in an industry that relies upon short-term contracts with a boom/bust cycle of employment, this understandably created anxiety in our festival management cohort. This influenced relationship-building with the larger North Coast festivals, which became less eager to engage with the project, preferring instead to focus on how to maintain their livelihood. The actual shutdown of festivals also impacted the project's timeline, as the beta tool was unable to be tested at a festival until they recommenced.

3.2 Position Statement

I am a CIS female of European descent and was an early-career researcher when applying for the funding to conduct this research. I have a history with the entertainment industry prior to my academic career and so had some small understanding of the challenges of event organization and the entertainment industry. I had also camped at many music festivals as a patron in my youth, which sensitized me to extraordinariness of festival spaces, as well as the nature of the problem. Over the course of my academic career, I have been slowly aligning my research towards matters of sustainability and nature. I engage strongly with the ideas of situated knowledges [ 15 ], located accountability [ 35 ], and the ethics of care [ 31 ], and this has framed my analysis and discussion.

3.3 Participants

Ethical approval by a university ethics board was gained to conduct interviews and co-design workshops with festival patrons who had camped at a multi-day music festival in the last two years, and similarly with festival management and related fields such as independent waste management contractors who worked with festivals.

Festival patron participants were recruited through advertisements in university noticeboards, relevant Facebook groups, through GMA's mailing list, and through the authors’ networks. Festival patrons were offered AU $ 20 for their participation in the co-design workshops. Festival management were recruited through the project partners’ networks and were not offered a financial incentive to participate.

As the interviews and co-design workshops occurred in this order, participants might take part in one or both methods. Of the interviews, 10 patrons took part and 7 festival professionals. Of the co-design workshops, an additional 9 patrons took part and 6 professionals, of which 2 were new participants. In total, the dataset reflects the inputs of 19 festival patrons and 9 festival professionals.

Festival patrons’ demographics were only collected for interview participation. They ranged between 19 to 53 years, but 8 of the 10 participants were 25 years old or younger, and 9 of the participants identified as women. Patron age was not collected for co-design participation but was similarly skewed towards a young adult age range. Festival professionals' ages were not collected at all, and of the 9 participants, 5 were women. For simplicity, quotes are identified using P for ‘Patron’ and FP for ‘Festival Professional’. 3.4 Procedure

Interviews and co-design workshops were carried out. These were used to explore the problem space and to generate a set of possible strategies. An analysis phase followed this to produce a set of five possible design directions to the festival stakeholders.

3.4.1 Interviews. Semi-structured interviews were conducted from 10 October 2020 to 28 May 2021. The patron interviews collected basic demographics, what festivals were attended, how they camped at festivals, what other campers were like, what their perceptions of festival management were, and what they noticed about waste in festival campgrounds. The management interviews collected participant roles, waste interventions that had been tried, perception of patrons, the types of waste that gets left behind, barrier to making change, festival timelines, site management, and questions specific to council members and environmental advocacy groups. The interview schedules can be found in the supplementary materials. Interviews were conducted both face-to-face and via Zoom. They were audio-recorded and transcribed, with the transcripts contributing to the dataset.

3.4.2 Workshops. The co-design workshops were conducted from to 9 February to 28 May 2021, online using a combination of Zoom and Miro. The co-design workshops differed for the patron and professional cohorts and were offered sequentially.

The patron workshops prompted discussion around the emotional experience of attending festivals and their experience of waste at festivals. Different types of festival were profiled using different camper personas (generated from interview data) were provided as provocations, and waste reduction strategies shared during the interviews were used to prompt discussion. Patron ideas regarding the factors contributing to waste in festival campgrounds, and a broad range of waste reduction strategies were captured.

The professional cohort was shown patron outputs to prompt discussion about what strategies had been tried already and which sounded promising, as well as collecting additional strategies. Further prompts for the professional cohort included a festival timeline to be filled in. All Zoom conversation were audio-recorded and transcribed. This plus the contents of the Miro boards added to the dataset. These Miro board contents are supplied as supplementary material.

3.4.3 Poll of final design directions. Initial findings were presented to a roundtable of music festival professionals and related fields, organized by GMA. Participants were also offered the five proposals described in section 4.2 and asked for their preferences. These proposals were then offered to a larger number of festival professionals in a poll carried out by GMA to determine which of these the project would focus on moving forward. The reasons for this approach were, firstly, that this project operated under an ethos of partnership and collaboration, and any proposal would preferably build stronger relations between festival patrons and professionals, or at least avoid conflict between these groups. Secondly, any ICT strategy that required festival promotion or use would require the buy-in of a range of festivals to be widely effective. Thirdly, one of the project's larger goals is to build good-will and trust to encourage festivals to enter open data sharing arrangements. Finally, we were aware that our understanding of the inter-festival politics of this domain was at best superficial, and that these politics could facilitate a sociotechnical intervention or create additional hurdles. As such, it was best that we invest in a strategy that had broadscale approval.

3.5 Analysis

The interviews were thematically analysed using Braun and Clarke's reflexive approach [ 3 ]. Familiarisation occurred while the automatically transcribed interviews were manually corrected by the author, and this same author completed the remaining steps. Coding began with a large and unstructured set of labelled data, which were then structured into nested themes that corresponded with the study's aims of identifying the factors impacting the abandonment of items in festival campgrounds, and sociotechnical strategies that aligned with these. Initial passes produced initial findings and sociotechnical strategies that were discussed with a music industry roundtable organized by Green Music Australia. A further set of coding followed this to better understand interactions between the factors contributing to campsite waste.

Both the interview and workshop outputs contributed to a better understanding of the problem space and the ideation of a multitude of approaches to combatting festival campsite waste. Choosing a set to present to our industry collaborators (as described in section 3.4.3) meant the author applied these ideas to a series of filters, as follows:

1. Thematic grouping into higher level categories using the reduce, re-use, recycle framework. This was discussed with a member of GMA. See Strategy Analysis 1 in supplementary material. This identified strategies that reduce waste: a project priority.

2. Splitting ideas into those that could be enacted via ICTs and those that required other forms of organizational support. For example, making sure that food vendors were affordable and used compostable plates and cutlery was an organizational challenge, while communicating that patrons didn't need to bring food onsite was something that ICT could support. See Strategy Analysis 2 in supplementary material.

3. Reduction of the problem space and further analysis of what forms of ICT and organizational support could be provided, e.g., information, services, site management, partnerships, etc. See Strategy Analysis 3 in supplementary material. Again, this was discussed with a member of GMA.

4. From this, a final set was chosen that:

a) Were discreet ideas that could be enabled by ICT.

b) Interacted with different parts of the festival timeline.

c) Could facilitate the emergence of other strategies (e.g., data collection on the brands of abandoned gear could help patrons make better choices and be used to lobby for industry's responsibility for waste reduction).

d) Encouraged further linkage between festival patrons and professionals.

e) Would work across a range of festivals and festival sites.

The resulting set was discussed with a member of GMA before presenting to the industry roundtable.

4 FINDINGS

The findings are broken down across two sections. The first describes the factors impacting music festival campsite and interactions between these (4.1). This is followed by a set of proposals and design directions mapped against the festival timeline (4.2).

4.1 Factors impacting music festival campsite waste

The following themes were framed by an understanding of the intersectional nature of sustainability problems and are described to gain both an understanding of their interactions and opportunity for intervention. The final themes included the proliferation of cheap, disposable items (4.1.1), festival management decisions (4.1.2), patron camping experiences (4.1.3), and the land these events are situated on, which are imbued with both physical and economic relations (4.1.4). Figure 1 maps these factors and some of their interactions below.

4.1.1 Cheap and Disposable: Feeding Consumer Culture. Cheap camping equipment was synonymous with disposable, a fact that caused the festival and waste management cohort no small amount of distress, as demonstrated by M3 “… most of the camping gear that's abandoned is cheap camping. Yeah, it's like your, you know, Anaconda $ 50 combo pack, $ 5 chair, like shitty esky, and shitty fucking umbrella or something like that .” However even more expensive items could be abandoned if no one person was financially or emotionally invested in the effort of keeping it:

… they're a group of twenty, they all chipped in ten bucks to buy this thing from Kmart. Right? So yeah, it was a $ 200 thing, but to each of them it was $ 10, and it served their purpose for four days and they actually don't need it again until the following summer and they've got nowhere to store it. (FP6)

Festival patrons reported making the choice to buy cheaply when being a student or unemployed (P4) or having an item that is recurrently stolen or broken (e.g., P1’s camping chairs). The increase in readily available disposable items was also described by patrons (P1, P4, P9) with online retailers repeatedly mentioned. The private sector was described as opportunistic, with P1 suggesting that one online retailer deliberately advertised cheap outdoor living products in the months leading up to major festivals. To those trying to reduce waste, this was overwhelming:

The fact that Kmart sells a $ 15 tent in my mind ... it really just undermines, so fundamentally, our plight in this space ... Just the degree to which the private sector is not accountable for any of this. Like they can supply at a price, just plastic in different shapes. (FP1)

The possibility of policy interceding, i.e., “ if Australia doesn't accept international single use plastic items, then we wouldn't have to talk about it ” (FP4), was desired, but other priorities overtook a concerted push from this sector. However, the abundance and ease of online purchasing was fueling the purchase of an increasing number of unnecessary camping items. This shift in festival camping culture was noted by one manager:

.. it used to be like, I'll bring a swag, and a slab [of beer]… And now it's: I'll bring a swag, and slab, and an inflatable unicorn, and a couch, and eighteen dresses, and like I want to make a fruit bar, and I'm going to bring like five boxes of fruit and not really eat most of it … and they're overwhelmed by how much crap they have, and they don't take it home (FP3)

4.1.2 Festival Management: Continuity of Actions. Interlinked management choices around messaging, site management, and sponsorship impacted patron demographics and related waste-related behaviours, as well as the festival's waste management effectiveness. Management choices were impacted by land ownership (described in 4.1.4) and the festival's priorities, the latter of which was visible to patrons through the continuity of management decisions.

Different festivals (reflecting patron demographics that were marketed to) were largely broken into ‘family-friendly’ and ‘over-18’s’ events, with the latter associated with more alcohol consumption. Participants offered examples of intoxication linked to thoughtless behaviours and exhaustion on the last day of the festival, such as P7 losing “ an air mattress a year at a festival from a drunk friend in our campsite jumping on the air mattress and bursting them ”. Similarly, the expectation of poor behaviour could reinforce the desire to purchase cheap equipment, or as P1 states, “ when you go to things like Splendour, people don't want to take their dad's really expensive camping stuff … because you don't want it to get stolen or wrecked ”.

Site management decisions directly impacted the capacity of staff and volunteers to monitor and manage the campsites as well as the odds of gear abandonment. For example, festivals varied in terms of whether patrons can drive their car to their campsite, with this producing mixed results: driving in made it possible to both bring in excessive amounts of gear, but also remove said gear. Parking and walking in was aided in some cases by the festival provision of wheelbarrows, but upon the last day of the festival patrons were reportedly less eager to do multiple trips with wheelbarrows to remove their gear. A reluctance to communicate the distance between the parking and the camping areas was reported in one case, as this was expected to impact ticket sales (FP6). The approach of “ pack ‘em in, get ‘em out ” (P2) at some festivals also did not support place-making, such as Woodford Folk Festival ’s campsite street signs.

The decision to outsource commitments also undermined sustainability strategies. For example, outsourcing a reusable cup scheme to sponsorship partners introduced a conflict of interest, as P1 reported when asked to buy multiple reusable cups that belonged to rival beverage brands. Relatedly, some festivals were able to manage waste sorting and clean-up in-house, while others relied upon outsourced labour. Festivals using the latter approach were reported by one waste manager (FP2) as tending to produce more waste, while the former approach demonstrated “ a better continuity of ideas and beliefs ... and then that filters down to how the patrons perceive it on the ground ” (FP2). In some cases, this was aided by attracting patrons with an established culture of care for the environment:

We piggybacked on twenty plus years of fantastic work from a culture that, I think, just by its nature is nurturing ... we had probably a 50% attendance or more ... of people who had been to the circus festival [previously, on the same site] … they were effectively holding the hands of the music fans, so it made it easy for us. (FP1)

This festival achieved almost perfect zero waste in the campsites, and other factors attributed to their success include keeping the event small (less than 2000 people on-site); providing no general waste bins; and clear and constant communication about these expectations. The crew's care for the festival land itself was also emphasised as something the “ patrons see ” (FP1).

4.1.3 Camping Practices: Capital and Sociality. Camping was a highly social practice that required patrons’ social capital to organise and achieve. Social groups with varied levels of closeness formed and were reinforced through the festival camping experience, and these strongly influenced decision-making. Herd behaviours could drive product purchasing and abandonment in campgrounds.

Patrons who had experience camping with family were both experienced enough to be able to set up and pack down a campsite efficiently and were able to borrow equipment that was both of a high quality and needed to be returned to family members. This appeared to moderate any effect of exhaustion on the last day, as P9 (sister of P8) describes: “ mum and dad will always be like, you know, 'pack up your tent' … Like we know how to do it, we've done before, just do it again ”. Patrons with families who camped also appeared to have access to most of the equipment that would be used by a group. For example, P8 was able to borrow from her father, “ his tent which is a six men tent and four stretchers and a couple of sleeping bags ”. The likelihood of these campers returning with all the gear was supported by manager observations, that “ it's more about whether or not the stuff that they're bringing means anything to them. And if it doesn't, they sort of leave it behind as our problem ” (FP7).

Patrons who didn't have a history of camping relied heavily on other people (e.g., “ girlfriends or friends ”, P3), producing this equipment, and large social groups would often accommodate friends without equipment and those who might be travelling from interstate. Considerable coordination was required to organise equipment, food, travel, and setting up a combined campsite. Coordination was carried out through social media, with some members coordinating the provision of larger shared equipment and food. Many of the patrons interviewed stated that they took the lead in organizing the camping or providing the larger shared items (P1, P2, P4, P8), though organization could also be modular, e.g., organized per car (P9, P9) or per couple (P7).

Importantly, camping was a source of social interaction and community for patrons. Participants desired to camp near friends and even re-unite with people they only saw at festivals. Groups that camped together could be up to thirty people (P1), though were more often ten or less (P4, P2, P5, P7, P8, P9). These groups were sometimes formed by ‘absorbing’ neighbours who were friendly and findable over multiple festivals (P1, P2, P3, P4). Not being able to choose who to camp with was a disincentive to making use of a festival's glamping options, along with the cost (P1, P3).

Large numbers of people camping near each other also encouraged herd behaviours. Several festival professionals (FP2, FP3, FP6), and patrons (P1, P5, P6), reported that early abandonment of gear could encourage neighbouring campsites to also abandon gear, with this likened it to ‘ a contagion ’ (P6). Similarly, social norms could fuel the purchase of poor-quality gear, e.g. “ I bought a tent and ... it was like 20 quid. And I was like, oh, let's go, everyone does it ” (P4). Seeing others with extravagant campsites could also drive purchases for later events, or as P1 states, “ there's certain things that I probably buy that I don't need, but it would just be a really awesome thing to have ‘cause then it's also props to you ”. Conversely, social proof could also encourage patrons to clean up. This phenomenon was so powerful that managers were reluctant to test waste reduction approaches that might interfere with this effect. This included the use of ‘eco-camping’ areas, which were seen as “ dangerous because it doesn't let that critical mass spread ” (FP2), and rental schemes that were integrated into the main camping areas:

The model that was being suggested was that they'd leave it, and you know our teams would go and pack it up … I was very hesitant to do anything that lowered the social proof of 'yes, we need to pack up our campsite'. (FP6)

4.1.4 Land: Relationships and Weather. Finally, the land had its own part to play in the creation of gear abandonment, the effort put into cleaning up by festival management, and opportunities to change the environment. The land is conceived here as relationships to land including ownership, and its physical features including the weather.

Smaller festivals that negotiated directly with private landowners were greatly accountable to that relationship:

Our festival is on private land. It's on a farm. So, we really go the extra mile to make sure that there's nothing left in the farm fields for the safety of the animals and for the relationship with our landowners. (FP7)

Larger festivals were more likely to be hosted on council land with waste management driven by accountability to council waste management plans or to be accountable to their initial development approvals, which may or may not be in step with current environmental practices. While larger festivals faced a greater battle with campsite waste, those with long-term leases or site ownership could also choose to make environmental improvements such as Woodford Folk Festival's ability to compost waste on site and plant trees for shade creation. Lack of shade was more likely in very large events/campgrounds, and where the organisers did not have control over making environmental changes.

Lack of shade impacted waste, as the anticipation of extreme heat during pack down encouraged speedy exits, sometimes during the night after the last act. Anything left on the ground for the remaining patrons to sight the next day would fuel the social proof of abandonment, while the heat of the day combined with patron exhaustion would also discourage everything being packed up. Similarly, extreme wet and windy conditions tested poorly set up and cheap tents and marquees and could disperse items across the campgrounds and beyond. Some festivals provided services to encourage repair during the event, e.g., Mullum Cares presence at Splendour in the Grass . However, cheap purchases that were damaged at the end of the event were likely to be abandoned, which again fuelled the social proof of abandonment of things. As extreme weather events are likely to increase in frequency and magnitude due to climate change, awareness of how to manage campsites and set up robust equipment in the face of the ‘unexpected’ is likely to increase in importance.

Finally, land that was aesthetically pleasing with many natural features was seen as encouraging patron ‘ownership’ and ‘investment’ in keeping it that way (FP8). Relationships to the land were also expressed in terms of relationships with First Nations custodians. However, engagement with First Nations people varied greatly in practice across festivals, from mentions on websites, to holding a Welcome to Country, to participation in the line-up or in the festival organisation.

4.2 Strategies for Combating Campsite Waste

Any ICT intervention into festival campground waste requires an ‘artful integration’ [ 35 ] into existing practices. This was ensured by including both festival patrons and professionals in the interviews and co-design workshops. As noted in sections 3.4, the workshops engaged first festival patrons and then professionals to explore the problem space and brainstorm a wide-ranging set of strategies. For example, see Figure 2 below for some of the patron workshop thoughts on the problem space.

These were then reframed as design goals (with more ideas added at this stage by participants) and used to brainstorm responses to. For example, ‘No culture of repair’ was reframed into the design goal ‘encourage repair and reuse’. The patrons brainstormed ideas such making reusable cups collector items, donating gear back to the festival, or having a repair van make the rounds of the campgrounds. These outputs were shared with festival professionals in a second workshop, and these ideas were discussed in terms of whether these had been tried already, what the barriers might be, what additional ideas might be built off them, and nuance regarding specific groups of patrons and contexts. Additionally, the professionals provided more information on the festival timeline to better understand the context of different potential interventions. Workshop outputs can be viewed in the supplementary material; see Codesign Stage 1_patrons; Codesign Stage 1_professionals; Festival Timeline.

The total list of proposed strategies captured both organizational approaches (e.g., having equipment to enable tent repair on site), and ones that could be enabled by ICT (e.g., weather warnings to enable patrons to stop their gear from being damaged). The complete range of strategies were discussed with Green Music Australia and can be found in the supplementary material in the ‘Strategy Analysis’ files. These were then filtered down to the final set of five below, using the process and criteria outlined in section 3.5. The proposals are listed below in relation to known practices during the festival timeline. Technological barriers and opportunities varied depending on which stage in the event management timeline they were placed, with this broken down into before (4.2.1), during , which includes entry, camping, and egress (4.2.2), and after the event (4.2.3). The next steps are briefly outlined in 4.2.4.

4.2.1 Before the event. Festival waste management strategies were planned six to nine months prior to an event. Any messaging planned for patrons regarding their on-site experience could begin three months out from the event, with this linked to ticket release, and followed up through social media communications. These would increase in frequency and detail as the event grew closer. Waste management plans were designed and approved by local council two to three months out where necessary.

As the lead up to an event is dominated by pre-planning and communications, intervening in patron and professional practices must focus on supporting patrons to plan what to bring to the event from ticket release, and longer-term consultation with festival management to ensure continuity of management decisions. Models of awards, certification and consultancy support the latter, with A Greener Festival [ 1 ] championing this space. The former, patron planning, was poorly supported.

Proposal 1: Patron resourcing and organisation. A simple website or phone application to enable a group to plan for camping at a festival. Choices are curated to minimise gear brought to a campsite. Options for festival management input into camping requirements. Possible gamification.

4.2.2 During the event. Once an event begins, waste management becomes one of mitigation. A limited amount of time is available to volunteers to communicate the festival's waste management during patron entry, during which they must also check tickets, direct cars, and in some cases, search cars for contraband. During the festival, multiple informational and opportunistic strategies are in place with signage and making bins available with recycling streams being extremely common. Community groups partnering with festivals offered repair services in some instances, e.g., Mullum Cares [ 30 ]. Patron egress was identified as a key moment in stopping the abandonment of gear in the campgrounds. Removing visible waste quickly could also encourage patrons to do the same (social proof).

Challenges include swift patron egress, extreme weather events, and not enough staff or volunteers to monitor very large campgrounds. Electronic communications are not a given due to varied on-site internet and mobile reception, varied provision of battery charging stations, and some festivals actively discourage phone use. However, electronic signage (e.g., projections on stages, or portable) is present at some festivals, and festival staff sometimes have limited internet access. Cashless systems tied to RFID wristbands are growing in popularity, but sensors are limited to market areas and bars.

Proposal 2: Campsite identification. Tie patron identity to campsite location to make other waste reduction strategies possible. These include targeted messaging and incentivisation (e.g., neighbourhood collaboration and competitions); services such as vendor meals delivered to campsite (reducing food waste by patrons); waste bonds on top of ticket price; blacklisting. Requires changes to campsite management, e.g., delegated campsites using pre-registration or opt-in registration on-site using services such as What3words [ 18 ]. Other option include integration with cashless RFID systems. Regardless, each of these requires significant changes to most campsite management practices, and this project recommends group opt-in rather than individually forced or stealth data capture.

Proposal 3: Patron on-site reporting . Enables patrons to report waste issues (e.g., overflowing bins, problem campsites) immediately to waste management teams. Provides multiple-choice options using a simple web-form. Could be integrated into festival apps or located on a community noticeboard located near high traffic areas like the toilet block. In cases where festival sites have mobile reception it could also log users’ GPS location to reduce data entry. Reporting would be greatly aided by the provision of campsite maps with named neighbourhoods, street names or landmarks rather than completely unregulated camping.

4.2.3 After the event. The annual nature of most festivals means that there is a yearly chance to make improvements on waste reduction strategies, and communication of intentions and successes with patrons. Event waste managers are often employed across multiple festivals and as such, they had a strong sense of the impacts of different strategies. However, there was no broadscale sharing of that information, and data sharing of actual waste statistics was not the norm. Formal evaluation of different strategies was also hampered by the number of different approaches that might be trialled at a single event. Enabling open data sharing within a culture that relies very heavily on ticket sales is one that will require the establishment of trust, levels of control, and reduced costs/added value in other areas. For festival patrons, the aftermath of a festival includes returning gear that was borrowed, disposing of any waste they removed from the site, posting photos of their experience, and deconstructing their experience on social media.

Proposal 4. Festival management-led strategy evaluation . A web-based tool that tracks festival waste over time to enable waste managers to evaluate the success of different waste strategies for reporting purposes. Requires the uploading of corresponding data and generation of reports. Provides options for personalised and aggregate data made available to participating organisations. This data to be made publicly available in aggregate form to allow festival patrons to be aware of the waste generated by music festival events generally, and to allow individual festivals to choose to share detailed data with their patrons and reinforce positive progress toward waste-reduction goals.

Proposal 5. Patron-led gear evaluation. A simple web-form to enable patrons to evaluate their camping gear. Data can be entered by both patrons as well as festival staff and volunteers. Designed to provide feedback e.g., star rating of gear, and provide a larger dataset tracking key producers and retailers to focus social campaigns on. This could be expanded to include patron-led evaluations of the festivals they attend along a range of criteria including the congruence of sustainability messaging and actions. A similar tool could also be provided for repair services operating on-site to collect data on the types of equipment that are frequently breaking, their brands, and place of purchase. Data such as this could be used to promote their inclusion in Right to Repair policy, inform consumers, and ‘name and shame’ retailers of poor-quality equipment. Ideally this data would be open and collated across festivals.

4.2.4 Next steps: A festival camping planning tool. Proposal 1 was chosen by festival management, via GMA polling (described in section 3.4.3), for further development. The aims of this tool are to help a social group plan what to take to a festival, who is taking it, and how they are sourcing it. Immediate development goals include the need for:

  1. Weather notifications to help patrons plan for hot, cold, windy, or wet weather.
  2. Minimal lists of camping provisions to reduce the likelihood of overpacking, with tailored content from each festival.
  3. Messaging within the tool to reduce the items being brought to the campsites.
  4. Festival tent hire information integrated into the gear list, if the chosen festival provides this service.
  5. Tips and hacks collected from both festival professionals and patrons, provided as both text and instructional video, e.g., a stop motion video of how to pack up a tent.
  6. Sharing lists within friendship groups.
  7. Saving lists for re-use, as well as being able to change group membership or tailor lists for different festivals.

Further development goals include the collection of user-generated camping gear evaluations (folding in Proposal 5); presenting feedback on waste from participating festivals; enhanced Care for Country messaging and fostering those relationships in festival partners; finding further opportunities for exchange between festival professionals and patrons; and furthering the Party With The Planet alliance [ 13 ]. Encouraging further festival buy-in will be enabled by inter-group collaboration with Green Music Australia, which will take on the role of content collection, partnership development, and operations. This tool's design process and impacts will be reported on after further development and once officially launched.

5 DISCUSSION

Caring about things is a difficult undertaking when so many things call for our attention. This study of music festival campsite waste highlights various factors contributing to the problem. No single factor – patron camping practices, management decision-making, the market for cheap, disposable goods, or the land's naturecultures – stand alone. Rather, each is capable of magnifying or diminishing the effects of the other. However, it is exactly these relationships that offer opportunity to sociotechnical intervention.

The proposals made here focused on facilitating organization, evaluation, and feedback to aid the alignment of different participant groups, communication between them, and a stronger voice in negotiations [ 8 ] as these were best served by ICT. There is an abundance of other strategies that could also be enacted by other actors to influence the political and cultural aspects of the problem, and some are mentioned below. This study has proceeded in the knowledge that no complex problem will ever be ‘solved’ – let alone by one approach - and every approach creates more challenges. However, changing the nature of the problem, for example, from ‘how do we stop festival patrons leaving stuff behind’ to more specific ones such as ‘how can we help patrons pack better?’ or ‘how can we draw attention to the producers of breakable camping equipment’ produces openings for HCI to make many small differences, and through its social research, to focus approaches for other fields. This differs substantially from approaches that focus on a top-down approach to Sustainable HCI, i.e. a blanket focus on policy [ 4 ]. As useful as some policy changes would be for campsite waste, the multi-headed nature of wicked problems – and like the Hydra, when one is cut off, two more grow back - will continue to resist singular approaches. This research demonstrates that technology design for wicked problems must consider how any counter-acting strategy will operate within the expansive relational webs that generate them, and to consider a range of strategies. The following presents positions to take when designing for relational and cultural change that remain responsive to uncertain futures and uses [ 23 ].

5.1 Situating relations

The research and design process followed in this project has sought to explore all options through the intersecting lens of different participant groups. More groups could have been included (e.g., landowners or custodians; producers of cheap goods) and if so, more strategies may have resulted or less, as a number were cancelled out as knowledge of the problem and context grew in depth. Regardless, in this project, the time, place, and relations that it was positioned within, surfaced the strategies described in this paper, and delineated what was possible. This was most literal in our giving the right of veto to festival professional groups - so unlike typical co-design practice, which typically centres the experiences of the disempowered and marginalized and seeks equity. However, had this project not tried to reconcile the needs of both the producer and consumer, or had proceeded without festival professionals as collaborators, it may have been easier to dismiss the producer's concerns, to not understand the constraints they operate within. In turn, this may have delivered antagonist designs from outside of their political ecology, as well as ones that had already been trialled and failed. Honouring the experiences of both patrons and professionals and acknowledging their unequal power, yet similar frustrations, opened a third space in which relations between patrons, professionals, and others yet to be included can drive design.

Of course, working in this space also provokes relational tensions, which can and should be mapped for future reference. For example, when the idea of tying a patron's identity to their campsite or camping neighbourhood (Proposal 2) was first flagged, this produced mixed reactions amongst the festival professionals. It raised the possibility of accountability but also surveillance, and the marketing optics and cost of implementation were not good. Had this idea been accepted more broadly it seems likely that unintended consequences would also result, as any number of additional surveillances, data collection, and marketing opportunities would arise [ 6 ], some of which may have driven campsite waste. As such, this proposal (and the polling overall) acted as a provocation that highlighted tensions between the different festival professional roles, as well as that of resourcing and brand. The least controversial proposal (an organizational tool for patrons) was better accepted by the festival professionals, and it sidestepped the irreconcilable tension of designing for both accountability (what the waste managers wanted) and an escape from everyday life (what the patrons wanted and what festival marketing promised). This design direction does not negate others that may yet come to pass, but instead opens into a new set of possibilities by reframing the design problem to that of organising relationships and resources, and the possibility of promoting minimalist camping as a new kind of freedom.

It is also possible that some contexts are fundamentally at odds with sustainability goals. Large-scale events are particularly challenged when it comes to reducing campsite waste, as very large campgrounds challenge effective site management, and these events tend to be aligned with other management decisions that drive consumption and abandonment. Reduction of campsite waste in this context is possible, but zero waste may not be. In this, broader social strategies that encourage de-growth will aid this endeavour, as well as local political actions, e.g., council limitations on festival sizes. This suggests a different set of partnerships, ones that include higher levels of governance, but also the fostering of grassroots activism. HCI can contribute to this space through collaboration with social actors across a range of institutional contexts, through informatics [ 4 ], and by designing interfaces that magnify the voice and impact of caring individuals [ 8 ]. Overall, situating sustainability-focused design in a web of relations requires a documentation of these processes, partnerships, and the cuts made through various design choices, rather than glossing over them in the rush to a perfect singular solution.

5.2 Making meaning

This research shows that designing for care for things or for meaning to be attributed to a product, depends upon the relationships that this object conjures up. Things belonging to parents were cared for and returned. Objects bought as a group, with no home to return to, were not. Mass produced objects purchased cheaply at the last minute were the least likely to be cared for. Attributing value to these things requires their entry into other fields of care and obligation, whether families or companies. To that end, tent and marquee hire is an ongoing strategy that festivals are using [ 29 ], though this study's participants believed organized tent hire areas to be more expensive and less social and fun than autonomous campsites. The primary challenges to this proposal (and every proposal) are the attractions of the free market and festival cultures that lean towards excess. One way to change this culture is through the collection of data on the suppliers of brands and items that have been shown to be unreliable (Proposal 5) to place the ethical onus of gear quality on the supplier rather than the user. However, this doesn't account for excessive purchasing, plus the great diversity of objects left behind may be too many for any policy to account for. Instead, the social desirability of things – that distinguish a campsite, signify hedonism, and provide conveniences – is the true battleground. Other values, such as ‘not doing’ have been embraced in the festival context [ 5 ]. Designing for the value of ‘having less’ will require relational approaches that tap into youth culture's search for meaning, novelty, and the extraordinary. They could also usefully engage with behaviours related to social proof, which both this and earlier research [ 12 ] finds impactful. A challenge will be making minimalist camping as visible and socially meaningful as maximalist camping to promote a shift in its desirability.

Another approach that this research highlights depends on relations to the land. Designing to enhance the relationship of people to Country and Indigenous worldviews is one that could be more fully explored, as a degree of kinship felt to flora, fauna, and landscape can shape how land is valued and positioned, i.e., belonging to Country, rather than land belonging to us. Proposal 1 seeks to acknowledge and facilitate stronger relations between events and the traditional custodians of the land they are held on. These land relations have the power to overlay ownership relations and produce narratives that can possibly counteract narratives of excess by instilling meaning. Other HCI initiatives working towards fairer futures could also consider how to foster meaningful connections to land and how existing social relations and institutions might frustrate or assist this.

5.3 Telling invisible stories

The invisibility of waste – what happens to it once the patrons leave the site, once it is transported off site and out of a shire, out of a country – offers a more-than-human narrative worth bringing to light. Outsourced labour on festival campsites completes the work of globalization, with this labour and the waste itself invisible to or forgotten by the patron who created it. Information about volumes of waste is similarly obscured, and this information siloed. One opportunity involves supporting contracted waste managers, who often work across multiple festivals, to make comparisons as to the effectiveness of their interventions (Proposal 4). However, truly open data in this arena will continue to be hampered by other management roles with other concerns until value-adding systems and governmental requirements for data reporting are created. Though this may seem to prioritise policy change agendas, that approach is slower moving and potentially more fraught than smaller interventions [ 23 ], and the latter has the advantage of being nimbler, with more periods for reflection and redesign. Uncovering the story of waste and replicating this across festivals will require infrastructure that builds a commonality of purpose [ 22 ] and shared responsibility, e.g., through data aggregators with levels of control, managed by trusted third-party organisations with a mission.

What invisible stories are hiding in other sustainability problems that HCI practitioners are well-placed to uncover? Communicating the hidden flows of capital and other data that sustains corrupt practices are one. Collecting and sharing the stories of the lands and people that resources are taken from or dumped in are another. Positioning HCI work as one of telling invisible stories requires careful consideration of the relationships that projects are embedded within, as well as who to partner with (as noted in 5.1).

5.4 Leverage points: leadership and temporal rhythms

The creation of new relations may also be aided by the illumination and valuing of hitherto invisible labour. For example, a great number of the patron participants were responsible for the work of group organisation for festival camping. This emotional labour of caring (about friends, future experiences, sustainability research) offers the opportunity to support them, and through them, their social groups. Within groups, multiple roles emerge including leadership, which is a potent leverage point to identify in the design of a sociotechnical intervention. While it would be disingenuous to state that this research isn't interested in creating individual behavioural change – it is – the means through which this may be achieved requires a focus on relationships, capacities, and practices to enable a change in culture and power, as is called for [ 20 ]. Addressing ways to support the different roles and relationships that groups are comprised can bring needed nuance to the shift from designing for individual persuasion to structural change and collective approaches [ 8 , 20 ]. In this ICT has much to offer, and to this end Proposal 1 can potentially streamline communications for social organisers, illuminate sources of social capital that patrons can draw upon, and if its intended design is realized, facilitate broader partnerships to involve festival management (e.g., encouraging on-site hire options).

Another opportunity emerges from considering timelines [ 23 ]. Scaling up interventions across multiple sites is challenged by the diversity of festivals in terms of their resources, audience, ethos, and scale; meaning that no one intervention will be received in the same way across all festivals. This permutation is anchored, however, by a shared temporality. Events by their nature have a before, during, and after, and often, repetition. Considering this and other sustainability issues and their interventions as events on a timeline leaves room for reflection, reorientation, and reinvention, key approaches to addressing systemic problems [ 23 ]. Also, cultural change can be slow or swift but guiding a change in a particular direction will require longer timelines than those available to academics on a funding cycle. In this, partnerships with third-party organisations that care (often not-for-profits) can enable longer effective timelines to deal with the possibility of dead ends, pivoting, and emerging uses. Supporting the leadership, capacity, and work of not-for-profits with sustainability agendas should be considered part of the Sustainable HCI agenda, as it enables understanding and designing for impact on a targeted problem over the long-term.

5.5 Limitations

This study did not attempt to map all festival professional practices though this would no doubt have yielded more insight and other opportunities for design. The reasons for this include the nature of employment (festival professionals rarely have ongoing roles and instead will work intensively during a contracted period) and the lack of festivals occurring during the period of study due to the Covid-19 pandemic. Future study could look at these employment conditions in more detail to better understand how they impact sustainability framing and decision-making. Similarly, the impact of sponsorship choice was briefly touched on in this study but this and other economic decisions could be greatly expanded upon.

Also, youth culture is complex and constantly changing. However, within the context of music festivals it will most likely continue to represent life lived large and openness to experimentation. This study did not attempt to research other aspects of the hedonic experience of festivals, but a focus on youth substance use could yield some compelling insights for the design of sustainability interventions linked to wellbeing. As pivotal experiences, music festivals have the potential to shape young peoples’ social habits and habits of care for self (i.e., knowing when to stop, slowing down, being present), which could also result in greater care for the land they are on, and more thoughtful choices in other aspects of life.

6 CONCLUSION

This case study of music festival campsite waste outlined key factors that influence the abandonment of items in campgrounds. Namely, the proliferation of cheap, breakable goods, mismatched festival management decisions, social influence in the campsites, and land – its weather and custodianship – drives festival campsite waste. Sociotechnical strategies were proposed that address different aspects of these factors and relationships between them, with these paired to festival timelines. No strategy is ideal, no one alone could solve the problem of waste, yet the accumulation of multiple, relational approaches is strongly argued for.

Engaging with contrasting viewpoints within this project produced a sense of shared responsibility for the problem. For young patrons who are exploring ways of being in the world, intervention in this problem may produce new social practices. For professionals, who need to balance sustainability decision-making against the concerns of marketing and resourcing, intervention presents a riskier endeavour. Both require artful integration into existing practices. By situating intervention within relations, and by expanding those relations to include contrasting viewpoints, a space can be opened for design work that addresses the dynamics supporting unsustainable practices. Importantly, this research argues for work that maps out the multiple design avenues and partnerships that a single complex problem can produce, to remain receptive to future and other uses. This study indicates useful design directions including those that enhance collectivization, collaboration, and partnership. HCI is well placed to support these actions through the design of tools that enable better organization, communication, evaluation, and feedback. Moving forward, this research calls for Sustainable HCI research that illuminates the hidden relations propping up unsustainable practices, changes the narrative around land relations, and considers how degrowth and other anti-consumption practices might be made socially and politically desirable, as well as practically achievable. In this way, Sustainable HCI can continue to grow into the force for care and transition that these times require.
