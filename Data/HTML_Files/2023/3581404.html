Player-Driven Game Analytics: The Case of Guild Wars 2

Johannes Pfau , Computational Media, University of California at Santa Cruz, United States, jopfau@ucsc.edu
Magy Seif El-Nasr , Computational Media, University of California at Santa Cruz, United States, magyse@gmail.com

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581404
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Video game analytics are widely adopted within academia and industrial development – yet, the player's perspective is rarely considered as a driving factor, even though vast communities are interested in self-regulated learning, cross-comparisons and empirical trends or insights. In this work, we propose to empower the role of the player as the central impulse for analytics, collect requirements and data from the community of one of the most popular MMORPGs (Guild Wars 2), establish a tool over a 18-month period of participatory development and discuss the co-created features. With analytics for in-game logs from ( n = 175, 099) unique players and atomic game actions of over 2 million hours played, we contribute a large-scale, long-term iterative implementation and evaluation of a player-driven instrument to quantify popularity, viability and hierarchical inspection between classes or individual performances. This tool found frequent usage among the actual game community, delivering game data science to the very player.

1 INTRODUCTION

The analysis, interpretation and reasoning over video game data established a major research area in the field of game science. There are prominent examples and well-studied techniques and methodologies with the majority of work stemming from the industry itself, developers and/or academic initiatives [ 13 , 14 ]. This bears multiple substantial advantages, as companies can easily accumulate large-scale and empirically valid datasets from their titles, and behavioral data, parameters, opinions as well as general insights can directly influence or shape design, balancing and the direction of development [ 53 ]. However, this can raise ethical concerns about the privacy and ownership of behavioral data [ 31 , 45 ], and tight feedback loops as these do not always capture the experiences, perceptions and requirements of the actual player population [ 40 ], leaving players rather powerless with respect to the evolution of games. While the latter is often explicitly desired, we argue that empowering the role of the player in game development and analysis can add a powerful dimension to the concept and construction of video games. Player-driven design (in non-game context also referred to as user-driven, participatory or co-design [ 23 , 50 ]) tries to overcome the commonly existing gap between designers and actual users [ 19 ] by involving the target population in the the design process, so that “problems and solutions coevolve” [ 48 ]. With this in mind, we recruited players from Guild Wars 2 [ 3 ], as one of the most popular and successful Massively Multiplayer Online Role Playing Games (MMORPGs) of our time, to record and share their granular game playing logs and assess their requirements on how to make most use out of these throughout a participatory design approach over 18 months; including ( n = 175, 099) unique players in total. The scope of these logs focuses mainly on raid bosses and similar encounters from five- to ten-player endgame content, as these are set in very comparable environments and playing procedures (in contrast to player-versus-player (PvP) or unconstrained open-world content) which facilitates the interest of analysis, comparison and optimization within the player base. Specifically, recorded logs are constructed in the utmost level of detail that allows the monitoring of every particular in-game action and movement, features offensive, defensive and supportive performance metrics, as well as in-depth combat replay. This paper outlines the collection of a vast corpus of voluntarily provided in-game data, the development of the player-driven game analytics platform Guild Wars 2 Wingman 1 based on the requirements and opinions of the actual player community and discusses opportunities of elevating game development following insights from these. We contribute to the scientific areas of games user research and game analytics by presenting a long-term, large-scale participatory research endeavour and by providing an instrument that brought game data science to a broad audience of players.

2 RELATED WORK

When focusing on a higher directive or endeavour, communities can create and establish impressive accomplishments, sometimes even surpassing capabilities of fully-fledged industrial studios. This is certainly the case for crowd-sourced citizen science games such as Foldit [ 8 ], the integration of human computation into the AAA-title Borderlands 3 [ 52 ] or teaching robotic knowledge through play [ 35 ], but even reach opportunities way beyond mere data collection. Da Silva et al. utilized the collective power of a community to retrieve a widely nested narrative and story background merely from player input [ 9 ]. Shaker et al. highlighted the capabilities of player-centric approaches for procedural content generation [ 46 ], including personalization of in-game maps or experiences. Even completely player-driven game development cycles have been shown to result in novel experiences, dynamic design procedures and central game features that are inherently tailored to the actual target audience [ 23 ]. Canossa and Drachen argue that increasing the players’ agency and influence on the development process can lead to enhanced experiences and immersion when introducing play-personas for customized gameplay [ 7 ] – which arguably extends similarly to game analytics. Partlan et al. utilized comparable procedures of participatory design in order to assess requirements and develop design-driven features for co-creative game AI design tools [ 30 ]. In non-digital domains, player-driven video analysis has proven to make an impact on learning and performance, such as in talent development of soccer [ 18 ]. With these promising outcomes of player-driven applications that a) supplied vast amounts of data for large-scale analysis and machine learning [ 8 , 9 , 52 ], b) considered the requirements of a broader scope of the eventual end users [ 7 , 30 ] and c) went beyond the original developers’ creativity and design horizons [ 7 , 23 , 46 ], we strive to combine insights about the capabilities of player- or even community-driven design and evaluate if this will extend to the domain of game analytics.

Game analytics represents an important concept of game data science, a field that has seen tremendous research advances and prominent application within the last decades [ 13 , 14 ]. The majority of approaches are stemming from and/or focusing on delivering insights for the game industry (e.g. data mining, classification or prediction) [ 10 ], targeting measures against churn [ 17 ], facilitating content generation [ 43 ], or easing the burden of testing [ 2 , 36 ] (among other areas). Koskenvoima and Mäntymäki conclude that even smaller development companies already utilize game analytics, mainly to improve game design, reduce the risk of new launches and to foster communication with their publishers by adding quantitative measures [ 21 ]. Most of the remaining approaches follow academic interests concerning similar topics or fundamental (psychological or technical) regularities, structures and concepts [ 11 , 32 , 60 ], often employing visualizations to gather insights for researchers or analysts [ 6 ]. Prominent implementations target spatio-temporal movement [ 1 , 27 , 54 , 55 ], decision making of individual or aggregate players [ 24 , 29 , 37 , 49 ] or higher-level metrics and statistics [ 12 ]. While the primary application of game analytics thus mostly remains within academia or industry, making video game data transparent, explainable and applicable to its players is one of the driving topics within the neighboring areas of game-related explainable AI and player modeling [ 25 , 38 , 59 , 62 ]. Paralleling these recent endeavors could arguably shift or extend how we apply successful game analytics – by and for players.

With a predominant appearance of competitiveness in online games, successful industrial representatives already reached out to their player bases regarding the propagation of analytical tools and certain community-based tools established themselves, but so far, scientific studies about the usability, impact, user experience or player requirements remain in the early stages. Kleinman and Seif El-Nasr recently pushed a position paper on player-centric approaches for analytics and data utilization, discussing how players currently use and would like to use their data in the context of (competitive) esports [ 20 ]. They conclude that the state of the art of research necessitates empirical and granular investigations on the requirements and analytics behavior of actual players and communities. Wallner et al. consolidate these player demands and highlight that especially usable visualizations from a player-centric perspective are rare and under-investigated within academia and industry [ 57 ]. They deploy mixed-method studies of three popular competitive esports games and extract vital features for these particular applications, influenced by insights from previous surveys that produced multiple spatio-temporal analysis features [ 22 , 56 ].

To the best of our knowledge, no work has approached player-driven game analytics outside of competitive esports before and participatory development around game analytics remains an under-investigated area. We aim to close this gap in the scientific fields of games user research and data science, especially elevate the prior findings from smaller player studies to large-scale community evaluations over extended periods of development, and outline and discuss our approach in the following sections.

3 APPROACH

In the following, we lay out how we commenced with introducing the tool to the community, recorded player requirements over extended magnitudes of time and sample size, present the aggregated dataset that builds the objective foundation for the features, and briefly introduce terms and concepts of the underlying game to ease understanding. As this procedure included the recording and processing of human data, the participatory development was reviewed and approved by an institutional review board of one author's university.

3.1 Participatory Development

To kick off the idea of player-driven game analytics and cover the widest audience possible, we initially contacted and consulted representatives for both extremes of the proficiency spectrum; i.e. leaders and mentors of a guild specifically targeted at teaching and training raids for beginners, as well as the leader and members of one of the most prestigious and successful speedrun guilds 2 of Guild Wars 2 . Open interviews employing brainstorming and active discussions led to the first demonstrator of the platform (mainly integrating Encounter Analysis , cf. Section 4.3 ). This initial, web-based version laid ground for one of the major building blocks, produced a working feature that started to visualize useful empirical data about the viability of certain in-game classes at different encounters and identified the vital need of data privacy control. Starting with an initial dataset of about 30,000 logs, participants spread the word and advertised the tool among in-game and public communication channels and social media. Over the following 18 months and using a designated Discord messaging server, a community of players interested in their performances or data and analytics of Guild Wars 2 in general formed around this platform, who discussed interpretations of the aggregated data, opinions on balancing and optimization and expressed feature requests, ideas and adjustments on a perpetual basis. Following an iterative participatory development cycle [ 28 ], we incited discussions, let democratic votes determine controversial design decisions and integrated features and functionalities that satisfied the community's demands while offering new insights about the aggregated data. Among other insights, players were mostly interested in (a) how classes of the game usually perform against the various encounters, (b) how popular and (c) how efficient these classes and constellations of them are, (d) how difficult bosses are from an empirical standpoint, (e) how their damage per second (dps) values fare in comparison to other players, (f) if they and their groups improved over longer periods of time, (g) to what extent balance changes impacted the game, and obviously, (h) how to optimize and maximize their individual and group's performance to the extreme. Effectively, the collected requirements and resulting features were mainly classified into Aggregate Analytics (statistics and insights across a larger population of players), Individual Analytics (own performances and detailed observations) and aspects of using our tool as a Social Platform (sharing group logs between peers, earning bragging rights by reaching record performances or showing empirical proof of their in-game accomplishments). To satisfy privacy needs, we enabled the automatic classification of private logs (e.g. for speedrun groups that do not want to reveal their strategies prematurely), added the option to remove single logs, and integrated anonymization for users that do not want to appear or be findable by name.

Until the time of submission of this paper, ( n r = 5, 159) users registered on the website and/or joined the Discord server to be able to participate in the development of this tool, i.e. to request new features or extensions, report bugs, discuss about the tools or visualizations or findings that users derived, and to give their vote on controversial decisions.

Implementing an ongoing iterative development cycle, we pursued the following steps on a recurring basis:

  • Aggregate the (current) requirements of the player community (using designated qualitative channels)
  • Structure and categorize feature suggestions and questions that could be answered through these features
  • Rank these requests based on their contribution to produce novel insights, the interest of the community and feasibility/ease of implementation
  • Develop the feature (or extension) and release a first version
  • Adapt the implementation based on the qualitative and quantitative feedback of the community

As a larger pool of participants took part in this process, diverging opinions on implementation details and the design direction of features appeared frequently, e.g. the expression of relative performances as percentages or percentiles (cf. Figure 10 ), the leaderboard being built on either top or overall performances of players, or whether to consider a new patch as significant enough to be treated as a new era of balancing. These were always attempted to solve by democratic votes first, which was further refined into discussions if no decided majority appeared after a voting period of one week.

3.2 Dataset

Up to the time of publication of this work, we recorded ( n l = 5, 379, 121) encounter logs of ( n = 175, 099) unique players, recorded from the years 2017 to 2023 and encompassing over 16 billion character actions – covering playing time that adds up to approximately 2 million hours in total. Logs were either classified to be affiliated with a custom player group (for representation, reference or to hide them from public usage; $7.6\%$ ) or considered to be from random, non-organized parties ( $92.4\%$ ). In total, 108 groups requested affiliation of their logs, representing everything from casual players over organized static groups up to almost each professional high-end speedrun guild currently active in the game.

3.3 Game Terminology

In order to contextualize the approach and facilitate clearer understanding of the following features and concepts, we mention the associated game and outline its essential structure. Guild Wars 2 is a classical MMORPG featuring single- and multiplayer content in storylines, open world events, various PvP modes and endgame encounters such as raid bosses, fractals or strike missions. As the latter are set in fixed scenarios with only few probabilistic factors and established group compositions, strategies and roles, they enable very comparable analytics and large communities formed around discussions and optimizations to overcome these challenges (e.g. cf. Acknowledgements). As Guild Wars 2 is specifically designed to not feature power creep mechanics such as increasing level caps or item qualities over time, the vast majority of players of this endgame content participates on an identical or very similar character and equipment attribute level, and performance is mainly influenced by in-game proficiency, mastery of the classes, encounter knowledge, strategy and group composition, which further adds to the comparability of the data produced within these scenarios. Still, when accepting certain degrees of noise or acknowledging these factors of variance by clustering players into equipment tiers or approximately subtracting out these confounding variables, the same approach and features as presented here are likely to produce similarly powerful insights for instanced group PvE content in general, such as raids, dungeons, trials or ultimate encounters in World of Warcraft [ 5 ], The Elder Scrolls Online [ 61 ] or Final Fantasy XIV [ 47 ].

Guild Wars 2 features a variety of playable class options (from now on referred to as professions ), where each of the nine core professions can be expanded by particular specializations that can add further capabilities or open up new roles for this profession (e.g. the Ranger profession can be augmented with the Druid specialization to add a support dimension to the character or with the Soulbeast specialization to increase the damage potential). In theory, the professions of Guild Wars 2 allow abundant combinations of character constellations, such as individually distributed equipment attributes, chosen passive character traits and active weapon type and skill choices. In that way, players can represent and play out different roles within their party, such as dealing direct damage, damage over time, offering support or different degrees of mixtures of these factors. However, for the sake of optimization and role compression within the group, most of the time, these professions are min-maxed towards the roles of full support (heal and buff application), offensive support (dps and buff application) or maximal dps (either direct damage or damage over time). The majority of players uses builds and equipment that maximizes their functionality in one of these roles with only situational variation, following community guides and recommendations. Thus, the tool presented in this approach automatically classifies recorded players into buckets of full support, offensive support, direct damage and damage over time and lets players divide the displayed data within the features accordingly.

In the following sections, we outline the constructed features and visualizations that were sought and implemented so far, give examples for the kind of questions they can potentially answer and exemplify how players use these to generate insights.

4 AGGREGATE ANALYTICS

The following section illustrates features that visualize or describe insights aggregated over the whole dataset or producing general insights across professions, encounters, compositions or time. We follow a hierarchical approach of presentation starting with the highest layer of abstraction (all professions used in all raid boss encounters across all time periods) and gradually increase the level of detail (analysis on the group level versus a single boss; analysis on the individual profession level versus a single boss; analysis on the level of a very instance of a combat log; and analysis of a player's sequential actions within a log, in comparison to behavior of interest), concluding with a comparison across time.

4.1 Profession Popularity

One of the most requested and straight-forward visualizations of the dataset is the popularity of the professions over time (cf. Figure 1 ), where we chose the dates of releases of major expansions, significant balance updates or related reworks as meaningful milestones to consider (from now on referred to as patch eras ). Not only does it show how fancied particular professions are during different periods of time, but it already indicates the increase or decline of overall profession viability and portends the metagame (in the sense of: most e fficient t actic a vailable ); even if actual viabilities, performances and optimal strategies necessitate deeper analysis (cf. upcoming Sections 4.2 , 4.3 , 4.6 ).

Possible interpretations of this feature include the suppression of multiple core professions by the release of specializations that dominated them (e.g. the Engineer was replaced by its Holosmith specialization, as well as the Ranger by the Soulbeast after their release in the expansion Path of Fire (Sep 2017) ). Above that, slow adaptations to damage nerfs of classes can be identified (e.g. Deadeye or Weaver that start to decline from mid 2018), as well as damage or support increments (e.g. Firebrand ( Mar 2019 ) or Scourge ( May 2021 )) and the supplement of role coverage by other professions (e.g. historically, only the Chronomancer was able to provide a specific important buff, but when this was extended to the Renegade ( Oct 2018 ), the popularity of these two professions slowly balanced out, until the drastic suppression through the newly introduced Mechanist ( Feb 2022 )).

4.2 Group Composition Analysis

While the former visualization (cf. Section 4.1 ) is able to produce broad and overarching insights about profession popularity in general, it lacks detail about the actual performance and viability of those classes and completely disregards the diverse nature of the various encounters that - more often than not - encourage the use of group compositions, professions and set-ups specifically tailored to the particular boss. To bring this down to the encounter level and contrast popular to optimal strategies, we implemented the Group Composition Analysis feature (cf. Figure 2 ) that ranks groups as well as single professions regarding their usage frequency and empirical efficiency. This followed players’ requests to inspect what compositions are popular and which are more optimal for each single boss, while immediately highlighting that these can be drastically different. To showcase this difference, we depicted a prime example in Figure 2 where the group composition of makes up the majority of successful kills (1115) of this encounter, yet the average combat time of this composition is only half as fast (1:30 minutes, not captured by the figure) as the most efficient composition. Despite being a constellation that is not secret or unknown, the record composition (0:49 minutes) was only executed 120 times in this patch era. To explain these drastic differences between the compositions, users of our tool can closely inspect and compare single logs using these setups with respect to damage progression, movement and applied strategies (cf. Section 4.4 ); and/or consult qualitative explanations from partner websites (cf. Acknowledgments) that inter-exchange strategical clarifications for empirical validations with the project of this approach. To quickly resolve the question regarding this example, Firebrand as well as Scourge provide decent damage potentials while simultaneously offering vast contributions to group survivability (which pardons execution errors and explains why they are included in most of the popular compositions); yet the combination of Soulbeast , Berserker and Dragonhunter excels in burst damage capability that can exploit and greatly benefit from short boss phases – however, the most optimal execution and timing is way harder and more unforgiving to errors.

4.3 Encounter Analysis

Even if popularity of single professions and group compositions can be inferred from the features outlined in the previous sections, the contribution of the individual profession to the average efficiency was limited to an average measurement and lacks explanatory and exemplary value. To approach the major questions of “how well does a profession function at a given encounter?” and “how do my own performances compare to other players?” and to break down all measurable encounter-specific parameters on one page, Encounter Analysis presents comparative evaluations regarding overall durations, profession dps values, buff uptimes, boss-specific game mechanic handling and crowd control contributions through distribution boxplots, percentile views and timelines. Users can query and limit the selection of logs towards the inclusion or exclusion of (specific) groups or players, professions, patch eras or dates, can constrain them to only successful (or unsuccessful) logs, specify the minimum and maximum number of contained players, set an upper limit for the sample size and/or demand that the queried logs have to be among the top number of logs with respect to their duration. Figure 3 visualizes the dps distributions of all professions occurring in this sample of logs against one particular boss encounter. Users of this tool can inspect and modify this interactive plot at will, e.g. include or exclude supporters, split each profession into direct damage and damage over time or replace the underlying data by another measured variable. Highlighted values indicate data points from a user's own performances and thus immediately give an impression about their personal proficiency compared to other players. To further elaborate on this numerically, an adjacent view calculates how good the top and average performances of the active user are for each profession, expressed as percentiles. As users are inclined to analyze what factors (skill rotations, group constellations, buff uptimes or general strategies) might lead to certain performances (e.g. top values), each data point is linked to the detailed view and combat replay outlined in Section 4.4 . Aggregated models of one's own sequential behavior can furthermore be directly contrasted to behavior of top players (to compute differences that might shed light on improvement strategies) or the overall average play style through Rotation Comparison (cf. Section 4.5 ).

Venturing a broad interpretation of the distributions of Figure 3 , the Holosmith is arguably a good pick against this encounter, as it scores the highest median dps of all professions and can reach almost unparalleled values. However, some players of the Dragonhunter , Daredevil or Chronomancer even surpass the maximum values of the former, so players interested in the question under which conditions these professions excel can deeper inspect the top performance logs. One factor that can shift the ranking of medians is the inclusion or exclusion of supporters, as e.g. Scourge , Firebrand , Chronomancer or Tempest produce clusters very indicative for support roles. Above that, the density of data points again delivers one measure of popularity while simultaneously indicating one arguably major reason for this, which is the capability of contributing high damage values to the group (i.e. Holosmith , Weaver or Dragonhunter represent common and decent picks, in contrast to Necromancer , Thief or Ranger ). This however cannot implicitly convey any information about the difficulty of these classes and can only indicate survivability and group value. Additionally, where to draw the lines between professions becoming “optimal”, “still viable” and “not viable” remains a community effort of interpretation and discussion, as we are not imposing thresholds or judgments, but solely pave the way for deeper analytics using data-driven techniques.

4.4 Log Analysis

Finally, even if most of the logs are comparable (regarding a single encounter), the combinatorial explosion of decisions players can make (e.g. the sequence of skills they execute, the differences in attributes, equipment or traits or the setup and constellation of groups) paired with random factors of in-game calculation and continuous character movement, results in always unique situations, so that no two logs can be completely identical. To facilitate granular understanding about individual logs (e.g. finding out how a player reached so high Chronomancer dps values in Figure 3 or why the composition of from Figure 2 works so well), users can inspect single logs that display affiliated players, their skill sequences, buff uptimes, positioning and further important variables for each point in time (or overall), and moreover study an interactive combat replay video at their own pace, produced by Elite Insights 3 . Taken Figure 4 b as a reference (and considering the preceding and upcoming content of the replay), one can follow that the Druid tanks the boss away from the group, which itself stays mainly stacked on one position to focus heal and buff distribution, while a distant Herald takes care of an encounter-specific kite mechanic (indicated by red hazard fields). If possible, players avoid all colored hazard fields, as they might cause significant damage or cause other penalties to the group. In addition to the positional representation, skill sequence usage (from now on referred to as rotations , cf. Figure 4 a), current dps and health point values are displayed, to highlight which actions and behavior might be desirable (or undesirable) in which situation.

4.5 Rotation Comparison

Visualizing the sequence of actions a player executed (cf. Figure 4 a) adds exemplary value to the performance outcome of this player in this particular log. Yet, the mere repetition of this sequence is unlikely to produce the exact same outcome in realistic circumstances and hardly conveys a deeper understanding of the play style or strategy. From previous work using expert interviews and player modeling [ 37 , 39 ], we followed that players tend to follow rotations of skill sequences in these environments, repeating patterns and action combos based on preference and/or dps optimization. Using directed probabilistic sequence graphs, users of our tool are able to model and visualize player behavior from single logs, a collection of logs or even comparisons between populations of interest. Figure 5 contrasts the rotations of two sets of players performing the Catalyst class (238 in total) on a specific boss, split after the 95th dps percentile. Each node corresponds to the activation of a skill, while possible transitions to other skills are indicated and weighted by edges. Together with this visual contradistinction that already suggests a way more streamlined and consistent prioritization of skill sequences by high-performing players (especially in the (orange) fire and (blue) water parts of the rotation), the biggest differences between node frequencies and edge probabilities are also presented in textual form. In essence, the largest differences can be attributed to execution speed, the stringent adherence to optimal skill combos and the ability to carry out multiple actions in parallel. Thorough explanations of the functionality and the interpretation of the use case of this feature are examined and published in concurrent work [ 49 ].

4.6 Balance Patch Impact

Online games are subject to constantly occurring changes. Apart from the introduction of new content, balancing updates are necessary for a dynamic and adaptive metagame to introduce possible new dimensions and constellations and to increase or decrease the capabilities of imbalanced classes [ 4 ]. Traditionally, decisions leading to balance regulations stem from player feedback, too unpopular (or too popular) choices, or measurements indicating that an in-game entity is performing way too high (or low) to be compliant with the original intentional design [ 33 , 34 ]. As forming opinions about the current state of the balancing usually takes a long time, feedback from the community is often reactive or slow-paced and measures under ideal circumstances (such as in Section 5.2 ) do not necessarily apply to actual encounter experiences. Instead of merely relying on theoretical expert knowledge, heuristic assessment calculations or controversial community opinions, we approached the estimation of the balancing impact again from a data-driven perspective. More specifically, the Balance Patch Impact feature provides game update impact visualizations for one selected profession across all eras on a per-boss level (cf. Figure 6 ). These are produced by retrieving all performances of this profession and encounter during the particular patch era and comparing them to the former era via Welch's t-tests for independent samples [ 58 ]. This enables the measurement of implemented design decisions on actual in-game boss encounters and performances - and thus considers not only parametrical adjustments (e.g. if a certain profession or skill power was somehow altered), but also contains the actual player behavior and how they adapted to these changes. E.g., if skills are removed or redesigned, weapon choices were made more or less attractive, profession-specific mechanics, cooldowns or weights were tuned, this can likely cause a shift in player behavior, their preferences, decisions and rotations, for which in-game consequences are hard to capture by theoretical or qualitative measures, but inherently reflected in this approach. Figure 6 outlines the impact of balance patches on the Berserker profession, comparing two different bosses. While some changes produced significant performance differences across the board, some patches only influenced single encounters or possibly produced controversial results. In the interactive version of this tool, users can hover over the comparisons and are not only presented the outcomes and directions of the statistical tests, but also with the official list of patch notes that might have had an impact on this. Together with the option to inspect single logs again, this constitutes an arguably potent feature of identifying and reasoning over the actual implications of implemented changes and could be useful for developers and players alike.

5 INDIVIDUAL ANALYTICS

While aggregate statistics have proven to be useful for general insights on the game, professions and encounters – and thus are the vantaged approach for developers and the majority of scientific approaches – members of our participatory design approach could not stress the importance of individual analytics enough. Even if some individual comparisons and insights can already be found in the aforementioned techniques (e.g. Section 4.3 ), the following section will outline deeper means of individual analysis. All features in this section are only visible from the perspective of the respective user account.

5.1 Gameplay History

One important dimension of individual feedback turned out to be the history of weekly completion. Figure 7 visualizes the history of 45 weeks of raiding from a registered group of players. Users reported to keep track of their proportion of failed to successful boss attempts, the improvement or development of their duration over longer periods of time and to inspect where usually downtime (time not spend on actual combat) happens.

5.2 Performance Development

In order to increase their in-game proficiency, players draw on grinding (i.e. repeating the same encounter over and over again) most of the time. As this is arguably more complicated to organize for actual boss encounters, they commonly utilize Guild Wars 2’s training area that enables them to learn rotations and benchmark their individual dps capabilities under ideal conditions (unlimited buffs, no incoming damage or mechanics, no other players needed). Even if dps values are displayed in-game, manually recording these remains rather unhandy, distracts from the actual task to focus on polishing performance and might neglect subconscious improvements of muscle memory and profession understanding over time. Thus, we implemented a straight-forward Performance Development tool that keeps track over all logs produced in a training area, sorts them by profession and patch era and displays the progression of performance as charts (cf. Figure 8 ). Additionally, mean values and linear regressions over time are calculated to indicate the average performance in a given timeframe as well as expressing the magnitude of the learning effect and if this was statistically significant (in either direction). As a certain share of participating players expressed that they do not use the training area on the one hand, or would additionally organize prolonged boss grinds with their experienced group, we extended this feature to also capture the performance development of individual players on actual encounters.

5.3 Damage Progression

Training for maximum proficiency requires thus not only endurance and diligence, but most of the time comes with a community effort, as the most optimal way to play a game (or perform given a certain profession) is rarely presented from (or even known to) developers of any game. In order to compare different professions, builds and rotations – and even come up with guides as mentioned in the earlier section – players reportedly spend weeks to month of testing out, practicing and comparing different setups, constellations and rotations to come up with more and more optimized strategies on how to play single professions. Again, assessing and comparing single resulting values under ideal circumstances can still produce sub-optimal results when applied to the diverse nature of possible in-game encounters. One possible disparity might emerge when exposed to bosses with different phase lengths, as different professions (or builds) may ramp up their damage output in completely different progressions (due to the nature of the game, e.g. skill cooldowns, buff uptimes and direct damage versus damage over time). To give deeper insights about the Damage Progression of a player's performance (within single logs), we gave them the opportunity to combine performances from multiple logs and display dps progressions over time in contrast to other recordings. Figure 9 compares three professions with each three logs of similar rotations, recorded under the ideal constellations of the aforementioned training area. While the Chronomancer achieves the highest benchmark in the end and might be the optimal choice for long fights, this is not necessarily true for phases shorter than ten seconds, where the burst damage potential of the Dragonhunter excels. The Renegade apparently builds up his damage rather slowly, but catches up with the Dragonhunter after about 80 to 100 seconds. This feature reportedly supported group constellation construction, theory formation and in-depth optimization (as it is not constrained to the training area, but can be applied to investigate particular boss phases and damage progressions of players across different logs).

6 SOCIAL PLATFORM

Unsurprisingly, as soon as a community establishes around a topic, social exchange and status become increasingly important. Even if the insights of this section might not generalize to other games of the genre or produce technologies novel enough to be standalone contributions, we decided to briefly outline them and fully capture the community requirements for the sake of complete player-driven game analytics.

6.1 Records & Leaderboards

Competition can be regarded as one of the driving factors for optimization. Especially for members of speedrun groups, individual proficiency and performances might not be as important as the prestige and bragging rights achieved by elaborate compositions, fine-tuned rotations and mastered boss mechanics that result in unprecedented record kills. Based on this, we added a record overview that automatically computes the fastest performances of all time and displays their durations, players and group affiliations for all encounters on a single page. To not disregard the effort and impact that balance patches might have had on these records, they can also be viewed per particular era. For players less interested in the fastest clears, but rather in optimizing their favorite profession, we additionally implemented a leaderboard that grants the top three players a podium, calculated by their top performance on every boss in comparison to other players of the same profession, accumulated over all of the encounters. As the abundance of players, logs and encounters made it hard to pin down the own distance to the leading players, users requested an illustration of their overall progress with respect to the top performances ( Encounter Benchmark , cf. Figure 10 ).

6.2 Peer Network

Eventually, MMORPGs are social games that connect players which often leads to bonding, group formation or even friendship. Occasionally, communities establish that unite players and sometimes, they prefer to stay within their peer group. As the interest of understanding connections between players rose and these are not trivially identifiable from a set of logs or aggregated names alone, we implemented the Peer Network feature as exemplified in Figure 11 . Within this tool, users can modify a minimum threshold of connections (i.e. number of logs that include both accounts) and limit the set of logs similarly to the filters mentioned in Section 4.3 (such as patch era or group affiliation). They are then presented a network of nodes that correspond to unique other players, connected by edges if they shared mutual logs. Following an inverted physical spring system, users that play together more often align themselves closer to each other while being pushed from non-connected nodes. This results in smaller groups forming in the outskirts of the network, while randomly connected nodes remain in the fuzzy center.

Yet, striving for maximal benefit and usability for the target population, we followed an iterative cycle of requirement assessment, development and qualitative evaluation to come up with the features presented above. The majority of users was interested in Aggregate Statistics , as one the core arguments of this sizeable dataset lies in the immediate overview of the current and past states of the game, as users of the tool reported that “[it] gives a pretty good understanding of current meta and class favouritism” (Participant #2434). This includes the comparison and review of broad viability measures, but also deeper compositions and strategies, as “when [new instanced dungeon] was released, it was very cool to check it regularly for other kills, watch their replays and compare kill times and such, it's awesome” (Participant #849). The community states that “Wingman has continued to be invaluable to raiders” (Participant #526) and that the publicized access to how different professions, builds and compositions fare – for the first time in the game's history – changed the perspective of players and potentially even developers; “ever since we got Wingman we should not have any balancing problems anymore” (Participant #356). The drastically more prominent daily usages of Encounter and Log Analysis arguably stem from their queryable nature and the potential to observe nuanced subsets of the data (or sample logs, respectively) to answer specific questions of detail – which turns out to be the major time factor of how users employ the tool. Figure 12 depicts that users most likely start their journey on either of these two features (per session) and that they often transition to the detailed Log Analysis from higher-level analytics (such as Encounter Analysis, Encounter Benchmark or Performance Development), indicating that users aim to refine their insights hierarchically, from more abstract to more specific information.

Apart from the higher-level aggregated analytics, players frequently draw on the Social aspects of the platform. Profession leaderboards reportedly “enable a bit of a fun competition and room for improvement” (Participant #1562) among players of the particular classes and “it gives a lot of people something to do in the game” (Participant #985). This is underlined by the higher daily visit frequencies of the Leaderboard (where they check how well they fare on the profession ladders), and the Encounter Benchmark (where they can immediately check bosses they can still improve on). When following the journey in Figure 12 again, users interested in this question are likely to start on the Leaderboard, pass into Encounter Benchmark and proceed to Log Analysis (back and forth for specific comparisons), reiterating their top-down hierarchical information collection.

Even though Individual Analytics show less usage than the other features, players highlight that “so many insights come from Wingman” (Participant #279) with respect to deeper numerical analysis of rotations, combinations and adaptations to encounter phase lengths and personal accomplishments. Especially when it comes to Damage Progression for single encounters or training sessions, or their individual Performance Development over larger amounts of time, users tend to spend more time working out details than for the former aggregate measures which often allow to draw conclusions at a first glance. Performance Development and Gameplay History are most likely be followed by Log Analysis (to inspect or replay the combat of interest), whereas Damage Progression is above that coupled with assessing individual Encounter Benchmarks (as per Figure 12 ), presumably because users aim to draw connections between their improvement on an encounter over long term on the one hand and the relative quality of their best performance to the community in effect.

From an abstracted, generalized viewpoint, we conclude that the most important capabilities for game analytics tools involve giving quick answers to general overarching questions, well grounded in empirical foundations. These help new players to get into challenging gameplay by providing information about viable choices and strategies, and can give empirically grounded answers to hypotheses and opinions of more advanced players, which is not constrained to Guild Wars 2 in this case, but arguably holds for other games and genres up to a certain point. To ease players into making deeper informed decisions beyond surface statistics, we advise the hierarchical presentation of features across layers of interest (cf. Figure 12 ). One implemented example for this would be the link between Profession Popularity ( 4.1 ), Group Composition Analysis ( 4.2 ), Encounter Analysis ( 4.3 ) and Log Analysis ( 4.4 ): Players can dive deeper and get more detailed explanations just by clicking their profession of interest. That means to explain the overuse of Mechanist s in Figure 1 , users can infer from the following features that it (a) leads to higher efficiency on many bosses, (b) reaches above-average dps values across the board, and (c) only requires a simple and forgiving skill rotation. This hierarchical design traverses through the entire tool, aiming to facilitate not only understanding of the game, but also individual proficiency acquisition. Users who seek to hone their skills can e.g. inspect leading players of the Profession Leaderboards ( 6.1 ), break these performances down in the accompanied Encounter Benchmarks for that profession and get immediately referred to the comparison of Log Analyses ( 4.4 ) between their personal best and the top performance in this context – which can even be inspected precisely with a close-up Rotation Comparison ( 4.5 ) to reveal and visualize all the nuances of action differences. Apart from delivering empirical insights of the game, players of (online) games show elevated interests in comparing themselves to other players and strive to improve their performances and accomplishments. This is in line with the demand for perceived competence as one of the factors of intrinsic motivation after self-determination theory [ 44 ] and not a novel contribution itself, but we emphasize the benefit of connecting record and leaderboard displays with explainable analytical tools that foster reproducibility and learning in games.

8 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

The tool proposed in this paper obtained broad attention and usage from the Guild Wars 2 community, collecting data from and about beginners, intermediate players up to almost every active speedrun guild of the game. During our participatory development phase, it already found mentioning and application across the primarily important game-related communication channels, such as the in-house forums, Twitch, reddit or YouTube, indicating its impact on the player base. This however lacks a deeper empirical investigation about the usability and benefit of the introduced features, which will be tackled in subsequent qualitative and quantitative evaluations, to not overexpand the scope of this paper that focuses on the instrument to advance player-driven game analytics. These should explicitly assess the quality of conclusions that players can infer from the different features, as well as general mechanisms of how player communities work with data and visualizations, to identify gaps of understanding and misinterpretation.

For the interpretation of the data, in-depth expert knowledge of the game is occasionally necessary. The platform of this approach partners with multiple other tools and groups that qualitatively explain game concepts from the experience of professional players; up to the level of world-class speedrun groups (cf. Acknowledgments). To keep these interpretations as unambiguous and understandable as possible, we strive to increase self-explainability and reduce confusing factors, again relying on iterative community feedback for that. Users of this tool reported to interpret its features in a variety of ways. Among the most common statements is the general interest of the current state of the game, inspiration to play new professions, compositions or strategies, optimize and track their performance and certainly the prestige and satisfaction of reaching or exceeding personal or global records. While this work summarizes our contribution to participatory development for game analytics, Guild Wars 2 Wingman will persist to offer a player-driven platform open for and adaptive to requirements and opinions. As the necessity to consult third-party websites during or after play reportedly reduces the immediacy of a player's game experience that might impair the accuracy of the subjective proficiency assessment and/or the overall usability of the tool, we furthermore integrated an in-game plugin that serves the main functionalities of the tool immediately during play. This plugin adjusts to the currently fought or defeated encounter, compares the player's performance to median and record numbers instantly and keeps track of the most recent playing history, linked back to the website for deeper inspections. For future work, we aim to polish and adjust the functionalities of this plugin to the community's needs, while keeping a visually compact and non-obstructive interface.

This work is currently constrained to comparable endgame content and neglects analysis about unrestricted gameplay and PvP. While the latter is arguably harder to investigate using data-driven techniques, we argue that, once abundant data about similar game states is collected, general insights about profession viability, tactics, combos or counter-strategies can be extracted for PvP situations when utilizing appropriate pattern recognition approaches. As we were following the requirements and demands of a community majorly interested in instanced endgame group PvE, the generalizability of the current state of the analytics set is limited to comparable game modes (which yet make up large parts of most MMORPGs, e.g. raids, dungeons, trials or ultimate encounters in World of Warcraft [ 5 ], The Elder Scrolls Online [ 61 ] or Final Fantasy XIV [ 47 ]). The underlying metrics of interest such as popularity, viability, efficiency and detailed (aggregated) performance analyses however are arguably similarly expressive for single-player or competitive PvP settings such as in Multiplayer Online Battle Arena (MOBA) games (such as League of Legends [ 41 ] or Dota 2 [ 51 ]) or class-based first-person shooters (such as Valorant [ 42 ] or Borderlands 3 [ 15 ]) – which yields great potential when following player-driven development.

In addition to the vast amount of quantitative data collected in this work, one of the upcoming additions will integrate subjective statements in terms of perceived difficulty, interestingness, knowledge and general player experience to bosses, professions and other game entities. This might hopefully allow for combinations of data-driven and opinionative approaches and could help predicting success factors (for players and developers) and correlations.

Coming back to traditional applications of game data science and games user research, the data collected through this approach opens up a variety of affordances. For instance, the overarching game history of five years (up to now) together with preferences, social connections and the impact of game patches could consolidate our understanding and predictive abilities of churn. Above that, the vast abundance of granularly recorded player actions can serve as a potent and empirically valid dataset for deeper player models or imitation learning. With this number of comparable situations, machine learning could also be applied for identifying underlying factors of success, skill acquisition or development, empirical approximations of difficulty and more. For these reasons, we offer each interested party such as players, researchers or developers, to calculate own statistics from (the non-privatized part of) our data set, available through the public API of our tool 4 .

Lastly, having a comprehensive overview about the state of the game, the actual way players understand, interpret and perform with the designed professions – together with both popularity as well as viability distributions – can arguably lead to well-informed balancing evaluations and decisions. In future work, we strive to combine the various data-driven techniques of this approach with a large-scale community survey concerning underlying perceptions and subjective requirements for balancing. This conjunction can arguably lead to a novel player-driven approach of assessing, understanding and implementing adequate and fair balance.

8.1 Ethical Considerations

Apart from the technical and methodological limitations just discussed, certain ethical considerations were already raised or could become apparent. First of all, providing a public platform that allows the filtering and querying of specific players and constellations bears the hazard of stalking, trolling and/or discrediting individual players by spreading and/or belittling their worst performance logs. To counter these intentions early on, we gave registered users full control over the privacy and visibility of logs that contained their characters and let them create personal groups that automatically classified and excluded new affiliated logs from the public. Moreover, accounts can choose to be anonymized and excluded from appearing in any query.

While quantification of in-game metrics and statistics can help players to understand a game and improve themselves, it simultaneously raises efficiency demands that can considerably obfuscate player experience. Especially in looking for group (LFG) channels of multiplayer games, unknown players are likely to be rejected if they choose to play less efficient builds and classes, which would not happen if there would be no reliable way of quantifying these builds’ presumable performances. Even in single-player situations, the experience of playing a setup that produces great game feel, satisfying rotations or (audiovisual) appeal can easily be watered down by the awareness that it might not produce optimal outcomes. For the conjunction of the best of these two worlds, we are aiming to investigate how iterative game balancing can take on a player-driven approach in future work, unifying players’ requirements for gratifying experiences, diversity and numerical equilibria.

Apart from privacy and the right to experience a game undistorted from third party tools, Metcalf and Crawford [ 26 ] mention justice as one of the important ethical factors when reasoning about human data. This entails that visualized data should be drawn from a diverse pool of communities, backgrounds and abilities. To a certain extent, we approached this by sampling from and spreading the word among beginner-level, casual as well as high-end speedrun guilds, which still does not acknowledge potentially marginalized backgrounds and does not guarantee representativeness. Collecting (voluntarily declared) background information of players and incorporating these into the (still customizable) query process would be a first step towards a more inclusive portrayal.

On top of that, competitive leaderboards, rankings and immediate comparisons to other players and groups of the game can evoke or amplify perceptions of inferiority, especially for differently abled or impaired players. While this certainly poses a challenging factor, we a) try to produce intuitive and explainable visualizations that illustrate where and how players could improve themselves, b) congregated a supportive community that turned out to rather assist each other than to boast with selfish play and c) refer to partner sites that add qualitative explanations, guides and even low-intensity builds that produce decent performances without requiring overly optimized gameplay.

9 CONCLUSION

This work proposed and investigated the introduction of player-driven game analytics. Following a 18-month participatory development cycle, we established Wingman , a platform that brought game analytics to a broad community of Guild Wars 2 . Attending to the interests, requirements and opinions of players from all stages of proficiency (from beginners up to world-class speedrunners), we integrated and refined a battery of features that aim to elevate understanding of in-game concepts, performances, proportions and changes. Essentially, players are most interested in Aggregate Analytics , which can be descriptive empirical values of the current state of the game, popularity and efficiency of different classes, builds or options; Individual Analytics such as the comparison of own performances to a large player base, tracking of their own development, and visual and textual explanations of how they can improve; and building up a Social Platform among the community interested in data, analytics and optimization. Although interpretation is ought to be executed carefully by users of these functions, the community that formed around the platform and the analytics of Guild Wars 2 Wingman proved to produce meaningful and coherent reasoning. Player-driven game analytics can arguably complete the image of game data science from academic and industrial perspectives – empowering insights and features from players for players.
