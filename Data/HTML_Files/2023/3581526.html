The Intricacies of Social Robots: Secondary Analysis of Fictional Documentaries to Explore the Benefits and Challenges of Robots in Complex Social Settings

Judith Dörrenbächer , Ubiquitous Design / Experience & Interaction, University of Siegen, Germany, judith.doerrenbaecher@uni-siegen.de
Ronda Ringfort-Felner , Ubiquitous Design / Experience & Interaction, University of Siegen, Germany, ronda.ringfort@uni-siegen.de
Marc Hassenzahl , Ubiquitous Design / Experience & Interaction, University of Siegen, Germany, marc.hassenzahl@uni-siegen.de

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581526
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

In the design of social robots, the focus is often on the robot itself rather than on the intricacies of possible application scenarios. In this paper, we examine eight fictional documentaries about social robots, such as SEYNO, a robot that promotes respect between passengers in trains, or PATO, a robot to watch movies with. Overall, robots were conceptualized either (1) to substitute humans in relationships or (2) to mediate relationships (human-human-robot-interaction). While the former is basis of many current approaches to social robotics, the latter is less common, but particularly interesting. For instance, the mediation perspective fundamentally impacts the role a robot takes (e.g., role model, black sheep, ally, opponent, moralizer) and thus its potential function and form. From the substitution perspective, robots are expected to mimic human emotions; from the mediation perspective, robots can be positive precisely because they remain objective and are neither emotional nor empathic.

1 SOCIAL ROBOTS?

For Cynthia Breazeal, an influential pioneer of social robotics, “a sociable robot is able to communicate and interact with us, understand and even relate to us, in a personal way. [...]. In short, a sociable robot is socially intelligent in a human-like way, and interacting with it is like interacting with another person” [ 10 : 1 ]. In this view, social robots are envisioned as fairly sophisticated, almost human copies. Consequently, research on social robotics invested effort, time, and money to equip robots with speech recognition, emotion detection, learning, human-like voice output, facial expressions, or gestures. Thereby, work on social robotics largely focused on imitating humans or animals, and related forms of interaction, such as emotional expression or gestures. Studies in social robotics are primarily concerned with social cognition, that is, how individuals perceive and evaluate social cues when interacting with a robot. Typically, participants rate social robots based on its appearance or behavior according to characteristics such as trustworthy or likeable [ 7 , 49 ].

What is missing from these studies is an idea of the social environment in which the robot is deployed, the practices that evolve, and a reflection on how practices may differ when the social partner is a machine rather than a living being. In a sense, social robotics seems to assume that robots will stand in for any living being they might mimic (e.g., a pet, a nurse, a companion). This tends to underplay the intricacies of interacting with robots in complex social settings [ 5 ].

To address this, we explore three themes: (1) First, we are interested in meaningful applications for social robots: What are the scenarios in which robots can be socially effective? What roles would robots take on in these scenarios? (2) Second, how would robots specifically designed for social scenarios change those scenarios and associated practices in the long run? What are the social and ethical implications? What are possible positive and negative outcomes? (3) Finally, we ask how to design for positive experiences with social robots.

One way to learn more about these themes, and thus the opportunities and challenges of robots in the real world, would be to conduct field studies of robot appropriation [ 38 ]. However, there is a lack of available functional social robots. Existing studies are often confined to robotic vacuum cleaners (e.g., [ 28 ]) and similar devices. While such domestic robots impact social life, their purpose is not predominantly social. Other work, including sex robots [ 35 ], remains largely theoretical or conceptual. In addition, studies with existing robots only allow conclusions about already existing design approaches, such as social robots designed with the idea to mimic living beings.

An alternative approach to explore the three themes beyond what exists is design fiction. While fiction is of course speculative, engaging with it analytically is a helpful start point and a valuable approach to explore and reflect on technology, its impact on people and society, and the details of its design [ 42 ]. For the present paper, we asked students to design concepts of fictional social robots with a focus on the respective social application scenario rather than on the robot's immediate interaction with people or its form. The scenarios, the resulting fictional robots, and the impact of the robots, that is, their imagined benefits and challenges, were documented in form of fictional documentaries . We then used these documentaries as data in a secondary analysis to better understand the similarities and differences between potential scenarios, the respective robots, and emerging experiences. The main contributions of this paper are thus (1) new application scenarios with a special focus on mediation and human-human-robot interaction, (2) speculations about the impact of robots in different application scenarios, and (3) suggestions of how to best design robots for social application scenarios. In the following, we first discuss entanglement theories in Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) as a basis for our understanding of sociality and argue why speculative methods are suitable for this field of research. We then present our approach and the findings of the secondary analysis.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Complexity of Sociotechnical Dynamics and Entanglement Theories in HCI

Robots challenge conventional concepts of technology. “Conventional” technology is most likely experienced as an extension of one's own body and mind, such as a hammer or a calculator. It is largely passive, fully controlled by its users, and serves as a tool to support practical tasks. In the words of Don Ihde, such technology forms an “embodiment relation” with humans [ 37 :72] – for example, a person talks through a telephone. The telephone becomes an extension of the person's voice. As soon as someone starts talking with his or her telephone, Ihde calls this an “alterity relation” [ 37 :97]. Here, people and technology do not form a unity, but are perceived as separate, actively interacting with each other as counterparts. We subsume this type of technologies under the umbrella of “otherware” [ 36 ]. Similar to living beings, robots can show some autonomy, proactivity, and/or anthropomorphic elements, inviting “alterity relations”. Consequently, otherware blurs the boundaries between passive object and active subject. Humans and technology appear entangled in an explicit dialogue, changing each other through interrelated dynamics. In this sense, designers, who create social robots, do not solve practical problems, but design complex, enfolding social relationships. However, instead of focusing on the relationships desired, Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) predominantly understands robots as a practical technology for solving obvious problems of users.

Against the backdrop of new human-technology interdependencies, calls for a paradigm shift in Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) are growing louder. Researchers argue for a more-than-human design or more-than human-centered design [ 16 , 17 , 33 ], for decentering the human [ 15 ], for designing for cohabitation [ 58 ], xenodesignerly ways of knowing [ 55 ], more-than human-centered worlds [ 60 ], post-anthropocentric design [ 18 ] or non-anthropomorphism and the nonhuman in design [ 19 ]. In this context, so-called “Entanglement HCI” is even suggested to be the fourth wave in HCI [ 30 ]. While historically HCI has its roots in engineering and psychology, these new approaches refer to non-anthropocentric theories in philosophy and anthropology or science and technology studies (STS), e.g., actor-network theory, feminist new materialism, object-oriented ontology, or theories of new animism [ 20 , 29 – 31 ]. They argue that the blurring boundaries between humans and technologies and the hard-to-predict dynamics, make it necessary to look at the world as interrelations and dependencies and not as a collection of separate entities. Particularly sociality is considered neither to be situated in the machine nor in the human, but in their relation. There exist no separate “social aspects” of otherwise asocial things. In line with the philosopher Karen Barad [ 6 ], some claim, designers need to move beyond the concept of interaction design and think about intra-action design instead [ 24 , 30 , 59 ], that is to design with the awareness that nothing exists on its own and that only the relations matter. This relational mindset decenters humans and includes all other living and non-living entities as part of an interwoven assemblage, for example, in social practices (e.g., co-performance [ 41 ]), in design research (e.g., co-ethnography [ 34 ]), and in creative processes (e.g., co-design [ 18 ]). Thus, technology, such as a robot, is primarily understood through its relations with other entities, human and non-human, and not in itself, isolated from context.

This paradigm shift impacts ethics, method, and design in HCI. From an ethical perspective, we need to ask ourselves what kind of people we become when we intra-act with (i.e., become in relation to) technology such as robots [ 30 ]. From a methodological perspective, gaining knowledge about a particular artefact means tracing its relations with others. From a design perspective, it is not primarily about designing the function and form of an artefact, but its relationships with other entities. Confronted with fluid assemblages [ 52 ] and dynamics, where causality is hard to determine, designers need to deal with emergence [ 50 :XIV]. Thus, studies are needed, which focus on emerging relations as part of our ongoing re-configuring rather than on the impact of isolated, ostensibly self-contained technologies on users. To reach this goal in the context of more-than-human design, several researchers call for speculative design methods. They ask for “creative, political, controversial Participatory Speculation” [ 30 : 19 ], aim at exceeding the scope of speculative design [ 55 ], and explore “material speculation” [ 61 ] for more-than-human design workshops [ 47 , 51 ]. Fundamental to this focus on speculation is the idea that understanding complexity requires foremost imagination and tools to foster imagination [ 50 ]. Speculation helps to understand the implicit future social, ethical, and political consequences of the interrelations with technology rather than prematurely determining isolated solutions to specific problems.

2.2 Speculative Methods and Storytelling by Design

Speculative design and design fiction [ 4 , 9 , 25 ] have been successfully adapted by HCI and HRI through numerous speculative research approaches. Originally, designers created controversial futuristic artifacts (e.g., furniture, vehicles, medical device) with the specific means of design (e.g., 3D prototyping, web design, graphic design) to make their audience imagine (“make-believe” [ 25 , 62 ]) alternative worlds with plausible products (“diegetic prototypes” [ 40 , 44 ]). Here, the aim is to raise consciousness, debate, motivation, or even fears concerning potential futures. In HCI, speculative design and design fiction often go beyond merely stimulating debate. They became methods of a “research through design” [ 32 ] and participation, for example, by involving people in enacting and evaluating possible futures or even in designing them.

Our present approach of fictional documentaries bears parallels to what Lindley and colleagues call “anticipatory ethnography” [ 44 , 45 ]. Their method is based on already existing artefacts (“incidental design fiction” [ 45 ]), e.g., a science fiction movie, which can be interpreted as design fiction. People are shown these fictions as if they were real to gain actionable insights (i.e., knowledge that can be applied to design) about a technology that plays a role in the fiction. Lindley and Sharma [ 43 ] even already explored “otherware” in their case studies, with an anticipatory ethnography based on the movie HER and its protagonist Samantha, a voice assistant. The method reveals how people emotionally and intellectually respond to possible futures. However, it is only feasible when relevant material is already available, e.g., a science fiction movie about social robots. In addition, the emerging themes are largely tied to the stimulus material. A movie, for example, wants to entertain and, thus, may chose particular ways of presenting technology. Finally, in Lindley et. al's approach, participants – may it be design ethnographers or lay people – remain a rather passive audience to a fiction and do not create or enact it. The approach is – other than our fictional documentaries – neither about creating futures, nor about experiencing social dynamics.

Creating possible futures together with participants was already explored in several other speculative approaches [ 1 , 3 , 46 ]. Especially “futuristic autobiographies” [ 14 ] by Cheon and Su is related to our fictional documentaries , since they aim at eliciting values of participants – especially concerning robots. Here, participants are asked to complete a story of the future, presented by the researchers. They are asked to imagine that they are the main character of these stories. Thus, they involve themselves individually, with their own values – however, other than in fictional documentaries , not in an embodied way but with the help of written texts.

In terms of experiencing futures with one's own body, our approach is more closely related to performative speculative methods, such as “user enactments” [ 48 ], “speculative enactments” [ 26 , 27 ], “experiential scenarios”[13], “experiencing utopia” [ 22 ] or what is called either “multi-stakeholder theater” [ 54 ] or “stakeholder drama” [ 12 ]. These approaches actively engage participants to imagine a possible future, to interact with speculative artifacts, or to enact specific scenarios [ 57 ]. For instance, Odom et al. asked participants to act out different roles (e.g., parent, child) and interact with near-future technologies, such as smart home devices, presented in form of a low-fidelity prototypes. Elsden et al., who introduce speculative enactments as a “novel approach to speculative design research with participants” [ 26 :5386] focus on co-creating experiences with participants. Participants (e.g., professional actors or lay people) experience speculative activities, such as “meta-dating” or “quantified weddings”, through enactment. Thereby the participants reflect on the imagined experience of the activities and the speculative technologies involved. The outcome is primarily social and emotional. In these methods, the role of the design researchers is an active one. They make “offers” (situational, environmental, personal) and propose scenarios to try out. Thereby, “the speculation is negotiated, played out and pushed towards boundaries” [ 26 :5388]. Similarly to our fictional documentaries, Elsden et al. make video recordings of the enactments for a formal qualitative analysis to gain insight into a particular topic.

For all of the mentioned performative methods, mixing speculative and already existing routines of everyday life is the decisive factor. Participants are asked to take roles and to speculate, but always in relation to their own everyday practices and experiences. In addition, the methods use a research through design approach with co-design. Participants are placed into a speculative scenario, and are then urged to shape it and think about possible interactions and outcomes. These embodied approaches are idiosyncratic and insights often exist beyond spoken or written words – what was already identified as the greatest gift of enactment, but at the same time as the greatest challenge for research, since those insights are hard to report [ 64 ]. For our fictional documentaries , we include the idea of involving participants, making them act out roles in a speculative setting – however we focus on spoken words and aim at creating a fictional artefact (a documentary) to allow secondary analysis.

3 FICTIONAL SOCIAL ROBOTS, FICTIONAL DOCUMENTARIES

In a Master's course on robots and speculative methods, we asked 19 master students of Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) at the University of Siegen to develop fictional social robots and examine their social implications. They first analyzed and critiqued existing social robots. For this, they chose a social consumer robot (available, soon to be on the market or research concept, e.g., Aibo, Hobbit, Hitchbot, Kuri) and reviewed it (How does it look like? What is it able to do? What are envisioned scenarios? How do users interact with it? What kind of user feedback already exists?). Afterwards, they critically reflected on the robot and its usage in everyday life. This included positive aspects (e.g., “ Playing music while following the user could be stimulating when cleaning the house ”) as well as negative ones (e.g., “ Functions are arbitrary: Playing music has nothing to do with Kuri´s main idea of being a family member “). All in all, the students concluded that most “social robots” perform predominantly practical entertainment tasks, such as playing music, and in this sense function more as tools with a social interface than as a real social counterpart.

Based on these critical reflections of already available “social robots”, the students were asked to create not yet existing, but potentially meaningful concepts of social robots to increase human well-being in diverse social settings. However, the assignment was not primarily to design the technology, form, or interaction with the robot, but to imagine a meaningful purpose for a social robot, to elaborate on potential everyday scenarios, to determine the people and things affected by the robot, and to critically reflect the impact the robot might have in everyday life.

This served as the starting point for creating a fictional documentary about the robot. The students were asked to use video to introduce the robot and to show its application and consequences in everyday life from a perspective, where this robot is already widely used. For the latter, they created interviews with fictitious stakeholders. That is, they asked fellow students, grandparents, or friends to slip into the roles of stakeholders important to the scenario of the fictious robot, such as the CEO of a robot company, a passenger in a train, or a student living in a shared flat. Interviewees were selected based on how well the fictional role matched their real-life role. Participants were first introduced to the approach and the respective robot concept with the help of a description of the robot and a short scenario. Participants were then asked to imagine living in a world where such a robot was already widely available. Specifically, they should imagine first-hand experiences with the robot, with a focus on their (imagined) feelings, thoughts, and actions, as well as their emerging opinions about the robot itself. Participants’ imagination was prompted by asking interview-like questions, such as: "We heard you had a very exciting, positive experience with the robot last week, would you tell us about it?” This encouraged the participants to imagine and to reflect on various positive and negative experiences. Participants were asked to report their experiences as if they were real, in a roleplay-like manner. All answers were spontaneous and immediate improvisations. These performances were video recorded. In this sense, fictional documentaries are similar to “speculative enactments”, a participatory approach, were designers and participants co-create roles and experiences. Consequently, questions of the interview are part of the creation process itself and less neutral than in qualitative interviews. Through enfolding conversations, the students and the participants defined and expanded their roles, thereby co-constructing potential feelings and thoughts, as well as wider benefits and challenges of the robots themselves. To further stimulate participants` imagination, to encourage role-playing, and to create compelling video footage for the fictional documentaries , the students chose a suitable setting to film the interviews, e.g., next to a bus stop.

Finally, the students curated and edited their own and further found footage into short documentaries (6 minutes on average), some even with voice-over artists, who introduced the scenario from a present-day perspective, e.g., “The robot was launched two years ago, and a lot has changed on city streets since then. Today we want to find out how it was received by customers ...”. This strategy established a connection between today's world and a believable near future. Since students captured diverse viewpoints and let various fictitious stakeholders explain their specific everyday experiences with the robot, the videos did not resemble promotional movies, but documentaries, which showed controversies, skepticism, and enthusiasm alike.

All in all, eight teams created documentaries, each about a different fictional social robot and how it impacts a particular social setting (see table 1 for an overview and figure 1 for some video stills) – without ever showing the robot itself. A detailed description of each documentary, the robots, the social settings, and the fictional everyday experiences of the stakeholders are in the appendix.

4 SECONDARY ANALYSIS OF THE FICTIONAL DOCUMENTARIES

4.1 Method

To gain a more systematic overview of the application scenarios, the challenges and benefits of the robots, and the emerging relationships between people and robots, we used the eight documentaries as data for a secondary qualitative analysis. We focused on the imagined application scenarios of the robots and on the imaginary positive and negative experiences reported in the videos. From the positive experiences, we wanted to gain a deeper understanding of what might be meaningful to people and from the negative what to avoid in social robot design. In addition, we focused on the imaginary relationship, how it shaped the situation, the perception of the robot, and the person herself- or himself in an intra-active process. Please note that real people imagined those experiences in a future scenario, putting themselves into a possible (persona of the) future. The implications of social robots derived from this are thus based on the thoughts, feelings, and reflections of people today. In other words, both the real person and the imagined persona influenced the results.

We applied thematic analysis [ 8 ] to the videos. The first author watched the videos several times with a focus on the envisioned application scenarios and the experience reports of the people presented in the videos. Emerging themes were noted to further organize and cluster insights across the different documentaries (i.e., robots). These initial themes were then revised and completed by the second author. Those extensions and changes were incorporated by the first author into a consolidated analysis.

Our analysis revealed four categories of application scenarios and 19 experiences. The experiences were further distinguished into positive and negative. An experience was considered to be positive, if the fictitious interviewees described it with positively connotated terms such as “support”, “comfort” or a double negative, such as “less lonely”. An experience was negative, if it was described by negatively connotated terms, such as “aggressive”, “isolation” or a negation, such as “not helpful”. Negative and positive can relate towards the robot, towards oneself, or towards other people.

4.2 Application Scenarios

All robots addressed everyday practices of private persons. Five out of eight robots featured a dyadic interaction, with the robot as a substitute for a human social partner (i.e., human-robot interaction). Two of the five robots focused more on practical tasks in a rather tool-like fashion (HMO – organize appointments, ALFRED – tidy up) or to take on especially unpleasant social tasks (HMO – making doctor's appointments). The other three robots addressed the social: They watch movies with their “roommates” (PATO), teach cultural knowledge (POLY), or teach debating skills (TALKLIKE). These robots serve as a human substitute (for a roommate or a teacher).

The remaining three out of the eight robots went beyond the dyadic and mediated couples or groups of people (SEATY, SEYNO, PARFAR) (i.e., human-human-robot interaction). They operated in settings where humans need support with avoiding or resolving conflict, for example, by ensuring respectful behavior. Two of the mediating robots (SEATY, SEYNO) were intended for the public, that is, public transport. The third robot (PARFAR) mediated intimate relationships, mainly in private domestic settings. 4.3 Experiences

All in all, we found 19 positive and negative experiences. It quickly became apparent that there is a substantial difference in experiences depending on whether (1) the robot interacted in a dyadic way with a human, thereby substituting a human interaction partner, or whether (2) the robot mediated social processes within couples or groups. Consequently, we organized our findings into two overarching categories, namely a “substitution” perspective (human-robot interaction, HRI) and a “mediation” perspective (human-human-robot interaction, HHRI).

We further condensed the 19 experiences into seven themes, one neutral, two positive, and four negative. In general, people thought about positive and negative forms of cooperation with the robot or with other people against the robot, in the sense of coalition building. The theme relatedness encompasses the positive consequences of being social with a robot, from less loneliness or social shyness due to the robot's companionship to the robot as a facilitator in building relationships between people in a group, for example, as an “icebreaker”. The theme inspiration is about the robot as a source of knowledge and ideas in a dyadic situation and a potential model for appropriate social behavior in mediation situations. The theme protest/reactance encompasses a general skepticism about off-loading social processes to robots, especially the consequences of feeling forced to accept the robot as an authority, for example, when it is tasked to mediate conflict among humans. The theme social disintegration addresses the negative consequences of being social with a robot, from self-isolation due to the robot's perfection to social de-skilling due to relying on robots to manage social group processes. The theme of anti-social behavior addresses abusive behavior towards the robot (i.e., robot bullying) as well as the idea that people intentionally misbehave just to prompt a mediating robot to act. The theme embarrassment/shame reflects upon what others think about individuals, who are social with a robot rather than with other humans, and with the dangers of being exposed by the robot in front of other people in a mediation situation.

We cross-tabulated themes with the two perspectives, dyadic substitution, and group mediation, because similar themes played out quite differently depending on the perspective. For example, in dyadic substitution cooperation is about relationships of a human with a robot (i.e., a human-robot team) while in group mediation there are at least two potential teams, human-robot or human-human. Another example is inspiration : In dyadic substitution, the robot is inspiring for a human because of the knowledge it provides while in group mediation it is inspiring group dynamics, for example, by being a role model. Feeling exposed by of the robot – a robot could reveal intimate information or personal weaknesses in front of guests at home (loose contacts, colleagues) or in public spaces. Humans might feel ashamed because the robot makes no distinction between private and public (e.g., PARFAR).

4.4 Reflection

4.4.1 Application Scenarios and Roles. If we compare the application scenarios and roles of already existing social robots to those of the fictional documentaries , similarities and differences become apparent. Unsurprisingly, when using a dyadic substitution perspective, social robots most often mimic or replace other humans, e.g., a person watches movies with PATO instead of watching alone or with a friend; a person discusses politics with TALKLIKE instead of with real celebrities; a person learns about her or his cultural heritage through POLY instead of a friend, a teacher, or a family member. In this case, the emerging practices and the resulting relationships with the robot are mainly inspired by already existing practices and relationships with other humans. “Being social” is understood in a similar way independent of whether talking about humans or robots. This aligns with Breazeal's definition of social robotics and existing application scenarios, such as care work performed by a robotic nurse. From our perspective, these application scenarios – while seemingly helpful and obvious – are questionable. Either the technology will not be human-like enough to truly function as a substitute (e.g., a robotic nurse will lack emotional warmth), or it will be, raising difficult ethical questions (e.g., is artificial emotional warmth acceptable at all). That being said, the mere knowledge that a robot is only pretending to be human can destroy the illusion, however, perfect it may be.

The mediation perspective, this is human-human-robot interaction, is a less common approach to social robotics. For example, none of the robots criticized by the students in the first part of the course actually addressed the mediation of couples or small groups. A recent review [ 56 ] shows that while research on human-human-robot interaction exists, it remains rather rare. All in all, the fictional documentaries featured three different robots, which addressed mediation in public and in private contexts (table 2 ). These robots also mimic existing practices, such as settling disputes. However, the very same robot can have different roles depending on the social dynamics of the group (e.g., a mediator, a therapist, a role model, a black sheep, an ally, an opponent, or a moralizer). The roles seem less predetermined by design, but rather emerge from the various interacting people in context. For example, SEYNO, the robot designed to ensure politeness on long-distance trains, was seen as a helpful ally by shy people, while more straightforward people imagined they would feel provoked and viewed it as an unwanted moralizer for wayward and coward fellow passengers.

Overall, the application scenarios of HMO (organizational tasks) and ALFRED (tiding-up) are most similar to the existing application scenarios of robots. They focus on practical tasks, similarly to robotic vacuum cleaners. Instead of a counterpart, the robots remain tools to more efficiently deal with tasks. This can involve social elements, for example, participants projected human attributes onto the robot by saying that the robot might feel humiliated, but it hardly involves truly social interaction. In addition, in this category, the presumably adaptive robot and the human are often expected to converge over time. The robot cleans up or makes phone calls on behalf of its “user”. It becomes a personal doppelganger, and participants imagined to feel like being in a conversation with themselves (i.e., soliloquizing).

4.4.2 Challenges and Benefits. In the case of dyadic substitution, the documentaries addressed a wide range of anxieties: Anxiety of being replaced by a robot, of being deceived by a robot pretending to be human, of being confused about the uniqueness of one's own human identity, of transferring (impolite) behavior and interaction patterns from the robot to humans and thereby losing social skills, and of becoming too dependent on the perfect robotic social partner and thereby losing patience with real humans. All of these challenges depend on the idea that robots are similar to humans and, thus, compete with them. In all fictional documentaries interacting with a robot as if it were another human or a doppelganger appeared problematic, especially in the long run. Further, the participants anticipated shame and embarrassment as well as feelings of being judged for relying on a substitute.

In case of group mediation, the participants imagined they would lose their conflict solving skills as the robot would take this task from the humans (e.g., SEYNO). Some of the participants even questioned the rather super-human qualities of the robot, for example, when PARFAR is able to remain neutral and objective in a heated argument. They imagined to feel cornered. The robots are perceived as superior because they are able to approach the respective social scenario in a dispassionate and unbiased manner, providing facts and observations rather than taking sides.

All in all, the documentaries revealed a number of challenges, with a rather skeptical stance towards social robots. In dyadic substitution, various negative themes revolved around the human likeness of a robot and its social and personal consequences; in mediation people felt more negative about losing social skills and the lack of emotions with which a robot can act in supposedly emotionally heated situations.

The documentaries also mentioned a number of benefits of social robots, such as providing relatedness, inspiration, or a great potential for solving conflicts. At the heart of these benefits are imagined characteristics and skills, humans value but find challenging or impossible to realize in everyday social interaction. In dyadic substitution, the participants highlighted aspects, such as “always being present”, “always listening”, “never forgetting anything”, “learning individual interests perfectly”, “memorizing everything”, “having unlimited access to knowledge”, “having no self-interest”, or “always being motivated”. For example, ALFRED never grows tired of tidying up the flat, and PATO never interrupts when watching a movie together. It is never bored, knows all the trivia of a movie, is eager to adapt its movie preferences to the user, and all this 24 hours a week. No healthy human being could fulfill this the way the robot does. Moreover, participants mentioned attributes, such as “being able to change identities” or “representing people that are not present or that are even already dead” (TALKLIKE). Inspiration is a consequence of a robots extensive knowledge or its ability to slip into different identities (PATO, TALKLIKE, POLY). Thus, even the robots which primarily substitute other humans do not intend to offer an exact replication. For instance, when TALKLIKE transforms itself into famous people, it offers an opportunity, which most people will never be able to experience in real life: personal time with a celebrity. In this sense, TALKLIKE extends its users’ possibilities rather than merely substituting the absence of another person. These robots co-perform common social practices, but in an idealized, superhuman way.

In group mediation, robots solve conflicts among humans, bring people in contact with each other, and generally facilitate considerate social behavior among people in groups (SEYNO, SEATY, PARFAR, and even HMO, that is mostly experienced in one-to-one interaction, but at the same time also connects people – as a representative via phone calls). Participants mentioned positive robotic abilities, such as “being objective/neutral”, “having an outside perspective”, “concentrating on facts instead of feelings”, “being calm and self-composed”, “being patient”, “being persistent”, “having no sense of shame”, “being straightforward”, or “not judging”. For example, fictional passengers on trains reported feeling less personally attacked by SEYNO than by other people when they were criticized. People imagined that the robot would address inappropriate behavior directly and bluntly. It seemed crucial for the robot not to be overly understanding or overly polite, but to be rational and persistent in resolving conflicts between people. “Being social” in these cases obviously means something different to humans than it does to robots. In addition, the documentaries mentioned the robot's “naivety”, “simple-mindedness”, probably even its potential “behavioral imperfection” as a benefit to bring people in contact with each other. For example, they could laugh with each other about the “silly” robot. The same schadenfreude would, of course, be completely inappropriate towards another human, such as a train conductor. A robot without self-consciousness and feelings to be hurt makes this particular social situation possible.

All in all, the benefits are about robots, which are effective in social situations not because they are one-to-one substitutes of living beings, but because typical machine attributes (e.g., unemotional, not judging, not self-conscious) distinguish them from humans, usually in an idealized, “superhuman” way. The challenges are a mirror image of this. People are mostly concerned about the consequences of robots that are too similar to humans.

4.5 Thoughts on the Method

The performative approach of fictional documentaries combined with a secondary qualitative analysis was successful in gathering multiple and conflicting viewpoints about possible experiences with social robots. While the robot and the scenarios were the result of a creative process among student teams, the responses of the participants as fictitious users remained open. Participants were confronted with the concept and scenarios and encouraged to explore their own (imagined) feelings, thoughts, and, finally, fictitious experiences (i.e., speculative enactment). Due to this improvisation and since neither the robot nor the attitudes of the interviewees were pre-defined in detail, there was ample room for developing individual, quite reflective stories. The fact that the participants never got to see a picture of the possible robot – reminiscent of approaches, such as invisible design [ 11 ] – gave them the freedom to use their own imagination.

Imaginary worlds became more concrete and defined through interviewing. For example, participants spontaneously imagined functions or even the appearance of the robot, e.g.: “ When the robot comes towards me like this – as you know, it staggers so strangely when it does this, ... ”. Thus, the final documentary can be seen as the result of a critical co-creation through imagination and spontaneous reflection triggered by the initial concept. In general, participants became emotionally involved while performing. Several times the setting, e.g., in front of a bus stop, made them think of real experiences, they had in these places, e.g., being disturbed by a passenger playing loud music. This way, the imaginary accounts were linked to lived experiences and did not remain mere intellectual reflections. This blurred the boundaries between the actual (i.e., their present everyday life, their own identity) and the possible (i.e., a future world, possible identity). In addition, the approach encouraged a focus on change over time. In their imaginary accounts, participants often explained how their attitudes towards the robots changed from the first contact to getting used to them.

The imaginary experience reveal which ideas resonated with the participants and which not. For example, the student's original idea for HMO to find objects in apartments was apparently less interesting to the interviewees than the robot's more social task of having vicarious conversations with other people – most participants just ignored the pragmatic function and commented on the social ability only. Participants talked about relationships and experiences, thereby shaping their own fictional characters and the specific robot at the same time. This revealed their own values, as well as feelings and thoughts about the particular robots and the envisioned scenario. Although their reported experiences were not actually lived, but imagined, they are still valuable, since they depend on actual cultural background, values, and real world experiences – both, of the students, who had a story in mind, and the interviewees, who made it into their own stories. Thus, students thought about valuable scenarios in relation to their everyday life, shaped by their cultural background (e.g., they came up with POLY, since they themselves have an immigrant background, or they envisioned PARFAR and heterogeneous couples, since they regard heterosexuality to be a cultural norm). Obviously, through fictional documentaries we do not learn how people in the future might feel about social robotics, but rather how people feel now about the possibilities of social robotics. However, through this, potential challenges and benefits of an emerging but not yet widely existing technology can be gathered and be used to shape future forms of this technology through, for example, designerly mindsets or broader principles. In this sense, the emerging themes, fears, challenges, and benefits are imagined, yet real, and should be taken seriously.

Overall, participants imagined more negative than positive experiences. This can have many reasons. First of all, people were more skeptical about dyadic substitution than group mediation. However, more robots addressed dyadic substitution (5 out of 8), which may have skewed the number of positive and negative experiences towards the negative. In addition, students explicitly asked the interviewees to be critical about the concepts, which may have led to an over-pronunciation of negative issues. Finally, we may need to face the fact that the idea of social robots is not necessarily unanimously positive – at least in the view of the predominantly European interviewees.

All in all, a strength of the present approach compared to, for example, mere interviews is the relational view implied by role-play and a focus on everyday experiences. This helps to understand robots as part of a broader, evolving socio-technical ecosystem. Unlike most HRI methods, it does not put a focus on the robot itself as a technical product, but on the social dynamics and possible controversies that arise from using a robot in the world.

Of course, the approach has limitations. It depends on the imagination, the interest and talent for roleplay, the prior knowledge of the participants, as well as the ideas already embedded in the robots. For example, ALFRED and HMO had been less inspiring to the participants, and hence less productive for analysis. Since most participants did not have experiences with social robots at all, some of them just reproduced already existing well-known critiques of social robotics instead of actually putting themselves into the suggested scenario. This might also be due to using the word “robot”, which certainly prompts well-known science fiction clichés. In this respect, it might be better to not speak of robots at all, but to use other, more open terms, such as, “objects with intent” [ 53 ]. The dependency of the method on the conceptual input and the imagination of the participants themselves reminds us of the fact that speculative methods focus on finding problems rather than solving them. The method hypothesizes rather than confirms challenges and opportunities for social robots.

5 CONCLUSION: SUGGESTIONS FOR SOCIAL ROBOT DESIGN

Through a secondary analysis of fictional documentaries , we explored the application of robots in complex social scenarios and described emerging themes, challenges, and benefits. We found that in some of the experiences the relationship with the robots remains “embodied” (i.e., extension of the self) while others describe stronger “alterity relations” (i.e., counterpart). For example, creating a doppelganger of yourself and delegating it to make phone calls as a representative (HMO) implies an “embodied relation”. The robot becomes a replacement of oneself. The same is true for robots which tidy-up or search objects, such as ALFRED. These robots might remain extensions of one's self, even if one delegates tasks to them. Thus, not every technology that looks and behaves like a human at first glance implies enduring alterity relationships with humans. For example, a person teleoperating the domestic robot Astro [ 2 ] can use it to check whether the oven at home is turned off. Here Astro serves as a tool even though it has fake eyes. Taking this insight into account, we believe designers should clarify whether or not their technology should be “otherware”, that is, should invite and profit from an alterity relation. If not, it will be just an anthropomorphized tool. In this case, anthropomorphizing is not only superfluous but also potentially at odds with what is expected from a tool. Or to put it differently: Who wants to argue with a hammer about the best way to drive a nail? Designers benefit from to first define the desired relation with technology and only then to design the technology accordingly [ 21 ]. Thus, designers of robots first of all need to ask themselves what kind of relationship with humans they want to imply and whether this relationship is appropriate for the intended scenario.

In our documentaries, robot-human relationships which mimic human-human relationships received many doubts, were imagined as challenging up to causing anxieties. While roboticists assume that mimicking human abilities and behaviors ensures sociality, we would rather argue that strong anthropomorphism prevents satisfying social experiences with robots in the long run. This complements arguments currently provided by sociological perspectives on robotics. Kaerlein, for example, argues that there is a misunderstanding in robotics about how sociality works: The engineering-driven perspective reduces sociality to “a game of conversational turn-taking, adequately described in an exhaustive transcription of utterances and bodily positionings” [ 39 : 56 ], thereby downplaying the complexity and temporality of human-human interactions, with their constantly actualized re-interpretations in a series of exchanges. Social entities do not exist as such but are defined through their relationships. Sociality happens between entities – no matter if human or non-human: “The design of social robots could thus start with the acknowledgment that robots are not inherently social, but only temporarily become so within an intricate web of situated interactions. [...] In other words, don't design robot teachers or nurses, but robotic mediators with assistive functions for existing social settings” [ 39 : 57 ]. Following this insight, designers need to turn away from the robot itself and instead explore social scenarios with robots – what we did in the present paper. Interestingly, even in our documentaries most of the envisioned human-robot relationships bear a resemblance to pre-existing human-human relationships – in five out of eight concepts, robots interact in dyadic relationships and predominantly take on the roles of a human teacher, a human friend, or a human servant. Probably because of the idea that only humans, or at least living beings, can be social, we quickly get caught up in what we already seem to know. However, only robots that finally become part of beneficial social experiences and relationships are “truly” social robots. Thus, robotic mediators, facilitators, or moderators for groups of humans could point the way to a future of social robotics. We will miss design opportunities, if we conceive of sociality as something located in humans that must be transferred to technology. Thus, designers need to continuously remind themselves to not simply copy social entities as such, but to design innovative, meaningful and fulfilling social relationships to and even among robots. One way to achieve this is not to perfectly mimic living beings, but to extend and redefine relationships based on the unique possibilities “machine beings” offer. In the fictional documentaries , for example, relationships with robots are imagined as positive just because the robot (in contrast to a human) is objective and neither emotional nor empathic. With these insights, we contribute to the notion of “robotic superpowers” or “psychological superpowers” [ 23 , 63 ]. In this view, humans have social powers, such as being compassionate, due to their humanness; while robots have social powers, such as being patient, due to their machineness. Understanding both as potentially complementary could be key to shaping “real” social relationships with robots.

APPENDICES

The eight fictional documentaries

PATO – Watching Movies with Humans. The robot PATO is introduced by a TV spokesperson of a program called Contra : It basically lives on the couch in a shared flat and watches movies for and with people. It actively invites people to join it. After a while, PATO knows about people's preferences and makes suggestions about what to watch together. It knows about actors and other movie trivia. Contra enquires into the long-term effects of living with PATO and features first-hand experiences in interviews with users. All interviewees are less than 30 years old and share a flat. Robert Meyer, a tech enthusiast, usually spends his free time in his private room playing video games. Since PATO is around, he uses the communal living room more often and, thus, feels more sociable. However, he admits, his flatmates might even be annoyed of him and the robot blocking the living room all the time. Peter Peterson (Figure 1 ) enjoys the knowledge of PATO and spends more time watching (better) movies. Other than his human flatmates, PATO never interrupts movies through unnecessary comments. It replies only when asked. Peter worries that his guests might believe him to be a bit strange. Therefore, he hides the robot before they arrive. For Carla Dehrens the robot had an unexpected effect. The robot got on her nerves by asking her several times a day, if she would like to watch a movie with it. Almost trying to lure her in. Annoyed, she avoids PATO and the living room and hardly ever watches movies anymore. Now, she spends more time on other activities with friends outside the apartment.

ALFRED – Tidying up Shared Flats. ALFRED is a household bot organizing homes. Three students who share a flat talk about their experiences with the robot one after the other. Sam, 26, says that ALFRED makes them all argue less with each other: “It's like a fourth roommate, who takes all the work from you.” He also talks about funny or embarrassing situations, when ALFRED mixed up the underwear of people. Nick, 23, hates it when his guests intentionally throw things on the floor to get ALFRED to clean up. He feels pity: “ALFRED has a name and somehow also a personality.” Laura, 25, mentions ALFRED to be a bit over-eager and feels disrupted in her routines, when ALFRED puts away books, she still wants to work with. Beyond that, Laura speculates that it would be very weird to live again without ALFRED, since she and her flatmates became very lazy and dependent on “him”.

SEATY – Keeping Bus Seats Free. The robot SEATY is introduced by a bus driver: “ SEATY keeps a seat free and as soon as a person with reduced mobility comes in, SEATY stands up and asks a new person to get up to let SEATY sit. If this person explains not to feel so well today, SEATY believes that. In principle, SEATY always believes everybody. ” When nobody gets up voluntarily, SEATY makes people feel guilty by reciting wise sayings. Several passengers talk about their experiences. A young man with a broken hand appreciated SEATY, as he would have been too proud to ask for a seat by himself. A skeptical man in his fifties is horrified by the idea that people need robots to be kind to each other. An elderly woman above 80 years of age is positively surprised by the role model effect of the robot: “ In the meantime, it's even working quite well without robots. People are all attentive now! ” An adolescent admits that she and her friends always pretend to be sick when SEATY approaches them: “ It's just a robot. ” The elderly woman reports with amusement that SEATY was recently offering her daughter a seat, even though she is not even 60 years old yet. Luckily it was just a robot – otherwise the daughter probably would have been a bit miffed about this mistake, she speculates.

SEYNO – Ensuring Respect between Passengers. SEYNO is a robot for long-distance trains. Its purpose is to prevent people from being impolite and thoughtless towards each other. The narrator points out that currently already 70% of all long-distance trains are accompanied by SEYNO. A woman passenger, who describes herself as conflict averse and shy, is very grateful to SEYNO. For example, the robot pointed out to a seat neighbor that his music was too loud. The guy would not have responded as politely to her as he did to the neutral and unemotional robot (Figure 2). Three other passengers report that they find it cowardly when people send a robot ahead and don't dare to address problems themselves: “SEYNO only fights a symptom. Actually, we should start at the root and make sure that people behave more friendly in general.” They don't like the idea of unloading this burden to an “authoritarian” robot. People themselves should negotiate the rules with each other. Another woman experienced that passengers take less responsibility for their own behavior and for the behavior of others once the robot was on board: “There was this noisy guy just ignoring SEYNO. Nothing happened. The other passengers probably thought SEYNO should clarify the situation. This is the robot's job, not mine.” However, she also noticed children loving SEYNO: “It's like having a dog on board, interacting with everybody. (…) It is something to talk about. (…) Some even behave unpolite just to make SEYNO come closer.”

HMO – Performing Organizational Tasks. Ishan, a boy in elementary school, explains that HMO is able to detect household objects and can help to find lost things, such as pieces of Lego. More importantly, the robot is able to make phone calls for its users. A male in his twenties explains that HMO makes appointments at the dentist for him, which reminds him of when he was a child and his parents took care of the organization. He could be just care-free. Yet, he wants the robot to always make it clear to others that it is the robot and not himself calling. In general, he is ambivalent about the experience of interacting with the robot: “It somehow feels like talking to yourself, because you are just delegating tasks that you would normally do yourself.” Another male interviewee is ambivalent as well. He explains how HMO builds up a “wall” between the user and the outside world. On the one hand this is practical because unpleasant conversations are taken care of by HMO. On the other hand, however, he has the impression that the permanent use of HMO leads to social isolation.

TALKLIKE – Debating with Humans. TALKLIKE is a robot that is able to teach the debating skills of famous people by pretending and simulating these people. TV station Fox Business Network shows a woman in a political debate with a robot. After this, several users of TALKLIKE share their experiences. A man, who chose to talk to the moderator John Oliver through TALKLIKE realizes, that he actually adopted Oliver's way of talking after a while. A woman, who decided to talk to the already deceased singer Amy Winehouse, did not enjoy the experience: It was like talking to the dead. Another woman, who chose to interact with the actress Emma Watson to practice for her own presentation, noticed she learned a lot. However, she reported that she felt brainwashed after some weeks and pointed to the importance of not mixing up identities. Another woman reported that she felt her interaction partner Sheldon Cooper became more like a companion than a trainer. And a woman, who decided to talk to Amal Clooney, since she was interested in human rights and feminism, had inspiring political discussions every morning. However, she mentions, that the experience made her afraid the rhetorical skills of TALKLIKE could be used for propaganda purposes.

POLY– Teaching Cultural Knowledge. POLY is a robot for people with a migration background who want to learn about cultures and languages. Three people talk about their experiences with POLY. One man with a Croatian father reports that he never felt connected to this part of his cultural background because his parents divorced when he was a child. The robot helps him to not alienated when he visits his family in Croatia. He likes the robot better than a language learning program because it is more playful. Two of the interviewees – a woman, who came from Italy some years ago and a man with an unknown origin – are more ambivalent. They both explain that they initially did not like the idea of the robot. It felt like a substitute. They do not want “real” interaction to be replaced by POLY. However, when began to use POLY, they realized that the robot was different from a human. The woman says she perceives it more like an encyclopedia. Still, both would prefer to teach their children their cultural background themselves. The robot should support parenting and not replace it.

PARFAR – Solving Conflicts between Lovers. The CEO of Posch explains that their robot PARFAR supports couples and families in managing their arguments and disputes. PARFAR follows its users physically to learn their social behavior, their style of managing arguments, and to even simulate disputes for training purposes. This way people can look at their own habits from an outside perspective and reflect on their “culture of debate.” Two couples talk about their experiences with PARFAR (Figure 3). Both explain that the robot changed their relationship. The first couple was disappointed and frustrated with the robot. It made their disputes even more intense. The male partner was annoyed by the constant comments of the robot, always correcting him, for example, about how often the dishwasher was loaded by whom: “She [the woman partner] already knows everything better and then you also have that robot that cruises right behind you all the time – humming annoyingly – and adding its two cents to everything.” The woman felt vindicated and supported by the robot, because her partner, in her opinion, always exaggerates what he does. Nevertheless, she also felt that the robot was disruptive in their intimate disputes – a presumptuous faultfinder. They finally reveal that they broke up – despite or even because of the robot. The second couple explains how the robot started to imitate their discussions and even voices and how confusing this had been for them. However, it had a positive effect as well: “After a while, we improved our culture of debate to the point where we finally said the rights things that only the robot has said before.” They find the robot to be a bit embarrassing in public spaces, because it often repeats discussions loudly, which attracts attention of other people. Both partners confirm each other many times in the interview and apparently saw themselves more as a team vis-à-vis the robot or vis-à-vis the general public – probably due to the robot.
