Cognition-oriented Facilitation and Guidelines for Collaborative Problem-solving Online and Face-to-face
An in-depth examination of format and facilitation influence on problem-solving performance

CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

During the Covid-19 pandemic, more guidelines were created to teach people how to facilitate meetings online, but few were designed from a cognition-oriented perspective. Additionally, solving complex problems is essential in many occupations. However, the influence of online and face-to-face discussion formats on the performance in complex problem-solving tasks is unclear, even though remote working has become common over the past several few years. Hence, this study aims to answer two research questions: (a) Does problem-solving performance differ between online and face-to-face meetings? and (b) Does facilitation improve problem-solving performance when different formats are used? We conducted experiments with 40 groups using a 2 × 2 factorial design, which were controlled for both facilitation and format. Each group comprised two randomly selected participants, and each problem-solving discussion lasted between 1.5–2 h. The obtained evidence showed that format can influence the performance of balancing intercorrelated factors in a complex scenario, but it does not affect the performance of achieving a predefined goal. Instead, it we found that facilitation is helpful for achieving a predefined goal. Based on the results obtained, we propose future design directions for problem-solving centric computer-supported cooperative work systems from a cognition-oriented perspective.

1 INTRODUCTION

Collaborative problem-solving is an important aspect in businesses [ 1 ]. Prior to the Covid-19 pandemic, online problem-solving collaboration was a part of the daily routine of organizations with a distributed workforce. However, during the pandemic, the mode of collaboration changed significantly. Instead of face-to-face meetings, many problem-solving activities have been moved online. This hybrid working mode is expected to persist even after the pandemic, as online collaborative problem-solving has become common in numerous white-collar industries [ 2 , 3 ]. A report published by Dialpad, which surveyed 2,800 U.S. employees from businesses of all sizes and across industries, revealed that around 33% of the respondents, spend at least four hours each week in virtual meetings [ 4 ].

In another survey conducted by SessionLab in 2020, it was found that 81% out of 140 surveyed facilitators were offering at least half of their sessions online [ 5 ]. Hence, in recent years, various guidelines regarding the facilitation of remote meetings have been proposed. Research has shown that meetings are more likely to succeed when appropriate structures and interventions are offered to those involved [ 6 ]. In particular, previous studies that investigated meetings on various platforms aimed at remediating issues in online communication compared to face-to-face settings. For example, to overcome the limitations of online platforms in supporting the nonverbal context, Allison et al. [ 6 ] suggested that facilitators should organize a face-to-face kick-off session before moving the discussion completely virtual. Additionally, to overcome technical fatigue, Vreede et al. [ 7 ] proposed the concept of ThinkLet that integrates the use of digital platforms into various discussion stages. Similarly, many existing facilitation guidelines represent a macro-level, process-centric checklist, suggesting how meetings should be prepared, executed, and wrapped up [ 6 , 8 , 9 , 10 ].

Facilitation has been defined as a process in which a person diagnoses and intervenes to help a group identify and solve problems, make decisions, and increase effectiveness [ 10 ]. While the currently available guidelines are sequential-based action items, few of them involve aspects mentioned in the definition such as: “how to diagnose,” “when to intervene,” or “what to suggest.” These are regarded as micro-level, content-centric factors that are related to ongoing interactions, with limited coverage in the existing guidelines. In fact, the same report from Dialpad revealed that the top three causes of an unpleasant meeting are: not being helpful (32.7%), lack of progress and conclusions (24.5%), and failing to stick to the agenda (28.59%) [ 4 ]. Such causes are more likely to occur in problem-solving meetings, where higher level cognitive processes are used. Thus, there is an urgent need for these issues to be addressed by facilitation not only in the meeting set-up, but also through the thought process.

This study is part of a project aimed at developing platform-versatile facilitation guidelines for problem solving, and it aims to answer two research questions: (a) Does problem-solving performance differ between online and face-to-face meetings? and (b) Does facilitation improve problem-solving performance when different formats are used?

In this study, we propose complex problem-solving guidelines for real-world team meetings, as well as evaluation methods to assess problem-solving performance from various perspectives. A simulated complex problem scenario that generated values based on the inputs provided by problem solvers was used. For the experiments, 80 participants were recruited and divided into 40 groups to solve the problem. Quantitative comparisons were performed between online vs. face-to-face and facilitated vs. non-facilitated discussions. Furthermore, questionnaire were conducted with the participants to collect qualitative feedback regarding the facilitation provided.

The three major contributions of this study are as follows:

  • A quantifiable difference was discovered in the problem-solving performance between online and face-to-face discussions.
  • Existing facilitation guidelines were improved and the effectiveness of facilitation in problem-solving discussions was verified.
  • Future research directions for computer-supported cooperative work (CSCW) system designs that support complex problem-solving collaboration from a cognition-oriented perspective were proposed.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Comparison between remote and face-to-face collaborative tasks

The human-computer interaction (HCI) and CSCW communities have been studying remote synchronous interactions for decades. Recently, remote collaboration has become increasingly common, and office workers are required to use it frequently. There is a growing body of literature comparing the natures of remote and face-to-face real-time collaborative tasks in various applications, such as learning [ 11 ], telehealth [ 12 ], design collaboration [ 13 ], and business meetings [ 14 , 15 ], and qualitative differences have been discovered between them. The benefits of using video conferencing include the use of technological assistance, such as polling and chat, which enhances participant involvement [ 14 ]. Moreover, collaborative tools, such as Google Docs, which are equivalent to whiteboards in face-to-face meetings, are often used in online communication with improved trackability and recordability [ 13 , 16 ].

Despite the convenience of online technologies, multiple studies have shown that participants involved in face-to-face meetings were more satisfied than those involved in online ones [ 11 , 15 , 17 ]. Some possible reasons are that communication quality can be significantly affected by technical limitations, such as pool network connectivity [ 11 , 12 , 18 ] and the absence of non-verbal contexts [ 11 , 15 , 17 , 18 ]. Such limitations result in differences in conversation patterns and participants’ behaviors, including less turn-taking, more time for turns, fewer motions, increased fatigue, and difficulty in reaching a common ground [ 15 , 17 , 18 ]. Similarly, our work also looks into how satisfaction online is determined by the differences in patterns and behaviors of people during a collaborative discussion. Moreover, we extend our research beyond these behavioral differences to measuring performance on a problem-solving task. In previous studies investigating problem solving, such as science quiz tasks and abstract topic discussions, the researchers claimed that no performance differences were observed between discussions in the two formats [ 17 , 19 ]. However, it is still questionable whether these limitations affect performance in scenarios wherein more complex problems must be solved and building an accurate representation of the problem space is crucial for doing well. Thus, our first research question asks whether problem-solving performance differ between online and face-to-face meetings. While remote problem-solving collaboration is being increasingly adopted in all types of industries, understanding its potential impact is essential for system design and facilitation.

2.2 Facilitation in CSCW

One of the reasons why problem-solving collaboration on digital platforms is challenging is because problem solvers need to manage multiple aspects, such as social interactions, cognitive processes, and digital applications [ 20 ]. To overcome these challenges, past studies adopted two general facilitation approaches: proposing guidelines that define more appropriate roles and actions for facilitators and creating new systems that can resolve the aforementioned issues through automated assistance.

The former approach, regarding the role of facilitators in CSCW systems, was introduced in the early 1990s. As a general trend, most studies investigating the facilitators’ role have tended to improve collaboration quality by enhancing social interaction. For example, Viller [ 21 ] developed intervention guidelines that aimed to tackle generic meeting problem syndromes in team dynamics. Similarly, to further facilitate virtual teamwork, the guidelines proposed by Allison et al. [ 6 ] laid down six general steps, which covers meeting preparation, execution, and conclusion. Moreover, instead of suggesting a list of action items, Vreede et al. created a catalog of traits of facilitators that lead to higher participant's satisfaction [ 22 ]. Based on the findings of previous studies, we can see that as a result of the increased adoption of CSCW systems in various collaborative scenarios, from information sharing to problem solving, the skills required by facilitators have to expand accordingly. Specifically, facilitators are expected to facilitate not only the meeting process, such as timekeeping and agenda maintenance [ 21 ], but also the contents, such as pace control, idea stimulation, and providing value-added comments [ 23 , 24 , 25 ].

Meanwhile, studies adopting the second approach, which aims to resolve issues through automated assistance, mainly focus on the digital application aspect. The merit of automated assistance is the integrability with the online format without creating extra burden on social interaction. Some studies have obtained positive results for system-facilitated turn-taking [ 26 ], brainstorming [ 27 ], shared problem representation [ 28 , 29 ], and problem-solving thought processes [ 30 ]. However, despite these achievements, research has also revealed the lack of usability of such systems, even after training [ 22 ]. In fact, the types of collaboration that each CSCW system can support might be very limited because their design is only effective for certain stages of the thinking process, and few people switch between tools frequently based on their cognitive requirements [ 15 , 31 ].

Finally, regarding the cognitive aspect, compared to face-to-face discussions with whiteboards, paper, and post-it notes, research has shown that online platforms are more cognitively demanding when executing certain tasks [ 13 , 18 ]. Hence, some researchers proposed to choose the format based on the cognitive process at various stages throughout the collaboration [ 7 , 11 , 13 ]. Although there have been many useful theories of cognition in CSCW research, few have been applied systematically for guidelines’ construction, especially in the problem-solving-related fields.

Notably, the suggestions proposed by the extant studies focusing on the three aforementioned different aspects are, sometimes, not compatible with each other. For example, it is unclear how the facilitator's role changes when the settings in the aforementioned aspects change, and whether facilitation, from a cognition-oriented perspective, can eliminate the technical limitations. Thus, this leads to our second research question: does facilitation improve problem-solving performance when different formats are used?

2.3 Complex scenario simulation for problem-solving research

Since we are interested in solving real-world problems, which are mostly complex [ 32 ], collecting data with equivalent fidelity is essential to this study. With similar motivation, problem scenario simulators have been extensively used in problem-solving-related studies. The design of such simulators is critical for investigating problem-solving cognitive processes because the complexity and difficulty of a problem scenario may directly influence the quality of data collection.

The fidelity of simulators has been changing with the shift in research focus. Although studies regarding problem solving have employed simulators for complex scenarios since the 1970s, insufficient justifications have been provided regarding why certain complexities were selected. Early studies, especially those conducted from the 1970s to the 1980s, used higher complexity scenarios to test participants’ abilities to identify these system characteristics, such as the inventory problem [ 33 ] and moon landing [ 34 ]. In the 1980s, researchers claimed that complex problem-solving (CPS) scenarios were unnecessarily complex. During this period, three major areas of interest emerged: investigating the knowledge used [ 35 , 36 , 37 ], analyzing problem-solving dynamics [ 38 , 39 , 40 ], and understanding the role of learning in CPS [ 9 , 35 , 41 ]. Complex factors were selectively applied to test certain psychometrics. With the increase in computing power available, more studies started designing high-fidelity scenarios that replicated the problems of real-world scenarios and correspondingly examined specific problem-solving strategies [ 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 ].

To define the fidelity level of a simulated scenario, Hontvedt et al. [ 8 ] proposed a framework for simulator training design that included three aspects of fidelity analysis: technical, psychological, and interactional. They suggested linking the fidelity level to the problem-solving expertise targeted in training. Similarly, by proposing a taxonomy for fundamental scenario complexity analysis, Chen et al. [ 47 ] further designed the “café operation simulator,” according to the purpose of the problem-solving study. This knowledge-intense scenario simulates coffee booth operations, wherein problem solvers develop operational strategies for each month over a simulated year with the goal of maximizing the monthly revenue [ 47 ].

Similarly, we suggest that the fidelity of a simulator shall be properly justified in light of the research background. A report by Fellow shows that the weekly project meeting represents the most common meeting type [ 48 ], with the common agenda of information update, status check, and problem solving. Considering this background and the potential applications of the findings of this study, we sought a problem scenario with high fidelity that replicates the meeting mode, requires real-world knowledge, and has substantial problem space for groups to explore. Therefore, we adopted the “café operation simulator” designed by Chen et al. [ 47 ].

3 FACILITATION GUIDELINES PROPOSED IN THIS STUDY

We proposed using cue-based facilitation guidelines, which are effective metacognitive prompts for self-regulated learning [ 49 ]. In complex problem-solving activities, problem solvers must build accurate problem representations to obtain high-quality results [ 32 , 50 ]. In this study, it was expected that cue-based facilitation guidelines could prompt participants to build a more comprehensive problem space. The guidelines, presented in Table 1 , were designed based on the triple-space framework, which categorizes interactions from three space aspects: cognitive, social, and digital [ 20 ].

According to the normative problem-solving framework [ 51 ], problem solvers can use an iterative cognitive process to optimize their problem-solving performance. Such processes include building problem representations based on available information, developing solutions according to the problem representations, evaluating goal achievement, and actively analyzing new information after each execution [ 51 ]. Accordingly, the cognitive processes in the facilitation guidelines were categorized into information, problem representation, solution, and goal evaluation.

In sociology, social interaction is defined as the process of reciprocal influence exercised by an individual. Past research indicates that mental representations play a normative role in solving coordination problems [ 52 ]; hence, we facilitated information sharing and awareness of perception to enhance the quality of the mental models of both teams and individuals.

Finally, facilitation guidelines also included the platforms used. In this study, three platforms were used in the digital space: “simulator UI,” “digital whiteboard,” and “Google Sheets,” which, in a general sense, correspond to the “main information source,” “documentation tool,” and “analytic tool,” respectively. The facilitation statements in the guidelines did not necessarily include the use of digital platforms. They focused on scaffolding situation analysis instead of encouraging participants to provide undivided attention to documenting and receiving information from the digital space.

4 METHODS

The experiment was conducted between November 2021 and May 2022 with 80 participants. Participant demographics are described in Section 4.1. To obtain a standardized performance for problem-solving decision-making, the scenario simulator described in Section 4.2 was adopted. Further, as described in Section 4.3, problem-solving performance was evaluated from various aspects using three indicators: balance, overall performance, and satisfaction. Forty experiments with a 2 × 2 factorial design were conducted, wherein both the facilitation and the format were controlled. The experimental settings are described in Section 4.4. Finally, after the discussions, the questionnaire presented in Section 4.5 was provided to all participants to evaluate the discussion quality.

4.1 Participant demographics

A representative sample of young professionals who used video-conferencing software at work was collected. The sample included 80 participants who were recruited using random sampling through online advertisements (UBC participant recruitment site, UserInterview.com, and Facebook Ads) and posters (in Vancouver, Canada). Their ages ranged between 19 and 46 years (M = 26.99, SD = 6.17), and 58% identified as females. The participants belonged to 26 nationalities and 21 professions, and 63% had received training in shop and restaurant operations for one year or more. Regarding their educational levels, 54%, 29%, and 8% received their bachelor's, master's, and doctoral degrees, respectively, and the remaining 8% received other degrees.

4.2 Problem-scenario simulator

The café operation simulator used in this study was first proposed by Chen et al. [ 47 ]. The simulated system contains 27 intervention variables, 25 non-modifiable variables, 19 hidden variables, and 122 interlinks. Figure 1 shows the simulator UI prior to decision inputs. The left side contains the decision input area, wherein participants must decide the value for each individual element and run the simulation to implement their strategy. The decisions include human resources, promotions, operations, and menus. The right side contains the monthly performance report, which includes accumulated assets, monthly revenue and costs, balance, moral and mechanical faults, waiting times, stock levels, and units sold. No visual or verbal assistance was provided for identifying causal relationships between decision inputs and performance. Participants were required to search through the problem space to identify and predict the behavior of the system.

The complexity of a scenario is imparted through its opaqueness, nonlinear interlinks, and knowledge intensity. Six goal conflicts existed between the intervention variables in the system design. For example, although raising investment in human resources increases motivation of workers, the labor costs increase. Moreover, since the system includes time dynamism in seasonal demand, the number of goods sold still vary even if participants do not change the inputs in a new month.

The simulator can only receive inputs from a single computer. During the discussion, it was controlled by an investigator who received inputs from the participants. The participants could track their progress by referring to the time in the upper right-hand corner of the UI.

4.3 Performance indicators

Three indicators were adopted to evaluate the problem-solving performance from different perspectives: balance, overall performance, and satisfaction.

Balance, which indicates the extent to which a pre-agreed-upon goal is achieved, is the primary goal of this simulation. The balance is indicated in the upper right-hand corner of the UI. It is a naïve value that the participants aimed to maximize. By checking the balance, participants could quickly obtain information regarding their monthly profit and loss without considering the individual values in the performance report.

The overall performance is measured by the extent to which all performance parameters are optimized during problem solving. The overall performance is differentiated from balance by identifying whether participants consider the aspects more than the naïve indicator. For example, using low-quality coffee beans can reduce operational costs and increase balance, but it can also affect the number of sales. To achieve a higher performance score, participants must consistently monitor the changes in multiple performance parameters and adjust their inputs accordingly. The overall performance score was calculated using equation ( 1 ), which is the sum of the sigmoid values of the converted performance parameters. The converted performance parameters were obtained by dividing the original parameters with a power of ten to obtain a number with an absolute value smaller than one. This calculation converts all performance parameters into numbers between zero and one, which makes it easier to compare parameters of various scales. Satisfaction was considered as the final indicator in this study because it has been widely used as an indicator of discussion quality in previous studies [ 16 , 17 , 22 , 53 , 54 , 55 ]. It is a subjective evaluation provided by the participants after the discussion and is usually used when an effective objective performance evaluation indicator is unavailable. In this study, satisfaction was regarded as the average rating of the answers to the following three questions using a 5-point Likert scale: (1) Overall, I was satisfied with the discussion interaction; (2) Overall, I was satisfied with the outcomes; (3) I would be happy to have another meeting with my partner.

4.4 Experimental design and data collection

The experiment was designed to answer the two research questions: (a) Does problem-solving performance differ between online and face-to-face meetings? and (b) Does facilitation improve problem-solving performance when different formats are used? We used a 2 × 2 factorial design, which included four treatments; the two factors were format and facilitation. The two format types were online and face-to-face, and those of facilitation were non-facilitated and facilitated discussions. Eighty participants were randomly assigned to any one of the treatment groups. Eventually, 20 participants comprised 10 dyads for each treatment. Moreover, 40 discussions were conducted, each with a duration ranging from 1.5 to 2 h.

Because only the investigator could input values into the simulator, the simulator interface was shared with the participants during the discussion using the Zoom software. Participants discussed and analyzed the performance reported by the simulator with their partners and informed the investigator regarding their decisions. Subsequently, the investigator input the values decided by the participants. In addition to the shared simulator UI, the participants were asked to use a shared Google Sheet and online whiteboard, LucidSpark. As the problem scenario included several numerical values, the participants were asked to use a Google Sheet to record and share their calculations. Moreover, owing to the complexity of the scenario, they were advised to use an online whiteboard to visualize and share their problem representations. Additionally, since the scenario is knowledge-intensive, a four-minute scenario introduction video was shown to the participants prior to the discussion. Both the video and its transcript could be referenced during the discussion. To standardize the amount of information available in each dyad, participants were asked not to search online for additional information during the discussion.

Each participant was provided with a laptop and extended display. The laptop was placed in front of the participant, while the extended display was placed either to the left or right of the laptop at an angle of 120°. As shown in Figure 2 , the simulator UI was shared on the laptop screen, whereas the remaining information, including the Google Sheet, LucidSpark whiteboard, and video, were presented in different browser tabs on the side monitor, and participants could switch between tabs to access the required information. The activities on both displays were recorded using screen recording, and a GoPro camera was placed in front of each participant, below their eye level, to record their body language. Further, pin-microphones were used to collect high-quality audio during the discussion. We standardized the apparatus as much as possible to minimize the variance in the data collected caused by the experimental settings.

As shown in Figure 3 , in the face-to-face setting, two participants were asked to sit facing each other approximately 2 m apart on two sides of a table. Meanwhile, in the online setting, the two participants were placed in different rooms with the same set of equipment. In a non-facilitated setting, both participated in the discussion alone. The investigator connected with them through Zoom from a different room to share the simulator UI, but kept their camera and sound off. In the facilitated online session, the investigator, who also acted as the facilitator, turned both the camera and sound on for the discussion. In the face-to-face session, the investigator sat in the same room as the two participants.

The problem-solving effectiveness was evaluated using Bloom's Taxonomy, which classifies thinking into six cognitive levels of complexity: knowledge, comprehension (1.1,1.2), application (1.3,1.4), analysis (1.5), synthesis (1.6), and evaluation (1.7, 1.8) [ 56 ]. In this study, the knowledge was expected to be standardized through the video introduction before discussion; hence, no questions regarding knowledge were asked.

Meeting satisfaction was evaluated from three perspectives: discussion interaction (2.1), outcomes (2.2) and social dynamics with the discussion partner (2.3). The three questions were extracted from a previous study that examined satisfaction with operational meetings [ 53 , 54 ].

Facilitation was evaluated based on skills (3.1, 3.8) [ 57 ], cognitive processes (3.2 – 3.5) [ 51 ], and interaction (3.6, 3.7). Moreover, two short-answer questions (4.1, 4.2) were asked regarding the facilitation and its helpfulness.

4.6 Facilitation evaluation

Besides the quantitative data obtained in Section 3 of the post-discussion questionnaire, we focused on two types of qualitative data in this research: text-based short-answer feedbacks from participants regarding facilitation helpfulness and the facilitator's utterances. Through the two types of data, we obtained both subjective and objective evidence regarding the facilitation helpfulness.

4.6.1 Text-based short-answer for subjective feedback analysis. The text-based answers to the two questions (4.1, 4.2), regarding the most and least helpful facilitation received, were analyzed using a deductive coding method. Table 4 shows the coding scheme for text-based feedback analysis. Same as the facilitation statements introduced in Section 3, the coding scheme was designed based on the triple-space framework. With this design, the helpfulness of each facilitation criterion could be directly identified from the feedback analysis. All feedbacks were coded by two researchers with Cohen's Kappa inter-rater agreement calculated for each category. One feedback could be assigned to multiple categories as a participant might comment on several aspects simultaneously.

4.6.2 Facilitator's utterances for process analysis. After each session, the recording of the facilitator's utterances was transcribed. To evaluate the intervention in the problem-solving discussions, such as facilitation frequency and timing, the facilitator inspected the transcribed file and labeled the utterances with corresponding statements, as shown in Table 1 . Since the facilitation occurred as part of the conversation, the facilitator might occasionally rephrase the statements to convey the message better. To ensure that the message was consistent with the designed guideline, 20% of the labeled utterances were picked randomly and coded by another researcher who did not participate in the data collection process. Then, Cohen's Kappa inter-rater agreement was calculated to evaluate the messages’ consistency.

5 RESULTS

5.1 Effects of format and facilitation on discussion performance

The effects of format and facilitation were compared using boxplots as shown in Figure 4 . The facilitated discussions had a higher variance, which is evident by the balance and overall performance scores. In online discussions, facilitated discussions tended to have higher scores for both balance and overall performance. However, for the same indicators in face-to-face discussions, facilitation slightly reduced the performance. Satisfaction did not show a significant difference for online discussions across all facilitation conditions, whereas facilitated face-to-face discussions showed more variances than the non-facilitated ones.

The performance difference was further evaluated using a t-test. Table 5 presents the p-values of the t-test results of the online and face-to-face discussions based on whether facilitation was provided. According to the results, there was no statistical evidence to show that balance and satisfaction were different for online and face-to-face discussion formats, regardless of whether facilitation was provided. However, the overall performance score showed a significant difference (μ online < μ face-to-face ) between the two formats when no facilitation was provided.

The feedback's nature, either positive or negative, was determined by whether it answered the question regarding the most or least helpful facilitation aspect. The proportion test results indicated the significant positive nature of facilitation statements regarding “assumption identification” in the “problem representation” cognitive process (positive= 9, negative= 1, p-value = 0.027), and “rationale clarification” in the “solution” cognitive process (positive= 13, negative= 1, p-value = 0.003). The participants reported that the facilitation statement regarding proving their assumptions helped them develop their problem representation logically: (“I found it helpful when we were asked to reflect our assumptions,” “…asking us to explain our assumptions…which made us understand each other's way of thinking better,”, “…by reviewing if our assumptions were fulfilled, we could find the flaws in our logic successfully”). Additionally, for “rationale clarification,” the participants revealed that facilitation helped them avoid making decisions purely based on intuition: (“I liked how we had to have a rationale to base each decision on. That is something I would not have thought of without help from the facilitator,” “…being reminded to reconsider some actions made me realize my thought process may have been incorrect previously…,” “The questions asked by the facilitator made me reflect more deeply on my decision-making”). Although most of the feedbacks showed that the facilitation statements regarding assumption establishment and clarification of the rationale thought process were helpful, some respondents criticized that they made the problem-solving process unnecessarily complicated: (“the questions could be misleading, especially making us doubt our decisions, even though the questions might have been asked for explanation only”).

Facilitation regarding “information collection” was a controversial category, with 47% of the feedbacks being negative. According to the feedbacks obtained, the facilitator was expected to be a channel that provides quick and accurate access to the information available: (“It was helpful when the facilitator explained various elements in the scenario,” “The facilitator was always there to answer our questions regarding the exercise”). However, some expected the facilitator to extend their effort beyond simply pointing out the source of information by conducting a deeper analysis and explaining how the information is inter-related (“It wasn't helpful when the facilitator didn't provide context on why certain situation happened,” “There wasn't enough information regarding how to set up operation”). Moreover, the facilitator was also expected to provide effective information tracking methods that correspond to the problem solvers’ needs, given that some of the feedbacks were contradicting (“The charts were helpful,” “The facilitator should have explained the charts,” “Historical data were not provided”). In short, before guiding problem solvers through the normative cognitive process, the facilitator was expected to offer techniques that help the participants in accessing, tidying, and analyzing data at the information collection stage.

The positive feedbacks on “social interaction” focused on managing the discussion flow. The participants reported that it was helpful when the facilitator prompted turn-taking (“It helped when the facilitator asked the other participant to express their thoughts,” “Asking us questions especially when we got stuck and couldn't push the discussion forward”). Meanwhile, the participants expected more help for time management (“I wished there was more coaching for time management,”, “The time constraint sometimes made our decisions rushed”).

6 DISCUSSION

6.1 How does online problem-solving performance differ from that conducted face-to-face?

Based on the results shown by the three indicators defined in this study (i.e., balance, overall performance score, and satisfaction), we discovered that the problem-solving performance differed in terms of assessing various problem elements when the discussion was conducted in the two formats (i.e., online vs. face-to-face).

Consistent with previous research, when no facilitation was provided, no significant difference was observed in balance, which represents the ability to achieve a naïve predefined goal in complex problem-solving tasks [ 16 , 17 , 59 ]. As suggested by past research, conducting such discussions online requires more cognitive effort [ 17 , 18 ]; however, the participants in this study could adjust their communication according to the format used and eventually obtained good outcomes.

Nevertheless, another indicator used in this study, the overall performance score, showed a significant difference between the formats used (Table 5 and Section 5.2). This indicates that when the discussion was conducted online, the participants tended to focus on a single goal and paid less attention to balancing other factors. For example, a group that conducted the discussion online decided to achieve a higher balance by lowering the quality of their coffee beans and raising the price. This improved the balance owing to a higher profit margin, but since the drink quality was lower, the number of units sold reduced significantly. Relevant results were obtained in past CHI-related creative problem-solving research that face-to-face interaction is more effective for convergent thinking, such as adopting the correct approach or selecting appropriate solutions [ 13 ]. In the discussions with groups that achieved low overall performance scores, it was discovered that little attention was paid to considering multiple aspects in the problem-solving process. Although the online conference tools could compensate for audibility in synchronous collaboration, the resulting differences in behaviors and conversational patterns still resulted in less-than-ideal decision-making. In contrast, in facilitated discussions, the participants were asked to list the ideal operational conditions of the café. Through this process, they could determine the crucial elements and balance them. The results did not show a significant difference in the overall performance between the two formats with facilitation. Hence, when there is no scaffolding available, spontaneously listing the goals on a shared online document may help participants evaluate their performance from various perspectives in the discussion. Despite the results obtained thus far, there is still insufficient evidence on whether such a discrepancy was caused by the difference in social interaction [ 17 ] or cognitive effort [ 18 ]. Therefore, further research is required to address this gap in performance.

Moreover, in contrast to previous research [ 11 , 17 ], the results of this study did not show a significant difference in satisfaction between the two formats. This might indicate that, unlike in activities such as difficult problem solving [ 17 ] and learning [ 11 ], satisfaction in complex problem-solving discussions is less likely to be affected by the format used. As suggested by the results presented in Table 7 , satisfaction in complex problem solving is primarily driven by the problem-solving process itself, rather than social interaction, which greatly depends on the format used. Another possible explanation for the contradiction could be the change in the habits of participants. The study was conducted at a time when most people were more accustomed to online interactions. Thus, it is possible that the study's participants were more comfortable with virtual communication than those in previous studies, which might have affected the results.

Finally, based on the nature of problem-solving performance in the two formats, we suggest that organizations should carefully consider the purpose of a meeting before selecting the format. On the one hand, online meetings can be effective for quickly reaching a straightforward solution without compromising its quality, on the other hand, face-to-face meetings can be more appropriate where various factors need to be evaluated. Thus, organizations may use the online format for problem-solving activities, such as finding workarounds or temporary fixes, while they can employ the face-to-face format for meetings where multiple factors must be balanced simultaneously, such as solution proposal drafting.

6.2 How does facilitation affect problem-solving performance when different formats are used?

Overall, the cognition-oriented facilitation guidelines proposed in Section 3 have been proved to be an effective method for improving the performance of problem-solving discussions, while the degree of helpfulness can also be influenced by the format because of the different levels of context complexity.

According to the regression analysis presented in Section 5.2, the proposed guidelines have a positive effect on achieving the predefined goal (referred to as balance in this study). This could be attributed to the goal-driven facilitation style, which guided participants’ awareness to quickly evaluate their performance against the predefined target and accordingly adjusted their strategy. However, these facilitation guidelines were shown to be less effective in balancing the overall performance.

Moreover, the effectiveness of the guidelines might have been influenced by the context complexity. Although the results showed that the guidelines could improve performance regardless of the format used, according to the results presented in Table 6 , such facilitation was more effective in the online format. As shown in Figure 4 , although the variance in the performance indicator values increased when facilitation was provided, most of the values were higher than those of online discussions without facilitation. However, for face-to-face discussions, most of the values were lower for discussions performed with facilitation. This might be caused by the complexity of the interactions in face-to-face discussions. Miranda et al. [ 55 ] discovered that content facilitation has a negative impact on the meeting process, wherein participants must allocate some mental effort toward communication with the facilitator and understanding instructions. Therefore, as social interaction is more intense in face-to-face settings [ 17 ], facilitation might be regarded as an interruption instead of scaffolding.

As an increasing number of organizations undergo digital transformation, it is common for meetings to have cognitive, social, and digital interactions. We recommend a similar but more elaborated concept of ThinkLets, the pattern language for facilitated collaboration processes proposed by Vreede et al. [ 7 ], in which organizations use standard analysis and documentation systems to build consistent interaction protocols. A team may integrate their own tools, configuration, and script with the suggested problem-solving cognitive-process in this research. For example, in this study, when the facilitator asked a group to document their assumptions for the first time (F8), the participants might hesitate for a couple of seconds before opening the documentation tool. Then, the two problem-solvers discussed briefly on how the information shall be recorded and proceeded with the documentation. When the facilitator asked the group whether their assumption was proven (F7), the group would spontaneously refer to and tidy their notes. Throughout this process, the problem representation was updated and enhanced as well. Therefore, when the team undergoes the problem-solving process by executing standardized protocols, the cognitive-process could be automatically triggered even by action-oriented statements, resulting in less cognitive effort required for processing facilitation statements.

6.3 Exploring cognition-oriented facilitation in CSCW system designs

Based on the evaluation in Section 5.3, we discuss the effectiveness of the current guidelines in Section 3 and propose several design considerations when incorporating cognition-oriented facilitation guidelines into CSCW system designs in this section.

6.3.1 Format matters. According to the results in Section 5.3.1, in online discussions, the groups needed more facilitation on turn-taking, more prompts on recording their discussions, and reminders about using personal experience at an earlier stage of the discussion. Correspondingly, the resulted design can be different where CSCW systems targeting online collaboration need to incorporate more capabilities for supporting social contexts. For example, the system may use speaker recognition and silence detection algorithm to generate the conversation pattern of an online group, which can be further used as a reference for the facilitator to determine when to intervene and whom to prompt. Such function might be less necessary for a face-to-face discussion, where turn-taking happens more frequently and spontaneously.

6.3.2 Cognitive level defines functions. Ideally, the system shall provide different supports when the cognitive level involved in the problem-solving discussion varies. Such recommendation is made based on the results regarding “information collection” statements in Section 5.3.2. To elaborate, if the problem solvers only need facilitation at the information collection stage, the corresponding system shall provide functions for quick information access and structuring. Yet, if the latter stages such as “problem representation” and “solution” are involved, smooth “information collection” will become a prerequisite. Accordingly, the system shall support confirmation on available information, problem analysis, and consensus building. Thus, the functional requirements can be dissimilar. For instance, the former ones can be supported by visualization or data retrieval, while the latter ones need more interactive functions, such as voting and ranking. As suggested by Vreede et al. [ 7 ], a team might use different systems and configurations to support various stages in the problem-solving cognitive process. Apart from creating new functions to support higher level cognitive processes, future CSCW systems may also adopt consistent UI or data integrations to assist a smoother transition at different stages of the problem-solving process.

6.3.3 Dedicate flow and concise message. Since the facilitation occurred in form of conversation, the human facilitator shall be more interactive than automated systems. Nevertheless, as shown by the results in Section 5.3.1, the facilitator's utterances only comprised 8% of the entire discussion time, and each intervention was completed within 10 seconds. Moreover, as indicated by the results in Section 5.3.2, the assumption establishment and rationale clarification was an effective and satisfactory approach. This iterative thought process was mainly facilitated with three concise and intuitive statements: F6, F7, and F10. Therefore, effective facilitation might not necessary be elaborative. That is because once the problem solvers became accustomed to the flow, they were able to adjust the cognitive process when prompted, and little explanation of the facilitation statements was needed. This implies a design with a dedicate normative flow and concise prompts to manage the problem solvers’ expectations regarding the interaction, which could be achieved by a system tutorial before formally starting the discussion.

6.4 Limitations and future directions

As with any investigation, we recognize limitations to this study.

First, for the satisfaction measurement in the post-discussion questionnaire, another reason for the inconsistency with the results of previous research might be that the questions used for satisfaction evaluation in all these studies were slightly different. Although most of the post-discussion questionnaires covered interaction, discussion outcomes, discussion process, and opinions toward teammates, each study also included some unique questions, such as individual contributions [ 17 ], learning outcomes [ 11 ], and outcome feasibility [ 53 , 54 ]. Meanwhile, in the current study, not all the additional questions in previous studies were applicable to the simulated complex problem scenario adopted. Hence, we chose to use only the common questions that all studies covered. Although it is unclear whether fewer factors in the post-discussion questionnaire resulted in the discrepancy, we suggest that the results can be more robust and comparable if the evaluated factors are standardized because satisfaction is a subjective yet commonly used indicator in both CHI and cognitive psychology.

Second, the first author was the one who carried out most the experiments of the project. Although the core facilitation she provided was regulated with the guidelines in Table 1 , her background and personality still likely influenced how she intervene the discussion. Future studies may consider employing multiple facilitators to reduce the biases caused by single scaffolding style. Third, the facilitation was analyzed from only two perspectives: facilitation style and participant's feedbacks. Due to resource limitation, the data of thirty-hour facilitated conversation was not analyzed. As a supplement to the overall facilitation evaluation, future research may consider including sequential analysis to assess the immediate prompt effect after each intervention.

7 CONCLUSION

Problem-solving collaboration is an important aspect of business activities and there are various guidelines on conducting problem-solving discussions. During the Covid-19 pandemic, more guidelines were created to teach people how to facilitate meetings online, but few were designed from a cognition-oriented perspective. To bridge this gap, this study investigated complex problem-solving facilitation in both face-to-face and online discussion formats. It aimed to answer two research questions: (a) Does problem-solving performance differ between online and face-to-face meetings? and (b) Does facilitation improve problem solving performance when different formats are used? Therefore, we developed cognition-oriented guidelines that consider cognitive processes, social interactions, digital platforms, intervention conditions, and facilitation statements. We further validated the guidelines using a café operation simulator, which was carefully designed to simulate a knowledge-intense and dynamic problem scenario. Performance was evaluated from three aspects: the degree of achieving a predefined goal, the extent of balancing intercorrelated factors, and participant satisfaction. The results showed that the format used for conducting discussions does not have a significant impact on the performance of achieving a predefined goal, but balances all intercorrelated factors. The facilitation guidelines proposed in this study were designed using a goal-centric thinking pattern, and the results showed that these guidelines could improve the performance of achieving a predefined goal. No significant difference was observed in satisfaction owing to the controlled condition; however, the cause of satisfaction changed after facilitation was employed. The facilitation appeared to be more effective for online discussions compared to face-to-face discussions, and we believe that the guidelines proposed in this study can be considered as fundamental references from a cognitive perspective for future problem-solving facilitation-based CSCW system designs.
