Single-sided deafness (SSD) significantly restricts social participation in hearing/speaking cultures due to the person's difficulty hearing conversations on their deaf side. Although hearing aids for SSD are effective in social situations, the acceptance rate remains low at 4%. To address this problem, we designed and developed a bone conduction-based device to be worn with eyeglasses, involving 53 individuals with SSD including two authors. We conducted a four-week diary study comparing our proposed device with traditional Contralateral Routing of Signals (CROS) hearing aids and explored the factors that might affect the acceptance rate of assistive devices for SSD. The findings indicated that our design was more acceptable for users with SSD due to its effectiveness, social acceptability, and the ability for wearers to use other devices simultaneously, such as earbuds. Based on our results, we discuss implications for designing wearable assistive devices to promote greater acceptance among the target population.

1 INTRODUCTION

According to the World Health Organization, it is estimated that approximately 6% of the global population has disabling hearing loss [ 44 ]. In particular, 1% of the population in the US has single-sided deafness (SSD) [ 18 ]. People with SSD have three major challenges in their daily lives: (i) difficulty hearing sounds from the deaf side, (ii) difficulty hearing in noisy environments, and (iii) inability to tell where a sound is coming from [ 25 ]. Because of the functional disabilities, individuals with SSD report withdrawing from busy social situations, experiencing participation restrictions that significantly impact their daily life, and feeling the burden of relying on other people to communicate effectively [ 35 ]. As a result, they experience negative emotions such as denial, anger, depression, and isolation [ 7 ].

Contralateral Routing of Signals (CROS) hearing aids, Bone Anchored Hearing Aids (BAHA), and Cochlear implants have been shown to improve the quality of life and speech perception of people with SSD compared to unaided controls [ 10 , 23 , 58 ]. Despite these benefits, only 4% of people with SSD use hearing aids [ 18 ], which is significantly less compared to the prevalence rate (13.5%) of hearing aids among people who are hard of hearing in general [ 60 ].

Several reasons for the abandonment of hearing aids by people who are hard of hearing in general are known, such as poor benefit, wearability issues, appearance issues, and social acceptance issues [ 59 ]. One explanation for people with SSD refusing to wear or abandoning assistive devices is that using such devices to support their invisible disabilities may in turn render them visible to the public. As reported in recent research by Chang and Tucker [ 6 ], people with invisible disabilities, such as SSD, often experience self-stigma; feeling of fear that revealing invisible disabilities may cause negative outcomes such as prejudice and stereotyping.

However, very little is known about why people with SSD, in particular, use hearing aids less compared to people who are hard of hearing in general. Moreover, previous literature on assistive devices for SSD has mainly focused on the functional effectiveness of the devices [ 10 , 23 ], and less on the factors related to wearability, appearance, and social acceptance.

Therefore, our research aims to design and develop an assistive device with better effectiveness than existing hearing aids for SSD and a higher level of wearability, appearance, and social acceptance. Moreover, if we could reveal the difference in user experience over time between those who accept the device and those who do not, it may bridge the gap in previous literature on the user experience of hearing aids. Thus, we apply the framework and models proposed in the HCI community through longitudinal studies on technology acceptance of consumer electronics such as the iPhone and activity trackers [ 29 , 42 , 57 ] to assistive devices for SSD.

In this research, we first conducted a background study on 51 people with SSD to identify challenges with SSD and existing hearing aids. Based on the insights from this study, our team, with two members with SSD, underwent three iterations of prototypes of an assistive device, called asEars (shown in  Figure 1 ), designed to be more acceptable by people with SSD. To evaluate the proposed device and explore the factors affecting the acceptance and user experience, we conducted a comparative study with ten individuals with SSD where they were asked to use the proposed device and traditional hearing aids for SSD (CROS hearing aids) for two weeks each. During this period, the participants completed a daily questionnaire to report their evaluation of the device and related user experiences, and took part in interviews after two weeks of using each device. Based on the results, we answer research questions about what factors and how different phases of user experience over time affect acceptance of the devices, and provide insights into designing hearing aids and wearable assistive devices that will be more widely accepted by the target population.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Hearing Aids for Single-Sided Deafness

Regular hearing aids for partial hearing loss are usually not applied for SSD because people with SSD can hear well on one side and want to utilize the normal hearing ear instead of amplifying sound for the deaf side [ 23 ]. Alternatively, five types of hearing aids are applicable for supporting SSD [ 39 ]. Cochlear implants are devices that electrically stimulate the cochlear nerve of the deaf ear using a surgically implanted electrode array [ 62 ]. Bone Anchored Hearing Aids (BAHA) are surgically implanted into the skull on the deaf side, and the sound collected by a microphone is transmitted to the inner ear of the better ear through the skull [ 19 ]. However, these methods cost more than $ 8000, and adverse skin reactions around the skin penetration site may occur, posing a significant barrier for acceptance [ 34 , 38 ].

Bone conduction hearing aids that do not require implantation and are fixed to the head by a headband are another solution. However, because these devices need to apply a high static pressure on the head to present sound, it can be very uncomfortable for the user [ 39 ]. Another bone conduction hearing aid that can be adhesively fixed to the head has been recently introduced to make the device more comfortable [ 39 ]. Although the hearing aids that require implantation are more beneficial in terms of hearing and more comfortable  [ 58 ], bone conduction hearing aids fixed by headbands or adhesives are less expensive and easier to try for people with SSD [ 39 ].

Another solution for an assistive device for SSD is Contralateral Routing of Signals (CROS) hearing aids. CROS hearing aids collect sound by a microphone worn on the deaf side, and the sound is presented to the better ear by an inserted ear tip [ 36 ]. Prior work shows that both CROS hearing aids and BAHA improve scores of hearing-in-noise tests when the speech is presented to the deaf side [ 10 , 23 ]. Moreover, questionnaire results have shown that SSD participants feel it is easier to communicate and listen in noisy environments with these hearing aids [ 23 ].

In this work, we propose an assistive device for SSD that utilizes bone conduction and is worn with eyeglasses. Since the device is attached to the form of eyeglasses, we expect it to have higher social acceptance compared to existing hearing aids for SSD. Moreover, to make the device comfortable yet effective, the bone conduction speaker is adjusted to be in contact with the auricle (behind the ear). This is based on recent work detailing that the auricle can transmit sound efficiently without excessive pressure on the skin compared to bone conduction to the head [ 43 ].

2.2 Acceptance of Hearing Aids

Although CROS, BAHA, and cochlear implants have been shown to improve the quality of life and speech perception of people with SSD compared to unaided controls [ 10 , 23 , 58 ], the acceptance rate for hearing aids by people with SSD remains as low as 4% in the US [ 18 ].

Extensive research has been conducted to elevate hearing aid acceptance in general. To capture in-situ hearing experiences without recall bias, Ecological Momentary Analysis (EMA) requires participants to enter an ongoing listening experience periodically [ 21 , 56 ]. These studies confirmed that EMA has the potential to explain hearing aid acceptance better than in-laboratory listening tests. Although these works have found that real listening environments of individuals affect acceptance of hearing aids, they have focused on user experience related to hearing alone.

However, several reasons besides effectiveness can affect acceptance or abandonment of hearing aids, such as wearability, appearance, and social acceptance issues, according to surveys collected from hearing aid users on a clinical visit [ 59 ]. In previous work, comfort was one of the major reasons for returning hearing aids (i.e., abandonment) [ 31 ], whereas customizing hearing aids to be aesthetically appealing solves appearance issues and promotes social acceptance [ 37 , 47 ]. Furthermore, previous research has also focused on the relationship between users and hearing care providers rather than the hearing aid itself [ 27 ]. In one study that focused on cochlear implant users with SSD, the survey data revealed that the visibility of assistive technology is associated with self-stigma and anxiety about social interactions [ 6 ]. Self-stigma is the feeling of fear that revealing disabilities may cause negative outcomes such as prejudice and stereotyping due to the difference between the general society [ 1 ]. As a result, many people may refuse to wear assistive devices to support their invisible disabilities as it may show these disabilities to the public. One way to address this issue is to provide users with direct control of self-disclosure through physical design features, so the device can appear assistive only when it is necessary [ 46 ]. That is, acceptance of hearing aids cannot be enumerated simply by effectiveness alone but by wearability and social factors as well.

To evaluate the acceptance of hearing aids, we should also focus on the user experience of hearing aids over time because it is closely related to technology acceptance. According to the Technology Acceptance Lifecycle model, technology acceptance evolves through three consecutive stages: preuse acceptability, initial use acceptance, and sustained use acceptance [ 42 ]. However, there is not much research on the longitudinal user experience of hearing aids related to wearability, appearance, or social acceptance issues, particularly on assistive devices for SSD. To address this gap, we conducted a four-week diary study comparing our proposed device with traditional Contralateral Routing of Signals (CROS) hearing aids and explored the factors that might affect the acceptance rate of assistive devices for SSD.

2.3 Studies on Wearable Devices and Assistive Technology based on Technology Acceptance Models

To analyze the low acceptance rate of hearing aids for people with SSD, we first focus on general models to explain technology acceptance. According to the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), technology acceptance consists of perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and attitude [ 14 ]. The Expectation Confirmation Model proposes that consumers purchase products with some expectations, followed by the confirmation based on the perceived performance, resulting in satisfaction [ 4 ]. The Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) explains technology acceptance by the following four factors: (i) performance expectancy; the degree to which a user believes that using the device will help the user to attain gains in job performance, (ii) effort expectancy; the degree of ease associated with the use of the system, (iii) facilitating conditions; the degree to which a user believes that an organizational and technical infrastructure exists to support the use of the system, and (iv) social influence; the degree to which a user perceives that important others believe the user should use the new device [ 57 ].

Within the vast area of technologies, hearing aids can be categorized as wearable assistive devices. Previous works have applied technology acceptance models to wearable devices such as heart rate monitoring devices, location tracking devices, and devices for patients with dementia [ 9 , 13 ]. It should be noted that within acceptance factors of wearable devices, physical comfort and social acceptance are especially important because they are worn on the body and visible to the surrounding people [ 30 ]. UTAUT, with additional factors such as self-efficacy and anxiety, has also been applied to investigate the acceptance of assistive technology for visually impaired individuals [ 2 ]. As UTAUT has been applied to existing research on the acceptance of wearable devices and assistive technology, we constructed a questionnaire based on the factors that contribute to technology acceptance addressed by the UTAUT model to capture various acceptance factors related to assistive devices for SSD.

2.4 Longitudinal Studies on User Experience and Technology Acceptance

To assess the success of a product, capturing the longitudinal user experience is crucial as it evolves over time [ 32 ], and in this section, we introduce longitudinal studies that track how the user experience of technologies develops over time. The UX curve and the iScale ask users to retrospectively draw a curve on how their user experience changes over several months [ 28 , 32 ]. Both studies applied the method to observe changes in the user experience of mobile phones. Although these methods let users directly express the change in their user experience, retrospective reconstructions over a long period induce overemphasis on especially salient moments.

Aside from research relying on users to elicit and draw their user experience over time, longitudinal studies have also repeatedly asked the users the same questionnaire over the use period. For example, Harbich and Hassenzahl collected the user experience of software products over 13 weeks, and used a multilevel analysis to examine the temporal dynamics of satisfaction factors [ 20 ]. Clemmensen el al. investigated the longitudinal user experience of greenhouse growers [ 11 ] using AttrakDiff [ 22 ] and custom questionnaires. In this research, professional workers with an average of 30 months of system experience participated in the study and found that the largest slope of AttrakDiff constructs, regressed over ten days, was as small as 0.04. The use of activity trackers was also investigated in previous work, focusing on the period between three weeks and six months after the purchase of the product, to see the change in long-term usage [ 52 ].

A model has been developed to explain longitudinal changes in user experience and technology acceptance. Karapanos et al. conducted a five-week diary-based study that followed people after purchasing an iPhone [ 29 ] (see Section 4.4 for the detailed diary collection procedure). They found that hedonic aspects of product use were dominant in early experiences; however, prolonged experiences became more dominant in the later phase and suggested that prolonged experiences were the main reason for iPhone's success. Based on this finding, they advocated that user experiences emerge through the following three phases: orientation, incorporation, and identification. The orientation phase comprises two categories of user experience; learnability and stimulation (i.e., early experiences that occur mainly in the first week of use, such as excitement and frustration induced by novel features and learnability flaws). The incorporation phase comprises categories of effectiveness and long-term usability experiences (i.e., users use the product in their lives and experience ways in which the product is becoming meaningful). The identification phase comprises categories of personal and social experiences (i.e., the products were used in their daily routines, and the users form a personal relationship with the product).

In this work, to collect daily experiences from assistive device users, we employed the Day Reconstruction Method (DRM) used in the study by Karapanos et al. [ 29 ]. We then classify these experiences according to their framework to different phases of user experience to investigate the relationship between hearing aid acceptance and user experience over time, including factors related to wearability, appearance, and social acceptance.

3 DESIGN PROCESS OF ASEARS

3.1 Background Study

The first author has SSD and has difficulty hearing conversations on his deaf side in noisy places. He felt like he missed many opportunities because he often had difficulties participating in face-to-face group conversations. To overcome these challenges, he tried wearing CROS hearing aids. However, putting the ear tips of the hearing aids inside the ear was cumbersome, and the day-long use caused itchiness inside the ear. In addition, he lost confidence in his appearance because of the self-stigma of wearing hearing aids in public. After these experiences, he decided to tackle the issues with existing assistive devices for SSD, and started this project together with his friend, who also has SSD.

To discover other persons’ challenges with SSD and hearing aids, an online survey was conducted on 51 people who contacted us through our website 1 . We asked them to describe multiple situations in the past month in which they had trouble because of SSD. The majority of the experiences were on (i) hearing from the deaf side (N = 44), consisting of not being able to notice sound from the deaf side (N = 23) and not being able to understand speech from the deaf side (N = 21). (ii) Hearing in noisy situations (N = 27) was the second most common experience, and some respondents mentioned specific situations such as hearing conversations in a crowd (N = 7) and having conversations over plastic shields for infection control measures (N = 2). (iii) Not being able to tell the direction of sound (N = 10) was also mentioned. Six experiences were not coded because we could not clearly identify the described situation.

Among those who answered our questionnaire, 12 people had former experience wearing assistive devices. The assistive devices consisted of CROS hearing aids (N = 8), hearing aids for amplification on the deaf side (N = 2), smartphone apps (N = 1), and bone conduction hearing aids (N = 1). Ten people had already rejected using them, and we asked the reasons why they stopped using their assistive devices. These were coded and classified into the six categories of user experience according to Karapanos et al.’s framework introduced in Section 2.4 ; learnability, stimulation, effectiveness, long-term usability, personal, and social experiences. One experience was related to learnability: difficult to wear (N = 1). The experiences classified as the stimulation experience (N = 5) consisted of uncomfortable sound (N = 2) and uncomfortable to wear (N = 3). For long-term usability experiences, the assistive devices reduced the usability of the phone they originally used (N = 2). Some noticed that they do not need assistive devices in their lives (N = 2, personal), and others did not like it when people talked about their hearing aids (N = 2, social). Some participants mentioned hearing aids were too expensive to buy (N = 3); however, this was not classified as any of the experiences. Although none of the respondents mentioned low effectiveness as the main reason for rejecting their assistive devices, six people mentioned that the device was ineffective in some situations, such as in noisy environments and when whispered into the deaf ear.

From these findings, we constructed our ideas of a more acceptable assistive device for SSD. We first considered that wearability issues were caused by users having to insert ear tips of CROS hearing aids to hear the sound collected by the device. Therefore, we chose bone conduction to present sound, which transmits sound to the inner ear with vibration and without plugging the ear. Next, to address the problem that users do not like others talking about their assistive devices, we decided to put the functionality of CROS hearing aids into a pair of glasses. This is based on prior work explaining how assistive technology that resembles mainstream devices is more accepted by individuals with invisible disabilities, such as SSD [ 53 ],

Based on these ideas, we built an eyeglasses-type assistive device for SSD based on bone conduction as described in the following section. Our prototype is then used for a comparative study to answer research questions on factors and user experiences influencing the acceptance of assistive devices for SSD.

As discussed in Section 3.1 , since most people with SSD experience difficulty hearing sounds from their deaf side, we follow the basic concept of CROS hearing aids; collect the sound from the deaf side with microphones and present it to the hearing side. The first proof-of-concept model is shown in  Table 1 B). The microphone was attached to a 3D printed plastic glasses frame, and the bone conduction speaker was taped on the other side of the frame. At the same time, an external microcontroller connected by wires performed the voice processing. Bone conduction is a way to present sound by applying vibration to the head without inserting anything into the ear. The author with SSD confirmed the comfortable user experience of just wearing a pair of glasses enables the user to hear the sound coming from the deaf side.

To improve the mobility of the first model, we embedded the circuits and the bone conduction speaker inside the 3D printed glasses as shown in  Table 1 C). The position and angle of the bone conduction speaker must be adjusted carefully so that sound is transmitted efficiently to the user without causing pain due to excessive force. Unlike ordinary eyeglasses, the fully 3D printed glasses could not be adjusted finely to place the bone conduction speaker to the optimal position for each user. Another problem with embedding all the functions inside custom-designed glasses is that everyone has a different preference for the appearance of glasses. Although the appearance of devices significantly impacts the acceptance of assistive devices, meeting such needs is impossible due to the design constraints imposed by embedding circuits inside the glasses.

Therefore, we made the device attachable to the tip of existing glasses as shown in  Table 1 D), which means that every user can use our device with their favorite glasses. This device consists of two components; the hearing side with a bone conduction speaker and the deaf side with microphones. After wearing the glasses with the device attached, users join the connector at the back of their neck to use the device. We prepared the device with three length variations for the neck strap to fit participants with different head sizes. The position and angle of the bone conduction speaker can be adjusted by moving the grip position of the attachment, allowing users to adjust the bone conduction speaker so that it fits them comfortably. Compared to the former models where the bone conduction speaker faces the head, the bone conduction speaker in this model was designed to be in contact with the auricle (behind the ear). The position was changed because, according to recent work, the auricle can transmit sound efficiently without excessive pressure on the skin compared to bone conduction to the head [ 43 ]. Moreover, the speaker can be hidden behind the ears, unlike commercially available bone conduction headsets, with which the speakers are visible in front of one's ears. Volume switches were added on the deaf side to adjust volume, and there is an LED indicator and a USB connector for charging inside the battery unit.

In the following section, we describe our research questions on factors influencing the acceptance of assistive devices for SSD, and hypotheses focusing on aspects that may lead to greater acceptance of our proposed device over conventional CROS hearing aids.

3.3 Research Questions and Hypotheses

As explained in Section 2.1 , although previous work has shown that hearing aids for SSD are perceived useful, their acceptance rate remains low at 4% [ 18 ]. Several reasons for abandoning conventional hearing aids are known, such as wearability, appearance, and social acceptance issues [ 59 ]. Therefore, our first research question is as follows.

RQ1: What factors influence the acceptance of assistive devices for SSD?

To show that our proposed device is more accepted than CROS hearing aids by people with SSD, we build three hypotheses focusing on aspects that may influence acceptance of the assistive devices; performance expectancy, social acceptance, and effort expectancy.

Regarding performance expectancy introduced in the UTAUT model (see Section 2.3 for details), bone conduction can be effective because of the following reason; bone conduction can present sound without inserting anything into the ear, while CROS hearing aids require ear tips to be inserted inside the better ear, which in turn may affect original hearing. This is because although the ear tip has multiple holes to pass the original sound through, it attenuates sounds higher than 2 kHz [ 33 ], which is essential for speech perception [ 8 ]. Therefore, the following hypothesis can be constructed.

H1: Assistive devices for SSD are perceived to be more effective and sound more natural when the sound is presented by bone conduction compared to CROS hearing aids.

Regarding social acceptance, there are two viewpoints to consider; user's social acceptance and spectator's social acceptance [ 41 ]. The eyeglasses design of asEars may increase user's social acceptance because prior work suggests that assistive technology that resembles mainstream devices is more accepted by individuals with invisible disabilities such as SSD [ 53 ]. Moreover, if H1 is supported, bone conduction assistive devices may further decrease the difficulties due to SSD imposed on other people compared to CROS hearing aids. Previous work shows that providing a hearing aid to hard-of-hearing individuals reduces the difficulties of people around them [ 5 ]. This may result in an increase of spectator's social acceptance . Thus, our hypothesis is stated as follows.

H2: Bone conduction glasses are more socially acceptable as an assistive device for SSD compared to CROS hearing aids.

Next, regarding effort expectancy used in UTAUT, we address stimulation and long-term usability user experience issues, such as wearability issues that arose in the background study. We considered that such issues arise because users have to insert the ear tips of CROS hearing aids to hear the sound collected by the device. Using bone conduction, which transmits sound to the inner ear with vibration and without plugging the ear, can solve this problem. Therefore, our hypothesis can be expressed as follows.

H3: Bone conduction assistive devices have a lower effort expectancy than CROS hearing aids.

Previous studies suggest that examining user experience over time provides insights into designing a more successful product [ 29 , 32 ]. We focus on different phases of user experience based on Karapanos et al.’s framework [ 29 ], and our second research question is stated below.

RQ2: How do different phases of user experience affect acceptance of assistive devices for SSD?

In the following section, we describe our experiment to answer these research questions and hypotheses.

4 USER EXPERIMENT

We conducted a 4-week experiment with 10 participants with SSD to explore the factors influencing the acceptance of assistive devices for SSD. Instead of in-laboratory hearing-in-noise tests, we physically validated the functionality of the assistive device; it sufficiently routes the sound from the deaf side to the hearing side (Section 4.3 ). This procedure was taken because the method of adjusting the device was identical to that of CROS hearing aids in a previous study [ 50 ], and in-laboratory hearing-in-noise tests do not translate to real listening situations [ 21 ], which is the focus of this study.

4.1 Participants

The eligibility criteria for participating in the experiment were having single-sided deafness (worse ear: pure tone average threshold is 60 dB HL or worse, threshold at least two frequencies among 1, 2, or 4 kHz are 65 dB HL or worse, having sensorineural hearing loss, better ear: the average threshold is 30 dB HL or better [ 35 ]), having no previous experience with hearing aids , Japanese as mother tongue, age of over 18 years old, and having no problems sitting for a long period.

4.2 Experiment Process

We conducted a four-week longitudinal experiment, which obtained the Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval from the first author's institution. The assistive device for SSD was either a CROS hearing aid 3 or asEars 4 . As shown in  Figure 2 , we chose a crossover randomized design for this experiment: half of the participants wore a CROS hearing aid for the first two weeks and asEars for the next two weeks (P4, P5, P7, P9, P10). The other group wore the devices in the opposite order (P1, P2, P3, P6, P8). The participants were asked to complete a daily questionnaire described in Section 4.4 . On the last day of each two-week period, we conducted a semi-structured interview lasting 30–60 minutes on their experiences with the assistive device. Three participants had a delayed follow-up, and one participant had an early follow-up due to personal circumstances.

Response bias, the tendency of a participant's response to be skewed, is a concern especially for research on assistive technology [ 40 ]. For example, participants can give positive feedback to asEars to show support for our project, as mentioned in Ming et al.’s work [ 40 ]. Let us call this type of bias prototype bias in this work. Furthermore, since participants had no experience using assistive devices, novelty biases are expected [ 49 ]. Therefore, we asked the same participants to use both asEars, and CROS hearing aids at different periods to address these problems. We detail how we processed such biases for questionnaire results in Section 5.1 .

4.3 Fitting Procedures of the Assistive Devices

Next, we explain the adjustment method of the assistive devices. For the CROS hearing aid, we used the Phonak CROS P-R from Sonova with a microphone worn on the deaf side and the Phonak Audéo P-R from Sonova with a small speaker to present sound inside the hearing ear, as shown in  Table 1 A). The default settings of the hearing aids were used, except for the volume balance between the hearing and deaf sides. The volume balance was adjusted so that the sound from the speaker at directions 45 degrees diagonally forward at the hearing side and the deaf side matched when the sound was measured inside the ear canal of the better ear with a probe microphone [ 16 ].

asEars was adjusted with the same method as the CROS hearing aids. To ensure that asEars properly transmits sound from the deaf side to the hearing side, we measured the frequency response inside the ear canal of the hearing side. Hearing side w/o proposed device in  Figure 3 shows the frequency response when the speaker presents the sound at 45 degrees diagonally in front of the hearing side. Deaf side w/o proposed device shows the frequency response when the sound is presented by the speaker on the other side. As shown in Deaf side w/o proposed device in  Figure 3 , sound especially in the range from 2 kHz to 7 kHz is attenuated due to the head-shadow effect, which prevents propagation of sound from the deaf side to the hearing side [ 48 ]. Due to this attenuation, people with SSD have difficulty hearing conversations on their deaf side. When asEars is worn, Deaf side w/ proposed device shows that the sound coming from the deaf side is boosted to the same level as when the sound is presented to the hearing side, as intended.

4.4 Measures

We created a questionnaire consisting of Likert scale items for evaluating the assistive device, and a form for reporting daily experiences related to the device.

To collect the quantitative evaluations of the assistive devices during the experiment period, we asked participants to complete a 7-point Likert scale questionnaire. Although there exist questionnaires specifically designed for hearing aids, such questionnaires focus only on hearing or quality-of-life [ 12 , 61 ]. In this work, to capture various acceptance factors, we constructed a questionnaire based on the factors that contribute to technology acceptance addressed by the UTAUT model (see Section 2.3 for details) [ 57 ]. Among the various technology acceptance models, we selected UTAUT because it has been widely used for wearable and assistive technology [ 2 , 9 , 13 ] and contains the factor of social influence. However, the social influence used in UTAUT contains only the spectator's social acceptance explained in Section 3.3 ; therefore we modified the term to social acceptance to consider user's social acceptance .

Based on each factor extended from UTAUT, the following questions were adapted from the Quebec User Evaluation of Satisfaction with assistive Technology (QUEST) [ 15 ], Effectiveness of the Auditor Rehabilitation Scale (EAR) [ 61 ], and UTAUT [ 57 ] to address the hypotheses and research questions in this work. Two questions about satisfaction and intention to use were also added to measure the acceptance of the device, which is expected to be predicted by the factors mentioned above.

  1. Performance expectancy (related to H1)
    • Effectiveness: How effective is the device? [ 15 ]
    • Naturalness: How natural does the device sound? [ 61 ]
  2. Social acceptance (related to H2)
    • Appearance: How bothered have you been by how you look with the device? [ 61 ]
    • Spectator's influence: Do the people who are important to you think you should use the device? [ 57 ]
  3. Effort expectancy (related to H3)
    • Usability: How easy is it to use the device? [ 15 ]
    • Comfort: How is the physical comfort of the device after wearing it for a while? [ 61 ]
    • Sound Fatigue: Does the sound of the device makes you tired?
  4. Acceptance of the device (related to RQ1, RQ2)
    • Satisfaction: How satisfied are you with the device?
    • Intention to use: Do you intend to use the device? [ 57 ]

Next, to collect daily experiences, we employed the Day Reconstruction Method (DRM) used in the study by Karapanos et al. [ 29 ]. At the end of each day, participants were asked to reconstruct and write down their experiences for the day related to the assistive device. Then, they chose a maximum of three most satisfying or dissatisfying experiences of the day from the list, and were asked to write a story that describes the situation with their feelings and momentary perceptions of the product. The participants were requested to clarify the situations described in their daily experiences when necessary.

The questionnaire was translated into Japanese, and a consensus among the authors was made about the translation.

5 RESULTS

5.1 Analysis of Questionnaires

The response rate of the daily questionnaire was 91.4% (256/280 submissions). Three participants temporarily suspended the testing period because they had a fever, and the day count resumed from when the test was resumed.

Regression analysis of acceptance and acceptance factors. To reveal what factors influence the acceptance of assistive devices for SSD (RQ1), we used fixed-effects ordered logistic regression with a significance threshold at .05 [ 3 ]. Although ordered logistic regression is commonly used for analyzing ordinal scales such as Likert scale [ 17 ], since participants answered the same questionnaire every day, the longitudinal nature of the data should be considered rather than treating all submissions as independent submissions. Fixed-effects ordered logistic regression is suitable in this case [ 3 ].

Next, we briefly explain the model behind fixed-effects ordered logistic regression. Let the latent variable $y_{i t}^{*}$ underlying behind the observed Likert scale answer (satisfaction and intention to use) be y it , for individual i at time t , β be the slope parameter, ɛ it be the error term and x it be explanatory variables (i.e., answers to Likert scale questions related to user acceptance, such as naturalness, comfort, usability, appearance, effectiveness, sound fatigue, and spectator's influence). Dummy variables of prototype bias (i.e., indicating the employed device was asEars) and novelty bias (i.e., indicating the first period of the experiment) were also introduced as explanatory variables to explain the biases mentioned in Section 4.4 . The distinctive term for the model is α i : individual-specific intercepts containing personal attitudes and beliefs for the individual i . This term assumes that the intercepts of individuals are constant and independent of time. Using the terms introduced above, $y_{i t}^{*}$ can be written as follows [ 3 ].

\begin{eqnarray*} y_{i t}^{*}=\mathbf {x}_{i t}^{\prime } \boldsymbol {\beta }+\alpha _{i}+\varepsilon _{i t} \end{eqnarray*}
When the explained variable is the intention to use, satisfaction will be treated as an explanatory variable since satisfaction is thought to have a significant influence on intention to use [ 4 , 45 ].

Pairwise test of acceptance factors. To verify the hypotheses related to each user acceptance factor (H1, H2, and H3), we performed a paired test for each Likert scale item in the questionnaire. Since we treat Likert scale as an ordinal scale, we used a Wilcoxon signed rank test to test the statistical difference of each item for the first week and the second week. To avoid the risk of Type I errors, the significance threshold was adjusted to .025 with Bonferroni correction. The questionnaire results of different devices were paired by the same participant and same day counted from the beginning of each period, and unmatched submissions were omitted. Therefore, 248 submissions out of 256 were used for the Wilcoxon signed rank test.

Coding of daily experiences. All experience narratives were classified as related to one of the six categories proposed by Karapanos et al.’s work [ 29 ]; stimulation, learnability, usefulness, long-term usability, personal, and social, except for narratives related to bugs of asEars and device unuse due to illness of the participant. According to Karapanos et al.’s framework, each experience belongs to the following phases of user experience. Stimulation and learnability belong to the orientation phase, usefulness and long-term usability belong to the incorporation phase, and personal and social factors belong to the identification phase. Additionally, the narratives were classified into positive, neutral, and negative attitudes toward the device. The coding process was independently conducted by the first author. To validate the reliability of the coding scheme, an additional researcher underwent the same process for 20% of the narratives (Cohen's κ = .81 for the classification of six user experience categories, Cohen's κ = .90 for the classification of attitudes).

5.3 Performance Expectancy of Assistive Devices for SSD (H1)

The participants answered Likert scale questions about the effectiveness and naturalness regarding the performance expectancy of assistive devices for SSD. Wilcoxon signed rank tests indicated that the effectiveness of asEars (first week: Mdn = 5.0, M = 4.68, SD = 1.53, second week: Mdn = 5.0, M = 4.61, SD = 1.64), was significantly better than CROS (first week: Mdn = 4.0, M = 3.92, SD = 1.25, Z = 780.0, p < .001, second week: Mdn = 4.0, M = 3.74, SD = 1.53, Z = 537.0, p < .001). The same tests indicated that the naturalness of asEars (first week: Mdn = 5.0, M = 4.87, SD = 0.96, second week: Mdn = 5.0, M = 4.94, SD = 0.98), was significantly better than CROS (first week: Mdn = 4.0, M = 4.45, SD = 1.06, Z = 512.5, p = .006, second week: Mdn = 4.0, M = 4.21, SD = 1.17, Z = 588.0, p < .001). H1 was supported. We discuss the reason behind these results based on the participants’ interview comments in Section 6.1 .

5.4 Social Acceptance of Assistive Devices for SSD (H2)

Using the daily questionnaire, we measured social acceptability by asking the participants about the appearance of the device and the spectator's influence. Regarding appearance, the Wilcoxon signed rank tests indicated that asEars (first week: Mdn = 2.0, M = 3.05, SD = 2.0, second week: Mdn = 2.0, M = 3.08, SD = 2.13) was significantly worse than CROS (first week: Mdn = 2.0, M = 2.56, SD = 1.95, Z = 372.0, p = .006, second week: Mdn = 1.0, M = 2.40, SD = 2.14, Z = 459.0, p < .001). Since appearance was a reversed score question, the results above imply that the appearance of asEars was more bothering to the participants.

On the other hand, regarding spectator's influence, the Wilcoxon signed rank tests indicated that asEars (first week: Mdn = 4.0, M = 4.52, SD = 1.34, second week: Mdn = 5.0, M = 4.81, SD = 1.40) was significantly better than CROS (first week: Mdn = 4.0, M = 4.16, SD = 0.70, Z = 320.0, p = .012, second week: Mdn = 4.0, M = 3.97, SD = 1.28, Z = 953.0, p < .001). The results above imply that the participants perceived that the surrounding people think that they should wear asEars more.

H2 was partially supported; the appearance was preferred for CROS hearing aids, while participants were likely to perceive that people around them would think they should wear asEars more. The reasons why the two aspects of social acceptance had opposite results are discussed in Section 6.1 referring to the interview comments.

5.5 Effort Expectancy of Assistive Devices for SSD (H3)

Regarding the effort expectancy of both assistive devices for SSD, the participants reported usability, comfort, and sound fatigue. As for usability for the first week, a Wilcoxon signed rank test indicated that asEars (Mdn = 5.0, M = 4.63, SD = 1.78) was significantly worse than CROS (Mdn = 5.0, M = 5.15, SD = 1.27, Z = 322.0, p = .020). The same test for the second week indicated that asEars (Mdn = 5.0, M = 4.9, SD = 1.83) had no statistical difference compared to CROS (Mdn = 5.0, M = 5.08, SD = 1.41). Wilcoxon signed rank tests on comfort indicated that asEars (first week: Mdn = 3.0, M = 3.4, SD = 1.37, second week: Mdn = 3.0, M = 3.52, SD = 1.27) was significantly worse than CROS (first week: Mdn = 5.0, M = 4.85, SD = 1.26, Z = 125.0, p < .001, second week: Mdn = 5.0, M = 4.73, SD = 1.21, Z = 232.0, p < .001). Wilcoxon signed rank tests on sound fatigue indicated that asEars (first week: Mdn = 4.0, M = 3.94, SD = 1.66, second week: Mdn = 5.0, M = 4.02, SD = 1.53) was significantly worse than CROS (first week: Mdn = 2.0, M = 2.94, SD = 1.64, Z = 853.5, p < .001, second week: Mdn = 2.0, M = 3.15, SD = 1.61, Z = 442.5, p < .001). H3 was not supported.

As the results above show, comfort and sound fatigue were worse for asEars, and usability was worse for asEars only for the first week, compared to CROS hearing aids. According to the interviews, the participants experienced specific issues related to bone conduction. Six participants felt that the position adjustment of the bone conduction speaker was difficult (usability issue): a slight misalignment of the device may cause pain due to excessive pressure on the ear (comfort issue), or the sound cannot be heard at all (P1, P5, P6, P7, P8, P9). Three of the participants mentioned that they got used to the position adjustment during the experiment (P5, P6, P7). Additionally, seven participants indicated that howling occasionally occurred with asEars, whereas this was not the case for the CROS hearing aids (sound fatigue issue) (P1, P2, P3, P4, P5, P7, P9). We found that howling occurred only on asEars because the vibration of the bone conduction speaker propagated through the glasses’ frame, and the sound returned to the microphone on the other side of the glasses.

Although issues specific to bone conduction arose, the usability and comfort problems with CROS hearing aids seemed not to occur with asEars. Regarding usability, four participants mentioned that asEars could be used with their earbuds, while they had to remove the CROS hearing aids to use earbuds (P3, P4, P5, P6). Regarding comfort, four participants mentioned that they could not get used to the discomfort caused by the ear tip of CROS hearing aids, such as itchiness, whereas asEars did not cause such issues (P1, P2, P5, P10).

Therefore, we can conclude that the effort expectancy of asEars was worse compared to CROS hearing aids. Comments from participants suggested that issues specific to CROS hearing aids related to usability and comfort were solved by asEars, while problems specific to bone conduction came up. In Section 6.3 , we further discuss other usability issues of CROS hearing aids solved by asEars, which may lead to more acceptable assistive devices.

5.6 Different Phases of User Experience Affect Acceptance of Assistive Devices for SSD (RQ2)

To reveal the relationship between assistive device acceptance and user experience over time, we observed the differences in user experiences between those who accepted the assistive devices and those who did not.

First, we divided the participants into groups of those who accepted the assistive devices and who did not in the following manner. At the follow-up interviews, we asked the participants if they wanted to continue using the devices. If the participant rejected at least one device, they were classified as those who did not accept the devices (rejected CROS: P1, rejected both devices: P8, P9, P10). Reversely, the participants who accepted both devices were P2, P3, P4, P5, and P6. P7 was not grouped due to the low submission rate (25%) of the daily questionnaire.

Figure 4 shows the average satisfaction with CROS hearing aids and asEars in the first and second week of use, respectively. The blue points represent the evaluation of the first week, the green stars represent the evaluation of the second week, and the arrows represent the change in satisfaction for the same participant. Red arrows indicate participants whose average satisfaction level was less than 4.5 in the second week and decreased from the first week. We can see that these participants belong to the rejecting group.

In  Figure 5 , we show the number of user experiences for the participants who accepted the assistive devices, classified by the three phases and six categories of user experience according to the coding procedure described in Section 5.1 . We show the number of user experiences classified in the same way for those who did not accept the devices in  Figure 6 .

In the orientation phase, for participants who accepted the devices, the dissatisfying experiences tended to decrease intensively, while this was not the case for participants who rejected the devices. The slope of linear regression was β = −0.547 ( p = .020) for the accepting group, β = −0.321 ( p = .064) for the rejecting group. Regarding the incorporation phase, participants in the rejecting group had more dissatisfying experiences (accepting group: N = 37, rejecting group: N = 61). Whereas, for participants who rejected the devices, satisfying experiences were more sparse (accepting group: N = 73, rejecting group: N = 25). For the identification phase, participants in the rejecting group had less satisfying experiences (accepting group: N = 9, rejecting group: N = 1). Furthermore, only the participants in the accepting group had positive experiences related to long-term usability, personal, and social factors.

Regarding the shift in user experiences, Karapanos et al. found that users’ concern for consumer electronics (iPhone) shifts from learnability to usefulness over time [ 29 ]. However, we found that for wearable assistive devices, learnability, stimulation, and usefulness are primary concerns that occur almost simultaneously. Furthermore, Karapanos et al. found that negative experiences related to stimulation rapidly decrease. We found that the decrease slope differed between those who accepted and rejected the devices; negative experiences did not decrease significantly over time for those who rejected the devices.

In the interviews, we asked the participants why and when they wanted to stop using the assistive devices. They pointed out pain due to the device (P9, after asEars, stimulation, every day), howling (P5, after asEars, stimulation, only first day), loud sound (P8, after CROS, stimulation, every day), and no effectiveness (P1, after CROS, effectiveness, every day). Other participants mentioned that they did not feel they wanted to quit using the assistive devices during the experiment period. Therefore, we can see that P1, P8, and P9 belong to the rejecting group, and their dissatisfying experience related to stimulation or effectiveness persisted throughout the experiment period. On the other hand, P5, who accepted the devices, had a similar experience only on the first day.

In conclusion, participants who rejected the assistive devices had more negative user experiences and fewer positive experiences in each phase. Another distinction between the rejecting group was that dissatisfying experiences related to stimulation did not decrease as in the accepting group. Moreover, the rejecting group had negative experiences related to stimulation or usefulness, making them think about quitting using the devices, whereas the accepting group had fewer negative experiences over time leading to continued use of the devices.

To summarize our results, performance expectancy, spectator's influence, and usability contribute to the acceptance of assistive devices for SSD. When we compared the acceptance between CROS hearing aids and asEars, asEars had a greater acceptance due to its performance expectancy and spectator's social acceptance . Another insight was that more satisfying user experiences throughout all phases of user experience over time, and a sharp decrease in negative user experiences during the orientation phase led to a greater acceptance of the assistive devices for SSD.

6 DISCUSSION

6.1 Comparison between CROS hearing aids and asEars

First, we confirmed that bone conduction glasses have a higher performance expectancy than CROS hearing aids as assistive devices for SSD (H1). Contrary to previous work suggesting that bone conduction without implantation had limited hearing benefit for SSD compared to CROS hearing aids [ 39 ], our research results suggested that bone conduction on the auricle is more effective than CROS hearing aids for SSD. Moreover, our result was in line with previous work suggesting that implanted bone conduction hearing aids are subjectively more effective than CROS hearing aids [ 23 ]. However, the reason for the high effectiveness is not yet known. One participant had an interesting comment on the performance expectancy for each device: “the sounds (coming into my normal hearing ear) are more muffled with CROS hearing aids than with asEars – when I found it difficult to hear something, I sometimes took the CROS hearing aids off” (P8, after CROS). This insight is supported by the measurements reported in previous research that the ear tips of hearing aids attenuate sounds in the high-frequency range [ 33 ]. Combined with the findings of previous work, our results suggest that although hearing aids and earbuds with hole-punched ear tips that present sound without occluding the ear are gaining attention on the market [ 54 ], bone conduction on the auricle may be superior in terms of speech intelligibility or sound naturalness. Moreover, several participants felt that the effectiveness of bone conduction made them feel a sense of security in social gatherings, and they felt anxiety when they forgot to use asEars (P1, P7, P8).

Regarding social acceptance of assistive devices for SSD (H2), two aspects of social acceptance showed different results; user's social acceptance was better for CROS hearing aids, and spectator's social acceptance was better for asEars. Here we discuss the possible reasons why these two aspects had opposite results based on participant interviews.

Regarding user's social acceptance , three participants mentioned that the appearance of CROS hearing aids is better due to perceived invisibility (P5, P6, P8): “the CROS hearing aid is really like I'm not wearing anything, so, it is better for the appearance. The CROS hearing aids are really like a plus-minus zero” (P5, after asEars). Meanwhile, two participants favored the appearance of asEars (P1, P2). P1 mentioned self-stigma associated with the use of CROS hearing aids. “At first glance, asEars doesn't look like a hearing aid for SSD. – I don't think people probably feel like it's something like a hearing aid. – CROS hearing aids look completely like hearing aids. The idea of aging or, well, the decline associated with it is not a very pleasant one” (P1, after CROS). These comments are in line with previous work that even for assistive devices that are objectively less visible, users may still experience significant levels of self-stigma [ 6 ]. These comments also suggested that although asEars was more visible than CROS hearing aids to the surrounding people due to its form factor, the incongruent appearance to ordinary hearing aids increased user's social acceptance , but not to the level of CROS.

On the other hand, regarding spectator's social acceptance , some participants felt that surrounding people think they should wear an assistive device due to its effectiveness rather than its appearance. The participants mentioned that they often cause difficulties for those around them due to SSD; however, wearing assistive devices would eliminate the difficulty: “I thought it would be better for me to wear it for those around me, because I wouldn't have to worry about my seat position, and as a result, I wouldn't give them a hard time” (P3, after CROS). One participant even mentioned that he was strongly recommended to wear asEars: “I seemed to be unconsciously asking back quite a lot, and since I was asking my friend back less, he said, ‘you should wear it again, you should wear it all the time’ – I had to take my hearing aids off for a bit, so I took them off around that time, but he said, ‘you ain't hearing anymore,’ and when I was driving or something, ‘well, put them back on!,’ he said” (P4, after asEars). We learned from these comments that while both CROS hearing aids and asEars may reduce the difficulty for those around them, asEars may have a higher spectator's social acceptance since asEars has better performance expectancy as shown in Section 5.3 . Furthermore, the finding of previous work showing that providing a hearing aid to hard-of-hearing individuals reduces the difficulties of people around them [ 5 ] can also be applied to people with SSD.

Our third hypothesis was about the effort expectancy of assistive devices for SSD. We showed that comfort and sound fatigue was better for CROS hearing aids, and usability was better for CROS hearing aids only for the first week. The participants pointed out some reasons why asEars had a worse effort expectancy, related to asEars being a research prototype. Regarding comfort, P5 suggested that the discomfort of asEars was caused by the heavy weight of the battery unit behind the neck pulling the attached eyeglasses backward, resulting in excessive pressure on the nose bridge. Furthermore, some participants were afraid of breaking asEars, resulting in lower usability of the device (P2, P3, P5). Therefore, it is suggested that effort expectancy may have been low partially due to asEars being a research prototype rather than fundamental problems related to bone conduction assistive devices in general.

To conclude this section, we discussed how asEars had better performance expectancy due to its ability to present sound without occluding the better ear. Regarding social acceptance, CROS hearing aids had a better user's social acceptance because they were perceived as invisible. However, some participants felt self-stigma resulting in low user's social acceptance . For spectator's social acceptance , it was suggested that asEars was preferred because of a higher performance expectancy, resulting in fewer difficulties imposed on significant others due to SSD. As discussed in Section 5.5 , the effort expectancy issues of CROS hearing aids were solved by asEars; however, the overall evaluation was worse partially because asEars was a research prototype, and we introduced comments by the participants that imply further improvements regarding the effort expectancy of asEars.

6.2 User Experience and Factors Influencing Acceptance of Assistive Devices for SSD

Regarding the factors contributing to the acceptance of the assistive devices, the regression analysis revealed that for the proposed device, the intention to use included prototype bias because the device was seen as under development. We asked the participants why they reported a high intention to use while simultaneously reporting low satisfaction. P2 mentioned that they felt a sense of support towards asEars, and P1 pointed out that he felt the product would improve in the future. However, this comment from P1 cannot be strictly categorized as prototype bias since there are products, such as smartphones, in the market that are not prototypes but can improve through software updates. Therefore, while prototype bias may skew short-term user experiences, it is suggested that having a feeling of device improvement over time contributes to the intention to use.

Since the measure for intention to use contained some bias, while satisfaction did not, we focused on satisfaction to measure acceptance in this study. We found that effectiveness, naturalness, spectator's influence, usability, and novelty bias significantly contributed to satisfaction. However, surprisingly, comfort did not. This result contradicts previous work indicating that comfort is one of the major reasons to abandon hearing aids [ 59 ]. Some participants claimed that they had thought of taking off asEars because it was uncomfortable (P1, P5, P6, P9, P10), while others said they never minded the feeling of wearing the device (P2, P3, P4, P7, P8). In part, these individual differences may explain why our regression analysis did not show that comfort significantly contributed to satisfaction. On the other hand, as illustrated in  Figure 4 , two participants (P5, P6) in the accepting group who thought about taking off asEars during the experiment also reported an increase in their satisfaction from the first week to the second week. This suggests that comfort may be critical to the satisfaction of hearing assistive devices in the initial stage of use, but if wearers can get used to the device over time, the effect of comfort on satisfaction may diminish.

In this work, we classified five participants as those who accepted both assistive devices and four participants as those who did not. For participants who rejected at least one of the assistive devices, we found more dissatisfying experiences in their DRM narratives, especially related to learnability, stimulation, and usefulness, compared to participants who accepted the devices. Surprisingly, as detailed in  Figure 4 , the average satisfaction decreased from the first week to the second week for both devices for people who had low satisfaction at the end of the experiment period. One explanation for the decrease in satisfaction with both devices is that the participants rejected the devices due to personal attributes such as how much difficulty they have in daily life, expectations towards the devices, and their daily listening environments rather than their preference for the devices’ features.

Regarding long-term usability or personal issues affecting user experience, P9 pointed out that they had trouble changing the eyeglasses attached with asEars, since they used different eyeglasses depending on their situation: “I change my glasses frequently because I need different focal lengths for different situations in my daily life. With the current device, it is a bit difficult to change my glasses” (P9, after asEars). Being able to easily change and take off eyeglasses was also important for some participants in order to look at close objects (P1, P9 after asEars). However, this behavior was limited to asEars because the battery unit prevented the eyeglasses from being instantly removed.

Furthermore, even participants who accepted both assistive devices noted that they could not use them all day due to long-term usability or personal issues. Five participants mentioned that they were afraid to drop the CROS hearing aids (P2, P3, P5, P8, P9), and four participants mentioned that the CROS hearing aids could not be used with earbuds, whereas asEars could (P3, P4, P5, P6). For example, “I was afraid of (dropping and losing) my CROS hearing aids unless they were fixed (to my ears). – I can't do earphones with CROS hearing aids (so I wouldn't use them on a regular basis) –I would never get to the point where I would buy them because I can't use them regularly” (P3, after CROS).

In conclusion, participants who rejected both devices tended to have more dissatisfying user experiences related to learnability, stimulation, and usefulness. Since they further indicated that they would not like to use one of the devices anymore, they would be considered non-use users. However, among the participants who accepted both devices, no one wanted to use them all day (full-time use) and preferred part-time use. Their comments suggested that negative user experiences related to long-term usability or personal issues prevented them from engaging in full-time use of the assistive devices.

6.3 Implications for Designing Acceptable Assistive Devices

We suggest that there are three major stages of acceptance for assistive devices, and for users in the non-use stage, designers should focus on reducing the negative experiences of learnability, stimulation, and usefulness, as shown in  Figure 6 . On the contrary, for users who have reached part-time use, it is suggested that designers should focus on increasing positive long-term usability and personal experiences, shown in  Figure 5 , in order to approach full-time use.

Here, it is important to go beyond part-time use to as close to full-time use as possible. Since assistive devices are produced in small volume and expensive [ 24 ], it requires users to be willing to pay for the devices to gain actual acceptance in the market. The more frequently users believe that they will use a product, the greater the willingness to pay for it [ 55 ]. Moreover, by increasing the number of such users, the prices will decrease, and the assistive device will be more affordable for users in general.

Two design recommendations to promote the transition from part-time use to full-time use are as follows.

Users can use assistive devices with their favorite items. Two users who accepted both assistive devices spoke out that they would not use CROS hearing aids on a daily basis, mainly because they physically interfere with the use of earphones, and it is cumbersome for them to take the CROS device off every time (P3, P5). One way to address this problem is to combine consumer electronics functions into assistive devices, for example, by audio streaming sound from smartphones into hearing aids, similarly to Phonak's hearing aids [ 26 ]. However, it is common for users to already own other wearable devices, such as earphones. People may hang on to products because of emotional attachment, or they may think the product is indispensable because they need some unique functions not available in other devices [ 51 ]. Forcing the users to abandon their favorite devices will impose another barrier to using and accepting assistive devices.

Furthermore, the two participants were surprised to be able to hear both the surrounding sound and the sound from their earbuds while wearing asEars (P3, P5). This was an unexpected experience for them because people with SSD cannot enjoy the surrounding sound and the sound from the earphone at the same time unless they use earphones with an ambient sound hearing function. Therefore, we recommend designing assistive devices not to conflict with existing common wearable devices. Moreover, it would be even better to design assistive devices to enable users to enjoy the existing wearable devices while simultaneously supporting their disabilities.

Users get an opportunity to disclose their disabilities by making the device visible without evoking the disabilities explicitly. Some participants who are not strongly inclined to hide their hearing disabilities commented that it is unlikely to be talked about when wearing conventional hearing aids, even if the other person is aware of the device. On the contrary, because asEars did not look like a conventional hearing aid, many curious people often asked what the device was. The participants pointed out that when it is difficult for them to disclose that they have SSD, the device being mentioned by the other person creates an opportunity to disclose their disability. In this way, asEars made it easier for the other person to ask about the device because, and unlike with conventional hearing aids, the other person did not assume that they were hard of hearing, and there was no fear of seeming patronizing or saying the wrong thing when talking to people with disabilites [ 1 ]. Therefore, wearing asEars provided a communication opportunity for self-disclosure for the person with SSD.

In contrast, P1 wanted to hide his deafness and was unable to wear CROS hearing aids at work due to self-stigma. However, P1 was able to wear asEars to work, and when he did, he was bothered by his colleagues asking what the device was. Since asEars did not look like a conventional hearing aid, P1 explained to the colleagues that “this is a wearable device that collects sound and uses it for many things”, and thus, was able to hide his disability. This observation was in line with prior work that assistive technology that resembles mainstream devices is more accepted for individuals with invisible disabilities [ 53 ].

From the observations of participants with different preferences for self-disclosure, self-disclosure can be controlled by designing the device to be visible without evoking hearing disabilities and giving users direct control of self-disclosure through verbal communication. This extends prior work recommending control of self-disclosure through physical design features of assistive devices (i.e., making the device appear assistive at times and appear hidden or as a mainstream device at other times) [ 46 ].

7 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

Without an organized peer and support communities, recruiting a sufficient number of participants with specific disabilities is difficult. In this work, we recruited people who were interested in solutions for SSD through our website and kept in touch with more than 50 individuals over several years. This enabled us to work with many people with SSD from the design process of asEars and to recruit 10 participants who were cooperative during the user study. Therefore, creating a community is crucial for conducting similar research with under-studied disabilities. On the other hand, the participants may have had a positive attitude towards new technology, or they might have been especially in need of an assistive device because of SSD. Future work with participants who are not familiar with our project should be conducted to take into account such biases. Furthermore, the acceptance of two participants who did not own eyeglasses may have been affected since asEars had to be attached to ordinary pairs of glasses. Hence, it is necessary to investigate whether regular use of eyeglasses influences acceptance of asEars in future work. Another limitation is that we could not test how gender affects acceptance since we recruited only two female participants for this experiment.

In this work, participants used each device for two weeks, which is a relatively short period of time compared to existing longitudinal research on user experience (Section 2.4 ). However, although we motivated the participants by explaining that they would get paid according to the time they spent on the questionnaire, they tended to report fewer experiences as the study progressed. This may be because the assistive devices we used had fewer functions to explore compared to other devices, such as smartphones, or the participants got tired of answering the same questionnaire every day. To address the latter problem, similar studies could be designed to include two stages: first, conduct a full questionnaire similar to this work to extract the critical factors for acceptance in the short-term. Then, revise and compress the questionnaire to reduce the load on the participants and to gain data with higher quality over several weeks or months.

The effort expectancy of asEars was higher than that of CROS hearing aids due to problems specific to bone conduction. However, bone conduction in contact with the auricle has not yet been studied extensively, and we believe there is room for improvement with the technology. For example, detecting excessive pressure from the bone conduction device in contact with the user's skin before the user feels the pain could help alleviate issues with comfort. We believe this improvement would make asEars even more acceptable for users with SSD compared to CROS hearing aids.

In this study, those who did not accept the CROS hearing aids were more likely to have negative experiences with both asEars and CROS hearing aids. We suggested that personal background factors, such as the level of expectation for the assistive devices and the amount of trouble they usually have with SSD in their daily lives, may be contributing factors. However, investigating these factors specifically was beyond the scope of the present study and should be included in future works.

We also suggested that comfort may be critical to the satisfaction of hearing assistive devices in the initial stage of use, but if wearers can get used to the device over time, the effect of comfort on satisfaction may diminish. Thus, modifying the devices according to the user's accustomedness may promote further acceptance.

Lastly, the participants who accepted both assistive devices ended up with part-time use instead of full-time use. More research is needed on the user experiences of participants who reach full-time use to gain more insight into how to further promote the acceptance of assistive devices for SSD.

8 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we proposed a bone conduction-based assistive device to be worn with eyeglasses, called asEars, for people with single-sided deafness (SSD). asEars helps people with SSD participate in conversations on their deaf side by rerouting the sound from the deaf side to the hearing side using bone conduction without plugging the normal hearing ear. Our four-week user study showed that asEars was more accepted compared to CROS hearing aids due to its effectiveness, naturalness, and social acceptability but less accepted in terms of comfort and sound fatigue. Furthermore, the participants who accepted the assistive devices have different longitudinal user experiences from those who did not accept the devices. Finally, we detailed implications for designing more acceptable wearable assistive devices that would promote the transition from part-time use to full-time use. We hope that this work helps designers of wearable assistive devices to design and develop products with a higher acceptance rate, and help the target population fully enjoy their lives with the devices.
