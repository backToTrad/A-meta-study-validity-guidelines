Computational toys and apps, or coding kits, are the primary learning media for young children to engage in Computational Thinking concepts and skills. However, how coding kits are designed to welcome children of different genders remains unclear, a critical research gap given that computing is mostly a male-dominated field. Drawing on relevant literature, we develop a framework of gender-related design features in toys (i.e., boy-oriented, girl-oriented, or gender-neutral features) and employ it to examine gender-related design features in commercially available coding kits for young children aged seven and under (N=110). The findings reveal the lopsided gender representation in coding kits, e.g., their physical forms and supported coding activities are predominantly boy-oriented. We discuss design and research implications for coding kits to welcome participation from children of different genders, especially young girls whose preferred design features are underrepresented in current designs.

1 INTRODUCTION

As our everyday lives become increasingly digitized, the importance of introducing computational skills to young people has been widely recognized by education researchers, policymakers, and industry leaders  [ 34 , 50 , 193 ]. Computing-related literacy is often framed as “Computational Thinking” (CT), i.e., the ability to use Computer Science concepts and skills to solve everyday problems and learn about the world, such as thinking algorithmically and deconstructing large problems into smaller parts [ 193 , 194 ]. Early childhood has long been fertile ground to cultivate young people's interest in computing and promote their development of social, emotional, and cognitive skills [ 10 , 25 , 135 ], even mitigating the stereotype that boys are better than girls at coding and building robots [ 108 ]. Moreover, research shows children under seven can meaningfully participate in coding activities and learn CT concepts and skills, such as understanding abstraction, simple algorithms, and patterns  [ 41 , 46 , 47 ]. As such, much research has been focused on innovating computational learning experiences for children and developing tools to support this learning  [ 3 , 50 , 75 ]. Among these learning tools, computational toys and apps (also called coding kits) are the primary learning media for young children ages seven and under to engage in coding and CT  [ 24 , 201 ].

Coding kits take three design forms, including physical, virtual, and hybrid kits [ 201 ]. Physical kits include components that are all tangible. For example, Cubetto [ 40 ] is a physical kit designed for children between 3-9 years old consisting of a cubic wooden robot and a set of physical tiles to program the robot (see Figure 1(a). Virtual kits are computer-based applications without tangible parts, such as web-based coding games and mobile apps. Figure 1(b) shows one virtual kit example called ScratchJr [ 164 ], a visual, block-based programming tool on mobile devices designed for young children around 5-7 years old to create interactive stories and animations [ 52 ]. Hybrid kits consist of both digital and physical components, e.g., a physical robot and a mobile app to program the robot. Figure 1(c) shows Coding Starter Kit [ 130 ], a hybrid kit with three coding games allowing 5-10-year-old children to solve puzzles or compose music on a virtual application by manipulating physical coding blocks.

Although many coding kits are available for use  [ 201 ], much remains unknown regarding their impacts on young children, such as children's learning effectiveness and how they shape children's perceptions of coding and computing. This work aims to explore the designs of coding kits from a gender perspective, i.e., the representation of gender-oriented design features in coding kits, such as the colors and physical forms favored by boys and girls. This perspective is critical because children start to form gender conceptualization at a very young age, around 18 months  [ 111 , 159 , 191 ] and may have already formed the stereotype that boys are better at programming and robots than girls as young as six  [ 108 ]. Children's gender conceptualization further impacts their engagement choices with toys, i.e., avoiding toys that do not fit with their formed gender identity  [ 20 , 191 ]. Therefore, designing gender-inclusive coding kits is especially important to welcome participation from children of different genders, particularly given that computing is already heavily dominated by males (e.g.,  [ 66 ]). However, no studies have examined gendered design features in coding kits, and researchers and practitioners still miss the guidelines for designing gender-inclusive coding kits for young children. This work aims to fill this gap by exploring gender-related elements in current designs of theses kits. To do so, we ask: How are genders represented in the designs of coding kits for young children?

We collected commercially available coding kits ( N = 110) for children ages seven and under, summarized a framework of gender-related design features in toys from literature (i.e., boy-oriented, girl-oriented, or mixed features), and performed deductive coding using the developed framework to analyze gender-related design features of the collected coding kits. We followed a dichotomy in gender categorization (i.e., boys and girls) and did not include a third or fourth gender because the gender classification of young children – our focus group in this study – is dependent mainly on their biological sex, and the impacts of social environment on gender identity are typically found to manifest later in childhood (nine years old and after)  [ 174 ]. Our findings reveal unbalanced gender representations in the designs of coding kits in terms of colors, physical forms, and activity types. For example, boy-oriented design features (e.g., blue colors, vehicle shapes, and activity of motion-control) dominate all the collected kits, while girl-oriented features (e.g., pink colors, domestic-related items, and decoration activity) are underrepresented. Based on our analysis, we reflect on research gaps in understanding children's gender perceptions of coding kits and broader learning technologies, share design implications to encourage more gender-inclusive coding kits, and discuss how our analysis framework developed from literature may benefit the design of children's technologies and toys.

This work makes the following contributions to educational-related communities within and beyond HCI. First, we provide empirical evidence of how gender-related design features are distributed in current coding kits, revealing the alarming but long-ignored imbalance in gender inclusiveness in a primary computational learning medium for young children. Second, our findings highlight design implications for coding kits to include features that welcome participation from different genders, especially young girls whose preferred gender-related features are remarkably underrepresented in current designs. Third, the analysis framework of gender-related design features we developed from relevant literature can be employed by other researchers, practitioners, and designers in other disciplines as an analytical framework to examine gender representations in other toys and technologies for children as well as to inform the design of more gender-inclusive technologies.

2 RELATED WORK

This study aims to reveal the gaps in gender inclusiveness in current computational toys and kits from a design perspective and highlight design opportunities for future kits to better welcome participation from young learners of different genders. Therefore, what follows is an overview of gender development in young children, related work about gender-oriented design features in young children's toys, and gender differences in computer programming.

2.1 Gender Development in Young People

Sex is a determination given at birth made through biological criteria, while gender is a psychological, cultural, and social status associated with masculinity and femininity beyond the biological constitution  [ 97 , 192 ]. According to cognitive gender theories, social and cultural environments contribute to children's gender-related norms, conceptualizations, and stereotypes  [ 20 , 94 , 110 , 111 , 113 , 189 ]. Children actively and cognitively seek gender-related information surrounding them to process and construct gender schemas — beliefs about which activities, occupations, and behaviors are appropriate for males and females during their toddler and preschool years  [ 94 , 110 , 111 , 189 , 199 ]. Gender schemas include two cognitive types: an “ingroup/outgroup” schema and an “own-gender” schema  [ 20 , 110 ]. “Ingroup/outgroup” schema consists of general information that children extract and organize from gendered objects and activities in their environments, e.g., girls play with dolls more often and prefer pink while boys play with cars and prefer blue  [ 14 , 20 , 57 , 110 , 119 , 196 ]. Then they build gender-appropriate categories based on their sociocultural environments and use these formed schemas as a knowledge base for self-evaluation, i.e., assessing items in their environment as “for them” or “not for them”  [ 20 , 110 , 191 ]. As more gender-related information is taken, the “ingroup/outgroup” schema further forms the second schema – “own-gender” schema, a database with more detailed and specific scripts that directs children to carry out gender-appropriate behaviors  [ 20 , 110 ]. For example, a girl may build the schema that “girls do housework” through her daily observation. To act consistently with her own conceptualized gender, she may believe she should learn to do housework. The action patterns involved in housework then become part of her own-gender schema. In contrast, the same girl may learn that "boys fix cars" by referring to her formed schema, which is not gender-appropriate compared to the schema. As such, she will probably not be motivated to learn more about the subject, and the action patterns around fixing cars may not become part of her own-gender schema  [ 110 ].

Gender schemas play an essential role in guiding children's everyday behaviors, interests, and ways of thinking. Children make choices by following these schemas and selecting gender-appropriate activities, occupations, and behaviors while avoiding gender inappropriate selections even if they feel attracted to them  [ 189 ]. Once children form their gender schemas and recognize their gender group, they act like other group members. This gender identity formation process happens at an early development stage and will be consolidated rigidly by the ages of 5 to 7, followed by a relatively flexible stage where they expand what different genders can do as they continue to mature.  [ 22 , 111 , 181 , 196 ].

2.2 Gender-oriented Design Features in Toys

As aforementioned, sociocultural and physical environments both facilitate and reinforce children's gender schemas, which are also reflected in their interactions with toys. Children are more attracted by the toys and activities they consider appropriate for their gender while less likely to approach and engage with toys they believe are more appropriate for the opposite gender  [ 35 , 110 , 111 , 189 ]. Meanwhile, children are more likely to expect successful performance with toys and activities they believe are made for their genders  [ 111 ]. These gender-related toy preferences appear at an early developmental stage. At the age of 18-20 months, children already begin selecting gender-typed toys, and by two and three years old, they display the “hot potato effect,” i.e., avoiding toys considered to be appropriate for the opposite gender even if they are interested in it  [ 22 , 109 ]. Such gender-influenced toy preferences further impact children's development of cognitive abilities and social skills, and even their self-efficacy and future career paths  [ 57 , 159 , 191 ]. For example, building and construction toys have been focused on masculine themes and targeted at boys for decades  [ 119 ]. Therefore, girls may miss important opportunities to build STEM-related interests and competence provided by these learning tools  [ 57 ]. Simultaneously, children who frequently play with masculine toys may have fewer opportunities to develop motivations and skills related to comforting others, a skill emphasized by many girl-oriented toys  [ 93 ]. To cope with this issue, increasing the diversity of children's toy play, such as engaging them in across-gender and gender-neutral toys, is likely to advance children's integrated development  [ 14 , 190 ]. There are also positive developments in mitigating gender stereotypes in toys  [ 6 , 128 ]. For instance, Target eliminated gender labels on their toys in 2015  [ 143 ], and LEGO stopped categorizing its products based on gender in 2021  [ 144 ] after its product series LEGO Friends sparked debate around reinforcing gender stereotypes, such as reinforcing gendered occupations  [ 64 , 128 ]. In the meantime, academics and teachers have encouraged children to play with more moderately gender-typical, gender-atypical, and gender-neutral toys  [ 14 , 190 , 199 ].

Although many studies argue that gender stereotypes start to come into force and become stronger during the school-aged years (e.g.,  [ 112 , 114 ]), the toys for preschool and early-school young children are already gendered and influencing young children's toy choices. Specifically, boys generally believe that manipulative toys, vehicle toys, building bricks, and action figures are for them  [ 23 , 56 , 74 ]. Whereas girls’ interests are significantly higher in dolls, stuffed animals, fashion items like hairdresser's kits, and domestic items like tea sets  [ 14 , 23 , 119 , 126 , 152 ]. Even from the perspective of adults, dolls, domestically oriented toys, stuffed toys, as well as fashion and beauty items (e.g., clothing and jewelry) are seen more for young girls, while boys’ toys typically include transportation toys, military-related items, construction kits, sports equipment, and action figures  [ 14 , 119 ]. Another primary design feature that children use as gender cues is color. Colors have been demonstrated to significantly influence individuals’ toy classification and choice  [ 191 ]. Gender preferences for pink and blue appear as early as age two – girls prefer pink and pastel colors more than boys, and boys prefer blue and dark colors more than girls  [ 137 , 158 , 196 , 199 ]. Such visual preferences further impact children's interests in specific toys, even attracting their interest in gender-atypical toys, e.g., girls are more likely to engage with masculine toys if they are pink  [ 57 ]. Therefore, gender-typed colors can be used to mitigate gender-specific preferences to encourage children's engagement with toys outside their believed gender category because when toy colors of typically gendered toys are changed, children's play with gender-atypical toys increases  [ 97 ]. In addition to these physical and visual forms, gendered differences are also implicated in the characteristics of the supported activities by a toy. Toys oriented for girls are typically rated higher on creativity and nurturance, more focus on appearance and attractiveness, and emphasize the development of nurturance-, comforting- and domestic-related skills  [ 14 , 119 ]. Boy-oriented toys, in contrast, are rated higher on violence, competitiveness, aggressiveness, constructiveness, and risk  [ 14 , 57 , 119 , 190 ], and are more likely to support developing spatial skills  [ 14 , 165 ]. Additionally, toys focused on artistic activities and promoting children's academic skills are often considered neutral or moderately masculine  [ 14 ].

Introducing coding and computational thinking to young people under seven mainly happens through coding kits  [ 201 ], a critical development stage when young children consolidate their gender identities  [ 86 , 97 , 111 , 196 ]. Just like other toys, children may consider whether a coding kit is for or against their gender schemas, resulting in promoting or suppressing their interests in and actual engagement with the kit. Thus, it is necessary to examine the design features of coding kits for young children from a gender perspective so that future designs of coding kits can better welcome young learners with different gender identities.

2.3 Gender Differences in Computer Programming

Gender differences in computer programming have been studied for years due to the widely-recognized importance of addressing gender disparity issues in this traditionally masculine area  [ 167 , 173 ]. Generally, the quantity and quality of computational participation in girls are significantly lower than boys  [ 51 ]. As a result, girls usually have lower computer programming literacy, interests, and experience than boys (e.g.,  [ 63 , 173 ]). For example, Papavlasopoulou and colleagues  [ 134 ] found that, among all the participants in a coding workshop with children, most of the girl participants had little previous knowledge about what coding is and how to code with Scratch 1 , a block-based programming environment for children. Such lacking or limited computing-related literacy, interests, and experiences may further lead to girls’ lack of confidence in learning, practicing, and leading programming activities (e.g.,  [ 8 , 134 ]). These early-stage (in)experiences and conceptualizations about computing and computer programming can even negatively impact children's future college learning and career development (e.g., avoiding CS-related majors), finally resulting in women's under-representation in computing  [ 8 , 58 , 88 ].

Notably, gender stereotypes play a negative role in generating the current imbalanced participation in computing among different genders  [ 11 , 58 , 63 , 173 ]. For example, boys are often convinced that they have “technology superiority,” making them feel more comfortable with and confident about computer programming  [ 8 ]. These assumptions are further reinforced by boy-oriented features in many programming tools, such as coding motion and using icons with dark colors  [ 8 , 63 ]. Similarly, girls usually program stereotypical scenes or artifacts oriented toward girls in coding projects, such as houses, flowers, rainbows, unicorns, and Barbie dolls  [ 8 , 63 , 134 ], and are less interested in computing projects that incorporate boy-oriented features  [ 63 ]. Due to the negative impacts of gender stereotypes, such as undermining girls’ confidence and interest in computing  [ 167 ], scholarship suggests that starting computer programming in early childhood before “extreme gender stereotypes are ingrained”  [ 172 , 173 ] or designing gender-neutral features in programming environment  [ 63 ] could be ways to diminishing gender disparity in computing.

In summary, previous literature foregrounds the importance and urgency of addressing gender disparity in computer programming during early childhood. However, few studies have examined gender representations in children's programming environments  [ 63 ]. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no studies have yet investigated how gender-related design features are included in coding kits for young children. Without a comprehensive understanding of gender representations in coding kits, future designs may continue reinforcing negative stereotypes about computing in young people and exacerbating the already highly lopsided gender representation. Our study takes the first step by investigating the representation of gender-related design features in current coding kits and aims to provide guidelines for designing more gender-inclusive coding kits for young children.

3 METHODS

This section describes how we collected coding kits for young children, developed the analysis framework for gender-related design features from prior literature, and applied the framework for content analysis  [ 87 ] of gender-related design features in the curated kits.

3.1 Collecting Coding Kits

Coding kits include physical, hybrid, and virtual forms  [ 201 ] from the market or research labs. This study focuses on existing, commercially available kits because these kits are the ones children can access. We searched online markets (i.e., Amazon 2 , Target 3 , and Walmart 4 ) for physical and hybrid kits, and Google Play and Apple AppStore for virtual kits. Specifically, we employed the keywords “coding toys” and “programming toys” on Amazon, examined the toys under the pre-defined category “STEM Toys – Coding” on Target, and checked the kits classified as “STEM Toys – Technology – Coding toys” on Walmart. Regarding virtual kits, we used the keywords “coding apps for kids” and “programming apps for kids” on Google Play and Apple App Store. Note that search results on Apple App Store were endless, and new apps continued to emerge as we scrolled down the resulting interface. However, as we examined more apps, the newly appeared apps became less and less relevant to coding. Therefore, we stopped checking more apps after about 250 apps, the point we did not identify new coding apps. Additionally, we checked the Purdue Engineering Gift Guide 2021  [ 187 ], a collection of toys, games, books, and apps designed to promote engineering thinking and design in children ages 0-18. We looked into the toys labeled “Coding or Programming” and “Computational thinking” in the guide.

For the search results, we focused on coding kits designed for children ages seven and under. This age group was chosen because, as aforementioned, this early stage is a critical period for children to develop their gender schemas and form their gender conceptualizations  [ 111 , 181 , 196 ]. Also, many coding kits target young children of this age group [ 201 ]. We included a kit as long as the claimed users cover children ages seven and under. For example, Cubetto [ 40 ] is designed for children between 3-9 years old and therefore is included for analysis. Our other filtering criteria were informed by a previous survey of coding kits for young children from Yu & Roque [ 201 ]: the claimed learning goals of a kit are to support coding or computational thinking, and the descriptions and pictures of a kit are publicly available. Based on these inclusion criteria, we excluded kits without a specific recommended age range, and kits whose product information was unavailable.

In practice, the first author (A1) searched the target data sources in June 2022 and employed the inclusion criteria to screen the returned results. We created an Excel Sheet file to accumulate the kits A1 believed satisfied the inclusion criteria and categorized them into physical, virtual, and hybrid kits according to Yu & Roque's taxonomy  [ 201 ]. Some kits like Bee-Bot  [ 182 ] can be fully functional with only their physical parts, we still categorized them under hybrid kits since they include virtual applications for programming. A1 further checked these preliminarily curated kits and removed repetitive ones from different data sources. Through this process, we included 122 different kits: 58 physical kits, 30 virtual kits, and 34 hybrid kits. Then A3 examined these kits independently using the same filtering criteria and pointed out ambiguous kits (e.g., age groups and learning goals), which were discussed with A1 to double-check if the kits met our inclusion criteria. Through this second-round filtering, we excluded an additional 12 kits. We included 110 kits for the final analysis, consisting of 55 physical kits, 34 hybrid kits, and 21 virtual kits. Figure 2 shows our detailed processes of searching and filtering kits.

For each included kit, we collected product information from the kit website, product pages on its online shopping platforms, and/or YouTube (e.g., product introduction and tutorial videos) in the form of texts, images, and videos. For virtual kits, the first two authors split the apps and downloaded the assigned apps onto their mobile devices to experience the apps themselves, then collected data by screenshotting the kits’ play interfaces and writing fieldnotes of the supported activities, as a supplementary data source to the publicly available information from the apps’ developers, selling platforms, and relevant YouTube videos (if any). We also collected the apps’ icon images because an app's icon is the first design feature to be seen when one opens it. We put the collected and transcribed information (i.e., product name, link, text descriptions of components and supported activities, pictures, and screenshots) under each kit and organized the kits based on physical, virtual, and hybrid types into three Word documents for later analysis.

3.2 Analysis Framework: Gender-oriented Design Features in Toys

As mentioned in the related work, previous studies have examined many gender-related design features (e.g., colors, physical forms) in children's toys and labeled them as boy-oriented, girl-oriented, or gender-neutral (e.g.,  [ 57 , 119 , 199 ]. Drawing on these gender-related design features reported in the literature, we develop a framework of gender-oriented design features in toys (see Table 1) to structure our analysis of coding kits. The framework includes three dimensions – colors, physical forms, and activity types . In this section, we describe the analysis framework in detail.

Colors. Children show gender differences in their color preferences at 2-3 years old. For example, girls generally prefer pink while boys prefer blue  [ 137 , 196 , 199 ]. These gendered color preferences further influence children's toy preferences  [ 110 , 111 , 196 ]. Thus, colors can serve as reliable gender indicators in design. After examining relevant studies, we find that pastel and light colors (e.g., pink, purple, bright, or neon colors) are typically preferred by girls, while bold and dark colors (e.g., blue, black, grey, and brown) are generally favored by boys (e.g.,  [ 195 , 196 , 199 ]). Additionally, some colors like yellow and green are considered neutral  [ 199 ]. There are colors whose gender perceptions for young children are not revealed yet in literature and therefore not included in the analysis framework. For example, we did not find empirical evaluations directly with young children for the color red, although a few studies did evaluate adults’ or young adults’ perceptions (e.g.,  [ 33 , 68 , 73 ]).

Physical Forms. Another design feature linked to gendered preferences is a toy's physical form. Boy-oriented toys are typically associated with masculinity and take the forms of military equipment (e.g., guns and soldiers), construction blocks/bricks, vehicles (e.g., toy cars and trucks, garages, boats, kiddie cars, motorcycles, snowmobiles, trailers, trains, toy jet plane, pirate's ship), action figures (e.g., cowboys), sports equipment (e.g., balls, skates, kites), board games, or toys related to spatial and temporal concepts such as shape-sorting toys, clocks, magnets, outer-space toys (e.g.,  [ 14 , 23 , 74 , 119 , 126 , 152 , 158 ]). Additionally, bat-shaped and ghost-shaped icons were found in boys’ preferences  [ 63 ]. Girl-oriented toys, in contrast, are generally related to dolls (e.g., Barbie), domestic items (e.g., tea set, kitchen appliances, furniture, house), fashion and beauty (e.g., jewelry, clothing, clothing accessories), and stuffed animals (e.g.,  [ 14 , 119 ]). Adding to these physical forms, unicorn-shaped, rainbow, balloon-shaped, and donut-shaped icons were found frequently in the digital projects created by girls  [ 63 ]. Besides the dichotomy, some toy forms are categorized as gender-neutral, such as art materials and puzzles  [ 19 , 155 , 197 ].

Note that the gender categorization of some toy forms can be ambiguous mainly because of the different, even conflicting, findings in prior studies. For example, one study found that construction toys (e.g., LEGO) are gender-neutral [ 126 ], while other studies rate construction toys as masculine-oriented (e.g.,  [ 14 , 119 ]). After comparing these studies and group discussions (e.g., who were the participants), we classified construction toys as boy-oriented for our analysis. Additionally, board/table games (e.g., monopoly, magic boxes, chemistry boxes) are owned and played more by boys than girls and are therefore rated more towards boys  [ 126 ]. Lastly, the classification of animal toys is complex because their gender orientations are impacted by different factors (e.g., materials) and can be perceived as gender-neutral  [ 141 ], boy-oriented, or girl-oriented  [ 14 ]. Boys generally own more animal toys than girls  [ 152 ], and they prefer certain plastic toy animals more than girls, such as plastic dinosaurs, tigers, giraffes, and cheetahs  [ 14 , 21 ]. We therefore classify those specified plastic toy animals as boy-oriented and unspecified ones as “to-be-decided.” Simultaneously, stuffed animals are typically seen as more girl-oriented toys in multiple studies (e.g.,  [ 119 ]).

Activity Types. The toys’ physical forms further hint at their supported activities’ gender orientations. For example, vehicle toys, typically boy-oriented (e.g.,  [ 14 , 119 ]), engage children in controlling their motions, while dolls, a typically girl-oriented toy type (e.g.,  [ 14 , 119 ]), support children to do activities like role-playing and decorating. These supported activities of toys, like their forms, also indicate gender preferences (e.g.,  [ 14 , 119 ]). Briefly, the activities of boy-oriented toys mainly include controlling motions, competing with other players, and constructing artifacts (e.g., constructing machinery, and buildings, among others)  [ 14 , 119 , 158 ]. Whereas girl-oriented toys support interacting with dolls (e.g., nurturing, caring, feeding, decorating) and role play with domestic toys (e.g.,  [ 14 , 119 , 158 ]). Lastly, art and craft activities like drawing are typically rated gender-neutral  [ 155 ].

The Authors’ Positionalities. The first two authors are female, and A3 is male. All authors experienced negative gender stereotypes in their lives, a key reason that all authors felt the imperative to conduct the current research. As we developed the analysis framework, we were concerned about reinforcing the stereotypes about girls and boys. At the same time, all authors resonated with the gender-related orientations of different toy features pointed out in existing literature, especially connecting to their own grow-up experiences. Here, we want to emphasize that although certain design features may be preferred by the majority of children of a specific gender, there is no absolute line between which feature is for which gender. For example, some boys may like pink better than blue, and some girls may prefer playing with robots to dancing. Additionally, by classifying a design feature as oriented towards a specific gender, we mean that children of the gender tend to like that particular design feature more than children of the opposite gender, rather than that children of the opposite gender dislike that design feature. For instance, we classify board/table games as boy-oriented because boys tend to own and play with the games more than girls, which does not necessarily mean girls do not like this game type. In sum, the gendered preferences of different design features summarized in our analysis framework cannot be applied to all children. However, the framework is still valuable and meaningful for the current study because it captures common patterns about children's gender-related preferences for toy features.

3.3 Analyzing Kits

We performed deductive coding to examine the colors, physical forms, and activity types of the collected coding kits using the framework. As the first step, we randomly picked three coding kits from each category (i.e., physical, virtual, and hybrid kits, nine kits in total) and coded these kits (between A1 and A2) to test the operability of the analysis framework. Through this testing process, we surfaced some nuanced considerations when applying the framework. Specifically, we found that some kits include multiple colors, even more than ten in some virtual kits. As such, we decided to focus on the dominant colors in the major parts of a kit (e.g., game scenes, characters, and coding interfaces) and ignored non-prominent colors. Additionally, we noticed that the gendered categorizations of some kits’ physical forms and activities were not captured by our framework (Table 1), e.g., the gender orientations of Tale-Bot's  [ 117 ] physical form as a white square creature and the activity supported by Rami Code  [ 149 ] to sort colorful balls haven't been specified yet in literature. As such, we labeled design features beyond the scope of Table 1 as “to be decided” after group discussions. Finally, since virtual kits do not include physical forms, we analyzed the appearance forms of digital objects in these kits for the dimension of physical forms.

After confirming the framework's operation in analysis, we randomly selected another 12 kits (10% of the total, i.e., four physical, hybrid, and virtual kits, respectively) for inter-rater reliability (IRR) check. A1 and A2 coded the 12 kits’ design features independently following our analysis framework and achieved Cohen's Kappa score of 0.92 for the coding results, validating the effectiveness of the analysis framework and confirming the shared understanding of the framework's application in coding kits between the two coders. Then we started coding the remaining kits ( N = 89), which were split between A1 ( N = 59) and A2 ( N = 30). During this time, the research team met weekly to collectively review the coding progress and discuss any ambiguous issues that emerged, further ensuring the reliability of the coding results. Note that none of the authors are color-blinded.

4 FINDINGS

This section presents findings of current coding kits’ design features from a gender perspective. We structure the findings following our analysis framework: Colors, Physical Forms, and Activity Types. Additionally, many kits remix features of different gender orientations from multiple dimensions. Therefore, we added a section called “Cross-dimension Representations” to describe these cross-dimension combinations.

Additionally, 15 kits include red colors, among which one physical kit is primarily covered by red, a color whose gender orientation is missing in the literature for young children. Therefore, we labeled these kits’ red colors as to-be-decided.

Overall, the color patterns of physical and hybrid coding kits are heavily boy-oriented, such as blue, black, and dark brown, particularly hybrid kits whose color representations for girls like pink and purple are missing. Although such a lopsided color representation also exists in virtual kits, the function of color customization in many virtual kits, to some extent, balances their different gender representations.

All in all, girl-orientated physical forms are generally underrepresented in all kit types and almost missing in hybrid kits. In contrast, most kits include physical forms that may be favored by boys.

The gender differences in children's preference for these activities are unclear.

In short, boy-oriented activities dominate the examined kits, such as building artifact extensions for robots using bricks and controlling objects moving. Simultaneously, some kits also include girl-oriented and gender-neutral activities accompanying their boy-oriented activities, such as decorating a robot with artistic materials and drawing a robot's moving routes. Finally, some gender-unspecified activities are supported, like storytelling and project sharing.

5 DISCUSSION

Our findings provide insights into how gender-related features are (and are not) manifested in the designs of coding kits for young children through the dimensions of colors, physical forms, and activity types. In this section, we discuss our findings with a focus on the design gaps in current coding kits from a gender perspective. We then present the implications of our findings for designing more gender-inclusive coding kits for young children. Finally, we reflect on our analysis framework of gender-related design features in toys.

5.1 Gender Representations in the Designs of Current Coding Kits

We find that all kit types (i.e., physical, hybrid, and virtual kits) include two representation patterns of the three gender-related design dimensions used in our analysis (colors, physical forms, and activity types): single representations that only include features oriented toward one gender in a specific dimension, and mixed representations that combine features of different gender orientations in a specific dimension. The findings on color dimension show that many physical and virtual kits include mixed-representation colors in their designs, which can be attributed to physical kits’ features of including multiple different-color components (e.g.,  [ 123 ]) and virtual kits’ features of easily adding and customizing colors (e.g.,  [ 52 ]). Given the included girl-oriented and gender-neutral colors, kits with mixed-representation colors are generally diverse in their colors’ gender orientations. However, kits with single-representation colors are often dominated by boy-oriented colors like blue and dark colors and seldom decorated with girl-oriented colors (e.g., pink, purple, and pastel colors). For example, no hybrid kits exist in our pool whose colors are exclusively girl-oriented. Hence, girl-oriented colors are generally underrepresented in current designs of coding kits for young children.

Similarly, girl-oriented forms were not often observed in coding kits. Although some kits with mixed representations include girl-oriented forms, these forms are very limited in terms of variety, mainly presented as domestic icons  [ 69 ] and accessory decorative materials [ 84 ]. In contrast, boy-oriented physical forms not only dominate the collected kits but also include rich representations, such as in the forms of cars [ 82 ], weapons  [ 101 ], and spaceships  [ 55 ]. Additionally, most kits support controlling motions, but their movable parts take different forms, such as animal shapes, programmable cars, and various moveable digital items (e.g., action figures, cupcakes, and ice cream). These diverse representations of a kit's moveable part(s) may further complicate how young children perceive the gender orientations of vehicles and motion-control activities, which will be further elaborated on in the next paragraph. Also, the rich forms included in virtual kits can be explained by the affordances of being digital, i.e., more easily adding virtual items than building a physical object. However, the ease for virtual kits to include different forms and colors does not mean we encourage all coding kits to adopt virtual formats. Research shows that tangible coding interfaces are more engaging for children than screen-based interfaces  [ 67 ], even inviting more parental involvement during children's use of coding kits  [ 200 ]. Therefore, we recommend more coding kits to consider and include tangible forms in design, especially girl-oriented forms. Finally, the gender orientations of some kits’ physical forms could not be revealed or hinted at by our analysis framework (i.e., Table 1), such as robot-shaped kits or icons whose gender preferences have not been studied yet. Additional research will be required to more comprehensively investigate children's gender-related perceptions of different physical forms, especially given that new technologies for children are consistently emerging in this digital era, such as social robots that can interact and communicate with children  [ 99 ].

Regarding activity types, our findings reinforce previous findings that coding kits for young children support limited activity types, mainly controlling the motions of a robot or virtual character, storytelling around robot motions or digital animations, as well as art and craft activities like decorating a robot  [ 201 ]. Simultaneously, our study adds new insights by examining these activities’ gender orientations and highlighting that most kits include boy-oriented activities, i.e., controlling motions through coding. It is worth pointing out that we followed existing literature (e.g.,  [ 14 , 119 , 152 ]) to classify vehicle toys and their associated moving and motion control as boy-oriented activities. This preference for vehicles and motions could even be male's biological preferences  [ 9 ] and is also identified in male rhesus monkeys  [ 65 ]. However, motion control is inherently intertwined with coding kits for young children  [ 201 ], i.e., a fundamental feature of coding kits that is represented in various forms, such as moveable animals, cars, and robotics. Therefore, whether being a common quality of coding kits and represented in different physical forms will change young children's perceived gender orientations of motion control (or moveable vehicles) becomes a question – will coding kits’ features of vehicle formats and motion control still be perceived towards boy-oriented by young children, or changed to gender-neutral, even girl-oriented? Empirical evaluation with young children will be needed to answer this question, a critical research gap for future study. Lastly, we identified a few new activity types, such as dancing to robots’ motion  [ 84 ] and feeding animals  [ 102 ], which are more girl-oriented. These activities may not always relate to coding but serve as coding's accompanying activities. For instance, some virtual kits like Code Kingdom: Treasure [ 121 ] include the activity of feeding animals, which is irrelevant to coding and could be added for entertainment purposes. However, it is unclear how children's, particularly girls’, perceived gender orientations of these kits will be – or not be – impacted by such accompanying activities, which will need future investigation.

Notably, a coding kit may include design features of different gender orientations from multiple dimensions, complicating how a child perceives the kit's gender orientation. For example, some kits with boy-oriented physical forms and activities support adding physical extensions. These extension activities can be either boy-oriented (e.g., building a weapon extension to a car robot  [ 106 ], which further reinforces boy identity in the kit), girl-oriented (e.g., decorating a wheeled robot using craft materials, which adds girl-oriented representation  [ 84 ]), or gender-neutral (e.g., attaching a pen to a robot to draw its moving trajectories). However, our analysis followed a single dimension of design features (i.e., colors, physical forms, or activity types) and was unable to examine the compound effects on children's gender perceptions when design features of multiple dimensions interplay with each other. Importantly, a kit's single design dimension does not necessarily decide children's final perceptions of whether the kit matches their formed gender schemas  [ 94 , 111 , 189 , 199 ]. There are other influencing factors  [ 97 ], even factors beyond the scope of our analysis framework, such as a kit's materials and background story narratives (e.g.,  [ 78 ]). Overall, given that a kit design often mixes multiple design features of different gender orientations, empirical studies that engage children in assessing a kit's gender orientations will be needed to better understand the compound effects of such mixes in the future.

Our findings from a single-dimension analysis suggest that gender representation in terms of color, physical form, and activity type in children's coding kits is severely lopsided – that is, heavily dominated by boy-oriented design features. We call for more attention from technology designers, especially those designing STEM learning tools for children, to pay more attention to how design features like colors, physical forms, and activities that young girls may favor can be more included and supported.

5.2 Design Implications

In this section, we discuss two implications for designers’ consideration to increase gender inclusiveness in coding kits. We reveal that girl-oriented design features are mainly lacking in the examined coding kits. However, we are not arguing that future kits should only design for girls, but to include design features that represent different genders for young children's options.

Including Multi-dimensional Gender Representations. Our analysis reveals that many kits include mixed representations from multiple design features. Nevertheless, girl-oriented features are often presented through only one dimension (e.g., often colors) in a kit, while the kit's other design features remain boy-oriented. Although colors can increase children's interest in gender-atypical toys  [ 97 ], only applying gender-atypical colors is limited in fostering and maintaining girls’ interest in a coding kit since it mainly attracts children's attention rather than invoking their interest  [ 97 ]. The same may go for design features of other dimensions, such as physical forms. Therefore, we suggest including gender-oriented design features from multi-dimensions in one kit for a specific gender, especially girls. For example, a kit can incorporate colors preferred by girls and simultaneously consider other dimensions like physical forms and activities in girls’ preferences, e.g., dance and music  [ 63 ].

In addition, supporting children to customize a kit's design features is another effective way to achieve multi-dimensional gender representation, e.g., allowing children to find and create elements that align with their gender schemas. Some of the examined kits in this study already support customization, such as creating one's own digital assets in hybrid or virtual kits (e.g., ScratchJr [ 164 ]) or offering various combinations of colors and physical forms with different gender orientations (e.g., the kit Coding Critter series  [ 59 , 102 , 142 ]). Importantly, the dimensions included in our analysis (colors, physical forms, and activity types) are by no means exhaustive. There are other aspects impacting children's perception of a kit's gender orientations, such as its materials and textures  [ 119 ]. However, we still lack a comprehensive view of all gender-related design features, a research gap that needs to be filled soon. For any gender-unspecified design features, we encourage designers and developers to reflect within themselves and ask during their design practices, “will this design choice encourage or discourage children of a specific gender?”

Designing for Meaningful Combinations. Our findings show that girl-oriented activities are insufficient and often subsidiary. For instance, girl-oriented activities in some kits are not connected to coding, e.g., simply feeding animals [ 102 , 121 ]. However, coding-free activities, although rated as girl-oriented, may not effectively contribute to increasing girls’ interests in coding and enhancing girl representation in computing as both coding-related participation quantity and quality are inadequate  [ 51 ]. We recommend that future kit designs expand girl-oriented activities through a meaningful combination with coding, i.e., contributing to coding participation rather than being irrelevant to coding. For example, some physical kits (e.g., My First Coding and Computer Kit [ 140 ] and Coding Lab [ 138 ]) embed computational concepts into decorative activities instead of simply bringing the two activities together  [ 138 ].

5.3 Applications of the Gender Framework for Design Features and Its Limitations

We developed a framework of gender-related design features in toys from literature and applied it to examine gender representations in current coding kit designs. The framework includes three dimensions: colors, physical forms, and activity types. Analyzing the 110 collected coding kits of different types, we found that our framework's three dimensions can capture key gender-related design features. Given that this analytical framework is developed from prior literature on young children's gender perceptions of various toys’ design features (e.g., stuffed animals, building bricks, and car toys), it can be applied beyond the context of coding kits to other types of children's toys and learning technologies. On the one hand, the framework can be employed by relevant researchers, designers, and practitioners to examine gendered design features of existing toys and learning technologies through the lenses of colors, physical forms, and activity types, just as what was done in the current study. For example, toy companies can inspect if their products’ colors and physical forms align with the gender preferences of their target child users using our framework. The analysis results can reveal gender gaps in the products’ current design and provide strategies for improvement, such as enriching the colors or changing the physical forms of a toy. On the other hand, our framework can serve as a design reference to inform the development of gender-inclusive learning technologies and toys for young children. Specifically, designers can use the framework as a gender-representation checklist to choose design features that welcome children of a specific gender or to check whether their design decisions may exclude children of a particular gender. For instance, when designing learning technologies aimed at young girls, designers may want to increase girl-oriented colors like pink and pastel colors rather than only dark and bold colors. They may also want to consider creating or adding activities in the framework that are oriented towards girls that otherwise would not catch designers’ attention, such as nurturing- and caring-oriented activities.

Note that our framework is not comprehensive and should be used critically rather than as a doctrine. First, the gender orientations of many design features remain unclear. For example, we identified design features beyond the framework's scope, i.e., design features whose gender orientations for young children have not yet been explored and specified in the literature. Additionally, language-related features (e.g., descriptions on the coding kits’ packages) also deliver gender representations  [ 131 ] and influence the communication efficacy of the kits’ gender orientations  [ 136 ]. The current framework can be further extended to include those unspecified features once relevant research results are available. Second, the framework did not capture the compound effects on children's perceived gender orientations of a toy when design features of different gender orientations mix. As the findings of Cross-dimension Representations show, it is not uncommon that design features of varying gender orientations co-exist in one coding kit, e.g., including girl-oriented physical forms with boy-oriented colors. Such mixed representations will impact children's overall perceived gender orientation of a toy  [ 57 ]. Nevertheless, how different design features interplay with each other and influence young children's final gender perceptions of the toy remain unclear, which needs to be further examined in future studies. Third, the gender orientations of specific design features are not static but a dynamic process depending on the changes in societal norms or sociocultural environments. For example, pink was sometimes considered a more masculine color, conveying the meaning of being strong and decisive, while blue was considered a feminine color about being delicate and dainty in the early 1900s  [ 133 , 196 ]. However, their meanings have entirely reversed in contemporary society. Plus, relevant studies were mainly conducted decades ago (e.g.,  [ 119 , 152 ]), while new technologies and innovative designs are consistently emerging, such as toys combining physical and digital forms like the Frozen kit [ 78 ] whose gender orientations remain to be explored. Overall, gender-related design frameworks are time-sensitive and should be consistently verified and updated in response to changes in societal norms and cultures around genders. Practically, for design features whose gender orientations are unspecified in literature, we highly recommend designers test with their young target users to figure out children's real-world gender perceptions before incorporating that specific feature in the design. This can be achieved through various evaluation methods and tools, such as inviting target young children to rate a feature's gender orientation using Likert scale questionnaires  [ 127 ], interviewing young children to learn their preferences, or observing their toy choices. All in all, our framework provides the first important step towards the development of guidelines for gender-related design features in toys and learning technologies for children. We will continue to evaluate new design features and expand the framework in our future work. Simultaneously, we call for more researchers and designers to join this force.

6 Limitations & Future Work

First, our analysis was structured by gender-related toy design features identified in literature rather than evaluating children's real perceptions of coding kits. However, our findings are still valuable for frontline designers and practitioners as the analysis dimensions we used have already been verified by previous studies. Second, we were unable to analyze a kit's overall gender orientations when design features of multiple dimensions interplay with each other because 1) no existing research has provided an operational approach to assessing how mixed representations of design features decide children's final gender perceptions of a toy, and 2) we did not involve children in our assessment process. Third, our collected coding kits may not be exhaustive because the search results on shopping platforms may vary across periods due to stock availability and other reasons. Nonetheless, our kit pool ( N = 110 across physical, virtual, and hybrid kits) is representative enough for us to reveal the design patterns in current coding kits. In our future work, we plan to invite young children to assess the gender orientations of some representative coding kits, mainly focusing on design features whose gender perceptions remain unclear and the compound effects of mixed representations on children's overall gender perceptions, the findings of which will complement and expand those reported in this paper.

7 CONCLUSION

We collected 110 coding kits targeting young children aged seven and under and examined their design features from a gender perspective using a framework of gender-related design features in toys summarized from literature. The findings reveal an unbalanced gender representation in coding kit designs regarding colors, physical forms, and activity types – the application of boy-oriented design elements far outweighs girl-oriented design features. According to the results, we discuss implications for coding kit designers to make the design of coding kits more gender inclusive for young children, especially young girls whose preferred gender-oriented features are particularly underrepresented in current designs. Ultimately, we hope more designers of learning technologies for children can consider the gender implications of their design choices and create more inclusive learning opportunities for all.
