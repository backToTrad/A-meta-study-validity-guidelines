Following along how-to videos requires alternating focus between understanding procedural video instructions and performing them. Examining how to support these continuous context switches for the user has been largely unexplored. In this paper, we describe a user study with thirty participants who performed an hour-long cooking task while interacting with a wizard-of-oz hands-free interactive system that is aware of both their cooking progress and environment contexts. Through analysis of the session scripts, we identify a dichotomy between participant query differences and workflow alignment similarities, under-studied interactions that require AI functionality beyond video navigation alone, and queries that call for multimodal sensing of a user's environment. By understanding the assistant experience through the participants’ interactions, we identify design implications for a smart assistant that can discern a user's task completion flow and personal characteristics, accommodate requests within and external to the task domain, and support nonvoice-based queries.

1 INTRODUCTION

Millions of hours of how-to videos are hosted on video creation and sharing platforms (e.g., YouTube, Instructables) with ever increasing numbers of new content added each year [ 54 ]. However, users who wish to replicate a task by following a video (user task alignment) may find juggling between task performance and video navigation overwhelming, as this requires alternating focus between the primary context of completing a task and the secondary context of parsing video instructions. Consequently, there has been growing interest in what we refer to as hands-free interactive AI systems (HFIAI) to ease and enhance the process of following along video instructions to complete a task (e.g., cooking [ 13 ] and fitness [ 21 ]). Many of these systems enable users to search within a video or navigate a video to timestamps that will allow them to complete subtasks, but they have mostly been designed and evaluated without awareness of a user's context (e.g., their environment and task progress) while completing the shown assignment.

Prior work could be broken down into two main categories: (1) video question-answering (QA) [ 11 , 36 , 55 ], where the system answers the questions a user asks when watching a procedural video, or (2) video manipulation [ 13 ], where the system navigates the video timeline per user request. Researchers have examined both QA and video manipulation systems in a multitude of domains (e.g., movies [ 56 ], TV shows [ 22 ]), including cooking [ 13 ]. Cooking not only requires dexterous manipulation, but also demands users to keep track of multiple temporal and environmental contexts (e.g., ingredient preparation, cooking time, visual appearance of the prepared meal) [ 13 , 30 , 53 ]. These cognitive processes can also be seen in a number of other types of procedural tasks, such as applying makeup, assembling furniture, and improving home projects [ 13 ]. However, cooking also requires near real-time alignment to procedural instructions as the process often contains timing constraints to maintain final product quality (e.g., cooking ingredients for exact amounts of time to preserve similar degrees of doneness). For these reasons, many studies have used cooking as a archetypal task to develop design implications and evaluate hands-free interactive systems.

To understand how context aware technologies support user task alignment to procedural instructions, we conduct a wizard-of-oz user study where participants were asked to perform a long, complex activity (e.g., cooking) guided by an online how-to video. Researchers emulated an ideal HFIAI system that could provide the participant any relevant functionality to help them complete steps (e.g., video navigation, cooking reminders, answering questions about ingredient amounts, etc.). While many studies usually abridged the cooking task to evaluate navigation queries efficiently (e.g., up to 90 seconds [ 13 ]), we chose to have the participants in our study perform the procedure in its entirety. From ingredient preparation to plating, we recorded every real-time query and action while they cooked alongside the how-to video. This enabled us to examine the relationships between a participant's task progress and interaction initiation patterns. We would also be able to extract when user challenges occurred during the work flow. Through analysis on the recorded queries from every cooking session, we identify what types of interactions participants have with the system, when these interactions occur, and why.

These findings reveal a dichotomy between participant query differences and workflow similarities. For example, most participants initiated queries around the same time within each cooking step when completing the task. However, the semantics of each query differed based on a participant's cooking expertise, task completion patterns, and assistant needs. Furthermore, we found that while the queries in this study often required advanced natural language understanding capabilities, many of them also demanded functionality beyond what could be provided by an procedural video's metadata (e.g., audio, video frames, video description). These functionalities called for additional features from search engine-like capabilities to multimodal sensors such as cameras and environmental sound detectors. With these findings, we identify (1) certain groups of participant characteristics and queries for alignment timings that could be leveraged to understand user context, (2) calls for domain and nondomain specific requirements in query responses, and (3) the many interactions that require complex understandings outside of the video metadata that can help users align their task with the procedural instructions. We then provide corresponding design implications for future multimodal HFIAI system designs.

2 RELATED WORK

In recent years, many researchers, especially those in the AI, natural language processing, and computer vision community, have been interested in developing hands-free interactive systems for how-to videos. HFIAIs are particularly useful for users who wish to gain more detail while following a particular task procedure (e.g., recipe steps) to reach a goal (e.g., cooking a meal) [ 4 , 13 , 36 , 64 ]. These assistant interactions have been envisioned as mainly question-and-answer based, where a user asks a question (e.g., "how much sugar is needed?"), and the assistant replies with an answer parsed from the video or recipe metadata (e.g., "1 cup") [ 13 ]. Therefore, most prior work focuses on automating manual annotations of video content [ 11 , 38 , 40 ], creating domain-specific datasets of question-and-answers [ 22 , 30 , 46 , 56 ], developing natural-language-based question-answering systems [ 36 ], and building complex architectures to meet data requirements [ 25 , 26 ]. However, little work has been done to identify design needs from one such system when the assistant supports the user task alignment process by being both context and environment aware. In the sections below, we describe prior work in the three main components that make up the current assistant systems: video control and navigation, examining interactions with procedural (tutorial, how-to) videos, and finally, designing voice-based assistants.

2.1 Video control and navigation

One of the most researched and common voice-based assistant tasks for how-to videos is video control and navigation. The most common sub-tasks are repeating instructions, skipping content, and jumping around a video [ 14 ]. The HCI community has explored and developed a variety of navigation and browsing techniques to accomplish these sub-tasks. Swift [ 25 ] and Swifter [ 26 ] improved video scrubbing interfaces by incorporating pre-cached thumbnails into the video timeline to provide a concise, straightforward preview of the contents. Similarly, Panopticon [ 57 ] repeatedly animates video thumbnails in a grid while preserving the temporal layout to provide users with the full context of a video even in resource-constrained environments. Other video players like SceneSkim [ 29 ] and Video Lens [ 27 ] leverage additional information, like movie scripts and captions and video collections metadata, respectively, to improve the efficacy of video query tasks. Dragicevic et al. contend that for visual content query tasks, direct video navigation via dragging is more suitable than manipulating timeline interfaces [ 6 ].

Manual video control becomes more difficult when users need to navigate how-to videos that require them to perform tasks with both hands [ 13 , 36 , 64 ]. Natural language-based navigation would free users from having to focus on both the task at hand and controlling the how-to video. Voice-based video control strategies include temporal navigation via commands such as ‘fast forward 20 seconds’ and content-based navigation, which retrieves a specific moment in a video, but the latter has been much less explored [ 13 , 14 , 55 ]. Content-based navigation is more complex than pure object identification. A study on video browsing strategies has found that object identification requires localized attention, whereas video understanding requires global attention [ 17 ]. Zhao et al. [ 67 ] explored both time-based and content-based navigation commands, focused on improving voice-based video control, and indicated the need for context aware assistants. Extending on their findings, we investigate not only how to assist users with the secondary task of navigating how-to videos but also with the global task at hand. By expanding the scope of the HFIAI assistant mentioned in Zhao et al. [ 67 ] work, we reveal features that HFIAI may use to build context and task aware HFIAIs.

2.2 Interacting with how-to videos

Prior research in interactions with how-to videos has investigated systems that primarily assist with static tasks (e.g., learning an academic concept or using a software application), alleviating the need to consider how the dynamic context of a user completing a task might affect their usage of the system. ToolScape is an educational video browsing interface with a storyboard summarization and an interactive timeline that empowers learners to quickly filter, scan, and review several videos without needing to play them in their entirety [ 32 ]. Video Digests [ 28 ] and VideoDoc [ 33 ] provide textual summaries and textbook transcripts, respectively, alongside lecture videos to access the advantages of transcript-based interactions. LectureScape leverages a combination of large-scale user interaction traces and text and visual analysis to augment video timelines with more descriptive and meaningful navigation points [ 20 ]. Zhang et al. [ 65 ] have developed Smart Jump, which suggests the best position for a jump-back in Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs) based on users’ historical data of jump-backs and navigation.

Regarding the systems designed for software tutorial videos, Nguyen and Liu [ 47 ] have found that by interacting with a software application directly through a video, users would carry out the tasks at hand more effectively. Pause-and-play is a system that identifies important events in a tutorial video and connects them to the corresponding components of the target software application, and the system uses these connections to automatically pause and play the video to stay in sync with the user activity in the application [ 5 ]. FollowUs [ 24 ] and Wang et al. [ 35 ] both leverage community-generated videos for software tutorials to improve the user learning experience by providing demonstrations of each step and workflow recommendations, respectively. We extend this line of research by investigating user interactions with procedural cooking tutorial videos that require moving and working hands-on with physical objects in the kitchen. By asking participants to cook an entire dish while following along with a cooking video of their choice, we access a great variety of actions (via cooking techniques) and objects (via ingredients) in a real-world setting.

We also observe participants in real time as they alternated focus between the primary context for completing tasks and the secondary context for understanding video instructions. Of note, there exists a mature field of supporting alternating tasks through static graphical instructions. Many of this work has been done to assist in order picking [ 42 ], driving [ 68 ], and flying tasks [ 62 ] that require guidance that maximizes speed and accuracy while minimizing cognitive and physical load. These instructional interfaces often lack the ability to provide additional clarifications outside of the predetermined information. On the other hand, previous interfaces that support details beyond prearranged instruction usually do not account for user performance, including their task progress and environment state. However, cooking tasks frequently have temporal and precision constraints as individuals navigate their complex environments to ensure that steps are completed in a fixed allotted time. As follows, we designed a study to understand how and when context aware support helps users as they alternate between tasks.

2.3 Designing voice-based interfaces

Most voice-based user interfaces in the market have been embedded into everyday life via conversational assistant devices [ 51 , 52 ]. Despite the steady growth of voice interaction research, recent work has demonstrated that many problems remain unresolved regarding voice interface design. To improve the usability of voice interfaces, it is vital to address challenges beyond natural language-related errors like mishearing or misunderstanding users [ 15 , 63 ]. Previous studies have also hinted at relatively less straightforward issues such as balancing expressiveness and efficiency in assistant answers [ 61 ] and improving the discoverability of available commands and learnability of system limits and capabilities [ 10 , 16 , 43 , 44 , 50 ].

Current interactions with voice interfaces for video and audio control remain heavily transactional. They primarily support functionalities like basic content playback controls, keyword-based commands, and question-and-answers [ 34 ]. A wizard-of-oz study found that nearly one-fourth of all user-assistant exchanges arise from implicit conversational cues rather than explicit questioning, but for conversational agents designed to provide task support, user input is rather limited to explicit queries, keywords, or commands [ 60 ]. Chang et al. [ 14 ] corroborated the findings of Vtyurina and Fourney [ 60 ] and further highlighted context awareness as a potential solution to accurately interpret explicit commands from conversational cues. Rather than re-iterating the importance of understanding context, we extend this line of research by investigating several concrete approaches to empower the design of context aware assistants. We also introduce several other layers of understanding that are crucial to the effectiveness of a voice-based smart assistant.

Following these wizard-of-oz study designs, we describe a user study conducted with 30 participants who performed a roughly hour-long cooking task while interacting with an emulated HFIAI assistant. We had them initiate any and all interactions they felt were relevant to their task performance. As participants asked the context and environment aware HFIAI to guide them with instruction understanding and task progression, we sought to identify design recommendations for systems to have context awareness and abilities to support conversational queries and assist with mobile, procedural task alignment. Our analysis reveals gaps between how current voice assistants are designed for certain tasks (e.g., cooking) and the needs of real users. We not only describe the types of queries participants initiate and when, but also features that require a smart assistant design to go beyond accounting for advanced natural language queries.

3 METHODS

We conducted a study in which participants were given the opportunity to interact with a context aware HFIAI assistant while following a cooking video. We observed them for the full duration of the task to gain an understanding of how they take advantage of the assistant to help with context switches between watching the video and physical cooking. The Research Ethics Board at the University of Toronto approved this study protocol.

3.1 Participants

We recruited individuals in the United States and Canada through social network email chains, social media interest groups, and workspaces for cooking and snowball sampling. We then enrolled 30 participants who were average to intermediate cooks with varying familiarity following how-to videos and interacting with smart assistants as seen in Table 1 . Every participant received a compensation of 20 CAD.

3.2 Test dishes and videos

Researchers picked one of the five dishes from the demographics form for each participant to recreate in their individual cooking sessions. We limited the number of dishes that participants could choose from to ensure a level of consistency in cooking between participants, such as similar preparation time and number of cooking steps. For example, all recipes chosen required chopping board for ingredient preparation and stove top for cooking. In an attempt to better incite questions and encourage interactions, we chose the dish participants ranked simultaneously as the least familiar and most interesting among the five. If there was a tie, participants were given the choice to choose a tiebreaker. To further inspire a variety of interactions, they were allowed to choose the online video recipe on video sharing websites such as YouTube. The only limitations on the video were that it had a list of ingredients and their amounts, step-by-step instructions for the main recipe preparation process, such as mise-en-place and seasoning, a recipe duration that did not exceed one hour and the participant had never watched it before.

3.3 Procedures

To understand how participants interact with a cooking HFIAI in realistic settings, we conducted a wizard-of-oz user study in which they were asked to follow an online video recipe while performing a procedural cooking task. Before each study, participants were asked to fill out a demographics form. The form covered questions about their background, such as their age and vocation, and cooking history, such as how often they cook and cuisines they are most familiar with. At the end of the form, participants were provided five dishes to rank based on their familiarity with the dish, familiarity with cooking the dish, and interest in the dish. Researchers then used these rankings to select the recipe from one of the five dishes for participants to make, and they chose their own recipe videos to follow.

Participants were asked to view the video only once two to three days before the cooking session to situate themselves with the steps of the recipe without memorizing them. In this way, participants might focus less on discovering future video content (e.g., like participants in Zhao et al. [ 67 ] study did with a video that they were viewing for the first time) and more on exploring commands that help them complete the task. Before the cooking session, they were provided with the ingredients needed to complete the recipe either through grocery delivery services or reimbursement. Participants were asked not to prepare any ingredients (e.g., slice, cook, wash) prior to the study session as mise-en-place was a common step within all recipes. Each study session lasted around one hour. After each cooking session, participants also filled out a survey about their prior cooking experiences, opinions on using the assistant while cooking, and descriptions of how they would set up the HFIAI system (e.g., written, visual, audio components).

During the study session, participants joined a video conference call with the research team from their home kitchen. In the video conference meeting, the research team 1) provided participants with access to the online video recipe via screen sharing, 2) conducted a wizard-of-oz study using the shared video, and 3) recorded all interactions and queries instigated by the participants while cooking. Both user video and audio were collected by each recording, which captured not only all the interactions but also any utterances and sounds made by the participant (e.g., conversations with other people around them, background noises, talking to themselves). A mock study interface is shown in Figure 1 . While following the video recipe, participants interacted with a researcher emulating an ideal HFIAI. We defined this ideal HFIAI as one that could provide any relevant functionality that they wanted while cooking. They were encouraged to explore the assistant interface as much and as relevant as possible while cooking. At the start of each session, participants were also given example queries from past work that the HFIAI could fulfill, such as video manipulation and ingredient amounts [ 13 ]. We chose to do a wizard-of-oz study so that we could understand the needs of a user without current technological restraints (e.g., inaccurate computer vision) and support complex language and environment understandings. In turn, we may understand design implications to inform future designs. There was no deception used in the study as participants were made aware that a researcher would be emulating the ideal HFIAI.

3.4 Analysis

After the study, two researchers who were involved with the data collection reviewed and performed open-coding independently on the study session transcripts and open-ended survey questions line-by-line using thematic analysis [ 9 ]. We then calculated percentages for all non-open ended questions on the post cooking session survey. Through this initial analysis, key characteristics and frequent themes, such as effect of cooking expertise and cooking style, started to reveal themselves as distinct clusters of user characteristics . Using human-centered design methods [ 7 , 45 ], the two lead researchers then collaboratively built user personas and features for users of the cooking HFIAI until consensus was reached. Examples of themes that surfaced included "asking to replay video near end of cooking step", "non-domain specific query answers", and "interactions that require multimodal sensing".

4 RESULTS

4.1 Flow of interactions summary

Average video lengths, preparation times, and interaction frequencies did not differ significantly across dishes as shown in Table 2 . Each cooking video averaged 6m36s with a range of 3m21s minutes to 14m55s. Each cooking session averaged around an hour with the shortest around 26 minutes and longest session around 2 hours. There were 22 different cooking videos from 20 different cooking channels. On average, participants had 52 (min: 17, max: 124) interactions with the HFIAI during a session. No two cooking sessions were the same. Yet, almost all of the participants (N = 28) initiated queries aligned to each new cooking step in a similar fashion as shown in Figure 2 . We have included a breakdown of the interaction distribution for each individual in Figure 4 . Their descriptions are delineated in Table 3 .

Participants would start the video, watch a step in its entirety (e.g., finish chopping of an onion), and then ask to pause. The pause came almost always after a step was finished and up to a few seconds into the next step. One exception would be if the video contained a list of written ingredients as a video frame. In that case, they would pause there and treat that as a reference point to repeatedly go back to and watch. Then, while the video is paused, the participant might go grab ingredients and utensils. When they came back, they would either ask to re-watch the step to re-align themselves for the method they are about to perform or continue pausing. If participants already had materials nearby, this is when they would start asking for details about the method they are about to perform. These questions ranged from repetition of ingredient amounts to preparation tips for the correct technique. After the initial questioning, participants would start the method and then they might ask to rewind to the beginning of that method to re-watch for confirmation again. At that point, they would follow the "play" flow from the beginning again. If they are about to complete the method or have memorized how to complete the current method, they will ask about what to do next and/or to start playing the next step. Participants almost always asked to watch the next step before they are about to finish their current step as a means to anticipate what is to come, not break their cooking flow, and to re-align themselves with the video procedures. From there, the "play" process repeats again.

Although the preparation of ingredients and the cooking on the stove did not have drastically different progressions, there were some differences. These would include asking for cooking tips, temperature, timing, etc. questions in lieu of the ingredient amount and preparation questions. Adding oil to the pan was almost always the transition step between the preparation of the ingredients and cooking on the stove. One of the biggest differences in the stove top period was the waiting time. While ingredient preparation was almost all active hands-on time, stove top cooking often required participants to wait for their dish to develop. During this time, some of them asked the HFIAI to help them fill in the time with things to do. After the last waiting period is over, they finished cooking and plated their dish.

In other words, participants used a variety of interactions to re-align their current task state to the how-to video. Commands that manipulate the video timeline as well as establish reference points were used as procedural alignment to allow users to modify their environment to replicate video content and adjust the video instructions to parallel their task progress. Asset alignment queries, such as ingredients and utensils to gather, allowed participants to obtain the materials they needed to match those shown in instructional steps. On the other hand, step alignment interactions (e.g., ingredient prep tips, cooking clarifications, temperature) centered around ensuring high performance during each individual cooking step to guarantee a quality final dish. When participants wished to prepare for upcoming tasks, they asked questions like "what next" to pre-align and anticipate future contexts. 4.2 Personalizing HFIAI to users’ task alignment methods

Based on the types of interactions, preferred alignment strategies, preferred system designs (e.g., screen with visual instructions and voice assist), and cooking backgrounds of the participants, we found that patterns of different groups of interactions and intentions emerge. We grouped these patterns of behavior into three user personas (Figure 5 ): the “progressor”, the “multitasker”, and the “engager.” The breakdown of interactions throughout the cooking session (color-coded based on interaction types) for each user persona is shown in Figure 6 . Furthermore, Figure 4 organizes the distribution of interaction frequency for all participants by user persona type to visualize similarities and differences amongst and between different persona groups.

4.2.1 Rosa: the progressor. Rosa (the progressor) is a semi-novice cook. She has only a rudimentary understanding of cooking methods and usually only makes a meal once or twice a week. The recipes she makes are usually restricted to the same cuisine, but she does seek out a new cuisine once in a while. Her cooking process includes following steps in a linear fashion and usually only begins a new step after the current step is completed. Her interactions are primarily driven by video manipulation commands such as "play" and "pause". She heavily prioritizes the procedural alignment strategy to ensure that her progress is parallel to what she sees in the video. She feels slightly overwhelmed and high cognitive load due to her unfamiliarity with the task environment and the task itself. Thus, she is more likely to ask the HFIAI to navigate videos using estimated timestamps rather than remember explicit steps from the video. For example, she would say "go back 15 seconds" in lieu of "go back to the cutting of the onion." Actions such as "play" and "pause" also help Rosa keep track of where she is in the process. Before beginning a task step, Rosa will watch the instructions for that step. She will say "pause" right as the next step is about to begin. This pause will allow her to bookmark the step that she is about to do and complete. What drives her interactions center around understanding how to complete the task as described. Thus, if she feels like the video speeds through a step too quickly, she will ask for multiple rewatches and rarely asks any questions besides clarification of content provided by the video. To avoid keeping track of too much information at once, she prefers a system that relays visual information (e..g, video recipe) on a screen and audio-based assistant to provide hands-off instructions.

"Using the assistant with a video and written recipe would be a bit of an information overload." (P27)

4.2.2 Vivian: the multitasker. Vivian is in many ways the opposite of Rosa. Vivian is a more experienced chef with an established baseline of cooking techniques. She cooks multiple times a week and will seek out new recipes to try in different types of cuisines. She prioritizes cooking efficiency and will attempt to interweave steps together. She wants to minimize downtime by using that to progress the task instead. Thus, she is more likely to ask about upcoming steps moments earlier than Rosa. She will also move around the video content and prefers having as much information available to her as possible. As follows, she prefers a system that shows the written recipe and video recipe at the same time in a split-screen fashion and also enables voice-based assistant capabilities.

"Having all 3, cooking video, voice assistant, and recipe, would be the best because I would have the most information available. And using only the voice assistant would be the worst because as said before it would probably be best for me to have some form of visual aid." (P28)

When she initiates queries towards the end of her current task, she will ask to watch multiple steps (2-3 steps instead of just 1 (Rosa)) at once so that she can start planning for those steps in the back of her mind as well. A common question that she poses would be "what can I do while X is happening?" Perfect task performance would not involve waiting time. Thus, Vivian frequently leverages the pre-alignment strategy to not only ensure that she is ready to move on to the immediate next step, but also check whether and how she can spend any downtime she may have on preparing for future steps well ahead in time. While items are cooking, she is preparing ingredients for the next step. When ingredients are all added, she is cleaning up the kitchen. Her interactions are largely dictated by a need to plan out her time wisely as well as anticipate areas where she has failed before. This is where she uses the step alignment strategy to her advantage to help ensure high performance during each step, especially those that seem complicated, confusing, and/or personally challenging. For example, Vivian will also often ask many detailed questions about technique and ingredients especially around more complex and fast-paced steps. These are motivated by times in which she had found similar steps to be difficult or have failed to complete the step desirably in the past.

"[While a pepper mixture is cooking on the stove] Let's assume that pepper stuff is done, what is the next step" (P3)

"If I leave the guanciale like that now while waiting for the water, will the fat that was rendered become like hard, so it will become more solid? Do we need to later keep it on the heat? Like how can i keep it so that it doesn't go hard but also not burn" (P27)

4.2.3 Emerson: the engager. Emerson, like Vivian, is also interested in multitasking. However, instead of trying to balance different steps within the cooking task, he is interested in stimulation outside of the main task. He is social, talkative, and seeks multimodal incitations. His preferred system is also described as an HFIAI that combines visual video instructions with a voice-based assistant. He prioritizes engaging most of his senses.

"Augmenting the voice assistant with the cooking video I believe is having the best of 2 worlds. It will engage my vision and audio senses at the same time and I believe would make more efficient use of my time for making the dish...I want the ability to have fun conversations [with the assistant]." (P2)

"I would use the assistant to randomly talk at me in future cooking scenarios." (P20)

He cooks at a slower pace and mostly for pleasure. He will only do upmost two steps at once and usually allows for downtime. This does not imply that he is interested in silent waiting. To fill time, rather than trying to pre-align as much as Vivian, Emerson instead initiates interactions that don't really focus on the progress of his task or its alignment with the video content. When he feels like he understands the instructions of the current step and even nears the end of his current task, he will start asking the HFIAI to provide him with padding interactions before the next step. Padding interactions include asking the HFIAI to play music, put on a TV show, and even questions about the HFIAI itself (e.g., purpose, age, name).

"I think I'm going to get bored, can you play some nice music for me please?" (P2)

"How are you doing today. What mood, are you in today." (P2)

The most common padding interaction includes fun facts about ingredients, the preparation method, the recipe, and the type of cuisine.

"Do you know the history of omurice like when was it created." (P13)

4.2.4 Similarities across personas. As it stands, even though the semantics and motivations were different between groups of participants, patterns of how queries are incorporated into the task completion process for task alignment were very similar across users. As a semantics example, the multitasker may say "go back to the chopping of the onions" while the progressor says "go back 15 seconds" during procedural alignment, both after they ask clarifying questions for step alignment (e.g., "how many onions") as a means to "rewind and repeat visually" the ingredient preparation method that they need to complete at the current step. As a motivation example, both the multitasker and the engager will ask for things to do while waiting. They may even word the interaction in the exact same way, such as "what can I do while I wait." However, the multitasker would want the assistant to provide them with another recipe step to begin next, while the engager would want a source of entertainment outside of the cooking task (e.g., TV show, fun fact, music). In addition, most participants from all persona types said that they would not prefer to use a system that only had one modality (e.g., was just visual based like written, was just audio-based like voice assistant). They stated that each modality by itself would lack something that the other modalities could provide such as visuals to compare to for written only, hands-free navigation for video only, and quick references for voice only.

"The voice assistant is very useful, but sometimes you just want to take a quick glance at something to remind yourself of an amount or something and having to ask for it each time can be annoying, so having a visual reference is important. It is also important to have a reference to see if your cooking looks like the videos and a voice could not give you that." (P16)

"While the voice assistant was helpful, I only think it would help if it was paired with a display that would show me the cooking video." (P28)

4.3 Accounting for user alignment through augmented context understandings

Many interactions with HFIAI required information that could not be parsed from video content and metadata alone (e.g., audio content of the video, screen text, video visuals, captions, and/or descriptions). These often required the HFIAI to be context and environment aware of the participant's task completion progress. These interactions spanned multiple alignment strategies and required task context and: (1) video content comprehension, (2) domain comprehension, and (3) multimodal environment awareness. Almost all participants (N=29) had at least one, but usually multiple, of these interactions during the study.

4.3.1 Task context and video content comprehension. While completing the cooking task, the participants wanted to anticipate what they would need to do in the future while completing their current step. Across all of the sessions, we had more than 61 pre-alignment commands (e.g.,“what is next") to achieve procedural alignment. "Next" could be the next ingredient that they will need to prepare or add. On the other hand, "next" could also mean the next cooking step and/or method that immediately follows their current as well as remaining steps within the recipe that have yet to be started. Oftentimes, participants (N=22) expected the HFIAI to know what they were doing at all times, so they could get a clear answer for how to proceed even if the query had ambiguous language such as those in Figure 4 . Other times, while participants (N=8) expressed that verbalizing their current actions and thoughts during the task were mainly for themselves, a few expected the HFIAI to have taken note of their speech and interpret a few as implicit commands. P27 was in a middle of a task when she asked the HFIAI "It hasn't been 10 minutes yet, right?". Before this interaction, she had not asked the HFIAI to set a timer or keep track of task time. However, when the step had first begun, she had received the instruction to cook an ingredient for 10 minutes and proceeded to tell herself: "Alright, 10 minutes." It turns out that this utterance was not only a reminder and confirmation for herself, but also an implicit command for the HFIAI to keep track of time since she began the step. The HFIAI was frequently expected to understand participant's cooking context without any prompts and/or details to align to content or timestamps from the video.

4.3.2 Task context and task domain comprehension. In addition, participants also asked about task completion details that were rarely provided by the recipe videos they chose. For example, eight of them asked about the best way to chop an onion and none of their videos provided step-by-step instructions. Many of the videos that had visuals for ingredient preparation started the onion prep with specific task jargon such as "chop" and "dice", showed an almost completely prepped onion and then displayed the final prepped (e.g., diced) onion. Participants wanted a multi-step breakdown from placement of the onion on board to completed task. P1 and P13 even wanted tips for tear prevention. Many wanted the HFIAI to provide more examples and detailed descriptions of what jargon meant (e.g., "Diced means cutting the onion into small, equal-sized cubes about a quarter of an inch in size.").

"I like that the voice assistant provides more context on the recipe, verifying techniques, ingredient amounts etc." (P13)

To circumvent the lack of personalized advice from the video content, participants then asked for HFIAI-guided instructions (e.g., from search engine articles) and visual stills of the prepared ingredient extracted from the video for confirmation. Many of them (N=19) used the visual stills to confirm shape and size of the chopped ingredient with their own. Some (N=9) even asked the HFIAI system for visual confirmation. Common details that were asked but regularly not provided by the recipe videos included ingredient substitutes, cooking temperatures, utensils needed in the recipe, and serving sizes.

"[Shows onion to camera for size] I have this, does it looks like what he has?" (P15)

"A lot of the questions that came up were completely unrelated to the recipe, such as about the cooking utensils, techniques, and ingredients - so it would be useful to extrapolate beyond the scope of the video." (P7)

Another interaction that was asked by eight participants and was almost never covered (only 1 of the 22 videos included content on leftovers) contained interactions on what to do after cooking was completed. These interactions consisted of questions about ways to cook leftover ingredients and how to wrap the extra servings so that they last longer.

"Would ziploc bags or plastic foam be a better way to store cut vegetables in a fridge?" (P20)

4.3.3 Task context and multimodal environmental awareness. The final communication types included interactions that were not immediately resolved using either the wizard-of-oz camera and/or a search engine. These interactions entailed convenience functionalities such as time-based (after this much time has occurred, remind me to do this) and situation-based (next time this occurs, remind me to do this) reminders, and multimodal sensing capabilities (e.g., smell, sounds). During the cooking sessions, participants commonly had to complete a task that had a temporal component. Some examples include: an ingredient that had to be cooked for X number of minutes, a pot that needed to be covered for X number of minutes, or generally, how much time has passed so they understand how much more time is left. Participants not only wanted the HFIAI to provide timing capabilities when asked, but also expected the HFIAI to play an active role in keeping track of what they were doing and remind them of step details (step and procedural alignment synergy). For instance, P16 wanted the HFIAI to remind her that she needed two pans for the recipe after starting the cooking process in one single pan. She then proceeded to ask the HFIAI to keep a note for next time so she doesn't feel unprepared in the middle of cooking.

Other times, participants wanted the HFIAI to have advanced sensing capabilities beyond speech understanding. For example, they wanted to be able to communicate with the HFIAI through their device's camera. P2 pointed their device to their boiling pot and asked the HFIAI to identify if the water was boiling. P6 grabbed a fistful of pasta and showed it to their device camera, asking if it was enough for the recipe. When the current steps’ video visuals were not enough, vague, and/or missing, they wanted to rely on the HFIAI for confirmation. Even though the HFIAI often could not identify better than the participant and let them know of such, the participant was still adamant about the HFIAI giving them an answer since they did not trust themselves.

"[Asking for tips] I'm not entirely sure how I messed up but uh this is what the puree looks like right now [shows pot to camera] it's just a huge blob." (P20)

"Is the assistant capable of seeing like if I showed the ginger?" (P29) "Not very well, but I can try." (HFIAI) "OK. So I'm guessing is this large enough? A few slices of this?" (P29)

Similarly, participants also wanted the HFIAI to use their "eyes" to interpret the visuals within the video. P19 wanted the HFIAI to describe the doneness of the egg shown in the video to decide when to stop cooking his egg. P17 asked HFIAI to describe the colors in the video as a confirmation of what she was seeing. She also asked the HFIAI to describe the consistency of the mixture in the video, so she could obtain more details than what was provided in the video.

"I would use the assistant to tell me the physical characteristics of the ingredients when they have reached the correct cooking point." (P21)

A few participants (P14, P27, P28) even requested the HFIAI to "hear" or "smell" their cooking to determine task progress and performance.

5 DISCUSSION

While previous work have explored HFIAI interactions to make video playback more precise and accurate for users [ 13 , 34 , 55 , 60 , 67 ], we instead explore task-oriented interactions to better understand user needs beyond video control and comprehension. Based on our results, we propose features to enable HFIAI systems to have an understanding of the how-to video content, personal progress context, user characteristics, domain and non-domain information, and environmental context. In the following sections, we group these dimensions into three main takeaways and detail design implications for each takeaway.

5.1 Leveraging user characteristics and alignment query patterns for understanding context

Previous work highlighted the need to interpret user queries in context to provide optimal assistant responses [ 14 , 23 , 39 , 60 , 69 ]. Some have pointed out the potential of hyper-personalization and proposed the method of waiting for enough personal history to accumulate for best query inference [ 13 , 19 , 70 ]. From our analysis, we highlight specific factors that could be analyzed and observed to more efficiently parse user context from their interaction history such as grouped user characteristics and query alignments.

While examining patterns through our thematic analysis, we found that a participant's cooking expertise, cooking style, and HFIAI perception can all affect how they interact with the assistant. These different user characteristics were then identified and highlighted as three user personas: the progressor, the multitasker, and the engager. We found that these different user groups feature varying task completion patterns, alignment queries, interaction motivations, assistant needs, and system setup. This may indicate that by understanding these groupings of user tendencies and learning trends in user interactions, we could also better discern their task progress and context if we understand their typical workflow.

On the other hand, regardless of their semantics or intentions, participants almost always initiated alignment queries to replicate contexts within similar parts of the cooking step in their own nuanced ways. This indicates areas in which the HFIAI can both balance personalization and have a guide for when and in what contexts to expect certain types of interactions. In other words, HFIAI may be able to extract a user's context from a query by combining what they know about the alignment types that were already spoken and the user's task motivations.

Design Implications: By understanding different user tendencies, learning trends, and alignment strategies in user interactions, HFIAI designers may be able to tailor HFIAI behaviors to best suit the priorities and needs of each user. In this paper, we provide three user profiles to begin exploration. Future work might explore how these profiles generalize to and contrast with different large, diverse populations and tasks. We lay the groundwork for different cooking persona groups and their needs, and recognize the potential for future works to discover more user types, recreate the persona taxonomy for different types of task , and introduce additional layers of nuance to make designing even more customizable HFIAIs a reality.

The aforementioned user personas identify the variety of demands that our participants had for the HFIAI while cooking, but we also noticed that almost all of them share similar contexts for when queries are initiated. These may be leveraged as a grounding foundation to help HFIAIs better anticipate the type of interactions that are common to almost all users. We recognize that gathering and implementing designs that personalize HFIAIs to every user may be a difficult and complex challenge [ 49 ]. As a result, even if HFIAIs continue to lack more complex customization capabilities, context awareness, and/or multimodal functionalities, visualizing potential query patterns’ alignment to both task progress and the procedural video instructions may result in simpler solutions that can still be context and user aware.

5.2 Demand for both domain and non-domain specific query answers

Prior work on video players and browsing interfaces largely focus on analyzing the details of video content and metadata as a means to provide answers to the potential questions a user may pose to an accommodating HFIAI [ 27 , 28 , 29 , 33 ]. This metadata might include but is not limited to: speech transcriptions from the video audio [ 28 , 29 , 33 ], computer vision analysis of the video frames [ 5 , 11 , 40 , 56 ], and even the video's viewer comment sections and community feedback [ 24 , 66 ]. As follows, much of the work accomplished in designing HFIAI seek to optimize performance, accuracy, and usability in dissecting video metadata. However, we find that although video metadata may provide a good proportion of valid feedback to a user, the information isolated to the video environment is not enough to give participants all the information they wanted to complete their task.

Many context aware assistant studies describe systems that have domain-specific environments, such as retrieving the user location to provide tour guide assistance [ 3 , 48 ] or parsing file changes and project structures to provide software assistance with Git [ 8 ]. On the other hand, we found that cooking assistants required both domain (e.g., ingredient substitutes, serving size) and non-domain (e.g., fun fact, recycling rules) specific knowledge to grasp the environmental context and user progress. Common cooking details that were asked by a majority of participants, such as ingredient substitutes, cooking temperature, serving size, and utensils needed, were often never orally mentioned anywhere in the video, clearly shown, or supplemented in the comments. In addition, participants also frequently sought cooking tips in areas that they lacked knowledge in and procedural videos were rarely accommodating towards varying cooking expertise. With vague instructions acting as implicit prerequisites for the participants to achieve their goal, many reached out to the assistant for guidance on how to properly prepare for the step. Beyond cooking-based interactions, participants also initiated multiple interactions that required the assistant to expand its knowledge to outside of the domain as well (e.g., fun facts, recycling rules) . These included search engine-like capabilities such as knowing fun facts related to the dish (but not to cooking) as well as ways to store ingredients after cooking is complete.

Design Implications:

"I would use the assistant as it is in the "ideal form", but probably won't if it was on the level of typical voice assistants like Alexa." (P7)

We find that participants in this study seek HFIAIs that are equipped with knowledge beyond what can be parsed from video content and metadata alone. These findings motivate either top-down or bottom-up approaches for future system designs. In a top-down method, designers might pursue an all-inclusive HFIAI design that not only has knowledge from different domains outside of cooking, but can also handle context switches (e.g., switching between tasks) as well. To build such an HFIAI, a large amount of historical data is needed to account for population and individual differences in task management and differing alignment strategies and workflows between tasks. While this system may provide users with a breadth of knowledge and address some non-domain specific needs, technical constraints might prevent users from obtaining the depth of domain information they seek more often during tasks such as cooking.

In a bottom-up method, designers might alternatively augment a cooking specific HFIAI to have general knowledge outside of its domain. For example, search engine capabilities might be incorporated into the HFIAI to account for user interactions that require knowledge beyond the information provided by the video. Further analysis into users typical non-domain specific queries might also reveal the most popular areas of user concerns for cooking tasks that the search engine feature should address (e.g., fun facts, recycling). On the other hand, maintaining the upkeep of potential supplementary information needed can both be time-intensive and restrictive to those hand-picked by system developers. In either bottom-up or top-bottom approaches to HFIAI designs, understanding how to balance the trade off between collecting large amounts of non-domain data and designing an assistant that can provide information beyond video metadata may pose an interesting challenge for HFIAI designers.

5.3 Augmenting both the HFIAI's visual and auditory feedback

Following previous setups of HFIAI systems [ 13 , 67 ], we emulated an HFIAI that included a screen where participants could watch and hear video instructions from, and a voice-based assistant that could assist participants with the task through auditory guidance. By the end of study sessions, however, we found that participants expected or wished for both the system's visual and auditory aspects to be augmented further. For example, depending on the user persona type, some participants preferred different screen system setups. Engagers wanted both static (e.g., written recipe) and dynamic visuals (e.g., video instructions) to be shown simultaneously, while progressors were content with and preferred only dynamic visuals for reference.

Beyond visuals, participants wanted the system to not only understand video instructions and react to verbal commands and explicit speech, but also consolidate information from the surrounding environment. For example, a few of them asked whether the HFIAI could hear when cooking has completed by analyzing background noises and comparing them to typical sounds in the kitchen. Some participants also spoke to themselves about their own context (e.g., what step they are currently on, what they want to complete next) without directly addressing the HFIAI, but expected the HFIAI to have captured the think aloud intent. Beyond audio-based analyses, several participants wanted the HFIAI to also support visual-related functionalities by showing the assistant their progress in the task and then asking for feedback about what they just showed or olfactory functionalities with the assumption that the assistant would gain the ability to assess the task progress based on smell. To them, the ideal HFIAI was multimodal and could support a host of sensors to aid in the task completion process.

Design Recommendation: Our findings suggest that not every user will prefer video-based or even dynamic instructions to relay visual information. Extending on recommendations above for HFIAI designs to consider buckets of user characteristics, we also suggest leveraging these personas to design different static and dynamic implementations of visual-based instructions. For example, designs might feature methods to toggle on or split-screen written, static instructions, create or animate minimalist dynamic instructions according to a user's context, or integrating visuals directly into the environment (e.g., visual indicators on the task equipment).

Some HFIAIs in the market today are already connected to sensors and smart devices (e.g., camera, microphone, eye gaze trackers, augmented reality touch areas) that provide a multitude of functionalities [ 18 , 37 , 39 , 58 ]. However, interactions supported by current HFIAIs continue to remain primarily natural-language based [ 1 , 2 , 37 ]. Turning to many proposed context aware assistant designs [ 12 , 18 , 31 , 39 ], we argue that HFIAI designs should consider how to take advantage of these existing connections between itself and other device sensors or even integrate new modalities . These designs might entail introducing a multimodal experience model that could collect and learn a user's environment. Sounds made in the process of cooking (e.g., self-talk, chopping, washing, frying) and visuals of the cooking area may all contribute to helping HFIAIs understand the kitchen context and user progress. In addition, as cooking is a task that often utilizes all five senses, future designers might consider how different sensors might capture or provide information on lesser explored senses (e.g., smell, taste).

5.4 Generalizability to other types of tasks

Finally, while we suspect that some of our findings might extend to domains beyond cooking, we explored HFIAI system designs through mainly cooking specific lens. Thus, future work should investigate which implications are domain-specific to cooking tasks (e.g., designing for depth) and which designs might generalize to HFIAI systems as a whole (e.g., designing for breadth). For example, cooking consists of many timed, interwoven sub-tasks that involve a variety of objects and procedures; thus, our visualised workflow (Figure 2 ) may not be applicable to more linear tasks. Moreover, for simpler procedures that do not involve many different sub-tasks, the multitasker persona may no longer be as relevant for the system to consider. However, designers of non-cooking HFIAI might leverage our method of visualizing alignment timing and query strategies to build context aware flows for their specific tasks. In addition, for most tasks including cooking, future research could explore parsing common knowledge that HFIAI should acquire to account for interactions beyond video content and metadata assistance.

Furthermore, an HFIAI that is able to sense its environment in multi-faceted ways might be useful for other tasks (e.g., non-cooking) and populations (e.g., individuals with impairments [ 41 ]) as well. For instance, learning instruments might call for a music-domain HFIAI that can sense sound and touch. Future designers should consider the trade off between building a highly environmentally conscious assistant that could adjust how sensors are used to assist domain, task, and context switching or examine the best sensors needed to accommodate a certain task and its potential indicators of performance (e.g., taste and smell of dish).

5.5 Limitations and future works

Many cooking recipes and their respective techniques span regional, socioeconomic, and cultural intersections [ 59 ]. The authors and participants in this study came from a variety of cultural and culinary backgrounds in North America. We recognize that this may have impacted how the emulated HFIAI and the participants approached completion of the study. For example, more participants executed the recipe omurice than any other recipes; thus, the methods of this specific recipe are represented to a greater extent than others. Additionally, we did not get any interest from participants who had never cooked before or participants who considered themselves as experts in cooking. The participants in this study were also mostly in their 20s and all under 40 years of age. Although we hypothesize that this distribution might reflect users who would be interested in taking advantage of smart assistants and similar technologies for cooking, future work should expand the study to populations underrepresented in this study. In recognition of these limitations, we recommend that further analysis or studies catered to understanding these complex cultural dimensions on task fulfillment be done in future work.

In this work, we study specifically how people use HFIAIs to complete tasks by following an unfamiliar video. We note that the users interaction with the system might differ as they grow more familiar with the task and the video. Whether or not an HFIAI would still be used to support the completion of familiar tasks and well-versed videos, and how it might do so, are not examined in this study, but may pose interesting implications to be explored in future studies. We also acknowledge that experimenting with other active kitchen tasks, such as baking, may be valuable. We suggest future work to examine other types of cooking tasks as they may have distinct workflows. Video as well as audio were included in recordings of the study sessions, but the study intended to focus on understanding voice-based interactions rather than analyzing visuals. Future works could leverage user video content to study how visual factors like gaze, body language, physical gestures, etc. may impact the nature of user interactions with smart assistants.

We also provided example queries to participants so they might gain an impression of the variety and breadth of commands the HFIAI might support. However, we recognized that this may limit their expectations toward the system even if we emphasized the wizard-of-oz nature of the study and clarified to participants that they can and should initiate whatever interactions feel the most natural, helpful, and fitting for them anytime during the study session. Future study designs could explore how different types or lack of example prompts prior to the study might impact participants’ interactions.

6 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we describe a wizard-of-oz study in which participants interacted with a HFIAI while aligning their task to a how-to video. They performed one long cooking task with an emulated highly capable context and environment aware system with any user-desired functionality. With all of the interactions recorded during each session, we analyzed the needs of each participant and the motivations behind their queries. We found that although most participants followed similar query initiation patterns to align between instructions and tasks during each cooking step, participants also had different intentions and backgrounds that resulted in slight nuances to those interactions. In addition, we found that they expected HFIAI to have capabilities well beyond what could be dissected from a how-to video and its metadata alone. Thus, we provide design implications for future VA systems that are not only able to account for different user profiles, but are also task and context aware and able to leverage multimodal, multi-sensor functionalities.
