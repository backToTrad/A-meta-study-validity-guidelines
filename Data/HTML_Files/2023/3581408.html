AutomataStage: an AR-mediated Creativity Support Tool for Hands-on Multidisciplinary Learning

CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

The creativity support tools can enhance the hands-on multidisciplinary learning experience by drawing interest in the process of creating the outcome. We present AutomataStage, an AR-mediated creativity support tool for hands-on multidisciplinary learning. AutomataStage utilizes a video see-through interface to support the creation of Interactive Automata. The combination of building blocks and low-cost materials increases the expressiveness. The generative design method and one-to-one guide support the idea development process. It also provides a hardware see-through feature with which inside parts and circuits can be seen and an operational see-through feature that shows the operation in real-time. The visual programming method with a state transition diagram supports the iterative process during the creation process. A user study shows that AutomataStage enabled the students to create diverse Interactive Automata within 40-minute sessions. By creating Interactive Automata, the participants could learn the basic concepts of components. See-through features allowed active exploration with interest while integrating the components. We discuss the implications of hands-on tools with interactive and kinetic content beyond multidisciplinary learning.

1 INTRODUCTION

These days, the importance of creativity support tools in the context of multidisciplinary learning is increasing. Students are required to explore creative ideas combining science, technology, and art [ 8 , 11 ]. Hands-on activities are often used to facilitate active and creative exploration, rather than just listening to lectures and solving quizzes [ 12 , 26 , 40 ]. However, challenges exist in providing effective hands-on training because there are few contents and tools dedicated for such creative activities in the context of multidisciplinary learning, such as STEAM.

One of the ways to provide creative exercise for hands-on multidisciplinary learning is kinetic art creation with story [ 7 , 37 ]. In particular, we focused on interactive kinetic art with a story that moves in response to user or environmental changes as a novel content for hands-on multidisciplinary learning. Through creating the outcome, students come up with new ideas, explore each element, and integrate them to create their own stories. They can learn hardware structure, mechanism, electronics, programming, and artistic creation in an integrated way. Moreover, the creative activity with students’ ideas enables them to develop creative thinking, engage in class, and acquire self-efficacy [ 5 ].

However, tools scattered across digital and physical environments interfere with the exploration process and make integration difficult [ 4 , 43 ]. Students have difficulty developing ideas for stories. Moreover, managing interwoven workflow with various components is difficult, and students who have lost interest and concentration often fail to complete the task. A creativity support tool in multidisciplinary learning is necessary context to help students develop ideas and integrate the components with active exploration.

Prior works have proposed a learning tool that utilizes building blocks [ 3 , 22 , 36 , 38 ] and software [ 2 , 31 , 39 , 41 ] to support hands-on multidisciplinary learning. It promotes active exploration across digital and physical environments. However, hardware and software tools are still separate, and there is a limitation in integrating the creation process due to the gap between the digital and physical environments. Augmented reality learning tools [ 19 , 46 , 47 ] bridged digital and physical environments to support the learning process but only overlapped the related information or instruction, and it is still insufficient to support the idea development process.

This paper presents AutomataStage, an AR-mediated creativity support tool supporting hands-on multidisciplinary learning, and Interactive Automata, a learning content (Figure 1 ). As an approach for supporting idea development, active exploration, and integration, we applied a video see-through application associated with building blocks. We chose the video see-through technique that can be operated with a mobile device equipped with a camera because it is economical and widely used compared to projection AR or AR glasses. We focused on connecting the workflow from idea to physical outcome while exploring and integrating each component through the digital interface overlaid with the physical environment.

AutomataStage is specialized in creating Interactive Automata for hands-on multidisciplinary learning. Interactive Automata is an automata with a story that moves in response to user or environmental changes. Students can develop creativity through craft activities as well as learning sensors, motors, interaction authoring, and mechanisms while creating Interactive Automata using AutomataStage. We conducted expert interviews to identify the requirements and determine the design direction of AutomataStage. A user study was conducted for target users using content and system that reflect the results.

The contributions of this paper are threefold. First, it presents the creativity support tool for hands-on multidisciplinary learning, including artistic exploration, mechanism building, and interaction authoring. AutomataStage enables the student to create their idea with a combination of building blocks, low-cost material, and sketches. The generative design method and one-to-one guide support the creation process from idea to physical outcome. It also supports the iterative exploration and integration process with see-through features and visual programming with a state transition diagram. Second, it presents Interactive Automata as learning content that enables the students to experience and acquire knowledge, such as sensors, motors, mechanisms, and interaction authoring through craft activities. Finally, the user study demonstrated that the proposed system enables the rich creation of results and the learning process. The features of AutomataStage supported the idea development process, active exploration, and integration by bringing the physical environment into the digital tool. The user created their own automata with mechanisms and sketches. The idea of movement was well connected to the physical outcome. During the creation process, they actively explored, integrated, and tested the component. The real-time operation and state transition diagram supported the iterative trial-and-error.

2 RELATED WORKS

2.1 Tools for Movement and Interactivity

Tangible and modular building blocks increase beginners’ access to creative activities. Some tools utilized the building block to support the movement creation. Topobo [ 38 ] supported the movement authoring process by recording and replaying the movement with building blocks. LINKKI [ 36 ] is a learning tool that supports assembling link mechanisms with building blocks. The other tools utilized the building block for programming education. Horn and Jacob [ 15 ] introduced the tangible interface method for programming language education. StoryBlocks [ 27 ] supported the creation of audio stories, and MakerWear [ 22 ] supported the creation of interactive wearable devices with tangible programming while learning programming. LittleBits[ 3 ] is a representative building block for physical computing education. Rapid construction using building blocks helps students engage with the hands-on activity. We apply building blocks to support rapid exploration and hands-on activity. Furthermore, students can express more creative and abundant outcomes using sketches.

Creativity support tools using a shape-changing method were introduced. AnimaStage [ 33 ] proposed a method for creating movement by connecting flexible characters to a pin-based shape display. Various interaction methods have also been proposed for expressing movement through the actuation of characters. Magentform [ 50 ] supports the expression of movement by changing the shape of the surface using magnets. Different expressions can be created depending on the type of material and the movement of the magnet. ShapeClip [ 14 ] is a system that can display screen information in the z-axis by changing the height. It helps designers quickly represent dynamic physical transformations on the screen. Previous studies have proposed interaction methods for expressing and authoring movements using various materials. However, the focus has been on creative expression and interaction methods. In this research, we aim to investigate how to express creative ideas in a learning context.

Meanwhile, visual programming tools for authoring interaction have been introduced. Scratch [ 41 ] is a representative block-based programming tool for children. It supported animation production and physical computing using sensors and motors. Leonardi et al. [ 29 ] presented the hierarchical text block programming tool for authoring robot behavior. Trigger-Action-Circuits [ 1 ] applied wiring method for physical computing. The visual programming method enables novices unfamiliar with programming languages to author the interaction and grasp the whole at a glance. We applied the state transition diagram as a visual programming method to allow inexperienced students to design interactions. We also considered the process of developing students’ ideas beyond providing easy programming. The state transition diagram is similar to the sentence structure of the student's interaction idea. It not only makes the whole process easy to identify, but it also supports integrating the components.

2.2 Software Tools for Integrating Workflow

Several researchers applied the generative design method with the desired movement input. These researches focused on the framework and algorithm for generating the mechanism by drawing the desired movement with cyclic path [ 9 ], adjusting the extreme pose [ 32 ], applying the motion capture technique [ 6 ], or moving the 3D mesh file [ 53 ]. The generative design approach supports the idea development process with a top-down method that generates the result with the desired movement. AutomataStage also supports the top-down method that enables students to develop their ideas. The generative design method using the sketch allows for generating the movement, sentence about interaction is connected with the state transition diagram.

Several works have supported the making as well as the design and simulation of the kinetic artifact. In common, these researches provided the blueprint or 3d model for fabrication after design and simulation of movement [ 2 , 23 , 35 , 52 ]. The other works have supported the making process of the artifact including electronics. These works provided a blueprint and instruction for building a circuit and electronics [ 31 , 39 ]. Previous works supported the making process with digital drawing or instruction as well as the exploration process with various functions in the software. However, the exploration process still ended in software. There is a gap between the exploration and fabrication process. In the AutomataStage, the video see-through application associated with hardware bridges the workflow from idea development, and exploration to making.

Several researchers connected the hardware with the software for the exploration process. M.integrator [ 16 ] supported the simulation and testing process for interactive kinetic artifacts by blending the virtual and real components. Robiot [ 30 ] applied the generative design approach that utilized the repeated movement video of hardware as input for actuating existing objects. Previous studies have applied a method of connecting the hardware with software for a seamless workflow. In our work, we tried to understand how the exploration and integration method of connecting the hardware impacts hands-on STEAM learning.

2.3 Interactive Tabletop and Augmented Reality Tool

Several works proposed the education tool with an augmented reality environment. Meta-AR-App [ 46 ] supported the collaboration and RobotAR [ 47 ] supported remote education with AR applications. However, they applied augmented reality to visualize the related information on the physical environment. Several prototyping tools tried to display the digital simulation in the physical environment. These tools supported the interactive simulation that follows the tangible objects [ 10 , 24 , 45 ]. However, they focused on the simulation of digital content rather than physical outcomes. In the fabrication area, researchers proposed prototyping tools that support the design process in the physical environment [ 13 , 25 , 48 ]. Prior studies have the advantage of enabling exploration and integration of workflows by trial and error in the physical context. However, they focused on static artifacts. In our work, we expanded the content to interactive and kinetic artifacts.

Several works have applied augmented reality using physical objects for education. PrototypAR [ 20 ] simulated with the cutted paper, AR Math [ 21 ] utilized the existing object for math education. However, it does not support the creation process by conducting learning with fixed content. Mechanism Perfboard [ 17 ] generated the mechanism by moving the sketch as an input. WIKA [ 18 ], projection-based augmented reality, supported the whole creation process from interaction & movement design, and simulation to fabrication by using the component from the initial phase of creation. These studies utilize existing objects as an interface to help explore in augmented reality. Our study focuses on the creation of Interactive Automata. We used the see-through system, one of the augmented reality types, to explore the interface linked to building blocks for hands-on STEAM learning.

3 EXPERT INTERVIEW

The goal of the expert interview was to identify the requirements and determine the design direction of AutomataStage. The detailed questions of the expert interview included the following: What kinds of tools and contents are used in the educational field? What are the difficulties and requirements when conducting hands-on multidisciplinary learning? Is there a possibility of Interactive Automata as learning content? What are the appropriate user targets and teaching methods for hands-on multidisciplinary learning?

We interviewed eight teachers with four to ten years of STEAM education experience. They had experience conducting STEAM education for students between the ages of 10 and 14 in the fields of art, math, science, and practical course (technology and computer related class). First, we asked questions about the contents and tools used in the educational field. And then, we proceeded the main interview after introducing the learning content, Interactive Automata.

Teachers planned multidisciplinary learning according to the curriculum. In most cases, theoretical classes were conducted several times, and hands-on activity was conducted at the end. They mainly conduct hands-on activities related to engineering, science, and programming with a craft activity. We can identify the difficulties of hands-on multidisciplinary learning and the needs for new contents and tools. The interview data were audio-recorded, hand-transcribed, and iteratively classified it to derive the requirement. We summarize the results into five requirements with representative contents.

  • Interesting content that allows for a sense of accomplishment. Teachers welcomed Interactive Automata as a content for hands-on multidisciplinary learning. They said that students can simultaneously acquire knowledge and experience about mechanisms and physical computing (sensor, motor, programming) through craft activities using storytelling. In addition, they said that by teaching various things in connection, students will be interested, and the atmosphere of the class could be improved. They also noted that crafting will improve concentration, and the results will give them a sense of achievement.
  • Tools that are easy to use and have rich expression. Classes using Arduino to create interactive results are actively being conducted. Using Arduino allows students to get interested in the class, but it takes a long time to build the result due to the difficulty of the hardware. Teachers mentioned that it would be nice to have hardware that students could use easily and express various ideas. Several teachers used the finished product, such as a robot and AI speaker, for a programming class. They said that using the finished product makes it easier to conduct classes, but it is difficult to do creative activities. Most teachers mentioned that students prefer drawing and low-cost material because it is easy to handle.
  • Tools that help with the creative process while allowing students to progress on their own. In the animation making activity with a block-based programming tool, teachers usually managed the programming class with the goal of performing a given step by step task because it is challenging to take care of every student. The students created the animation by following the instruction with block-based visual programming tool. The teachers said that it would be nice to let students come up with ideas, but expected that students would have difficulty in coming up with ideas and programming them. In addition to tools to support the creation, content and strategies that can encourage students are essential.
  • Tools that allow for real-time feedback. In an engineering class, it is common for students to study the principles through the content, such as shooting a bow and arrow far or building a strong bridge. Teachers encouraged the student to improve their outcomes through trial and error. However, they said that students can only guess the results of their experiments and cannot accurately understand why they happen. They said it would be great if there were tools that could help explain the principles more accurately by repeatedly simulating them.
  • Tools that help with many iterations. Using the construction kit for automata, students follow the instructions and assemble well, but they lose interest too quickly. Students who are not interested in machinery or robots hesitate to make them. We could identify the need for tools that enable students to create the result for engaging in activity and induce active trials.

We also questioned the target users and methods to apply Interactive Autoamta. Teachers said that 11-13 years old would be appropriate considering the curriculum. Teachers noted that classes using Interactive Automata can be applied to regular classes. If there is a proper education tool, it is also suitable for short workshop activity that interests students. They mentioned it was necessary to make them feel the need to learn by explaining real-life examples.

4 AUTOMATASTAGE

AutomataStage (Figure 2 ) is the AR-mediated tool that supports multi-disciplinary hands-on learning through the creation of Interactive Automata as its content. We applied Video see-through interface with building blocks to support the entire creation process, from idea development, interaction authoring, and mechanism simulation to making.

  • Interactive Automata as a learning content. Interactive Automata is an automata with a story that moves in response to user or environmental changes. Students can develop creativity through craft activities as well as learning sensors, motors, interaction authoring, and mechanisms while creating Interactive Automata using AutomataStage.
  • Building blocks with low-cost material and sketch. Building blocks allow students to deal with components easily while using low-cost material and sketch to increase expressiveness. We provided assembled sensors and motors for easy use by students. To increase expressiveness, we allowed automata to be freely created. We used straws to easily create the desired mechanisms and sketch to represent characters. We also produced customized building blocks to allow users to make the desired movements.
  • Geneartive design and one-to-one guide. The generative design method supports creating and simulating mechanisms in conjunction with sketches, and one-to-one scale guides and building blocks help create mechanisms with straw.
  • The real-time and in-situ feedback. The hardware see-through feature helps students learn by showing the internal components and circuits of the building block. The operational see-through feature, which is linked to the physical environment in real-time, enables the students to check sensor values, define sensor conditions, check motor operation, and define the motor output for active exploration.
  • Iteractive exploration during the integration. We applied the visual programming method with a state transition diagram. It supports the iterative process of integrating and testing the outcome during the creation process.

4.1 Hardware Configuration

4.1.1 Sensor and Motor Blocks. The sensor block consists of light, sound, and rotary sensor (Figure 3 a). The sound sensor detects a change in sound, the light sensor detects a change in light, the rotary sensor detects a change in rotation, and the sensor value is marked 0-1024, depending on the intensity. The motor block consists of DC and servo motor (Figure 3 a). The DC motor rotates 360 degrees and can adjust direction (CW/CCW) and speed (0-90). The servo motor can be adjusted at an angle of 0-180 degrees. The sensor block and the motor block are made of a semi-transparent acrylic cover to show the circuits and parts connected inside, and the sensor blocks are made of blue and the motor blocks are made of pink. The Feather Huzzah ESP8266 board, an Arduino-compatible board, was installed in each block to communicate wirelessly with the Web server.

4.1.2 Stage. The stage (Figure 2 ) is a stage-type work environment that creates Interactive Automata, designed to match the height of the tablet's camera. The stage is made of a tin plate so that magnets can be attached. We installed the magnet inside the Building blocks to stick to the stage.

4.1.3 Snap-fit Joint. Snap-fit joint (Figure 3 b) is a customized part designed to make link mechanisms that are utilized to express the character's movement. The snap-fit joint is stackable to prevent overlapping links while rotating and can be assembled with straws.

4.1.4 Straw. Straws (Figure 3 b) were used to serve as links of link-based mechanisms. It was selected as an easily cut material according to the length of the link mechanism designed by the user.

4.1.5 Character Body and Character Block. The character body (Figure 3 b) is a component connected with link mechanisms to help express the character's movement. It is designed to plug the support according to the character configuration and connect with link mechanisms using snap-fit joints. Snap-fit joints can be mounted into the character body in any direction, allowing the user to design characters that move left/right or front/back.

4.2 Software Configuration

Video see-through application recognizes the AR marker of the sensor block and motor block and displays related information (Figure 4 a). The color of the sensor and the motor is represented by a blue square and a pink circle, the same as the block's color. The sensor value is displayed on the right in real-time. A sensor panel is shown when the sensor is selected, and a motor panel is displayed when a motor is selected. The defined conditions and outputs are displayed above the components in the form of a, b, and c. The left button at the bottom is the state transition panel button, which allows the user to author interactions by dragging defined conditions and outputs. The user can execute the authored interaction with the right button. The middle button is the screen capture button that can fix the screen when the user wants.

4.2.1 Sensor Panel. In the sensor panel (Figure 4 b), the user can define a condition of a sensor value to execute an event. The range of sensor values can be defined by the slider and acts as an if-statement when operated. On the left side is an illustration of the sensor associated with the parts and wiring of the sensor block.

4.2.2 Motor Panel. In the motor panel (Figure 4 c & d), the user can define an output of a motor. Moving the slider allows the user to check the motor operation in real-time and define the output. For example, the DC motor can adjust the direction and speed, and the servo motor can adjust the angle. On the left side is an illustration of the motor associated with the parts and wiring of the motor block.

4.2.3 Mechanism Panel. It can be opened with the mechanism button on the motor panel (Figure 4 e). The user can design, simulate, and make the link mechanism in the mechanism panel.

4.2.4 State Transition Panel. The user can author the interaction with pre-defined conditions and outputs in the state transition panel (Figure 4 f). It provides an interface to edit the state transition diagram. The operating state is displayed in yellow during the execution.

4.2.5 Software Implementation. Video see-through application is a Web-based application that is implemented with JavaScript and HTML. For wireless communication, a single Express server of Node.js is applied. All data of AutomataStage are shared via socket.io stream through the Express server. Video see-through application is deployed on the Safari browser of iPad. The video see-through application gets sensor value data from the sensor block and sends motor control data through the feather board that communicates with a Web server. To detect the AR marker position, we use the AR.js library. The application tracks the position of the customized AR markers via the device's camera.

The digital link mechanism generation, modification, and simulation are implemented using the source code of the M.Sketch [ 23 ] with three.js. We choose the Stephenson mechanism as a target mechanism because it can easily represent articulated characters and transmit movement far away (Figure 6 ). Point A, B, C, and D are already attached to the motor block, and the user can change the length of the remaining links to make the desired movement. Anchor point G is inserted into the character body with a joint, and the user can express an articulated character with the movement of the link GF. For mechanism generation, we generated 50,280 Stephenson mechanisms as a data set with different positions of point E, F, and G. The most similar mechanism was obtained by comparing the desired position of the anchor point G and desired trajectory with the data set.

4.3 Usage Scenario

To illustrate the usage scenario, we introduce how a user creates Interactive Automata. The example consists of a drum stick and saxophonist. When the drum stick sound is heard, the saxophonist plays the saxophone. The user can proceed to the desired step.

4.3.1 STEP 1: Ideation and Making Character. The user comes up with an idea about the movement and interaction with sketch and sentence. The user selects the sound sensor to detect hitting the drum stick and the servo motor to move the saxophonist. Next, the user draws the saxophonist and creates a character (Figure 7 ). The user cuts the sketch and attaches the lower body to the character body (Figure 7 b). And then, the user attaches the upper body to the joint and mounts it into the character body (Figure 7 c).

4.3.2 STEP 2: Exploring the Sensor and Defining Conditions. The user checks that the value of the sound sensor is about 345 when the drum stick is hit (Figure 8 a). The user sets the range of sensor values to be greater than 307 and less than 1024, in order to use the sound as a condition when hitting the drum stick (Figure 8 b).

4.3.3 STEP 3: Generating and Modifying the Mechanism. The user activates the mechanism panel by pressing the mechanism button on the motor panel. The user selects the Stephenson mechanism. And then, the user matches the pink square with the servo motor block to scale the digital mechanism and the physical environment.(Figure 9 a). After mounting a joint on the character body, the user moves the anchor point of the digital mechanism to the corresponding position, draws the desired trajectory of movement, and presses the generation button. Then, the most similar mechanism is generated (Figure 9 b). The user presses the mechanism simulation button to execute the digital mechanism and checks if the movement is correct. The user manually modifies the points of the link mechanism to fit the desired movement (Figure 9 c).

4.3.4 STEP 4: Making the Mechanism. When the desired movement is designed, the user creates the mechanism using a real-scale guide by pressing the making button. The user creates the physical mechanism by cutting straws and connecting joints after marking the length of the link on the screen (Figure 10 ).

4.3.5 STEP 5: Exploring the Motor and Defining Outputs. The user adjusts the motor angle in the servo motor panel to check the movement of the mechanism in real-time (Figure 11 ). And then, the user sets the desired angle. The user sets the angle at which the character bends forward to (a) and reclines backward to (b) .

4.3.6 STEP 6: Authoring Interaction and Executing. In the state transition panel, the user can author the interaction utilizing the pre-defined conditions and outputs (Figure 12 a). The user adds State #1 & #2 and Event #1 using the buttons. The user puts the Output (b) in State #1 and the Output (a) in State #2 as drag-and-drop. The user adds the Condition (a) to Event #1 .

The user connects the node of the event with the state to connect the interaction (Figure 12 b). After that, the user presses the execution button to check the interaction and movement. The user utilizes the delay to express repeated movements with the servo motor (Figure 12 c). The user can set the desired delay time in seconds by pressing the button. And then the user adds it to the Event #2 & Event #3 with drag and drop. To activate the next state after the set time. Then, when the interaction is executed, the next state is activated after the delay time.

5 USER STUDY

We conducted a user study to identify that creation activity with Interactive Automata and AutomataStage supports the hands-on learning process effectively. The detailed questions of the user study included the following: Is it capable of the student to create the diverse Interactive Automata with AutomataStage? What are the possibilities of the creation activity with Interactive Automata as hands-on multidisciplinary learning content? How do the features of AutomataStage affect hands-on learning activities?

5.1 Participants

After the pilot study of three students, we recruited five K-6 (ages 11-12) students and three K-7 (ages 12-13) students (three males and five females). The age range suitable for learning Interactive Automata was selected based on the feedback from the expert interview. Some students (P1, P4 & P8) have never used sensors and motors, others (P2, P3, P6 & P7) have used breadboards according to the teacher's instructions, and P5 has finished products with sensors and motors. All students had block-based programming such as Scratch, and some had experience learning Python (P1 & P4), JavaScript (P4), and Arduino (P7). All students had no experience in creating a link-based mechanism, and only P6 had the experience in assembling a cam mechanism.

5.2 Procedure

Participants built Interactive Automata with their own ideas as the main task. Before the main task, we introduced participants to the components of AutomataStage and how to use it. In addition, various applications were explained to help participants come up with ideas (e.g. Sound sensors detect the loudness level of sound and can be used to detect speech, music, and applause. A loop structure can be created using the state transition diagram, and multiple components can be used simultaneously.). In order to understand the effectiveness of learning, participants solved the questions in the form of a quiz at the beginning and end of the user study. The quiz questions consist of content that can be learned while creating Interactive Automata. Specifically, it is divided into five categories: sensor type & characteristics, sensor operation principle & circuit configuration, motor type & characteristics, mechanism characteristics, programming elements & composition.

The procedure of the user study is as follows in five steps. First, participants took a five-minute pre-quiz. Then, we introduced the overall component and function of AutomataStage for 20 minutes. During the introduction of the system and content, we explained basic knowledge of components and various applications. For instance, we explained that the sound sensor can detect voice, hand-clapping, or music. And then, participants made an example of Interactive Automata for 10 minutes. An example was the violinist playing when the coin was placed on the light sensor and stayed still five seconds later. Before the main task, the students showed an example video of kinetic automata for 5 minutes to help the ideation. After that, students conducted the main task of making Interactive Automata with their ideas for up to 40 minutes. The main task started with drawing and cutting the sketch after coming up with an idea in common and then proceeded without intervention. The link mechanism was limited to the Stephenson mechanism in which the generation function was implemented. After completing the main task, the post-quiz was conducted for 5 minutes with the same items as the pre-quiz. Finally, we were asked questions about Interactive Automata and conducted a semi-structured interview about the effectiveness and improvement points of AutomataStage for 10 minutes.

5.3 Data Collection and Analysis

We analyzed the results from the observations of participants’ activity and interviews. The initial sketches and creation outcomes were collected, and photos were taken. The pre-quiz and post-quiz were collected and scored. The interviews were audio recorded and hand-transcribed. The entire procedure of the user study was video recorded. We classified the activities of the participants and measured each time by watching the video. The activity was classified based on what functions are being used in the hardware and software of AutomataStage, and the result was cross-validated. Through iterative analytic induction, the research team analyzed the collected data (interview quotes, activities, and outcomes).

5.4 Result

5.4.1 Task Time & Activites. All participants successfully created Interactive Automata in a short session (28min 50sec - 41min). Even students with little experience completed the task without difficulty after a short tutorial. We divided the activity of participants into eleven categories and measured the time for each activity (Figure 15 ). Creation time was not affected by the participants’ experiences. Ideation, drawing & cutting character, and making & testing mechanism have a large part of the activity. The complexity of the idea and the number of mechanisms influenced the task time. Each of the rest of the activities (total was 30.6%) were short, but they were important activities that supported the exploration with sensors, motors, mechanisms, and integration through iterative exploration, execution, and modification.

Participants created Interactive Automata in the order they wanted. Some participants(P1, P2, P5, P7 & P8) authored the interaction after defining all conditions and outputs, while others(P3, P4 & P6) alternated between defining and authoring. Participants who took turns integrated the conditions and outputs into the state transition diagram one by one. After testing the mechanism, participants (P2, P3, P4, P5 &P6) mainly attached the character to the mechanism and define the output of the motor. It means that the mechanism is connected to the motor and can also be integrated into the state transition diagram. The participants integrated each component into the state transition diagram, starting with any of the individual components such as sensors, motors, and mechanisms. Integration did not proceed at the end of the task but continued throughout the process.

There were participants (P1 & P5) who designed and made the mechanism after the sensor and motor exploration, while the others started with the interaction authoring. P2 said, “I am not confident in the making, so I coded it first.” P3 also commented, “I didn't think making itself is that important. Because in order to check the movement, I have to ensure the coding was done properly. Therefore, I did something important first.”

5.4.2 Outcome. Participants created diverse results with their ideas. They created the movement of cooking (P1), taking the rabbit out of the hat (P2), raising the hand to hush (P4), asking for a snack (P5), and playing the guitar (P6 & P8). P3 expressed the two movements (dancing & greeting) with one arm. P1 expressed the movement of bending the upper body to pick up food. P6 came up with the idea that the character moved when the lower body is joined with the upper body without a link mechanism. P7 came up with the idea that the whole hamster character kept moving. There were also movement ideas that linked the sketches to each other. P2 used a hat to hide the rabbit, and P7 expressed the hamster's movement with the wheel in the background. P1 came up with the idea that the food on the pan falls when the arm moves.

There were two types of interaction ideas. The first is to stop and start moving under certain conditions (on & off). The second is to be in a particular position and change the position under certain conditions (change of state). P6 & P7 used the first interaction type. P2 & P4 used the second interaction type. P1,P3,P5 & P6 used both interaction type.

The participants selected the motor according to the movement and interaction idea. They used the DC motor to express the movement that repeatedly moved. An output with a value of zero and more than zero was used. The servo motor was used to express changing from one position to another. Two outputs with different angles were used.

Participants select the sensors suitable for the interaction idea. P2, P3 & P7 used the light sensor to detect darkening. P1 used the light sensor to detect objects by covering the light sensor with fallen food. All participants used sound sensors to detect various sounds. P1 detected an order to cook, P2, P6, P7, and P8 detected a clapping sound, P3 detected a music sound, P4 detected noise, and P5 detected a snack box shake. P3 and P4 create two sound sensor conditions and use them for two events when sound is heard and when sound is gone. P4 used a rotary sensor to try two conditions in one event without meaning.

Participants authored interactions with various types of state transition diagrams. Except for P1 and P3, the participants created a loop-type state transition diagram. Three participants (P2, P4, and P7) added sensor conditions instead of the delay to the event that returns to the first state. P1 created a sequential structure with different states at the beginning and the end. Most participants created the state transition diagram with two states and two events, but P3 used five states and four events because the idea was relatively complicated.

5.5 Findings

5.5.1 Possibility of Interactive Autoamta as Learning Contents. The creation activity with Interactive Automata allowed participants to focus on the learning process. Moreover, craft activities using the ideas gave participants a sense of accomplishment. P1 said, “I felt proud to have what I drew moving.” In addition, Participants said that the creation activity is more interesting than activities that use finished products and follow instructions. P3 mentioned, “I thought the existing class was obvious and boring. What we made were similar and we had to follow specified rules, but this time it was different! I could apply it on my ideas and had a try in various ways. ” Participants also said that it was exciting and immersed because the learning content included movement and interaction. In addition, elements of movements, sensors, and motors to be observed during testing and modification have increased the user's participation. P3 said, “When I put in the setting, the movement became more vivid and lively. I could be more immersed in the situation.” P7 added, “I thought it was fun to set and control the conditions or the movement of the motor from the beginning to the end.” Participants got a sense of achievement by completing the task with Interactive Automata that they thought was difficult to create. P2 said, “Before I wondered how to make such a complicated thing more than anything else. This time, I felt good to make it myself.”

Through the process of creating Interactive Automata, students were able to acquire various knowledge and experiences. As a result of scoring the quiz results, the understanding of the content improved from 14.66% to 83.19%. After the main task, students could answer how sensors and motors work, how mechanisms would move, and how to program the story with components. Participants said they learned thoroughly by exploring and operating the components with AutomataStage. P6 said, “At first, I was confused about the mechanism behind it. As I kept trying, I gradually understood each sensor and motor, and later I could even predict how the sensor would react.” P2 said he got confidence in making other things using sensors and automata.

5.5.2 Bridging the Workflow from Idea to Result. The idea of the character's movement was well connected to the process of designing digital mechanisms and making physical mechanisms. Video see-through application supported the desired movement design by supporting the generation, modification, and simulation of the mechanism associated with sketches. P6 said, “It was good to link the sketch (physical thing) and mechanism on screen (digital thing) to make it.” The making guide helped bridge the digital mechanism to the physical mechanism. P5 commented, “Usually, I have to measure the length and cut it. With the guide, it directly showed on the screen and I could do it right away.” In addition, Straws and snap-fit joints facilitated the rapid fabrication and modification of the mechanism and made it possible to connect with motors and characters.

The interaction idea is naturally connected to the interaction authoring process. Participants wrote their interaction ideas in sentences and linked them to conditions, outputs, and state transition diagrams. For example, P1 wrote the sentence “Stop - When saying make the dish - Move and drop the food - When the food is detected - Stop and bend his waist forward to pick up the food”. Sentences were divided into those related to sensors and motors, defined as conditions and outputs, and then integrated into the state transition diagram. The method of dragging predefined conditions and outputs in conjunction with the building blocks allows the integration of components while managing them. P3 said, “When I brought the conditions and outputs from here, I felt like I was making my own coding.” P7 added, “It was good to experiment quickly and bring it into codes.”

5.5.3 Active Exploration with Building Blocks. Building blocks enabled the students to actively explore without fear by narrowing the gap between components and students. Participants could use the system without barriers to entry by using the building blocks that eliminate unnecessary processes such as basic settings, wire connections, and uploads. P2 mentioned, “Previously, I had to connect the wires, check the wires... This time, however, through just a few touches, I could control it and test it RIGHT AWAY!” The characteristics of building blocks, which are well distinguished for each component, made it easy to grasp the hardware composition and change. P7 said, “When using the Arduino breadboard, it was hard to see because the circuit was quite complicated. This time as the sensor and motor were separated, this became much easier.”

The hardware see-through feature and the picture explanation in panels assisted the learning activity. Participants said that the parts and circuits inside were visible, so it was easy to understand by comparing them with the pictures. Some students were curious about how to implement the system while looking inside the transparent block. P2 observed the block and asked questions related to the implementation of the system, such as wireless communication and marker recognition.

5.5.4 Active Exploration and Integration with Operational See-through Feature. The operational see-through feature supported the thought process in the digital environment. P3 said, “I thought the best part is that can check it out and try many things directly.” P3 added, “What I manipulated looked exactly as what I thought.” Participants also said they were afraid of operating sensors, motors, and mechanisms in the first time. Still, it was easy to proceed thanks to a system that provides functions for each component. They could define the sensor conditions by checking the sensor values through the video see-through application. Controlling the motor by adjusting the slider allows the participants to set the speed and angle while testing the movement of the motor in real-time. P2 & P6 modified the position of the sketch and angle of the servo motor to express ideas linked to sketches. P8 added, “I could check everything easily because it moved the same as what I manipulated.”

Participants utilize the state transition diagram not only for integration but also for debugging. They repeatedly checked the errors and corrected the state transition diagram or modified the sensor conditions and motor output. During the execution, student checked the sensor value and motor movement. At the same time, they checked that the state transition diagram moved well to the next state according to the intended event. Through the state transition diagram, students could easily catch events or states that did not execute as intended.

The mechanism simulation supported the exploration of mechanism movement. P4 commented, “Rather than following an instruction to make a determined outcome, I was able to do some research, make some tests, and explore the ideas of movement. This was great indeed!” The process of making after simulation convinced the participants. P3 said, “Without this (simulation), it was hard to predict the movement and a lot of trials and failures would follow. After checking the simulation, I actually gained more confidence in what I did.” P6 also mentioned the role of simulation, “In my case, I used the simulation to check the (digital) mechanism in advance and it worked well. However, when it goes to this one (the physical mechanism), it continuously went wrong. Therefore, I calmed down and came up with the idea that the length of the stroke should be the reason.”

5.5.5 Improvement Point. There were some improvement points in the aspects of function and user interface. P6 mentioned the conditions and output defining interface by directly inputting numbers and suggested the auto-arrangement of the state transition diagram. P4 said it was inconvenient to check again because the defined conditions and outputs were notated as a, b, and c. There was also an opinion that it was difficult to work on the stage because the block was too big. It is needed to update the mechanism creation process. It was difficult to identify the location of the joint inserted into the character body and to align the upper & lower order of the snap-fit joint.

6 DISCUSSION AND FUTURE WORK

From user studies, we can identify that Interactive Automata is useful as learning content and positive effects of AutomataStage for hands-on multidisciplinary learning. The results illustrated that Interactive Automata draws attention, interest, and fun while acquiring various knowledge and experience. AutomataStage bridges the physical and digital environment with see-through features and state transition programming methods by managing the creation process, supporting the active exploration, and integrating components. We also identified several issues to discuss hands-on tools based on the tool development and study result.

6.1 Supporting the Creative Activity for Hands-on Multidisciplinary Learning

Hands-on multidisciplinary learning is an effective learning method of drawing attention, achievement, and interest through the craft with their own ideas [ 12 , 26 , 40 ]. Participants could get achievement and be more immersed in learning activities by creating Interactive Automata with their ideas. To develop creativity, the process of coming up with ideas is as important as the process of developing ideas. We showed various examples and applications in the tutorial process in a way that allowed students to come up with various ideas. Above all, students should know what they can do with components they are new to and go through the process of applying them. If a tutorial function is included to teach and experience examples using the video see-through application, it will help students effectively come up with ideas [ 34 ].

AutomataStage supports the creation of Interactive Automata by bridging initial ideas such as character sketches and sentences with a tool. The sketch is used to design mechanisms with the video see-through application. The sentence is used to design interactions using state transition diagrams. It will be difficult for less experienced students to develop ideas. We expect that it will be a practical hands-on learning tool if it guides the process of developing initial ideas. It would be adequate to suggest several alternatives and let them think about them, not to suggest solutions. Suppose the learning tool proposes alternative mechanisms from the character sketch [ 44 ] and suggests sensors, motors, and interactions from the sentence. In that case, it will be an effective AI-aid tutoring tool that allows the creation of various results while learning more [ 51 ].

Appropriate hardware is essential to support creative crafting activities that accompany various ideas. We provided sensor blocks, motor blocks, straws, and customized joints for making the link mechanism of different lengths. Moreover, the sketch combined with sensors, motors, and mechanisms enriched the output. Customized building blocks are essential to create variations, and applying elements that express results more freely, such as sketches, will improve ideas.

6.2 Supporting the Active Exploration

In multidisciplinary education, which includes many components at once, it is necessary to develop an exploration function for each component. AutomataStage supports the hands-on activity within a short session by applying see-through features for every component. The hardware see-through feature supports the hardware exploration, and the operational see-through feature supports the exploration of sensors, motors, and mechanisms. In addition, unnecessary processes such as upload and wire connection are omitted to support a smooth workflow.

In classes that make results first without theoretical learning, students may hesitate because they have no idea about what to do. For hesitant students, it will be possible to embed the mission in the learning tool to draw the active exploration of each step [ 28 ]. It is also a good way to apply gamification [ 49 ] that rewards students for achievement when they complete each mission.

6.3 Managing the Interwoven Workflow

It is necessary to consider how to integrate as well as explore individual components. In particular, it is more important when digital and physical components are mixed like Interactive Automata. In the AutomataStage, the state transition programming method integrates the digital and physical components. Video see-through application integrates the workflow of the creation process.

The process of dividing and integrating is the problem-solving ability that can be learned during physical computing and programming. We expect that participants could have learned new problem-solving strategies as well as learned about individual components in the process of creating Interactive Automata. However, students become afraid when they try to learn with complex content. It is necessary to consider how students manage and integrate interwoven workflow from ideas to result without giving up learning easily.

6.4 Enabling Low Floor and High Ceiling

For creativity support tools, it is important to enable a low floor for lowering the barrier and a high ceiling for idea expression [ 42 ]. In the context of multidisciplinary learning, AutoamtaStage lowers the barrier with building blocks and video see-through interfaces. A generative design approach with sketch was applied as a method to increase creativity. However, the ideas that students can come up with are limited by the type of hardware.

To support a high ceiling, it is necessary to expand the system so that various components can be used. It is possible to increase the types of sensors, motors, mechanisms, and programming methods to create various Interactive Automata. The tool can support various parts if we make it possible to replace sensors and motors, even if we hide microcontrollers like Arduino or Raspberry Pi. The motor block can be updated for connecting the cam, crank, or gear to the motor to increase the expressiveness of character motion. In the case of programming, there are ways to increase the visual programming grammar or make it possible to do textual programming.

6.5 Prototyping Tool for Interactive Products or System with Movement

In the prototyping tools for a product or system, such as robots, the most important thing is to support the function of coming up with various ideas and the process of developing ideas. Even if learning is not necessary, in order to produce creative results, understanding the characteristics of the components and exploring the new components is necessary.

There are three suggestions for the prototyping tool for interactive products or system with movement: First, the prototyping tools should provide examples or alternatives to support the ideation. Second, the prototyping tools should support the idea development process that contains the structure of the idea. It will be intuitive if the tool supports the development process in conjunction with sketches or hardware. Finally, the prototyping tools should support the interface that bridges the hardware and software. It supports the exploration and debugging process while allowing the user to manage complicated workflow.

6.6 Limitation & Future works

There is a limitation in the system, which tracks the physical object with the AR marker. We designed the character body to allow the character to move forward and backward by placing it perpendicular to the motor block. Still, adjusting the direction of the see-through application to track the AR marker is inconvenient. There was also a problem that hardware obstructed the AR marker when using multiple components. The camera vision can be adapted to track the physical object without the AR marker. In addition, it will be cost-effective to make the smartphone version many students already have. If students use AutomataStage at the same time, it will be able to develop into a collaborative tool [ 46 ]. With the augmented reality feature, it will be possible to support the process of discussing and combining individual tasks.

We conducted the user study with a short one-to-one one-day class. Although it is verified that there is a possibility of Interactive Automata as learning content and the effect of AutomataStage as a learning tool, improvement is required to apply it in an actual educational environment. First, it should be a deployable system. It is necessary to solve hardware durability, network stability, and battery problems for long-period usage. The database is also needed to manage the history of results of multiple students.

The curriculum should be developed, and the tool should be updated accordingly. Creating Interactive Automata using AutomataStage could increase students’ interest in learning. Although students can understand the basic principle and usage of sensors, motors, mechanisms, and programming, it is insufficient to understand detailed principles and apply them to other projects. The curriculum should be planned so students can learn more detailed principles and use components for various projects, and tools should be updated to support them.

7 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we presented AutomataStage, the AR-mediated creativity support tool for hands-on multidisciplinary learning. The see-through interface associated with the building blocks supports the whole creation process from idea development, interaction authoring, and mechanism simulation to making. AutomataStage supported the active exploration of sensors, motors, and mechanisms. The state transition programming method effectively integrates the components and workflow. The user study revealed that AutomataStage facilitated a rapid and rich creation of the Interactive Automata through idea development, exploration, and integration. We expect that this research will inspire the development of hands-on design tools that apply interactive and kinetic elements as an outcome.
