Agriculture is foundational for food security on our planet. Considering climate change and other pressures on food production, HCI scholars have increasingly begun to examine how the field should approach agricultural innovation. We conducted a literature review of HCI research through the lens of competing future visions for good food systems : a “conventional” vision of profit-oriented production, and an “alternative” which prioritizes sustainability and community-led practices. Leveraging the concept of sociotechnical imaginaries, we provide an empirical analysis of how HCI and adjacent applied computing projects align with these competing visions for agriculture. This review reveals, amongst other findings, a prioritization of the perspectives of the Global North and a need for more careful attention to the constraints and aspirations of subsistence farmers. Finally, we note the limits of the conventional-alternative binary that shapes much of contemporary HCI research focused on agriculture and offer opportunities for transcending this framing.

1 INTRODUCTION

The viability and security of global food systems is a serious concern for the future survival of humans and non-humans. Strategic directions for policies, services and technologies intended to support the future of food systems are commonly divided between conventional, industrialized agricultural practices and alternatives[19]. Conventional practices are rooted in Western scientific agricultural initiatives, which prioritize profitability and efficiency as driving values for agricultural innovation. The tractor, seed hybridization and Green Revolution development packages all exemplify such Western-rooted technologies, which were developed to increase the scale and efficiency of food production systems [ 68 , 166 ]. Alternative agricultural practices aim to sustain the natural environment, usually through the development of small-scale initiatives that rely on as few agricultural ‘inputs’ - such as chemicals and non-renewable resources - as possible [ 19 , 29 , 39 , 58 ].

These two visions are coherent and widespread, and have existed for over 60 years [ 81 ]. Scholars across disciplines describe these different, even divergent, visions as “competing agricultural paradigms” or “dominant vs. reformist visions” [ 19 , 39 ]. In a preliminary review of HCI literature addressing agriculture, we identified a similar pattern amongst HCI scholars, who described a binary between “organic vs. non-organic farming” and “agricultural intensification vs. sustainable agriculture.” This binary framing appears to be particularly used by scholars who are dedicated to sustainable agricultural practices and who wish to move away from industrialized farming [ 15 , 16 , 22 , 141 , 153 ]. Given the critical and urgent nature of a sustainability transition in food production, it is important to investigate how scholars in HCI and applied computer science domains such as robotics and IoT materially and conceptually address farming in their research. From our preliminary review, we identified a divergence in current HCI scholarship on farming in terms of research objectives, ideas, and sociotechnical outcomes. While differences are not inherently bad, building alliances and finding common ground for disciplinary objectives would be a significant contribution to HCI literature and practice as they would help to determine HCI's role in supporting and designing sustainable and just technological innovations for agriculture. Therefore, in this paper ask: What kinds of agricultural challenges does HCI address and how does the scholarship seek to meet these challenges through technological support and intervention? How do projects from HCI scholars align with these long-standing, competing agricultural paradigms or visions?

We turn to the concept of sociotechnical imaginaries to frame our investigation of these questions. Sociotechnical imaginaries is a framework grounded in Science and Technology Studies (STS) epistemological commitments that technology is not neutral but value-laden; shaped by the social, political, and economic values of those who make it [ 23 , 182 ]. Like paradigms, sociotechnical imaginaries are encompassing and widely held worldviews that are institutionally stabilized [ 19 , 23 , 95 , 104 ]. Sociotechnical imaginaries are future-oriented, “publicly performed visions of desirable futures, animated by shared understandings of forms of social life and social order attainable through, and supportive of, advances in science and technology” [ 95 ]. Within a sociotechnical imaginary framework, technologies are the result of, or materialize from, a sociotechnical imaginary, which shapes the values and motivations of human actors and institutions and the actions following from. In recent years, HCI scholars have begun using a sociotechnical imaginary framework to investigate competing worldviews and visions related to agriculture and our paper builds on this scholarship [ 72 , 160 , 161 ].

In this paper, we systematically map the existing landscape of ACM Library papers that focus on agriculture in two ways. First, we investigate and classify papers according to the agricultural visions and concerns proposed in the literature and how these ideas are materially met with technological support and design. Second, we consider how the corpus aligns with the two competing visions: conventional vs. alternative agriculture. In the first part of our analysis, we borrow from Jasanoff and Kim's process for sketching out and understanding sociotechnical imaginaries as (1) differences in opinions, ideologies, and technologies, (2) temporal changes and how they are interpreted, (3) spatial arrangements and scales , and (4) relationships between collective formations and individual identity [ 95 ]. In the second part of our analysis, we evaluate each paper according to Beus and Dunlap's key elements of the two competing agricultural paradigms [ 19 ] (Table1 ). We define agriculture as “the science, art or practice of cultivating the soil, producing crops, and raising livestock and in varying degrees the preparation and marketing of the resulting product” [ 46 ], and rely upon this definition to determine the scope of our review at the broadest level.

As researchers concerned about the future of our food systems, we are committed to more sustainable and socially equitable paths for technological development in agriculture. Our aim is to conduct this literature review to expose potentially polarizing viewpoints on agriculture and to identify any gaps or excluded agricultural imaginaries that may prevent us from working toward more ecologically sustainable and just systems of reform via HCI. In this paper, we first provide exegetical background on conventional and alternative agriculture as well as existing HCI research that addresses agriculture. In Section 4, we share the results of our literature review which we sorted using Beus and Dunlap's key competing agricultural paradigms and then analyzed using Jasanoff and Kim's [ 95 ] categorization of elements of “sociotechnical imaginaries” as an heuristic tool. In Section 5, we show that while the corpus indicates a stronger alignment with the alternative agriculture imaginary, there is still a significant number of papers that align with a conventional agriculture imaginary or that do not fit into either binary. In Section 6, we discuss the significance of the results from Section 4 and 5 and outline future disciplinary directions for HCI research that addresses agriculture.

From our analysis, we learned that geographic location, particularly between Global North and Global South regions, is strongly tied to specific agriculture site types, sociotechnical outcomes, and social actors. While papers in the Global North strongly align with one agricultural vision or the other, Global South subsistence farm needs and concerns are not sufficiently represented in conventional vs. alternative sociotechnical imaginaries. As approximately two-thirds of the Global South's 3 billion rural people live on subsistence farms, and this farm type was the most prominent agriculture site type represented in the corpus, we believe that is critical for the HCI community to focus more on how we may approach subsistence farming in our disciplinary vision for agriculture and the future of our food systems. For future HCI work addressing agriculture, we call for HCI scholars and practitioners to consider environmental justice as a lens and to also consider situating themselves and their proposed sociotechnical outcomes in their work. Considering the prominence of commercial farms particularly in Global North settings, we also found a lack of scholarly attention to these large-scale farm types and the many farm workers that are frequently employed there. Finally, given the prominence in the corpus of non-traditional project outputs that focus less on generating new technologies and more on establishing alternative ways of engaging with agriculture and nature, we believe it is important to rethink what constitutes a sociotechnical outcome. Rethinking what a sociotechnical outcome means offers us as space as HCI scholars to consider how some of these technologies, particularly the unsustainable and socially unjust ones, could be undone through research and design.

2 BACKGROUND

In this section, we outline the histories and practices of conventional vs. alternative agriculture to situate our review. We also describe the common subdomains that address agriculture in HCI: (1) Information and Community Technology for Development (ICTD), (2) Community Informatics, (3) Sustainable HCI (SHCI) and (4) Rural Computing. We focus on these HCI subdomains because each holds a set of epistemological assumptions and missions that influence how scholars in these areas engage with participants and design sociotechnical outcomes. These variations result in differing strategies for how scholars address food system innovation and transformation.

2.1 Conventional vs. Alternative Agricultural Practices

2.1.1 Conventional Agricultural History and Practices. Conventional agriculture can be defined as “capital-intensive, large-scale, highly mechanized agriculture with monocultures of crops and extensive use of artificial fertilizers, herbicides and pesticides, with intensive animal husbandry” [ 102 ]. These agricultural practices were initially spurred by post-WWI technological developments, which led to such agricultural innovations as the gasoline-powered combine harvester and tractor [ 68 ]. In the 1930s, the Great Depression and the Dust Bowl led the U.S., Canada and Western Europe to adopt agricultural protectionist policies. These policies sheltered farmers from volatile prices in exchange for the government's ability to purchase surplus crops at set prices [ 39 , 65 ]. Agricultural science institutions, set on building strategies for productivity and profitability increases, were also established out of these crises [ 65 ]. Compared to other industries such as textiles, where the production processes are carried out in controlled factory settings, technological advances in agriculture have been relatively slow and limited.

Liodakis [ 118 ] outlines four idiosyncratic material conditions which have prevented the agricultural sector from similarly commercially expanding as other industrialized labor spheres. First, the sector remains dependent on the natural environment for production. The environment cannot be completely anticipated nor controlled. Unlike insulated factory settings, agricultural production usually occurs outdoors, which means commodities are particularly vulnerable to environmental conditions. Second, different food commodities also demand individualistic production processes related to soil treatment, irrigation and so on, making it difficult to streamline a technological solution for multiple commodities. Third, technologies have restricted capacity to support those farming activities, particularly fruit harvesting, that demand both speed and careful dexterity [ 17 , 69 ]. Finally, there are a host of pressures to keep food an abundant and affordable resource for consumers; this creates a price cap on commodities, restricting the potential for capital accumulation and making the margins in agriculture very thin.

These conditions have influenced the historical development of agricultural technologies. While the impact of automation has not been as radically transformative as in other industries, technological developments have still drastically shaped the direction of the sector albeit in a piecemeal fashion [ 68 ].  The rise of manufacturing industries, lack of social security benefits, the effects of the “cost price squeeze” (the narrow gap between farm input costs and commodity prices) [ 42 , 63 ] have all contributed to many smallholder farmers in the U.S. and Canada to quit their practices. The remaining farmers have had to contend with the “treadmill of technology” [ 42 ], which refers to the cycle where farmers are encouraged to adopt new technologies to produce more commodities at lower unit production costs. Once more farmers adopt new technologies, commodity prices are depressed due to overproduction, further contributing to the cost price squeeze and the need for farmers to continue to adopt new technologies [ 31 ].

Western agricultural technologies have also had a significant impact from a development perspective. Agricultural protectionist acts of the 1930s eventually led to a massive grain surplus in North America following WWII. In response to these surpluses, the U.S., Canada, and Western Europe established food aid development programs , which created serious grain import dependencies for Global South countries [ 39 ]. When the World Food Crisis hit in the 1970s, Global South countries were no longer able to afford imported foods from these development programs [ 39 ]. At this time, the Cold War and the rise of communism across Asia created a volatile global political and economic environment, particularly for Global North countries trying to preserve the existing world order [ 44 ]. The U.S. government introduced Green Revolution packaging, which mainly consisted of fertilizers, pesticides, and hybrid seeds, as a means of feeding developing countries. The U.S. intended for these technologies to support growing Global South populations by increasing crop production and resilience [ 44 ]. But this food security was also seen as an antidote to communist uprisings; well-fed people were viewed as less likely to support regime change in favor of communism [ 39 , 44 , 166 ]. Despite creating abundance in certain commodities, scholars such as Shiva argue that Green Revolution technologies have led to the deterioration of native farming practices in countries like India where the land is left with “diseased soils, pest-infested crops, waterlogged deserts and indebted and discontented farmers” [ 166 ]. Hybrid seeds and newer agricultural biotechnologies are critiqued by Shiva and others [ 101 ] for decreasing biodiversity and reducing nutritional diversity and value [ 39 ].

The above described practices all fall under what can be described as a conventional imaginary for agriculture. As Clapp highlights, a conventional view of good farming points to improvements rather than radical changes to our current food system features. These incremental improvements include further investments in biotechnologies aimed at the Global South, firmer control over the environment using technologies like chemicals, and freer international trade [ 39 ]. These strategies for the future of our food systems inherit many of industrialized agriculture's historical legacies and perspectives.

2.1.2 Alternative Agricultural History and Practices . Alternative agricultural practices include “organic agriculture, sustainable agriculture, regenerative agriculture, eco-agriculture, permaculture, bio-dynamics, agroecology, natural farming, low-input agriculture and others [ 19 , 30 ]. In the Western world, organic farming originated in the 1940s with Sir Albert Howard, who developed the concept of soil fertility after many years working in agriculture research centers in India [ 81 ]. Howard popularized composting and developed an agricultural system that was later coined “organic” by Walter Northbourne [ 81 ]. In the 1940s, Jerome Rodale, an American publisher and early adopter of organic farming, began writing about organic farming practices in the U.S. [ 19 , 81 ]. It was not until the early 1960s in the U.S. when social and environmental concerns regarding agriculture began to take shape and influenced the reception of organic methods and even social movement activism supporting this style of farming. Organic farming became more nationally recognized in the U.S. in the 1980s. In 1990, the U.S. passed the Federal Organic Foods Production Act, establishing a national standard for organic products [ 81 ].

Fair Trade, Food Sovereignty and Global Food Justice Advocacy are all examples of alternative agriculture as social movements – ones that work together to restructure global food systems in an attempt to be more sustainable and socially equitable. These movements seek to create alternative supply chains that offer a stable and ecological means of engaging in international food trade, creating more localized and direct food systems, and initiating legal changes in governmental structures and food policies that enable the other elements [ 39 ]. In what could be called the alternative imaginary, our current global food system is thought best radically transformed or even dismantled. In this vision, alternative food production strategies ought to be widely adopted to solve for food access issues, distance in our food system, global food price volatilities, and the deterioration of the environment [ 39 ].

2.2 Common HCI Sub-Domains that Address Agriculture

In Section 4, we describe the ACM Library literature addressing agriculture in detail. First, however, we want to outline four HCI subdomains that commonly address agriculture: (1) Information and Communication Technology for Development ( ICTD), (2) Community Informatics, (3) Sustainable HCI (SHCI) and (4) Rural Computing. We describe these subdomains because each one varies in its epistemological commitments, geographic and spatial areas, design research practices and sociotechnical outcomes. These variations in turn shape their arguments regarding how to address change in our food systems.

ICTD projects typically focus on designing and implementing technologies that help with socioeconomic and international development initiatives [ 177 ]. The ICTD projects related to agriculture in the ACM Library are concentrated in the Global South. These projects generally aim to improve subsistence farmers’ livelihoods through increased access and support with information and resources. Projects are often framed in terms of the need to address poverty at the individual and national level as well as to respond to the demands of a rising population. Technologies are often designed and implemented using a top-down approach (conceived by the government, technology companies or other organizations and then actualize [ 72 , 175 ] although there are examples of ICTD projects that focus first on understanding farmer needs before moving to “tech solutionism” [ 38 , 72 , 145 , 183 , 187 ].

Community Informatics refers to participatory projects that focus on complex social or environmental issues such as homelessness or climate change [ 56 ]. Community Informatics projects are often volunteer-led and use bottom-up participatory action research or other democratic research methods. Many projects focus on the “smartification” of cities and the roles that people will play in these innovations [ 35 , 52 , 83 ]. Projects that address agriculture tend to take place in Global North urban environments. These projects are framed around urban population growth, the need for climate action, and the need to collectively imagine what the future of food systems should look like in urban places [ 35 , 36 , 83 , 84 , 85 , 179 , 188 ]. IoTs and ICTs are often employed to monitor and manage agricultural sites.

SHCI calls for researchers to direct their projects toward reducing material effects of technology, improving ways for maintenance and repair, encouraging people to behave more sustainably [ 25 , 26 , 51 , 131 ]. There is also a posthuman aspect of SHCI, which aims to decenter humans as a way of responding to climate change. This movement is rooted in feminist technoscience scholars and post-humanists such as Haraway [ 73 , 74 ], Puig de la Bellacasa [ 152 ], Braidotti [ 27 ], and Hayles [ 80 ] who offer post-anthropocentric approaches to design and climate action. SHCI papers addressing farming prioritize and design with community-oriented values on small organic farms or in urban garden settings [ 22 ]. This focus on values in design illustrates how SHCI projects often adopt a bottom-up participatory approach to research. As this domain is rooted in alternative agriculture principles, many SHCI scholars criticize HCI's focus on industrialization and encourage the discipline to move away from supporting commercial farming practices [ 16 , 22 ].

Rural Computing scholars are dedicated to “understanding, designing, and building computing technologies that are particular to the needs, aspirations, and practices of rural communities around the world” [ 76 ]. These scholars argue that HCI often adopts an excessively urban perspective in assuming how digital infrastructures work and what user needs are. As Hardy et al. [ 77 ] demonstrate, rural needs are unique in terms of digital infrastructure, location, culture, relationships to nature, and so on. Rural Computing scholarship is dispersed globally [ 76 ]. Projects that address agriculture range from Global South subsistence farm sites to Global North organic farms. These projects aim to understand smallholder farmers’ needs and values, and design for socially responsible and inclusive technological interventions [ 141 , 161 , 171 , 183 ].

3 METHODS

We carried out analysis of agriculture-related ACM Library literature in two phases. First, we conducted an in-depth survey of papers that addressed agriculture in the ACM library from 2009 to 2022. The aim of this analysis was to classify existing scholarship according to 15 factors such as farm type, location, social actor, and sociotechnical outcomes (Appendix 1). We leveraged Jasanoff and Kim's [ 95 ] four investigative questions for understanding for sociotechnical imaginaries – or widely held visions of technologically-mediated social change – using these questions to develop an heuristic tool that guided our analysis. Second, based on codes established from our first phase, we surveyed each paper again and coded them according to Beus and Dunlap's six key elements of competing agricultural paradigms [19] (Table 1). We chose Beus and Dunlap's key elements as a basis for evaluation because their representation of the competing agricultural paradigms is grounded in a thorough review of academic literature on farming practices and is considered a foundational text across different disciplines [ 19 , 20 , 21 ]. We note, however, that the authors created their representation of competing agricultural paradigms from a Western-centric corpus, which makes sense given that their scholarship is primarily situated in an American agricultural context [ 19 ]. The use of this framework assumes a capitalistic economic model for agriculture – one wherein farmers, even alternative farmers (e.g. organic), grow good for commodity exchange. We nonetheless rely upon Beus and Dunlap's framework not because it inclusively characterizes all farm types but because we think, based on our preliminary review of the HCI literature, that it is likely to allow us to capture HCI visions and areas of concern for agriculture. In our discussion, we hope to open up possibilities for extended scholarly imaginaries that may be excluded from the analytical framework; as well, we hope to interrogate the assumptions, values, and concerns underlying these two (conventional and alternative) paradigms.

3.1 Literature Survey

A systematic review of agriculture-related literature specific to the ACM Library is valuable to help HCI scholars and practitioners understand the current landscape of the corpus, draw connections between projects and subdomains and pursue research in critical or underdeveloped areas. This survey builds on the work of authors who have written directional overviews of agriculture in HCI [ 16 , 22 , 120 ] or have conducted narrower surveys specific to a certain technology or need [ 135 ] such as on e-commerce [ 117 ] or blockchain [ 49 ]. Our review is methodologically influenced by DiSalvo et.al.’s mapping of SHCI literature [ 51 ], and Soden et.al ’s review of SHCI literature that use speculative design methods [ 167 ], both of which limit their search to the ACM Library. We follow a similar process of using the background literature to determine key search words, to design a set of questions for analysis, and to inform our reading of each paper to answer our set questions and write narrative summaries. We acknowledge that by limiting our scope to the ACM Library, we exclude important farming-related projects such as Klerkx. Et.al.’s [ 98 ] review of social science literature on agritech and Parikh et.al.’s [ 146 ] survey of agricultural information systems in the Global South. Despite this limitation, we believe that the papers included in this survey provide a comprehensive, if not exhaustive, representation of agriculture-related HCI literature that is enough to map out key imaginaries, concerns, and outcomes.

We began by searching the ACM Library under the keywords “agriculture” and “farming”. We chose these keywords because we wanted to conduct a broad search of publications related to agriculture and technology. Our search initially produced 434 unique papers. We then carefully reviewed each paper abstract according to the following criteria: (1) is agriculture the central focus of the paper; (2) is the HCI community the intended audience? And (3) if HCI is not the intended audience, does this paper involve human or nature-computer interactions that involve farming? For this last criterion, we extend our interest to technologies that automate or simulate human roles or the environment.

In total we selected 107 papers to review: 87 project papers and 20 programmatic papers. Like Disalvo et al. [51], and Soden et al. [167], we define project papers as peer-reviewed journals, conference papers, or extended abstracts. Programmatic papers often aim to provide direction for a specific sub-domain such as SHCI. After selecting these papers, we developed an heuristic tool using key information from each paper and based on Jasanoff and Kim's line of inquiry for understanding sociotechnical imaginaries [95]: 1) why was the research conducted and what outcomes were proposed, (2) when did the research occur, (3) where did the research occur geographically and what kinds of agricultural sites are represented, and (4) who are the primary social actors (human or non-human) and how are their needs and values reflected in research's problem framing and outcomes? In total, we evaluated each paper according to 15 factors, which are listed in Appendix1 . We conducted multiple readings to ensure we agreed in our analysis and development of the codes, and we resolved any conflicts as a team. We then analyzed the papers according to how authors framed the significance of their research as well as their priorities and visions for agriculture.

3.2 Beus and Dunlap Binary Framework

Rather than attempt to accurately map all possible visions for agriculture, Beus and Dunlap use the representation of the two paradigms for “clarifying opposing positions, [and] facilitating comparisons” [ 19 ]. We rely upon Beus and Dunlap's key elements for this intended purpose of discussion and comparison. We feel it is valuable to use the binary framework to evaluate how the most common farm types in the literature (subsistence, commercial, small-scale organic, and urban gardening) are represented by the binary description.

3.3 Researcher Positionality

Explicitly stating researcher positionality is critical to situating the standpoints of scholars. This practice matters because it allows for readers to evaluate the direction and conclusions from a study. Even as part of a literature review, we feel it is important to share our backgrounds as we believe this contextual information is necessary for building a more critical and self-reflective dialog within agriculturally-specific literature, but also across HCI scholarship more generally. All three authors were born in Canada or in the U.S., which are both global northern and wealthy, democratic countries. All three authors are white settlers whose first language is English. One of the authors identifies as a male and the two others as females and all three are cis-gendered. Authors have combined academic backgrounds in Sociology, Computer Science, and Information Science and Environmental Studies. One author is an expert in Sociology of Technology specific to agricultural technologies (agtech) and one author is an expert in tech-related sustainability and development initiatives in the Global South. Although our backgrounds may not give us full access to the diversity and complexity of farming practices across the globe, our primary intent in this paper is to characterize how HCI research currently engages with agriculture. Throughout the analysis process for this literature review, we have attempted to remain self-reflective about the potential influence of our positions on our analysis.

4 RESULTS

In this section, we provide an overview of the corpus based on our heuristic inspired by Jasanoff and Kim's [ 95 ] process for sketching out and understanding sociotechnical imaginaries. Our aim in this investigation was to identify patterns and gaps that emerge in the agriculture-focused HCI literature by studying differences, changes, spaces, and social actors. From our analysis, we learned that subsistence farms in the Global South are the most prominent agricultural site in the corpus, followed by commercial farms, organic small-scale farms, and urban farms or gardens. These last three agricultural site types are mainly in the Global North. Most authors framed their papers around sustainability, pursuing technological opportunities, bridging knowledge gaps, and supporting farmer livelihoods. ICTs are the most common technology proposed, particularly in subsistence farm settings. Many projects, particularly those conducted by the SHCI community, did not generate new technologies or systems but rather focused on ways for HCI to support alternative farming practices. Less than half of the corpus mentions climate change and the papers that do tend to address organic or urban farm settings. Smallholder farmers are the most common primary social actors in the corpus.

4.1 Growing Research Area

The corpus of papers was published over a 14-year period, from 2009 to 2022 (Figure 1 ).Of 107 total papers, 87 are project papers and 20 are programmatic. 70% of the total corpus (75/107) were published in the past five years, indicating a significant increase in attention toward agriculture in HCI and adjacent areas. Additionally, 80% (16/20) of programmatic papers were published in the past five years. These programmatic papers include literature reviews on digital agriculture [ 172 , 178 ], workshop papers and design research papers that focus on how HCI may support sustainable agriculture missions [ 15 , 16 , 22 , 26 ]. This high volume of programmatic papers shows how HCI is in the process of determining how it should engage with agriculture. The most common venue, with 32 papers, was Human Factors in Computing (CHI) or a regional CHI-related venue such as OzCHI. Information and Communication Technologies and Developments (ICTD) was the second most common venue (13 papers), followed by Computer-Supported Cooperative Work and Social Computing (CSCW) (7), and Designing Interactive Systems (DIS) (6). Other included venues address areas like participatory design, human-robot interaction, and IoT. 32 papers were from venues that only appeared once. These venues mostly focused on applied computing domains such as information systems, business, and machine learning.

It is important to note that these numbers refer to the intended agricultural site rather than the sites where these projects were conducted. Many technological projects occurred in a lab or experimental farm settings rather than in a representative agricultural site that reflects where the technologies are meant to be used [ 85 , 86 , 87 , 161 ]. Many of the papers did not declare an intended site type or location [ 47 , 86 , 87 , 91 , 97 ].

Organic farms papers represent 47% of programmatic papers. This is a high percentage considering how organic farm papers form less than 20% of the included corpus on agriculture site types (19/102). Conversely, only five programmatic papers address subsistence farming (less than 20%) despite this farm type accounting for 39% of the corpus. These findings suggest that organic farms are overrepresented while subsistence farms are underrepresented in how scholars are promoting the future directions and outcomes for agriculture-related HCI research. From a geographic lens, these findings also illustrate how most programmatic papers are written by authors from the Global North where commodity rather than subsistence agriculture is the norm. Given that programmatic papers set agendas for how HCI scholars should engage with user groups, conduct research, and design sociotechnical outcomes, the concentration of organic farm papers in the Global North is significant.

4.3 Who is Farming?

Smallholder farmers are the most common primary social actor featured in HCI scholarship on agriculture (46 papers), followed by urban/community gardeners (21), actor replacement – when the actor is only referred to in context of how the technology will be replacing his or her labor – (10), technologists (7), and farm workers (6) (Figure 4 ).Nine papers do not specify a primary social actor. Smallholder farmers represent a mixture of subsistence farmers who grow food to survive and small-scale organic farmers who occasionally pursue farming as a full-time profession [ 16 , 98 , 109 , 121 , 122 , 123 , 171 ], but more often farm on a voluntary basis [ 140 , 142 ]. The economic, educational, and occupational agency differentiate smallholder organic farmers from subsistence farmers. Urban gardeners follow a similar trend to small-scale organic farmers: most are farming or gardening on a voluntary basis [ 82 , 83 , 84 , 85 ] or in an experimental setting such as an office [ 62 ], home [ 93 ] or publicly accessible fruit trees [ 51 ].

The 10 papers labeled with “actor replacement” [ 3 , 10 , 47 , 86 , 87 , 91 , 132 , 147 , 172 ], which occurs when the actor is only referred to in the context of labor automation, all address commercial farm settings. These papers are published in two main venue types: applied computing venues dedicated to machine learning or HCI venues focused on development such as ICTD . In the applied computing papers, authors depict watering, harvesting, weeding as “time-consuming” [ 3 ], “labor intensive” [ 86 ] jobs that are ripe for automation. Authors describe commodities (e.g. strawberries) as fragile and in need of human-assisted rather than automated production practices [ 18 , 173 ]. The balance between optimizing worker efficiency while respecting the fragility of commodities is exemplified through such projects as a wearable sensor system that trains workers to have defter movements when picking blueberries

[ 174 ] and a machine learning vision visor that helps workers quickly determine the appropriate height for cutting sugar cane [ 165 ]. To make these technologies, researchers follow a common model for design and experimentation in a lab setting, followed by testing with participants in a simulated environment or at a farm site. 18 of 25 commercial farming papers were published in applied computing venues, illustrating a lack of attention on farmworkers in HCI scholarship.

Finally, technologists represent the main social actor in five corpus papers. These people are typically developers who are experimenting with building more inclusive and customizable cloud technologies for farmers [ 161 ], greenhouse technicians [ 2 , 39 ] or HCI researchers contemplating best practices for how HCI should engage with farming [ 55 , 85 ].

4.4 How are Problems Framed and What Sociotechnical Outcomes are Proposed?

We address two elements in this sub-section: how authors describe the need for their projects and what corresponding sociotechnical outcomes are proposed. We use the category “sociotechnical outcome” rather than “technology” as not all authors aspire to create technologies. Instead, some researchers study how people interact with their existing surroundings with the aim of designing more inclusive tools and mitigating the risk of non-adoption [ 145 , 183 ] or redirecting HCI in new sustainable ways [ 15 , 16 , 22 , 26 , 119 , 121 , 122 , 123 ]. The most common frames used to describe problems are sustainability (37) followed by knowledge gaps (35), technological opportunity (33), farmer livelihood (31), profitability (29), community values (28), efficiency (25), population growth (22), profitability (29) and HCI discipline opportunity (20) (Figure5 ). Pressures related to industrial farms, labor shortages, literacy challenges and the need for environmental control were also often mentioned. The most common sociotechnical outcomes are ICTs (39), followed by IoTs (21), farmer expertise (19), mobile apps (19), machine learning (15) and design methods (10) (Figure6 ). Image recognition and robotics are also common sociotechnical outcomes.

Authors describing organic or urban agricultural sites typically frame their papers around sustainability and climate action outcomes. They argue for the need for HCI to nurture posthuman or human-nature relations [ 16 , 22 , 119 , 121 , 122 , 123 ], to combat agricultural industrialization [ 14 , 15 , 16 , 119 , 120 , 140 , 141 ], to engage in community-led research practices [ 35 , 36 , 51 , 83 , 84 , 141 , 171 ], and to support the needs of alternative agriculture farmers and gardeners [ 140 , 141 , 142 , 171 ]. Ardianto's [ 13 ] paper on the use of social media in urban farming communities exemplifies how sustainability and community-led values are often intertwined: “Climate change is an increasingly urgent, complex problem, with consequences threatening human and non-human lives across the globe. Legislative and citizen-driven responses are valuable but insufficient, and their practical feasibility is unclear.period” In support of climate action missions, Ardianto advocates for more community-led design research methods that support climate action. Community values are often tied with sustainability missions, which is exemplified in how many SHCI researchers adopt bottom-up participatory design research approaches in their work [ 1 , 16 , 35 , 51 , 82 , 109 , 121 , 122 , 13 , 140 ].

While papers centered on urban and organic farming tend to emphasize and enhance farmer expertise, many papers addressing subsistence farming focus on the opposite – how to address knowledge deficits. Common knowledge gaps include lack of access to farming information related to product pricing, weather conditions and supplies such as seeds and fertilizers [ 10 , 14 , 34 , 37 , 54 , 88 , 143 , 156 , 184 ], lack of communication with extension officers [ 1 , 143 , 148 ], and literacy challenges [ 4 , 37 , 88 , 89 , 92 , 138 , 169 ]. As subsistence farmers depend upon agriculture to survive, papers are often framed around the need to support farmers with resources to enable better agricultural decision-making, such as just-in-time market pricing information [ 100 , 129 ], which in turn allows for profit increases. The following quote, from Jain et al.’s [ 92 ] ICT project in India, exemplifies a common framing pattern for subsistence farm papers: “Farmers constitute 54.6% of the Indian population but earn only 13.9% of the national GDP. This gross mismatch can be alleviated by improving farmers' access to information and expert advice.” The author couples farmers’ low income status with the need to improve access to information. The proposed solution to this knowledge gap is “FarmChat”, a conversational agent used to answer farmers’ questions. Similar to other ICTs developed for subsistence farmers [ 1 , 138 , 143 , 148 ], “FarmChat” uses voice recognition technology to support literacy and digital challenges. ICTs or ICT-related research such as on low smart phone adoption [ 145 , 183 ] are proposed in all subsistence farm papers as sociotechnical outcomes. Mobile apps (16) are the most common device used by farmers to access these ICTs.

For papers that address commercial farming, the most prominent problem framing areas are efficiency (15) followed by technology opportunity (8), and population growth (6). Machine learning (9), robotics (7) and image recognition (6) are the most common sociotechnical outcomes proposed to address these needs. Most of these outcomes were developed in lab setting (19/25) and (14/ 25) focus on agricultural production surrounding a single commodity such as strawberries [ 47 , 86 , 87 ], mushrooms [ 91 ] or maize [ 3 , 9 ].

4.5 How is Climate Change Considered?

Given the significance of climate challenges, we labeled whether each paper explicitly addresses climate change. We found that 42 papers mention climate change and 65 do not. Figure7 illustrates that most papers situated in the Global North, that is, North America (10/14), Europe (11/18), and Australia (3/4), explicitly reference climate change. Most papers in the Global South, that is, Africa (11/13), and Asia (31/37) do not. These findings also map onto climate change analysis by the most common farming site types . Figure8 shows an inverse relationship between the four agricultural site types: organic and urban farm projects are more likely to mention climate change while subsistence and commercial farming papers are much less likely to mention it. These results are unsurprising given that most organic and urban farming papers frame their papers around sustainability and climate change and are situated in the Global North. The results, however, do not reflect the current threats of climate change, particularly for subsistence farmers.

5 CORPUS IN RELATION TO COMPETING AGRICULTURE PARADIGMS

In this section, we evaluate the corpus papers according to Beus and Dunlap's key elements of competing agricultural paradigms [ 19 ] (Table 1). We conduct this analysis so that we can better understand how HCI scholarship addressing agriculture is represented and unrepresented by these two prominent visions for our food systems.

5.1 Conventional vs Alternative Agriculture Binaries Across the Corpus

As shown in Figure9 , we coded all 107 papers according to the six binary elements outlined in Table1 , creating a total of 642 possible codes. Each paper was coded six times according to the six binary elements. Possible codes in each of these instances were either conventional, alternative, or uncategorized. Papers were assigned only one code for each of the six elements. Across the entire corpus, 52% of all codes (336) correspond with alternative farming practices, 30% (189) correspond with conventional farming practices and 18% (117) were coded as uncategorized. These results indicate that while the alternative agricultural elements are the most prominently represented in the literature, the corpus still aligns with some conventional elements or could not be grouped according to either paradigm. We unpack these findings below.

The first binary elements, Centralization vs. Decentralization , address how production processes, marketing, actors, land, resources, and capital are distributed. We coded 78% of papers (83/107) with Decentralization , which indicates that most papers focused on local production processes and serving the individual needs of farmers. The second binary, Dependence vs. Independence , considers whether farmers rely upon larger capitalist actors and specialist experts for the resources, technologies, and knowledges needed to conduct farming activities, or if farmers are self-sufficient in attaining these assets. We coded 76% (81/107) with Independence showing that most papers focus on supporting self-sufficient farming practices and resources. The third binary elements, Competition vs. Community , focus on how farm work is perceived and carried out by farmers. For these elements, we coded 40% (43/107) with Competition and 54% (58/107) with Community indicating that the literature was divided on how authors framed farm work and intra-farm cooperation. As we will describe further in Section 6, this division is likely occurring because of the differences between the farm types that relate to differences in global development contexts. Papers coded with Competition tend to align with papers addressing subsistence farming as these projects aim to support farmers’ livelihoods and individual self-interests as a basis for survival. Papers coded with Community , meanwhile, are often papers written about organic small-scale or urban gardening settings in the Global North where people are often farming on a voluntary basis or as a chosen way of life.

The fourth binary elements, Domination of Nature vs. Harmony with Nature address farmers’ perceived relationship with nature and the kinds of nature-based vs. synthetic practices and resources with which they engage. The fifth binary elements, Specialization vs. Diversit y, consider the types of production systems that farmers are using, evaluating if farmers engage in monoculture vs. polyculture systems or if they use standardized vs. locally adapted production systems. The final binary elements, Exploitation vs. Restraint consider the sustainability of the resources and production processes used over the long-term as well as people's lifestyles and rationale for wanting to farm. These final three binary elements reveal a common pattern: papers are fairly evenly distributed between conventional, alternative, and uncategorized codes. This dispersion of codes indicates that the corpus is divided in how it addresses humans’ relationships with nature as well as the types of agricultural resources and production systems that are used.

5.2 Conventional vs Alternative Agriculture Binaries by Agricultural Site Type

To further understand the results from the binary analysis, we will now consider this analysis in relation to the findings about the four most common farm types in the HCI literature: (1) subsistence farms, (2) commercial farms, (3) small-scale organic farms and (4) urban farms and gardens. As previously noted, subsistence farms make up 39% of the total corpus, followed by commercial farms (25%). Organic farm papers make up 19% of the corpus and urban farming papers, 18%. As shown in Figure10 , subsistence farming papers have a total of 28% (67/240) conventional codes, 37% (88/240) alternative codes and 35% (85/240) uncategorized. While there are more total alternative codes for subsistence farms, these codes are concentrated within two key elements: Decentralization and Independence . Given how ICTs that address knowledge gaps are the primary sociotechnical outcome in subsistence farm papers, it is unsurprising that the papers are prominently coded with these two elements because they connect to assumptions that ICTs will support self-sufficiency and the development of personal knowledge and skills. Subsistence farming papers are evenly coded between Competition and Community. This division could reflect how many subsistence farm papers focus on filling knowledge gaps [ 9 , 156 ] while others develop ICTs that encourage peer-to-peer knowledge sharing [53.143, 144 , 148 , 149 ].

For the three final binaries, Domination over Nature vs Harmony with Nature, Specialization vs Diversity , and Exploitation vs Restraint , we coded most papers as uncategorized. The prominence of uncategorized codes shows that many subsistence papers do not address relationships with nature, growing practices, or crop types. The volume of uncategorized codes also shows how Beus and Dunlap's six elements do not adequately represent the needs and values highlighted in subsistence farming papers. For example, subsistence farm papers are more commonly coded with the conventional binary elements of Domination over Nature and Exploitation. As subsistence farmers rely upon farming to survive, they need to prioritize crop productivity and profitability. Furthermore, many subsistence farm papers are in India, Pakistan, and Kenya, countries that are still dependent upon Green Revolution resources, which are heavily rooted within conventional agricultural practices. Given that about a third (12/40) of subsistence farm papers are published in ICTD , a development-focused venue, the HCI corpus does not focus enough on the pressures and tactics of international development aid and how these elements often determine agricultural practices in the Global South.

As shown in Figure11 , commercial farm papers generally align with the key elements of conventional agriculture (76% (114/144). 15% (22/144) of codes align with alternative agriculture and 9% (13/144) are uncategorized. The low number of uncategorized labels indicates that the commercial farm papers align strongly with conventional element descriptions. Figure12 illustrates organic farms papers’ relationship with the competing agriculture elements and Figure13 shows the same thing for urban gardens and farms. Both figures strongly align with alternative agriculture elements with organic farm papers aligning 96% (109/114) and urban farm papers aligning 88% (95/108) of the time. Both farm types also have a low number of uncategorized labels (4% for organic farm papers and 7% for urban farm papers), indicating that the key elements align strongly with how authors describe agricultural values and production processes. There is a small cluster of urban garden papers that align with Domination over Nature . These papers are framed around urban population growth and the need to grow food indoors as a way to enhance food security [ 62 , 179 ].

6 DISCUSSION

This section contains reflections and recommendations that are directed toward HCI researchers who focus on agriculture but also could be extended to researchers more broadly situated in SHCI, Rural Computing, and ICTD. We begin by discussing what the competing imaginaries within the literature indicate about the corpus. We found that corpus papers situated in Global North settings are more likely to align strongly with one of the two competing imaginaries: while papers focused on commercial farm contexts are firmly linked with conventional agriculture, those on organic and urban farms strongly align with alternate agriculture. Subsistence farm papers, however, which are dominantly situated in the Global South, are insufficiently accounted for in either imaginary. Indeed, across the literature we observed a bias toward farm systems which presuppose a capitalist food system wherein even alternative farmers or those considered non-dominant are farmers growing crops for sale (as opposed to bartering or subsistence production). We suspect these incongruities stem from misaligned definitions of “need” and “profit” and transnational entanglements between Global North and South in terms of agricultural production needs, labor power, and trade. In the second part of our discussion, we describe recommendations for future HCI work on agriculture. These suggestions include expanding problem framing beyond conventional and alternative agriculture imaginaries, defining what sociotechnical outcomes should look like for agriculture in HCI, and expanding HCI's focus beyond small-scale farms and farm owners.

6.1 What Do the Competing Sociotechnical Imaginaries Tell Us About HCI Literature on Agriculture?

One of our central aims in writing this paper was to identify how HCI literature on agriculture is represented by two competing imaginaries of conventional vs. alternative agriculture practices. This binary is influential and widely adopted within food studies, critical social sciences, and, as we have shown, within HCI, as a way of framing current and future food system pathways [ 15 , 19 , 20 , 22 , 39 , 153 ]. In Section 5, we demonstrated how farm sites located in the Global North, which formed the majority of papers written on commercial, small-scale organic and urban gardening farm types, strongly align with one imaginary or another. As expected, papers addressing commercial farms strongly mapped to conventional agriculture elements. Conversely, papers addressing organic or urban farms strongly align with alternative agriculture practices. For subsistence farming papers situated in the Global South, however, we did not identify strong alignments overall with either conventional or alternative agriculture (Figure10 ). We actually found that a significant portion of farm papers, 35% (85/240), were left uncategorized, meaning that they were not represented within the binary framework.

This lack of representation of subsistence farming papers in Beus and Dunlap's binary framework does not come as a surprise given that Beus and Dunlap's elements are built from a Western-centric approach to agriculture – one that assumes a capitalist economic model. For one, Beus and Dunlap's rubric assumes that food products, even when grown at a small scale, are consumer commodities intended to be sold for profit. The economic, social, and political assumptions embedded in the framework likely do not hold within the context of subsistence farming, where many farmers are growing and selling for their own consumption or in local bartering economies. Unpacking HCI's subsistence farming papers in relation to Beus and Dunlap's framework is important because these findings reveal critical information for establishing new scholarly and practitioner imaginaries outside of the typical binary of conventional vs. agriculture farming.

In Figure10 , we found that subsistence farm literature aligns with the alternate binary elements of Decentralization and Independence . These symmetries make sense given the corpus’ focus on supporting farmer livelihoods by building ICTs to provide access to knowledge and resources. On the other hand, however, subsistence farm papers are more likely to match the conventional binary elements of Competition and Domination over Nature and Exploitation . These elements suggest that farmers perceive farm labor as “drudgery” and primarily focus on financial livelihood in their agricultural activities. These elements also propose - either through the researchers’ project framing, research findings or recommended sociotechnical outcomes - that humans should control nature with synthetic materials. We believe that the subsistence farm corpus’ perceived alignments, particularly with conventional binary elements, occur for two reasons: (1) from incongruent definitions of key terms such as “profitability” and “need” across development contexts and (2) from how subsistence farming practices have inherited resources and production practices from Western scientific agricultural initiatives.

One example of the incongruity of Beus and Dunlap's terms is from a description for Exploitation : “Financial success; busy lifestyles; materialism” [ 19 ]. The alternative to this is “Self-discovery; simpler lifestyles; nonmaterialism” [ 19 ]. These statements assume that farmers are not only able to sustain themselves through their business but also make a profit, which is not the case with many subsistence farmers who are often poor and food insecure [ 154 ]. In another set of binary descriptions for Exploitation , the authors describe “Short-term benefits outweigh long-term consequences” [ 19 ] vs. “Short-term and long-term outcomes equally important” [ 19 ]. These statements imply that to focus on short-term benefits means that farmers value profitability over long-term sustainability. Excluded from consideration is how many subsistence farmers are forced to focus on short-term benefits for survival. As ICTD scholar Kentaro Toyama shows us, “needs” in the development context address elements that are critical for survival such as nutrition and health [ 177 ]. This definition greatly varies from how needs are defined in technology design contexts, where user needs are equated with consumer choices [ 177 ]. While HCI was not the primary audience for Beus and Dunlap's competing key elements, Toyama's point can still be extended here as we have found that HCI does generally fall within the competing paradigms and HCI thus caters to Western understandings of need.

The second reason why we think ACM research on subsistence farms may share similarities with conventional agriculture is because international development aid and colonialism, dominant industrial agricultural forces, have shaped contemporary food production in many parts of the Global South. Green Revolution seed-chemical packages are technologies created by Western based institutions with the objective of increased productivity of global commodities through heavy chemical-usage. As Shiva [ 166 ] illustrates, these technologies have caused devastating decreases in biodiversity, furthered financial inequalities between subsistence farmers and larger food system actors (e.g. chemical supply firms) and created environmental dependencies on foreign aid packaging for successful production. Furthermore, during the World Food Crisis of the 1970s, the U.S. government refused to supply India with food relief supplies until the Indian government agreed to adopt Green Revolution packaging [ 39 ]. These conditions suggest that subsistence farms’ alignment with conventional farming practices coincides with and reflects colonial influences of Global North countries vis-à-vis their development qua commercial agricultural packages and strategies. We are not, however, saying these farm types are similar for the most part. The alignment between subsistence farms and conventional agricultural practices only suggests a common dedication to synthetic agricultural materials and standardized production processes. There are clearly vast differences between these farming contexts in terms of profitability, scale, resources, and commodity-types.

Now that we have addressed why we believe subsistence farm papers align with descriptions of conventional agriculture, we turn to why so many subsistence farm papers were coded as uncategorized in our schema. Papers were most often uncategorized for the last three binary elements which focus on the natural environment, and perceptions of science and technology. We noticed that many of papers do not consider farmers’ relationships with nature, growing practices or production resources. We suspect that the lack of attention to these topics is owing to a focus among ICTD authors on the areas of greatest need in subsistence farming, which often relate to bridging knowledge gaps regarding pricing or weather information. These knowledge gaps also often support gaining access to foreign agricultural resources such as fertilizers and seeds. The need for these imported supplies exemplifies how development initiatives have played and continue to hold a critical role in shaping Global South farming toward conventional agricultural practices.

In summary, by analyzing the corpus in relation to Beus and Dunlap's key elements of the agricultural paradigms, we learned that papers situated in Global North settings generally strongly align with one paradigm or the other. Papers on commercial farms are firmly linked with conventional agriculture practices. Papers addressing organic or urban farms have even stronger attachments to alternate agricultural practices. Beus and Dunlap's framework does not, however, sufficiently represent Global South subsistence farm needs and concerns. We suspect that unlike other farm types located in the Global North, Global South subsistence farms operate under vastly different sociopolitical and economic circumstances, namely farming for survival rather than profit. These different conditions reveal incongruities in Beus and Dunlap's binary rubric, particularly in how profitability, need and efficiency are defined. Furthermore, we suspect that alignments between conventional agricultural practices and subsistence farms can be explained by colonialist transnational relationships where Western scientific agricultural initiatives have influenced how agricultural production operates in the Global South. Given that subsistence farms made up the largest agriculture site type in the selected corpus and that approximately two-thirds of the Global South's 3 billion rural people live on subsistence farms [154], it is critical that the HCI community turn its attention to how we can include the needs, hopes and concerns of subsistence farmers in how we plan for the discipline's engagement with agriculture as well as imagine the future of our food systems.

6.2 Recommendations for Future HCI Work in Agriculture

6.2.1 Expand Problem Framing Beyond Competing Agricultural Paradigms. We have demonstrated how many needs, particularly those of Global South subsistence farmers, are left out of visions for technology-mediated social change imagined by HCI researchers and practitioners. Given that HCI scholars appear to often rely on the binary imaginaries to frame research related to agriculture, it is important to expand this framing to include subsistence farming needs and contexts. For example, we may need to reconsider the value judgements we place on such terms as “profitability” and “efficiency”, which are often associated with Western large-scale commercial farming businesses. These terms hold different meanings in subsistence farm contexts or simply are not applicable in the same ways. A subsistence farmer in the Global South may need to primarily focus on the profitability and efficiency of their agricultural practice to survive, which represents a very different circumstance to how these priorities are understood in an industrialized agriculture context.

As design researchers dedicated to designing sociotechnical outcomes that are both sustainable and socially equitable, we believe that one way forward is for HCI researchers to attend in greater detail to the sociopolitical and economic contexts of the field sites and social actors under study. For example, SHCI authors investigating how HCI should support more-than-human design practices for agriculture could first establish that their ability to move in these exploratory directions depends on the financial and political stability of the social actors being studied. Alternately, an ICTD scholar may acknowledge the inability to focus on mitigating climate change in their project because of the pressing realities of needing to support farmers living day-to-day.

We also believe that HCI scholars can expand the problem framing of their research beyond the current binary by broadening what constitutes “alternative” farming practices. For example, there is an entire area of alternate farming practices that is not represented in Beus and Dunlap's framework nor in the selected HCI corpus – this is one that relates to fair and direct trade as well as to food sovereignty movements [ 139 ]. Peasant-led movements situated in Latin America such as Via Campesina [ 106 ] exemplify an alternative vision for agriculture situated in food sovereignty. The term “food sovereignty” was first used in response to how Global North's food systems dominated the global food market in terms of consumption and consumerism [ 39 ]. At World Trade Meetings in the late 1990s, Global South farmers began insisting on the term “food sovereignty” as a means of responding to their financial inability to compete with Global North subsidized food staples [ 40 ]. Groups like Via Campesina attempt to draw attention to limitations in how Global North actors define food security, which often does not include the right to particular forms of peasant and subsistence production methods, which are grounded in sustaining local social and environmental relationships alongside producing commodities that can be bought and sold [ 106 ]. These peasant-led movements are not well-represented by the reigning imaginaries in HCI and thus reflect a gap in work that operates outside of a Western-centric economic system and that aims to develop alternative systems for food security in peasant and bartering economies. Indeed, it is noteworthy that only three out of 107 selected papers for this review focus on farming in Central or Latin America.

In this regard, we see environmental justice principles and commitments as useful resources for HCI researchers to help the shift the alternative agricultural paradigm to be more inclusive of peasant and subsistence farming. We believe environmental justice commitments may allow HCI researchers and practitioners to capture a broader cultural definition of food system sustainability. Although it is not prominent in the literature included in this corpus, advocating for social justice in environmental contexts is a key objective of SHCI [ 56 , 103 ]. Environmental justice not only seeks to reduce environmental waste, but also aims to support marginalized communities through socioeconomic redistribution, legal and cultural recognition, and political representation [ 5 , 63 ]. For example, HCI scholars engaging with farmers could adopt environmental justice principles in some of the following ways to include a wider array of farmer types and farm strategies: (1) contextualizing national and local histories [ 168 ], (2) identifying the impacts of agricultural production methods and resources on humans and the environment and tracing said impacts back to colonial influences when relevant, and (3) tracking power dynamics between farmers and other relevant social actors as a means of shifting the status quo. Furthermore, addressing the inequitable distribution in impacts of climate change could be one way to shift the focus toward an environmental justice frame and draw greater attention to Global South agricultural systems in the scholarship. This attention is important given how Global South is particularly vulnerable to environmental harms such as floods, desertification, extreme weather events, coastal erosion, and other environmental disasters [ 178 ]. These conditions make it even harder for subsistence farmers to grow crops and care for livestock. Ongoing environmental crises currently cause an average of 20 million ‘climate refugees’ to leave their homes each year [178].

6.2.2 Manage Researcher Positionality And The Materiality Of Technologies. When analysing the corpus, we attempted to geographically situate the researchers and the proposed sociotechnical outcomes. We also tried to identify how researchers positioned themselves in relation to their projects by asking the following questions: Why is this project a topic of scholarly concern for the authors? What are the authors’ relationships to the primary social actors and to the field site? These questions draw from Jasanoff and Kim's [ 95 ] process for sketching out and understanding sociotechnical imaginaries. The questions also align with decolonial computing commitments which seek to locate both body-politics (who is speaking on behalf of whom?) and geo-politics of knowledge (where are actors and technologies located and how do they travel?) [ 7 ]. These interrogations are important because researchers may be divorced from the realities of farm lives and environments. As Hardy et al. [ 77 ] observe in their methodological recommendations for designing for rural settings, technological hubs and universities are often based in urban settings, which can create urban biases for research and design. This separation may be particularly acute in ICTD papers where Global North researchers often engage in Global South subsistence farming contexts [ 14 , 53 , 54 , 92 , 169 ].

Overall, we found that very few authors explicitly located themselves in their papers by addressing the above questions. A handful of papers, particularly those engaging in autobiographical design methods [119.120, 161 ], include details regarding how their researcher position guides and influences the research process. For example, Liu [ 120 ] describes how their position as a racialized minority led to “awkward moments of fieldwork” for both them and participants, who were “overwhelmingly white.” Rubambiza et al. [ 161 ] share how as technologists, they wish to create the “seamless visions” for farmers who want to be able to operate and customize technologies on their farms. The authors share how these visions are hard to manifest because of logistical limitations but also because they are too far removed from farmer perspectives and environments to be able to identify what is useful for the farmers. Such critical reflections situate the researchers in their field sites and technological practices and generate important insights regarding racial tensions on U.S. farms and the unanswered questions and uncertainties that developers face when designing technologies in isolation from end users .

Except for select “posthuman SHCI” which focus on farmer expertise such as Liu et al. [ 122 ]’s “nature” tools to support permaculture practices, most authors, particularly those designing technologies as sociotechnical outcomes, do not share where their technologies, materially, come from. Most studies position technologies as inert physical artifacts, often isolated from their material origins. The lack of attention to material sources of technologies in development is an important exclusion as technologies are always dependent upon processes of “resource extraction, exchange, management, and consumption” [ 61 ]. Identifying sources and material components of technology design and development can also help with repair and maintenance. Holding the tools and knowledge of how to fix technologies is a significant concern for smallholder farmers in the Global North and Global South alike [ 31 , 72 ]. For example, in their investigation of farmworkers’ hopes and concerns regarding the implementation of drones on a commercial farm site in Rwanda, Hanrahan et al. [ 72 ] describe how the drones are owned and operated by a Japanese tech company. International technological interventions are common in subsistence farm contexts [ 1 , 148 , 184 ], and these transnational arrangements raise important questions regarding technological decision-making and maintenance.

6.2.3 Define What Constitutes A Sociotechnical Outcome For Agriculture. As explained earlier in this paper, we used the term “sociotechnical outcome” instead of technology because many of the corpus project outputs were design process recommendations, implications, provocations, visions, or methods. Most of these outputs resulted from SHCI-oriented papers that address organic or urban farm contexts and utilize community-led participatory design methods as a way of supporting and enriching alternative farm practices. These outputs are not generative in a traditional HCI sense of problem solving through creating new technologies or systems. It is worth considering how these projects fit into HCI's definition of digital agriculture. While many papers that address commercial and subsistence farm settings continue to design new technologies, papers aligned with alternative food practices seem to shift further away from these types of outcomes. We ask: what is enough of a technological intervention or output in agriculture to be considered HCI? If generating new technologies is not the answer, what are other ways that can HCI scholars can support sustainable and equitable future food systems?

6.2.4 Consider Commercial Farm Settings And Farm Workers More. By conducting a review of the ACM Library that included both HCI papers and applied computing papers that address technologies that automate or simulate human roles or the environment, we identified that farm workers besides the farmer (e.g. migrant fruit picker) are important actors that are commonly excluded from the HCI literature. Meanwhile, many applied computing papers addressing commercial farms focused on farm workers as the primary actor or aimed to replace them via automation. Given that small-scale farms in the U.S. make up less than 10% of total output (for organic farms, this was only 1% as of 2013) [ 24 ], it is notable that there is a lack of attention on commercial farms in HCI literature. Farm workers make up most of commercial agriculture's workforces [ 163 ]. In 2018 in Canada, over half of all farm workers were temporary foreign employees from Mexico, the Caribbean and other Global South countries [ 17 , 168 ]. Like the U.S, Canada relies on racialized and undervalued temporary agriculture labor forces to maintain its food systems [ 120 , 163 ].

While we are not encouraging scholars to perpetuate the unsustainability and social injustices embedded in many industrialized agricultural settings, we do think that as a discipline HCI should provide a counterpoint to the imaginaries generated in applied computing domains that often advocate for increased worker efficiency or actor replacement via automation as preferred farm workforce strategies. Furthermore, even if there is a consensus that alternative agricultural practices are the right path forward for HCI, we must remain grounded in the current reality that for researchers in the Global North, large-scale commercial farming remains the dominant mode of production. Rather than researching around this fact, we should also develop tactics to better understand how technologies designed for commercial settings work, and how they are impacting the actors who are using them. For example, in their discourse analysis of agtech start-up websites in the U.S., Steup et al. [ 171 ] critically assess the practices and imaginaries of commercial agtech by questioning how start-ups define their own sustainability data indicators. By self-determining data indicators, start-ups run the risk of setting inadequate sustainability targets, which could undermine rather than support climate action. Steup et al.’s work [ 171 ] is a good example of how we think HCI scholars can study and, when appropriate, counter commercial agricultural innovations.

We may also consider how some automated labor technologies, particularly the unsustainable and socially unjust ones, could be undone through design. One way forward for HCI could be a strategic step back to reconsider how technologies can be made in ways that do not inherit the profit-driven and racist legacies of agriculture sciences and undervalued workforces [ 120 ]. How might HCI sociotechnical outcomes support migrant farm workers in commercial farm settings who are restricted to compensation and labor equity requirements well below legal national labor standards [ 69 , 163 ]? The Global North's dependency on temporary farm labor from the Global South exemplifies transnational entanglements between commercial and subsistence farm settings. This assemblage shows how competing conventional and alternative agricultural sociotechnical imaginaries do not fully capture the global reality of today's food production systems. These entanglements could be an important site for HCI to explore how the principles and commitments of environmental justice can further interrogate the equity and sustainability of industrialized agriculture.

7 CONCLUSION

As researchers concerned about the future of our food systems we are committed to more sustainable and socially equitable paths for technological development in agriculture. We conducted this literature review to investigate the different and often polarizing viewpoints on agriculture within HCI scholarship and practice and to point out any gaps in HCI that may prevent us from working toward positive food systems reform. We were particularly curious to see how ACM Library papers that address agriculture were represented and unrepresented by the two competing sociotechnical imaginaries of conventional vs alternative farming. We analyzed sociotechnical imaginaries in two major ways: we first evaluated the corpus according to Jasanoff and Kim's [ 95 ] four investigatory lenses of difference, change, space and relationships between collective formations and individual identity. We then coded each paper according to Beus and Dunlap's binary framework (Table1 ).

We learned that geographic location, particularly between Global North and Global South regions, is strongly tied to specific agricultural site types, sociotechnical outcomes and social actors. Papers in the Global North strongly align with one dominant agricultural vision or the other. Papers on commercial farms are firmly linked with conventional agriculture practices and ideologies. Papers addressing organic or urban farms have even stronger attachments to alternate agriculture. Global South subsistence farm needs and concerns, however, are not sufficiently represented in conventional vs. alternative sociotechnical imaginaries. Given that approximately two-thirds of the Global South's 3 billion rural people live on subsistence farms, and that this farm type was the most prominent agricultural site represented in the corpus, it is critical that the HCI community turn its attention to how we can approach subsistence farming in our disciplinary vision of how to focus on agriculture and imagine the future of our food systems.

For future HCI work addressing agriculture, we recommend that HCI scholars consider environmental justice as a lens and for researchers to situate themselves and their proposed sociotechnical outcomes in their work. We also suggest that HCI researchers more readily address commercial farm settings and farm workers as a user group, as these are under-looked areas in HCI. Global North commercial farms particularly complicate the distinction between conventional versus alternative agriculture as these industrial farms depend on temporary transnational workforces of Global South farm workers to operate. In the Global North, we have workers from subsistence farm backgrounds employed temporarily in for-profit conventional agricultural businesses. The imbalances in power relations and incongruities in what constitutes a decent wage and labor conditions raise critical questions of how innovation develops in and across these contexts [ 134 ]. Finally, we ask HCI researchers to consider what sociotechnical outcomes should look like for agriculture. Our results revealed that authors turned to non-traditional project outputs in part because these outputs reflected farmer interests, and also because many authors are intentionally trying to shift the discipline toward alternative ways of engaging with agriculture and nature. Rethinking what constitutes a sociotechnical outcome also offers us space to consider how some of these technologies, particularly the unsustainable and socially unjust ones, could be undone through design or extravention.

A APPENDICES

We share the 15 questions that we used for the first phase of evaluation of the 107 papers included in our survey. These questions were informed by Jasanoff and Kim's [ 95 ] four investigative questions for understanding sociotechnical imaginaries.

A.1 15 Questions for the First Phase of Literature Review Analysis

  1. What year was the paper published in?
  2. What venue was the paper published under?
  3. What is the research objective or question?
  4. Where is the research conducted? (in terms of geographic location as well as urban/rural settings)
  5. Where are the researchers located?
  6. Who is the primary social actor?
  7. What type of farm is discussed?
  8. What kind of technologies are mentioned or developed?
  9. What are the proposed sociotechnical outcomes?
  10. Do scholars mention climate change?
  11. What methods do scholars use?
  12. Do scholars leverage any theoretical frameworks or lens?
  13. Based on the abstract, how is the objective or need for the research framed?
  14. How is agriculture framed within the paper (e.g., as a way of life, occupation, so on)?
  15. How is HCI or technology framed in relation to agriculture in the paper? (e.g., technology as a means of bridging knowledge gaps)
