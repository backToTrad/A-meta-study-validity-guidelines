OK Google, Let's Learn: Using Voice User Interfaces for Informal Self-Regulated Learning of Health Topics among Younger and Older Adults

Smit Desai , School of Information Sciences, University of Illinois, Urbana-Champaign, United States, smitad2@illinois.edu
Jessie Chin , School of Information Sciences, University of Illinois, Urbana-Champaign, United States, chin5@illinois.edu

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581507
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

In this paper, we present Health Buddy, a voice agent integrated into commercially available Voice User Interfaces (VUIs) to support informal self-regulated learning (SRL) of health-related topics through multiple learning strategies and examine the efficacy of Health Buddy on learning outcomes for younger and older adults. We conducted a mixed-factorial-design experiment with 26 younger and 25 older adults, assigned to three SRL strategies (within-subjects): monologue, dialogue-based scaffolding building, and conceptual diagramming. We found that while younger adults benefit more from scaffolding building and conceptual diagramming, both younger and older adults showed equivalent learning outcomes. Furthermore, interaction fluency (operationalized by the number of conversational breakdowns) was associated with learning outcomes regardless of age. While older adults did not experience less fluent conversations, interaction fluency affected their technology acceptance toward VUIs more than younger ones. Our study discusses age-related learning differences and has implications for designing VUI-based learning programs for older adults.

1 INTRODUCTION

Voice User Interfaces (VUIs) allow users to communicate naturally with devices using voice or speech. Commercial VUIs—including Amazon Alexa, Apple Siri, and Google Assistant—are integrated into various products (e.g., smartphones, computers, and speakers). VUI-enabled smart speakers like Amazon Echo and Google Nest Home are becoming increasingly common in U.S. households. According to Pew Research Center [ 81 ], about half of Americans use VUIs daily, and a quarter of them have a smart speaker in their homes [ 4 ]. Smart speakers serve as digital assistants and are mainly used for playing music, performing information-seeking tasks, controlling household appliances, and social companionship [ 8 ].

Due to the speech-based nature of VUIs, they are comparatively more accessible to older adults [ 87 , 88 , 107 ] and people with disabilities [ 102 ]. This might be due to graphical user interfaces (GUIs) presenting auditory, visual, physical, and motor-based interaction barriers [ 101 , 107 ]. In particular, older adults are believed to be a targeted market for smart speakers [ 101 ] as they provide a lower entry barrier than other input modalities and can be easily incorporated into daily lives [ 58 , 117 ]. Chin et al. [ 20 ] demonstrated the feasibility and acceptance of using VUIs to deliver physical activity programs to older adults, in which older adults showed equivalent levels of technology acceptance as younger ones. In a thematic analysis of media articles to investigate the portrayal of older adults’ use of VUIs, Sin et al. [ 101 ] highlighted that VUIs could be beneficial to older adults for “improving health, decreasing social isolation and loneliness, and supporting a convenient, independent, safe, and secure life.” However, a significant obstacle to VUI usage among older adults, especially, is a recurring high volume of conversational breakdowns [ 57 ] due to errors in natural language processing (NLP), unfamiliarity with recognizing user intents, and feedback and system errors [ 79 ]. We operationalize all these categories of conversational breakdowns as “interaction fluency.”

Although VUIs have a long history in Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) research [ 28 ], there has been a renewed interest in VUIs in the HCI community, signaled by a rise in literature discussing speech interfaces since the introduction of commercially available smart speakers in 2014 [ 24 ]. One of the more popular research applications of VUI usage is in the field of education [ 105 ]. The possibility of using VUI devices in a classroom setting [ 71 ] and personal tutoring [ 111 ] has been discussed—especially in research with younger adults. A study has also seen VUIs referred to as “learning devices” [ 88 ]. Still, research is scarce in studying the use of smart speakers in informal learning settings—even though users have expressed a need to use VUIs for autonomous learning and VUIs have continually shown their potential to be a helpful resource in providing informal learning. Learning happens ubiquitously in our everyday life. There are increasing opportunities for adults in their later adulthood to pursue learning on their own. Commercially available VUIs, including Google Assistant and Amazon Alexa, have shown the potential to become accessible and scalable solutions to support this kind of informal self-regulated learning, given its natural speech-based input modality. In a mixed-method longitudinal study, Sciuto et al. [ 98 ] placed smart speakers in users’ homes and found that users tried to learn music and language using Alexa. Furthermore, VUIs have been successfully deployed in various pedagogical roles [ 35 ], including a coach for reducing public speaking anxiety [ 111 ], a tutor for teaching coding [ 56 ] and language [ 95 ], and a therapist to provide mental-health support using active listening capacities [ 77 ].

Informal learning—which is learning done outside of traditional and structured pedagogical environments—is often self-regulated; that is, it is done independently without external human intervention. According to Zimmerman [ 118 ], self-regulated learning (SRL) involves self-directed processes and beliefs that transform learners’ mental abilities into actionable skills. SRL typically consists of setting learning goals, forming strategies to attain those goals, and monitoring progress through self-assessment [ 90 ]. Learners often do not engage proactively in conscious SRL; however, they can be incentivized through motivational feelings and metacognitive strategies [ 119 ]. Consequently, to provide positive learning experiences, it is essential to develop learning tools grounded in SRL theories like scaffolding building, self-explanation, self-assessment, and motivation [ 46 ]. Computer-Based Learning Environment (CBLEs) like hypermedia (e.g., MetaTutor), interaction-based pedagogical agents (e.g., Betty's Brain), and Intelligent Tutoring System (e.g., AutoTutor) are commonly adopted in formal and informal learning environments. CBLEs are typically based on SRL theories and provide resources to learners to engage in deeper learning activities. However, CBLEs suffer from disadvantages like lack of affordability and inaccessibility [ 30 ], due to which their adoption is limited. Building SRL agents in commercially available VUIs has the potential to tackle these weaknesses, given their accessible and scalable dissemination to diverse users.

In the present study, we aim to present VUI-enabled smart speakers as a viable informal learning option—especially for older adults to support their needs for healthy aging in place. For this, we investigate the feasibility of implementing SRL strategies in commercially available VUIs to enable learning for younger adults (below the age of 60) and older adults (above the age of 60) and examine its efficacy on learning outcomes. We choose health information because there is an increasing trend of adults searching for health-related information on the internet. In the United States, 75% of younger adults [ 47 ], and 43.9% of older adults [ 53 ] sought health information on the internet in 2018. Among all information-seeking needs, health information is the most salient [ 47 ]. In a previous 3-week longitudinal study with older adults, researchers observed older adults asking more health-related questions to their smart speakers than other informational categories [ 88 ]. Furthermore, during the COVID-19 pandemic, we saw a rise in people seeking health-related information from their voice-enabled devices [ 3 ]. In a longitudinal study carried out across nine countries, McKillop et al. [ 68 ] found that VUIs can be successfully deployed for pandemic response and education. Our study has tremendous applications in scenarios like these where there is an urgent necessity to disseminate effective health-related learning programs to a multitude of people across all demographics.

Previous research has shown that younger and older adults tend to learn in different ways when using technology [ 18 , 22 , 55 , 115 ]. In general, younger adults tend to perform better on learning tasks involving novel technologies [ 18 , 55 ]. However, it is possible that these differences are because most interfaces are not designed with the unique needs of older adults in mind. Furthermore, older adults are often underrepresented in learning studies, possibly due to stereotypes about their attitudes toward new technology and learning [ 18 , 32 , 50 , 74 , 75 , 110 ]. Therefore, it is important to study age-related differences in learning to develop more inclusive technology-based learning systems. As our study involves younger and older adults, we are able to (1) test if age-related differences in learning occur in interaction with VUIs as well, (2) understand how older adults’ learning performance compares to younger adults, (3) identify the unique design needs of older adults, and (4) present older adults’ design preferences in the form of guidelines.

We develop a virtual voice-based learning agent, a Google Home Action, “Health Buddy” (HB), capable of delivering informal learning using successful SRL strategies rooted in natural language-based pedagogical agents’ literature [ 34 , 39 , 44 , 46 ]. We developed three modes of HB representing the three SRL strategies: dialogue-based scaffolding building (SB)—utilizing conversational pumps and prompts, motivational language, and self-assessment (e.g., quiz) to help learners build their understanding of the topics during learning, conceptual diagramming (CD)—utilizing conceptual maps to facilitate the integration of concepts during learning, and monologue (M)—utilizing instructional-style monologues, delivering materials to the learners with minimum interaction. There are three research questions in this study:

RQ1(a): How does older adults’ learning outcome compare to younger adults?

RQ1(b): What are the effects of different learning strategies on the learning outcome of younger and older adults?

RQ2: How does interaction fluency affect the learning outcome of younger and older adults?

RQ3: How do interaction fluency and learning performance influence technology acceptance of younger and older adults?

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study investigating self-regulated informal learning of health topics using commercially available VUIs. Taken together, the present study would (1) discuss the feasibility of VUIs as potential informal learning partners, (2) expand our understanding of how we can support self-regulated learning using commercially available VUIs for both younger and older adults, and (3) examine age-related differences in learning with VUIs. The findings from the present study could provide design implications of SRL- supported VUIs and serve as a call for further research in harnessing the potential of smart speakers as informal learning partners.

2 RELATED WORK

Voice user interfaces have a long history in HCI [ 24 ]. However, the widespread availability of commercial VUIs, acting as digital assistants, is a relatively recent development. Most notably, Apple introduced Siri in 2011, which was followed by a wave of other VUIs, such as Google Assistant and Amazon Alexa. Smart speakers became commercially available in 2014. To date, the applications of these VUIs have been limited to specific use scenarios and research domains. While there is no existing research on using commercially available VUIs for self-regulated learning of health topics in informal settings, the most relevant themes that are relevant to the present study include (1) VUIs and learning, (2) VUIs and health information seeking, and (3) older adults and VUIs. In the following sections, we describe these domains to situate our study within the broader scientific literature.

2.1 VUIs and Learning

While some studies use chatbots and intelligent tutoring systems in pedagogy [ 82 , 95 , 104 ], there is limited literature discussing VUIs for informal self-regulated learning. The studies that have utilized VUIs have done so in three particular scenarios: (1) teaching a second language, (2) teaching STEM, and (3) mentoring.

A relatively efficacious deployment of VUIs has been to teach a second language. There are examples of studies investigating the effectiveness of using Amazon Alexa to teach English to university students [ 38 ] and children [ 29 ]. These studies concluded that VUIs are a helpful resource for language learning and improved participants’ performances.

In the domain of teaching computational skills, Dietz et al. [ 37 ] developed a voice-based smartphone application leveraging storytelling techniques to teach computational thinking skills to children. At the end of this study, researchers found that participants could successfully perform near-transfer concept recognition tasks and learn from the application. Similarly, Jung et al. [ 56 ] developed an interactive VUI game, “TurtleTalk,” that converted children's utterances into code and helped them learn programming concepts, such as ‘sequencing’ and ‘iteration.’ Wang et al. [ 111 ] developed and successfully deployed a VUI to provide mentoring and mitigate the public-speaking anxiety of the participants. In this study, the researchers noted that participants indicated not feeling judged by the virtual tutor and specified the need for a sociable tutor for enhanced learning experiences.

In a previous study [ 34 ], researchers conducted a systematic analysis of commercially available smart speakers using Work-Domain Analysis (WDA) and identified feasible SRL strategies that could be implemented in VUIs. These strategies included scaffolding building, self-assessment, self-explanation, and motivation. Out of these, scaffolding building, self-assessment, and motivation were adjudged to be “highly feasible” to integrate into commercially available VUIs. We use these strategies in the development of Health Buddy. Table 1 provides a summary of SRL strategies with definitions and examples. Our present study builds on this work and lays the groundwork for using commercially available smart speakers grounded in SRL theories to support informal learning in the general learning domain.

Table 1: Summary of SRL strategies with definition and example of each strategy
Strategy Definition Example
Scaffolding building Breaking learning into parts and providing support for each part using tools like pumps, hints, and prompts Using pumps and prompts to guide participants through learning materials and quizzes to let participants reflect on their learning

Overall, there are scant examples of using VUIs for learning in the scientific literature, but there are discussions about harnessing their potential. Furthermore, there are numerous anecdotal pieces of evidence in news blogs and media articles. Amazon urges developers to build Alexa skills for schools or universities and provides resources and help for the same [ 126 ]. It is important to note here that these voice-based learning resources and the learning applications (mentioned in this section) are mainly developed for children and younger adults and do not consider the learning needs of older adults—a point we will revisit in the discussion section of this paper.

2.2 VUIs and Health Information Seeking

Using VUIs for seeking health information is a commonly found user activity among studies that have explored the long-term use of smart speakers in naturalistic settings. Although health information seeking and health information learning are two distinct activities that require different strategies, needs, and levels of engagement, health information seeking can often lead to learning and can serve as a starting point for health information learning [ 61 , 66 ]. The examples in this section show that VUIs are already seen as a suitable medium for acquiring health information, and failing to account for this behavior may have unintended consequences for the end-user.

Pradhan et al. [ 88 ]—in a 3-week field study of smart speakers in seven older adults’ homes—found that all the participants, except one, asked health-related questions to Alexa. These questions included specific health conditions (e.g., Parkinson's, blood pressure, Rheumatoid Arthritis, Hepatitis), day-to-day health-related occurrences (e.g., headache and foot pain), and medications (e.g., Prozac, Prevnar 13). In another 5-day diary study with 30 low-income Black older adults, Harrington et al. [ 48 ] observed that the participants made regular queries regarding managing an existing chronic condition or illness (e.g., Diabetes, Bronchitis) and general health information (e.g., sugar intake, COVID-19 vaccines, influenza).

Prior work has showcased VUIs as an accessible medium for seeking health information. In a randomized trial comparing health information seeking with conversational interfaces to traditional web interfaces, [ 11 ] found that people with low health literacy significantly favored seeking health information from conversational interfaces. During the COVID-19 pandemic, there was a significant rise in interest in investigating the feasibility of using VUIs to deliver pandemic-related information. McKillop et al. [ 68 ] developed and deployed a conversational interface to answer pandemic-related information across nine countries. This interface handled 6.8 million messages in about 5 months.

The studies mentioned in this section strongly indicate that adults either want to or are already regularly engaging in health information seeking behavior with VUIs. However, the currently available versions of commercial VUIs are not designed to handle such requests. A fact-based question, “what are the symptoms of diabetes?” might get the user a short answer retrieved from reputed sources (e.g., Mayo Clinic), but an interpretive question, “should I get a flu shot if I have the flu?” would result in questionable information from unverified sources or a conversational breakdown [ 14 ]. The response to the fact-based question lacks nuance, while the answer to the interpretive question lacks veracity. A lack of nuance and veracity in the case of health information could have severe consequences for the end-user. Bickmore et al. [ 10 ], in an observational study with 54 participants using Alexa, Siri, and Google Assistant for searching health information, found that about 29.2% of health-related information task scenarios could have led to the user suffering some degree of harm, and 16.1% of the scenarios could have led to death.

Thus far, all the studies with VUIs in this field have been limited to health information seeking, but there is limited prior work looking at health information learning using commercially available VUIs. However, there are examples of using other forms of conversational agents, including chatbots and social robots, to provide medical information and interventions to a diverse set of users [ 10 ]. Sillice et al. [ 100 ] developed a relational agent to promote exercise and sun protection behaviors and found this intervention successful, with participants noting the relational agent to be less judgmental than its human equivalents. Similarly, Bickmore et al. [ 9 ] developed an embodied conversational agent acting as an “exercise coach” to promote walking among sedentary older adults. The researchers found that the conversational agent was effective over the short term. Other similar systems have been developed to educate the users about vaccinations [ 85 ], counselling [ 77 ], and medication adherence [ 65 ].

It is important to connect adults seeking health information to accurate VUI-based learning programs. Our present study is an attempt to fill this gap by providing accurate health information to the users in an effective way by designing voice agents rooted in self-regulated learning and metacognition literature.

2.3 Older Adults and VUIs

Aging is marked by physiological changes, such as “reduced acuity of vision and hearing, slow reaction time, and impaired balance” [ 54 ], along with widely common social stigmas resulting in negative attitudes towards older adults such as “prejudices, ageism, creating of popular stereotypes and taboos, damaging self-beliefs, lack of public discussion, and alarmist popular and professional statements about burden and costs” [ 33 ]. Consequently, older adults could struggle with feelings of alienation, anxiety, and depression [ 51 ]. Rising life expectancy rates, complemented by falling fertility rates, have resulted in a rapidly aging global populace [ 6 ]. By the year 2060, 25% of the global population will be older adults [ 109 ]. Due to the ongoing concerns regarding aging, the United Nations (UN) declared 2021-2030 “the decade of healthy aging” [ 128 ] with increased impetus on research efforts to support aging in place. The role of technology in these efforts cannot be understated. Older adults’ use of technology has been linked to improved physical and mental health, increased quality of life and autonomy, and decreased loneliness [ 27 , 32 , 72 , 112 ].

However, unfounded myths about older adults’ attitudes towards technology continue to be widely prevalent among computer scientists and the general public [ 32 ]. Wandke et al. [ 110 ] identified the prevailing myths in HCI and defined them as—older adults are disinterested in using ICTs, older adults consider modern technology to be useless, older adults lack the physical prowess needed to use technology, older adults lack a basic understanding of computing technology, and it is challenging to teach technology to people at an old age. Although there is some evidence of older adults’ difficulties with technology adoption [ 32 ], further investigation suggests it is often due to overgeneralized design and lack of technological support [ 32 , 50 , 74 , 75 , 110 ].

The simple nature of voice-based interactions [ 69 ] provides respite from limitations of GUIs such as sensory and cognitive overload, difficulties with using input modalities such as mouse and touchscreens, dependence on vision acuity, and lack of confidence due to the perceived complexity of using GUIs [ 17 , 32 , 74 ]. Compared to GUIs, VUIs offer a more accessible, easily learnable, and usable interaction modality [ 12 , 84 , 88 ] and, therefore, are positively perceived by older adults [ 102 , 117 ]. In a longitudinal study conducted at older adults’ homes, Pradhan et al. [ 87 ] found that VUIs assuaged feelings of loneliness, with participants valuing companionship provided by these “invisible friends.” Correspondingly, in another longitudinal study, Kim & Choudhury [ 57 ] found that older adults cherished conversational features of VUIs more, were less worried about making mistakes, and coped with functional errors of VUIs better with time and experience. Yet, analysis of literature also reveals many challenges for adoption of VUIs by older adults, such as auditory issues pertaining to age-related hearing loss [ 57 ], unfamiliarity with what to say and how to conduct conversations with VUIs [ 57 , 87 , 96 ], high volume of conversational breakdowns [ 57 ], and privacy concerns [ 23 , 99 , 106 ]. Currently, the majority of the literature is focused on deploying VUIs as digital assistants to help older adults perform activities of daily living and companionship [ 57 , 102 ]. However, other emerging applications of VUIs for older adults include assisting in healthcare by providing easy access to health information [ 12 ] and medication instruction [ 5 ], performing physical activity [ 20 ], and skill development [ 1 ].

Early evidence in the literature suggests that VUIs have the potential to alleviate the lives of older adults and support aging in place. However, limitations exist, and there is an exigent need for further work in this area. Currently, there are several gaps in the literature, such as—developing heuristics for designing for older adults; exploring novel application areas to enrich older adults’ experiences; understanding older adults’ long-term behavior, changing use and perception of VUIs; and comparing older adults’ use and performance of VUIs to other age groups. Furthermore, there is no prior work on utilizing smart speakers for self-regulated learning among older adults. Our present work is a part of an ongoing holistic effort to take advantage of VUIs to help older adults live better independent lives and dispel myths about their competence in using new technology.

3 DESIGNING HEALTH BUDDY

3.1 Designing the voice agent

We used Google Dialogflow ES, a natural language understanding platform, to design Health Buddy. In the back end, Dialogflow is able to recognize user intent and context, and respond to the users using Google's Cloud Text-to-Speech API. We trained the platform to understand a wide range of training phrases (e.g., “fact two” vs. “topic 2” vs. “relationship between heart health and cholesterol”), variations in answers (e.g., “sweets and pastries” vs. “dessert” vs. “puddings”), input requests (e.g., “practice question” and “repeat”), and fillers in answers (e.g., “I think the answer is <answer>” or “Uhh, maybe <answer>”). Additionally, to ensure higher interaction fluency, we also ensured variations in the pronunciations of different words were accounted for. For example, based on context, Health Buddy could understand “Liver,” “Lever,” or “Leaver” as the right answer to the question, “What organ naturally produces cholesterol?”

The front end of Health Buddy—often referred to as the ‘system persona’—was designed using Google's Conversation Design Guidelines [ 127 ] and design guidelines and implications from previous studies with VUIs [ 57 , 87 , 88 ]. Particularly, we identified the following four key design criteria that were most applicable to the design of HB:

  • To provide the users a mental model of how they can interact with HB, we used the concept of a ‘system persona’ [ 127 ]. We designed HB using the metaphor of an instructor and used Google “Female Voice-1.” During the design phase, the operative adjectives for HB were calm, friendly, knowledgeable, and confident. Further, we use the word “buddy” to elicit anthropomorphism useful for establishing familiarity with the participants, especially those interacting with a VUI for the first time.
  • Users, especially older adults, value the sociability aspect of the voice agents [ 57 , 87 ]. As a result, we designed HB to be polite by making use of pleasantries (e.g., “good morning/ afternoon,” “sorry,” “thank you,” and “please”) and unassuming and gender-inclusive language.
  • Since VUIs do not offer visual cues, user must remember certain keywords and commands to perform actions, resulting in high cognitive load on the users [ 88 ]. In order to mitigate this, we reminded user of where they were in the interaction and what commands they could use to progress. For example, at the end of Fact 2, if the user did not command HB to move to the next fact, HB would remind the user using the prompt, “We have come to the end of Fact 2. You can move to next fact, using the command ‘Fact 3’ or ‘next fact.’ You can also relearn Fact 2.”
  • Establishing credibility is important for health information. In GUIs, it is comparatively easier to identify the source of the information; however, in interactions with VUIs, the source needs to be explicitly stated in the conversation [ 88 ]. Consequently, for transparency, we disclosed the source of the health information to the users in interactions with HB.

In addition, we designed Health Buddy to have the following basic functionalities: (1) stop/ resume, users could stop or pause the interaction with HB by saying various variations of the word “stop” (e.g., “pause” or “let's take a break”) This was done to ensure participants could take breaks, if needed, during the interaction with HB. Users could resume the interaction when they were ready to continue; (2) repeat, users could avail repeat at any point of the interaction to repeat instructions, learnings, or a question; (3) practice, users could go back to the practice question at any point if they had difficulties using HB, and (4) navigation, users could freely navigate from one health fact to another by stating the fact number (e.g., “Health Buddy, take me to Fact 2”) or the content of the fact (e.g., “Health Buddy, let's go back to the heart health fact.”).

We introduced participants to learn six health topics using conversation scripts based on the materials from credible health information websites, including MedlinePlus, Mayo Clinic, and American Cancer Society. 3.3 Designing the learning strategies

We designed HB to incorporate three learning strategies grounded in self-regulated learning (SRL) literature: monologue, conceptual diagramming, and scaffolding building. These strategies were selected based on their feasibility for implementation in VUIs [ 34 ] and their effectiveness in previous HCI literature on learning [ 44 ]. These learning strategies function as modes of Health Buddy:

  • Monologue (M) is commonly observed in pedagogy and is one of the most popular strategies used in pedagogical agent based SRL, especially for vicarious learning with a virtual tutor [ 39 , 60 ]. It is often contrasted with dialogue-based learning in literature, similar to HB in scaffolding building mode. Since the monologue learning strategy requires minimum back-and-forth interaction with HB, it minimizes distractions and exerts a less cognitive load on the user [ 52 , 67 , 73 ]. HB, in monologue mode, delivers instructional-style monologues about individual health topics. Users can request HB to repeat the learning if they choose.
  • Conceptual diagrams (CD) are used to facilitate learning and involve bidirectional diagrams with concepts or topics and a line connecting them, denoting some form of relationship [ 80 ]. Falkner et al. [ 44 ] found conceptual diagramming to be the most successful SRL strategy to support computer science students learning autonomously. HB, in conceptual diagramming mode, accompanied health information with on-screen conceptual diagrams to augment learning. Initially, we planned to use a screened smart speaker (e.g., Google Nest Hub) to show the diagrams. However, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, we could not conduct in-person studies to mitigate potential harm to the participants, and consequently used the screen-sharing functionality of Zoom to display the diagrams while providing learning instructions with HB.
  • Scaffolding building (SB) involves providing learners with a structure or framework for learning, using metacognitive tools (e.g., pumps and prompts), and providing feedback and guidance to help them develop their skills. To deliver self-regulated learning through conversations, we used several tools, including (1) prompts , elicitations to lead the participants towards relevant information; (2) quiz , questions to assess the participants’ learning with pertinent questions; (3) pumps , subtle assertions or questions to nudge the participants to engage deeper; and (4) motivation , assertions to encourage and provide positive reinforcements to the participants. These strategies have been deployed in artificial intelligence-based pedagogical agents via natural language before [ 46 ], but their applicability to commercial VUIs is currently limited to specific contexts. Most notably, Xu and Warschauer [ 116 ] successfully built and deployed a storytelling conversational agent for children based on scaffolding-building strategies.

Notably, the content of the health topics remained the same across all three modes of HB, and the same information was provided using the three different learning strategies. As a result, the conversation length was approximately the same. For example, Figure 1 illustrates how health information in Fact 2 (relationship between heart health and cholesterol) was instructed to the user using the three learning strategies. We randomly selected Fact 2—using a dice roll—to illustrate all the examples in this paper. In the monologue mode of Health Buddy, the information was presented to the user with no back-and-forth interaction. However, users could ask HB to repeat the instructions or take breaks. The same information is presented to the user in CD mode with the help of a conceptual diagram. For SB, Health Buddy quizzes the user on the provided information to ascertain that the learner is engaging with the information. Further, in SB mode, metacognitive tools like prompts, pumps, and motivation are also utilized. To maintain consistency in the interactions, the number of quizzes in SB mode and the diagrams in CD mode remained the same (3 or 4 for each health topic). In CD mode, HB took a brief pause at the same locations to let the learners crystallize their learnings. It is important to note here that Figure 1 only illustrates a snippet of the learning provided in Fact 2.

3.4 Bringing it all together

Health Buddy can be accessed with Google smart speakers, including Google Nest Mini. It was designed to deliver health-related learning about six common health topics using three learning strategies (M, CD, SB) grounded in self-regulated learning literature. The three learning strategies represent the modes of HB. All basic functions of Health Buddy are available in all modes. Figure 2 provides a summary of the components of Health Buddy.

The logic of the interactional conversation with HB is designed as follows: (1) greetings, (2) practice questions, and (3) learning health information. To ensure the validity of our experiments, we designed three versions of HB (alpha, beta, and gamma) that were identical in content but differed in the topics assigned to each learning strategy and the order of the strategies. For example, a user interacting with HB alpha would receive facts 1 and 2 in scaffolding building mode, facts 3 and 4 in conceptual diagramming mode, and facts 5 and 6 in monologue mode, while a user interacting with HB beta would receive facts 1 and 2 in monologue mode, facts 3 and 4 in scaffolding building mode, and facts 5 and 6 in conceptual diagramming mode.

Irrespective of the versions, all interactions with HB begin with the invocation phrase “Ok Google, Talk to Health Buddy,” followed by a greeting by Health Buddy to establish familiarity with the user (e.g., “Hello there! This is Health Buddy. Today I am going to talk to you about six common health-related topics. For each topic, you will be having a brief 3-5 minute conversation, where I will be giving you some health information, asking some questions, and providing visual aid for some health information. If you have any questions at any point, please ask the researcher, and if you want to stop, just let me know. Should we start then?”). Once users indicate their readiness to begin the learning, they are asked practice questions to prepare them for the ensuing learning session (e.g., “Let's practice some questions before we begin: True or False: Chicago is a city in the state of Illinois?”). This design decision was especially useful for older adults and infrequent users of VUIs, as prior research indicates that these users tend to have difficulties knowing what to say and how to operate the device [ 31 , 57 ], resulting in frustration and discontinued use. After the practice question, Health Buddy delivered health-related learning of the six health topics to the user using the three learning strategies, with two topics in each mode. 4 PRESENT STUDY

4.1 Participants

51 participants from a medium-sized midwestern town in the U.S. were recruited for a 90-minute online study session from the university and the local community. Younger adults were recruited using departmental emailing lists, and older adults were recruited with the help of weekly local newsletters and advertisements in retirement communities. We reimbursed the participants for their time with $ 15 Amazon gift cards. Of the 51 participants, 25 were older adults (21 female, 4 male; M=66.84 years old, SD=6.03 years), and 26 were younger adults (17 female, 8 male, 1 non-binary; M=24.8 years old, SD=5.34 years). Since the average age of the older adults was 66.84, they could be categorized as “young-old.” Out of the 25 older adults, 24 self-identified as Caucasian and 1 as Asian. Out of the 26 younger adults, 13 self-identified as Caucasian, 8 as Asian, 3 as Black or African American, and 2 as Asian and Caucasian. All the participants either had a college degree or were enrolled in a degree program. All the participants were native speakers of English except one younger adult, who—although self-reported as being fluent in English—indicated that their native language was Chinese. On average, both younger and older participants reported low VUI use and familiarity. Approximately 70% of the participants indicated that they hardly or never used VUIs, and about 90% of the participants reported no or less familiarity with VUIs. However, although still low, younger adults used VUIs significantly more frequently and were more familiar with VUIs compared to older adults. The demographic information for all participants, including age, gender, race/ ethnicity, native language, frequency and familiarity with VUI use, is included in the Appendix of this paper.

4.2 Apparatus & Materials

Apparatus. We used Google Nest Mini Gen. 2 for interacting with the participants. We chose Google Assistant because along with Amazon Alexa, it is the most popular and commercially adopted VUI [ 13 ]. Further, in previous literature comparing various aspects of Google Assistant with Amazon Alexa, Google Assistant was regarded as the most “natural” at answering questions because of the tone and pace of its synthesized voice [ 63 ], and it is perceived as more approachable, genuine, intelligent, and professional compared to Alexa [ 59 ]. Since Health Buddy is designed to act as a learning instructor, these perceptions are preferable.

Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, we could not conduct in-person studies to mitigate the potential harm to the human participants. The studies were done via Zoom, a teleconferencing video software. The smart speaker was placed on a table—clearly visible to the participants on Zoom. The diagrams in the conceptual diagramming (CD) mode were shown to the participants using Zoom's screen-sharing functionality.

Questionnaires . To gain a better understanding of the participants and their interactions with Health Buddy, we administered three types of questionnaires to all the participants:

Demographic questionnaire collected participants’ information like age, gender, ethnicity, educational background, technology experience (the frequencies of VUIs usage), technology familiarity (their perceived amounts of knowledge about VUIs), and prior knowledge of the health facts (their perceived familiarity about a topic).

Cognitive Measures included working memory (fluid cognitive ability) and verbal ability (crystallized cognitive ability) tests. We measured working memory using Letter Number Sequencing Test [ 113 ], in which participants were asked to recall the letters and numbers in alphabetical and ascending order, respectively, after listening to a sequence of letters and numbers varying in lengths. We measured verbal ability using the Advanced Vocabulary Test [ 42 ], in which participants were asked to recognize the meanings of 18 English words. These measures are commonly used in learning studies (especially with older adults), as they are predictors of cognitive abilities, which is important for knowledge acquisition for learning [ 7 ].

Technology Acceptance was measured using the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) questionnaire [ 108 ]. Participants were required to rate their agreements with the descriptions of the interactions with Health Buddy on a 5-point Likert Scale (1=“completely disagree” to 5= “completely agree.”). The main constructs of UTAUT include factors related to technology acceptance (such as performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, attitude towards technology, facilitating conditions, self-efficacy, and anxiety) and behavioral intention (the intention to continue using Health Buddy). We used UTAUT to investigate if the users would adopt and accept Health Buddy into their homes and to better understand and identify the factors (e.g., interaction fluency and learning outcome) influencing the perceived long-term use of Health Buddy.

4.3 Design & Procedure

We adopted a 2 age group (between-subjects variable: young, old) x 3 learning strategies (within-subjects variable: monologue (M), dialogue-based scaffolding building (SB), and conceptual diagramming (CD)) mixed factorial design in our study. Participants learned six topics assigned to three modes (also operationalized as learning strategies) in a counterbalanced order (2 topics in each mode). Figure 4 provides a summary of the study design and procedure.

Once participants showed interest in the study by filling out a screening form, they were invited to a 90-minute Zoom meeting by an experimenter. Upon the onset of the study, participants first completed an online informed consent form in accordance with the requirements of the Institutional Review Board and an online demographic questionnaire (approximately 15 minutes). Then, they received brief training from the experimenter about how to interact with the Google Home Mini Gen. 2, and practice to make themselves familiar with VUIs by talking with Health Buddy to go through the instructions of the study (approximately 5 minutes). When participants indicated their readiness to begin learning, a session with Health Buddy was initiated.

Participants interacted with Health Buddy in all three modes, with two topics in each mode. While interacting with the participant in CD mode, an experimenter shared the conceptual diagrams using screen-sharing on Zoom. Unless there was a major conversational error and the participant could not continue the interaction, the experimenter did not interfere with the learning session. After interacting with Health Buddy (approximately 45 minutes; 15 minutes in each mode), the learning outcome was measured in a cued recall session guided by the experimenter. The participants were asked by the experimenters to remember what they learned about each topic individually, using single prompts like “What do you remember about the relationship between cholesterol and heart health?” (approximately 10 minutes). During the cued recall session, the experimenter provided no additional hints or support to help the participant recall more or any information about the health topics. If the participant could not recall anything about a particular health topic, the experimenter moved to the next topic. The participants then completed the measures of cognitive abilities (working memory and vocabulary tests) and technology acceptance (approximately 10 minutes). The study session concluded with a debriefing by the experimenter.

5 RESULTS

In this study, we are measuring learning outcomes using a cued recall process as a proxy for learning. During this process, after learning with Health Buddy, participants were asked to recall what they had learned about each health topic as much as possible in a fixed order. Such a recall-based protocol is common in studies involving text information learning [ 19 , 40 , 91 ] with older adults in short learning sessions. For example, in a study to understand how online health information could be better designed to support older adults’ learning goals, researchers used cued recall scores to measure learning performance [ 19 ]. Similarly, in another study assessing the efficacy of using conversational agents to deliver medication instructions, recall scores were used as evidence of learning [ 5 ].

5.1 RQ1(a). How does older adults’ learning outcome compare to younger adults?

Learning performance was scored as the total number of ideas (i.e., propositions) recalled using gist-based scoring, used to measure the learning outcome from texts (e.g., [ 19 ]). Under each topic, there are five most important ideas which were previously identified by two researchers independently and reconciled through discussions. Based on the completeness of the response, participants received up to 3 points for each important idea recalled and 1 point for each general idea recalled. To keep the learning performance limited to just the information given by Health Buddy, no points were given to the participants if they mentioned relevant information about a topic that was not part of their interaction with Health Buddy. Two independent raters scored the recall (Cohen's kappa > 0.85). The average total number of ideas recalled is 21.3 (SD=6.48), and the average proportion of important ideas recalled is 41.92% (SD=12.77%). There were no age differences in either the total number of ideas recalled, t(49)=0.75, p=0.46 (Young: M=22, SD=6.50; Old: M=20.64, SD=6.51) or the proportion of important ideas recalled, t(49)=0.85, p=0.40 (Young: M=43.42%, SD=13.52%; Old: M=40.36%, SD=12.01%). Both younger and older adults learned equally well through the interaction with Health Buddy.

5.3 RQ2. How does interaction fluency affect the learning outcome of younger and older adults?

Interaction fluency is operationalized by the reverse grading of the number of breakdowns or failures in the conversations between participants and Health Buddy. We used the max number of conversational breakdowns or failures (N=6) minus the actual number of conversational breakdowns or failures as the interaction fluency score. For example, if during a conversation with HB, a participant faced one conversational breakdown, it would lead to a high score of five, meaning high interaction fluency and a smooth interaction. Conversely, if a participant faced four conversational breakdowns, it would result in a low score of two, implying low interaction fluency and a relatively less smooth interaction. The interaction fluency score for each participant ranged from zero to six. A conversational breakdown or failure is recorded when the VUI fails to respond to the user or provides an irrelevant response. In accordance with [ 79 ], we went through each transcript to code four categories of conversational breakdowns—unfamiliar intent, natural language processing (NLP) errors, feedback errors, and system errors. High interaction fluency would indicate fewer conversational breakdowns (across all four categories) and vice versa. We found occurrences of all categories of breakdowns in our study. It is important to note that these categories of errors are prevalent in all existing commercial VUI applications, regardless of the platform. In previous studies with older adults, error rates with commercial VUIs have been reported as high as 33.6% [ 57 ].

Overall, our participants experienced an average of 1.26 times conversation breakdowns or failures in the study. The error rate was 11.45%. There was no age difference in interaction fluency (t(49)=-0.70, p=0.49). Younger adults experienced an average of 1.17 times (SE=0.19), and older adults experienced 1.36 times (SE=0.19) of conversational breakdowns and failures in the study. Older adults did not experience less interaction fluency than younger adults during the interactions with Health Buddy.

6 DISCUSSION

We found that both younger and older adults performed equivalently well and effectively learned information using Health Buddy. Although there were no age differences in overall learning outcomes, younger adults took more advantage of scaffolding building and conceptual diagramming than older adults. Furthermore, we found that interaction fluency was associated with learning outcome regardless of age. There was no age difference in interaction fluency with Health Buddy; older adults experienced the same number of minimum errors during the interactions with Health Buddy as younger adults. Older adults showed higher associations between technology acceptance with VUIs and interaction fluency than younger adults. Using our results, we identify broad conceptual implications for HCI that might be helpful for designers and researchers.

6.1 Age-related similarities and differences in informal learning with VUIs

The implementation of SRL strategies to facilitate learning yielded equivalently positive results for younger and older adults. All three learning strategies resulted in good enough learning performances among older and younger adults (i.e., their proportions of recall of important ideas were comparable with previous studies in reading comprehension (e.g., [ 41 , 114 ])). These results have long-term implications for the future of accessible and affordable learning:

Low entry point to learning . Typically, informal learning has been realized with interaction-based CBLEs, including pedagogical agents using “structured dialogue moves” [ 46 ]. However, these pedagogical agents have been conventionally text and screen based and suffer from a lot of limitations systemic to GUIs. Most relevantly, they require high computer literacy skills and include a steep learning curve. In our study, about 70% of participants indicated that they never or rarely used VUIs and about 90% of them reported having no or little familiarity with VUIs—nevertheless, it did not affect their learning performance. This result suggests that VUIs offer a low entry point by levelling the learning curve, as indicated in other VUI studies [ 12 , 84 , 88 ].

Suitability to older adults. It is well-established in the learning literature that older adults tend to perform significantly poorly in recall-based tasks and take more time to learn the same amounts of information compared to younger adults [ 49 , 94 ]. However, we did not observe any such differences in our study. This is surprising and encouraging. Currently, there is some research investigating how voice user interfaces could be deployed to provide learning solutions to children and younger adults—as previously mentioned in the related work section of our paper—but no research investigating the same among older adults. However, there is a comparatively significant body of work (e.g., [ 57 , 87 ]) studying how VUIs could provide companionship to older adults. Our findings indicate that more effort must be expended to study learning among older adults by projecting VUIs as not just social companions but also learning partners to provide more holistic support to older adults.

Revisiting learning strategies. Implementing SRL strategies to facilitate comprehension, such as scaffolding building and conceptual diagramming, are especially effective for learning among younger adults, consistent with previous findings [ 44 , 46 ]. However, older adults did not take advantage of scaffolding building and conceptual diagramming compared to monologue, even though previous studies with pedagogical agents [ 44 , 46 ] suggested these strategies could effectively improve learning from text reading. These differences could be because compared to SB and CD, monologue exerts the least amount of cognitive load on the participants, as there is minimal back-and-forth between the users and Health Buddy. Further, this finding (1) provides further evidence to the growing body of literature on learning with conversations [ 60 , 78 ], showing that dialogue-based strategies do not always result in better learning outcomes if key variables like the content and instructor's virtual presence are normalized, (2) highlights a major need for studying learning strategies more suited to older adults, with an emphasis on cognitive load and less-effortful strategies like “teach back” [ 76 ], and (3) exposes a major population gap in learning literature which has traditionally prioritized younger adults and sidelined developing pedagogical agents for older adults due to myths surrounding their attitudes towards technology and learning.

6.2 Interaction Fluency is a major determinant of learning

As extensively noted in VUI literature, VUIs are not “conversational” in nature [ 24 , 25 , 31 , 36 , 45 , 83 ]. They adopt a request-response mechanism and fail to grasp the subtlety of the logical model of human-human conversations [ 83 ]. One major obstacle inhibiting natural conversation is VUIs’ inability to recover from errors. We found in our study that once the VUI failed to understand the participants more than twice, it would simply quit by saying, “Sorry, Health Buddy is not responding right now. Please try again soon,” or worse—quit without any response. However, all our participants were able to recover from breakdowns or failures in no more than three turn-takings given previous training by the experimenter. As previously stated, Myers et al. [ 79 ] identified four categories of obstacles prevailing in all interactions with VUIs: unfamiliar intent, NLP error, feedback error, and system error. They found that NLP errors were the most frequent out of the four categories. Similar to their finding, we also found NLP errors to be the most common cause of conversational breakdowns. In these instances, Health Buddy (HB) failed to recognize the speech from the participants (P) and asked participants to repeat their responses. For example, in order to move to “Fact three,” participants could either say “next fact,” “fact three,” or “three,” however, occasionally, when the participant said, “fact three,” Google Home would provide a listing of factories nearby. Similarly, when the participant said “liver,” HB would misunderstand it as the name of a location and provide weather information. As noted by [ 79 ], participants would often try to recover from these breakdowns using “hyperarticulation,” “simplification,” or “using new utterances,” as shown in the two examples below:

Example 1.

1. HB: To move to the next fact, please say fact three.

2. P: Fact three.

3. HB: I found a few factories near you. The first one is… ((NLP error))

4. P: FACT THREE. ((hyperarticulation))

5. HB: I found a few factories near you. The first one is… ((NLP error))

6. P: Three. ((simplification and using new utterances))

7. HB: ((Starting Fact 3)) To discuss some current treatments for Alzheimer's disease…

Example 2.

1. HB: From what you learned so far, what organ naturally produces cholesterol? Heart, liver, or stomach?

2. P: Liver

3. HB: Currently in [Location Name], it's minus three degrees and cloudy today. ((NLP error))

4. P: LIVER ((hyperarticulation))

5. HB: Correct. The liver produces cholesterol per day….

Another common type of conversation failure is that HB did not recognize the intentions of the participants (unfamiliar intent) and responded differently. Rarely, the VUI would stop responding due to unknown reasons (system error) and provide improper feedback to the participants’ requests (feedback error). The below interaction provides an example of these three obstacles,

1. HB: Which of the following hormones helps the body stop hunger? Ghrelin, leptin, or cortisol?

2. P: Glarin.

3. HB: Sorry I didn't understand you. ((unfamiliar intent)) Could you repeat your answer again? Ghrelin, leptin, or cortisol?

4. P: GLARIN. ((hyperarticulation))

5. HB: Sorry, health buddy isn't responding right now. ((feedback error)) Please try again soon . ((system error))

We use “interaction fluency” to discuss all four categories of obstacles participants face in interacting with VUIs during learning. Our results show that older adults, as well as younger adults, faced similar number of obstacles during the interactions with Health Buddy (<2 times in total). We did not find one age group to be more affected than the other in terms of their learning outcome. Both age groups showed worsening learning performance if they experienced poorer interaction fluency. Other studies in metacognition and self-regulated learning also showed that there were no age differences in fluency experience (such as perceived processing ease of encoding fluency) on learning outcome (e.g., [ 21 , 40 , 43 ]). Hence, our study confirmed that the “fluency” experience is critical for learning with VUIs, including interaction fluency with the learning agent, regardless of the age of the learners.

While confirming that interaction fluency is critical for learning, some of these interaction fluency related problems could be resolved by providing better error recovery feedback to the participants. Often, participants were simply unaware of what went wrong with the interaction—VUI's chimeric system response: “Sorry, I could not understand,” is insufficient in moving the conversation forward. Participants—especially older adults—often blamed themselves for a failed interaction. One of the older participants exclaimed, “She is not liking me today,” when the VUI could not parse her intent. Using conversation analysis, Fischer et al. [ 45 ] devised suggestions helpful to designers to ascertain that the VUI's responses to errors could be fruitful for ‘progressivity.’ One of the suggestions was to provide an “account of what went wrong.” We found this suggestion to be particularly helpful. In the case of our study, while most of the participants were able to recover from conversational breakdowns, the experimenters would offer suggestions on what might have gone wrong, and what the participants could say to recover the conversation if the frustrations persisted. Surprisingly, we did not find any relation between prior experience and familiarity with using VUIs with cognitive abilities or interaction fluency. That is, participants with less experience and expertise in using VUIs, did not experience significantly more breakdowns during the interaction with VUIs compared to expert participants. Since conversational breakdowns and failures are difficult to be predicted, guidelines on how to recover from breakdowns would be an important solution for learners to use VUIs for SRL. Developing VUIs with error recovery mechanisms will also be a promising direction for future VUI design.

6.3 Design implications for older adults

Since the majority of SRL literature with older adults was conducted using GUIs (i.e., text reading, e.g., [ 26 , 91 ]), our findings have unique contributions to understanding age differences in self-regulated learning with VUIs. Older adults learn as well as younger adults with VUIs; however, older adults were more sensitive to interaction fluency while developing their technology acceptance compared to younger adults, impacting long-term adoption of VUIs. This finding is consistent with previous work in older adults’ use of VUIs [ 57 ], wherein older adults were critically affected by functional errors of the device and were inclined to (unfairly) blame themselves rather than the device for the poor performance. Hence, elder-friendly design considerations need to be implemented to promote the long-term adoption of VUIs and ascertain that older adults benefit from their learning advantages. Since our study involved younger and older adults, we were able to identify the unique needs of older adults compared to younger ones. Based on our study, we recommend the following design guidelines (DG):

DG1: Taking accountability for failed interactions by the VUIs could help older adults not blame their technology skills for operating new devices. Instead of responding with, “Sorry, I could not understand,” a better response could involve the VUI taking the blame for the failure with the following prompt, “Sorry, sometimes I have difficulties understanding. I am working on it.”

DG2 : Accommodating longer response time for older adults by implementing longer delays to elicit answers from older adults during learning. Older adults take longer to respond and are more verbose than younger adults in discourse [ 57 ]. During our study, we found instances of older adults taking too long to respond and being interrupted when they were still speaking.

DG3 : Incorporating auditory affordances to signify that the VUI is listening and waiting for the participant's response. We observed that the older adults often missed the visual affordances (four white lights on the device), indicating it was their turn to respond.

DG4: Keeping interactions simple by reducing cognitive load on older adults with the help of cogent dialogs that encourage progressivity.

DG5: Providing a list of sample commands older adults can use to restart or repair a conversation in the event of a conversational breakdown.

Nevertheless, most of the literature—including our findings—does suggest that VUIs are comparatively more accessible to older adults. With necessary design changes, VUIs could be better potential allies to competently support aging in place by helping older adults acquire self-care techniques and become lifelong learners [ 62 , 70 ]. Developing VUIs with applications on learning needs to be further considered to facilitate fluency experience and the recovery process for older users as they might adopt different strategies to compensate for their decline in fluid cognitive abilities for learning. According to Merriam & Kee [ 70 ], “formal and informal learning activities of older adults promote an active and engaged lifestyle that helps create and preserve community.” This attempt to seek engagement in the intellectually challenging and enriched lifestyle could also lead to long-term cognitive resilience and socioemotional well-being during our late lifespan (e.g., [ 2 , 16 , 64 , 86 , 92 , 97 , 103 ]). Hence, our research would be the first attempt to study VUIs as informal self-regulated learning partners for older adults to engage in intellectually enriched lifestyles, which will have implications for successful aging and aging in place.

6.4 Limitations and Future Work

Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, we were forced to conduct online Zoom teleconferencing studies. This affected our study in two ways: (1) participants interacted with VUIs using their computers or tablets, due to which we sometimes faced technical issues running these studies. Efforts were made to counteract this limitation by not including data from study sessions that suffered from technical problems (e.g., internet connectivity issues, excessive background noise, etc.), and (2) due to the online nature of this study; participants were required to have access to a laptop or a tablet—consequently, participants with comparatively higher levels of technological literacy were enrolled. Although we did attempt to prioritize recruiting participants who indicated they had low technological familiarity in their screening forms, we might have still failed to recruit participants with even lower technological literacy (e.g., adults with no knowledge of email basics).

Our study mainly comprised North American native speakers of English. Previous VUI literature suggests that there are key differences in how native and non-native speakers perceive and interact with VUIs [ 31 ]. Further, although our sample included a relatively diverse group of younger adults, the older participants were mostly Caucasian. Harrington et al. [ 48 ], in a study with 30 Black older adults, concluded that cultural factors influence human-VUI conversations, and not accounting for them could result in low technology acceptance. It is unclear how these factors affect learning outcomes, and we encourage researchers to examine learning strategies with non-native speakers from diverse backgrounds.

Measuring learning outcome is complex and commonly happens over a longer period of time. In this study, we are using cued recall as a short-term proxy for learning, as typical in reading comprehension studies [ 5 , 19 , 40 , 91 ]. In the future, we plan to conduct a longitudinal study to examine the self-regulated learning behavior in the naturalistic setting (e.g., homes, community dwellings) to further investigate the effects of VUIs on learning health information over a longer period of time. We also want to incorporate a broader variety of learning strategies to help us identify the best VUI-specific strategies. This would help us better understand how learning behavior changes over a longer period, and the effects of time on learning outcomes. Such a study would ameliorate our knowledge of designing learning interactions to promote the dissemination of learning programs to diverse people.

7 CONCLUSION

Our study has demonstrated the feasibility of implementing multiple SRL strategies to guide adults to learn information independently using smart speakers and discussed the efficacy of the smart-speaker-delivered programs on self-regulated learning for both younger and older adults. Although younger adults took more advantage of the SRL strategies for learning, both younger and older adults were able to achieve good enough learning performance equivalently. Analogous to metacognition research, our study showed that interaction fluency (i.e., the number of conversational breakdowns or failures) during learning with smart speakers was associated with learning outcomes for both younger and older adults. Moreover, interaction fluency proved to be more crucial to technology acceptance for older adults than for younger adults. Hence, interaction fluency would be critical in determining the success of self-regulated learning with smart speakers. Finally, the study discussed age-related differences in learning and proposed elder-friendly design guidelines. In sum, we conclude that commercially available smart speakers have demonstrated the potential to become a generalizable, accessible, and scalable medium to facilitate the dissemination of self-regulated learning programs to adults for informal learning.
