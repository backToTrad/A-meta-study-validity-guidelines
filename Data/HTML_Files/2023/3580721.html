About one-third of autistic people are nonspeaking, and most are never provided access to an effective alternative to speech. Thoughtfully designed AR applications could provide members of this population with structured learning opportunities, including training on skills that underlie alternative forms of communication. A fundamental step toward creating such opportunities, however, is to investigate nonspeaking autistic people's ability to tolerate a head-mounted AR device and to interact with virtual objects. We present the first study to examine the usability of an interactive AR-based application by this population. We recruited 17 nonspeaking autistic subjects to play a HoloLens 2 game we developed that involved holographic animations and buttons. Almost all subjects tolerated the device long enough to begin the game, and most completed increasingly challenging tasks that involved pressing holographic buttons. Based on the results, we discuss best practice design and process recommendations. Our findings contradict prevailing assumptions about nonspeaking autistic people and thus open up exciting possibilities for AR-based solutions for this understudied and underserved population.

1 INTRODUCTION

About one in three autistic children and adults are nonspeaking [ 10 ]: some do not talk at all, some say a few words, and others can repeat back words they have heard [ 1 ]. But what this heterogeneous group has in common is an inability to effectively share information, opinions, or memories using speech, often despite years or decades of interventions focused on spoken language [ 17 , 37 ]. Unfortunately, most nonspeaking autistic people are never provided access to an effective alternative to speech [ 5 ], severely limiting their ability to participate in educational, social, community, and employment settings.

Thoughtfully designed and implemented software applications delivered via wearable augmented reality (AR) have considerable potential to provide structured learning opportunities to nonspeaking autistic people, including training on some of the skills that underlie alternative forms of communication [ 22 ]. For example, in addition to not being able to communicate effectively using speech, many nonspeaking autistic people have difficulty with motor planning and control [ 11 , 15 ]. AR's ability to customize the size of the objects with which users interact provides a means to personalize and practice the motor skills involved in alternative ways of communication, e.g., typing, in a controlled manner.

Furthermore, many nonspeaking autistic people have extreme sensory issues, which can lead them into constant motion [ 16 ]. Head-mounted AR devices can track these movements and ensure that a virtual object is always in the user's field of view. Indeed, one reason wearable AR (as opposed to Virtual Reality (VR)) may be particularly well-suited for this population involves safety; AR provides users with an immersive experience without isolating them from the real world, so users can see the physical space they are moving through. Finally, many nonspeaking autistic people's ability to learn is routinely called into question in educational settings because of the way they present [ 19 ], even though research has shown that their abilities are vastly underestimated [ 9 ]. An AR system could allow them to autonomously access age-appropriate and interactive learning modules, bypassing limitations on academic content that may otherwise be imposed by harmful assumptions about their ability.

As will be described in detail in Section 2 , there have been some studies designed to investigate how AR/VR could be used to support autistic people. However, this earlier work has focused almost exclusively on speaking rather than nonspeaking autism (e.g., [ 2 , 23 ]). Indeed, nonspeaking autistic people are rarely included in research (e.g., [ 39 ]) because of the earlier mentioned assumptions about their abilities (or lack thereof) and because this population is sometimes considered difficult to work with (e.g., [ 7 ]). In fact, in grant reviewers’ and human subjects review board members’ comments to us, we have encountered considerable skepticism about nonspeaking autistic people's ability even to wear a head-mounted device, let alone to interact with virtual objects projected by the device. Furthermore, most past work on AR for (speaking) autistic people has focused on its utility in presenting information to them rather than on their ability to interact physically with virtual objects (e.g., [ 32 ]).

Thus, a necessary first step toward creating AR-based software that could be used to provide nonspeaking autistic people with structured learning opportunities is to investigate their ability a) to wear a head-mounted AR device and b) to interact with virtual objects. These were the goals of the study presented here. Specifically, we developed a game that runs on the HoloLens 2 where subjects learn and practice pressing virtual 3D, i.e., holographic, buttons to see a holographic character run, jump, and do somersaults. The game, designed with extensive input from the community, was implemented such that the interactions with the buttons got progressively harder. For example, the size of buttons decreased, and the number of buttons increased as the rounds progressed, and each round included an untimed task (where subjects could take as long as they wanted to push a button) and a timed task (where they had to push a button before a 10-second countdown timer ran out or they had to start the round again).

We tested our proposed game with 17 nonspeaking autistic adolescents and young adults. We recorded game statistics, a first-person video of the subject interacting with the holograms, a third-person video capturing the interactions, and hand and head tracking data. We also collected observational data and reports from caregivers.

Our results contradict prevailing assumptions about the ability of nonspeaking autistic people to wear the device and to learn to interact with virtual objects. All 17 subjects in our study tried on the device, and 15 ( $88\%$ ) wore it long enough to begin the game. Of the 15 subjects who began the game, 11 completed all three of its rounds, and 3 completed two of the three rounds. Most subjects got faster at pressing the holographic buttons over the course of the game, even as the game became more complex in each round.

Our findings open up exciting, new possibilities for the design of AR software for nonspeaking autistic people, including software focused on building skills that underlie alternative forms of communication, such as typing. Furthermore, our work provides design and process recommendations for conducting usability research with nonspeaking autistic people, which will benefit other researchers interested in developing and testing technology for this understudied and underserved population.

2 RELATED WORK

As immersive technologies have become more popular and accessible, their potential to assist autistic people has emerged as a promising area of study. The results of this previous work are generally positive, but published studies differ from ours in three important ways: They used either hand-held AR devices or previous-generation wearable devices, e.g., Google Glass. Neither of these categories has the features of modern head-mounted AR devices (like the HoloLens 2 used in this study). Further, these studies are generally not designed to investigate interaction with virtual objects, and they do not have nonspeaking autistic people as their target group. We elaborate on these three key differences next.

First, much of the research in AR with autistic people has been conducted with non-wearable devices, including computers, phones, and iPads [ 21 ]. For example, in an effort to improve recognition skills in autistic children, researchers introduced a program in which students could hold their smartphones up to an object to see its name appear [ 38 ]. In addition to not requiring a user to wear a device, these past studies did not employ 3D holograms, which we see as a central point to our long-term goal of providing an interface for practicing the skills involved in alternative forms of communication.

Some studies have used wearable devices–most often wearable VR devices (e.g., [ 4 , 12 ]). However, the completely immersive nature of VR may present challenges for autistic people whose sensory sensitivities can be extreme, as is the case for our target population [ 29 , 30 ]. Wearable AR, in contrast, can limit sensory overload and has the additional advantage of allowing a caregiver or teacher to be visible to a user at all times. The few studies using wearable AR with autistic people have shown a high degree of tolerability for the device [ 32 , 33 , 41 ]. However, these studies used Google Glass, which is much lighter than the HoloLens 2 device we used (36 vs. 560 grams) and, therefore, potentially easier for autistic subjects to tolerate. Google Glass does not offer features such as hand tracking, which enable direct interactions with virtual objects.

Indeed, a second key difference between previous studies and ours has to do with the type of virtual interactions subjects in the earlier work engaged in. For example, in one of the studies using Google Glass, the system responded to where users were looking [ 32 ]. One game, for example, prompted autistic users to direct their visual attention toward a conversational partner's face; when the device detected that they had done so, they received a point. In this system, subjects did not directly interact with virtual objects. Instead, the system presented them with virtual information and responded to their head movements. In other words, this system was more informative rather than interactive. In our study, we seek to understand if autistic subjects can interact with virtual objects, and we provide detailed metrics about those interactions.

Even in the few studies where autistic people interact with virtual objects, there are important differences from our work. Magrini et al., for example, developed a system using Microsoft Kinect in which autistic 6- to 10-year-olds interacted with material projected onto a wall [ 24 ]. In this system, by moving their hand, children could control a virtual hand on the wall to select which of several pictures matched a sound. Children's ability to interact in this way improved across ten 45-minute sessions. Note, however, that this study did not employ wearable AR and did not measure subjects’ ability to interact with digital 3D assets. One research group (Holo'Autisme) has developed a suite of HoloLens applications to improve motor and social skills in autistic people. 1 However, to our knowledge, there are as yet no published reports from that group about the tolerability of the HoloLens 2 for autistic people or about their ability to interact with 3D objects in an AR environment.

Finally and crucially, there are no studies to our knowledge that focus specifically on wearable AR for nonspeaking autistic people–a group with unique sensitivities and support needs. For example, the earlier described work using Google Glass [ 32 ] includes mostly speaking autistic individuals. The two "nonverbal" individuals who were included in the study (both five years of age) were the only subjects who were not able to tolerate the device. Another study by the same group, also focusing on Google Glass, reported that the two nonspeaking individuals in that study (also five years of age) "did not pass the initial 1-minute tolerability threshold as they would not continue to wear the smartglasses once placed" [ 20 ]. A study using the HoloLens with cognitively disabled people (which is a separate diagnosis from autism) reported that the device was reasonably well-tolerated [ 13 ]. However, that study did not report whether any of the subjects were autistic (or nonspeaking and autistic), nor did it report quantitative results about tolerability.

Of the 20 studies included in the most current systematic review of autism and AR [ 2 ], none focused exclusively on nonspeaking subjects, and only five included nonspeaking subjects. Of those five studies, only two used wearable systems: the Google Glass system (referenced above) [ 32 ], and Google Cardboard [ 36 ]; neither of which studied how users interacted with 3D virtual objects. Thus, an important contribution of our work is to provide a test of nonspeaking autistic people's ability to wear a modern AR system (in our case, the HoloLens 2) and to interact with 3D virtual objects.

3 THE BUTTON GAME

This study aims to assess the usability of an interactive head-mounted AR system for nonspeaking autistic people. This section describes the development of a HoloLens 2 game designed for this purpose. We first provide a rationale for using the HoloLens 2. We then outline how stakeholder input influenced our game design. We conclude by describing the game.

3.1 Selection of the AR Device

The HoloLens 2 is a head-mounted AR device designed by Microsoft weighing 560 grams with the strap. Among the different options available at the time of this study, we selected the HoloLens 2 for a number of reasons. First, the device is self-contained and does not need to be tethered to other devices, e.g., a PC. It is crucial for a nonspeaking autistic person to be untethered since they have sensory and attentional challenges that can compel them into constant motion [ 16 ]. Second, the HoloLens 2 supports hand tracking, thereby enabling simple interactions with 3D virtual objects ("holograms") using fingers in contrast to the use of specialized controllers. One of our eventual goals is to design AR applications that teach typing skills, which can facilitate alternative forms of communication for this population. Hence, the HoloLens 2 allows us to train subjects on the act of pressing holographic buttons using an index finger as a gateway to acquiring typing skills.

Furthermore, the HoloLens 2 supports advanced features (e.g., eye tracking, spatial audio, spatial awareness, and Cross Reality (CR)) that could be useful to this population and that we hope to explore in later work. For example, a recent study by Jaswal et al. suggests promise for eye-gaze-based interactions for nonspeaking subjects [ 17 ]. Furthermore, the CR capability can facilitate a multi-player experience where caregivers can support a nonspeaking autistic person to interact with virtual objects. The work presented in this paper is an essential step toward those later efforts.

3.2 Stakeholder Input

To characterize nonspeaking autistic people's ability to tolerate the HoloLens 2 and to interact with virtual objects, we created a game that involves button-pressing tasks of increasing complexity. Following a user-centred design approach, the game was designed with extensive input from parents of nonspeaking autistic individuals, professionals who work with them, and nonspeaking autistic people themselves. We collected design requirements over the course of six months by involving 5 consultants.

As noted earlier, most nonspeaking autistic people are never provided access to an effective alternative way to communicate [ 5 ]. However, over many years, some have learned to communicate effectively by typing what they would like to say rather than speaking it (e.g., [ 28 ]). Some of these individuals have graduated from college [ 6 ] and written best-selling books [ 16 ] and award-winning poetry [ 34 ]. Their typed testimony has provided important insights into their condition, informing the design of the game and our study.

For example, nonspeaking autistic people have written that their difficulty speaking is due at least in part to the considerable challenges they face in planning and producing intentional movements (e.g., [ 3 , 14 ]). Indeed, speech is the finest of fine motor abilities, and both experimental work and qualitative research have pointed to nonspeaking (and speaking) autistic people's difficulty in planning and executing motor movements (e.g., [ 11 , 14 , 40 ]). Although typing requires a different set of motor skills than speaking, it too requires an individual to be able to coordinate the thought they would like to express with the movements required to type it. Nonspeaking autistic people who communicate through typing have explained that they benefited from repeated opportunities to practice isolating and pointing at targets using their index finger–initially at large targets, progressing to smaller ones [ 14 ]. Thus, as will be described shortly, the game we designed begins with a single large virtual button, and later rounds involve successively smaller ones.

Nonspeaking autistic people, families, and clinicians also have emphasized the importance of tactile feedback when interacting with objects [ 25 ]. Providing tactile feedback in the current work would require users to wear, for example, haptic gloves–an additional sensory burden we did not wish to impose on our subjects in this initial study. Instead of tactile feedback, we used sensory substitution: When a virtual button was pressed, its colour changed, and a clicking sound was heard.

Nonspeaking autistic people have reported their need to move constantly [ 16 ]. As mentioned in Section 1 , wearable AR such as the HoloLens 2 can accommodate this constant motion because holograms can travel with the user. Additionally, these individuals have described benefiting from instruction that includes simultaneous audio and visual components [ 19 ]. As a result, the instructions delivered to subjects within the game were delivered visually, word by word, as they were read aloud. Our professional experts also emphasized the importance of visual and auditory cues in the holographic animations we created as part of the game.

Finally, nonspeaking autistic people who type have explained that throughout much of their lives, their cognitive abilities were underestimated (e.g., [ 19 ]). (Note that a recent study found that the cognitive abilities of nonspeaking autistic children have indeed been vastly underestimated [ 9 ].) As a result, they report being spoken to in infantilizing ways, treated as if they were incompetent, and assumed to be unable to learn [ 19 ]–all of which can reduce one's motivation to engage or participate. Recognizing that our subjects may have difficulty learning to coordinate their movements to interact with holograms (which pilot testing revealed can also be difficult for non-autistic people), we designed our game to be a simple one. We endeavoured in the design of the game itself to "presume competence" [ 18 ]: Following the suggestions of nonspeaking autistic informants, we used language that was appropriate for adolescents and young adults in the game and allowed them to take as much time as was available to play it.

3.3 Game Flow

Figure 1 provides a schematic diagram of the flow of the game. The game involves completing three rounds of increasing complexity. In each round, the goal is to push one or more holographic buttons. Buttons increase in number by 1 and decrease in diameter by $20\%$ with each successive round of the game. Each round has an untimed task, where users can take as much time as they need to push a button, and at least one timed task, where users have to push a button within 10 seconds. When they push a button successfully, an animated fox hologram appears and runs, walks, or does somersaults for 10 seconds. Each of these actions is accompanied by music, which was suggested by our professional experts as a cueing strategy to foster better attention and engagement. During the timed task of a given round, if a user does not push a button before the countdown timer runs out, they have to repeat the round, starting from the untimed task of that round. There is no limit to the number of times a round can be repeated. In the game, instructions are displayed and read aloud at the beginning and before start of the timed task of each round, and after failure in a timed task.

We incorporated the timed tasks after extensive discussions with professionals to add a challenge that could potentially increase subjects’ motivation and enjoyment of the game. We attempted to reduce any anxiety that could be triggered by requiring a response within 10 seconds by allowing unlimited opportunities to repeat rounds and to take breaks as needed. Results reported in Section 5 suggest that our efforts were successful.

Round 1 involves a single button, 11.5 cm in diameter, shown in Figure 2 . It begins with instructions, visually provided by text, with words appearing one at a time and by automated speech (Figure 2a). In the untimed task of Round 1, the "hand coach" of the HoloLens 2 appears, guiding users to push the button (Figure 2b). When they do so, the fox hologram becomes animated (Figure 2c). The timed task of Round 1 then begins. Here, users’ task is to push the button before the countdown timer runs out (represented by a progress bar that shrinks as time elapses; Figure 2d). The hand coach also appears during the timed task of Round 1. If the user pushes the button before the countdown timer expires, the animated fox again appears. Otherwise, the user begins Round 1 again, starting from the untimed task.

On successful completion of Round 1, Round 2 begins. Round 2 involves two buttons arranged horizontally (each 9.2 cm in diameter). In the untimed task, users can take as much time as they need to push either button, at which point the fox animation appears. (There is no hand coach in Round 2.) Next, in the timed task, both buttons are visible, but the previously pressed button is greyed-out and inactive, and users have to push the other button before the countdown timer runs out. If they are successful, the fox becomes animated. If they are not successful, they begin Round 2 again, starting from the untimed task. As in Round 1, instructions are provided visually and by automated speech before each of the tasks.

Round 3 begins on successful completion of Round 2. Round 3 is similar to Round 2 but involves three buttons arranged horizontally (each 7.35 cm in diameter). In the untimed task, users can take as much time as they need to push any of the three buttons, at which point the fox becomes animated. (There is no hand coach in Round 3.) There are two timed tasks in Round 3: In both tasks, the three buttons are visible; buttons pushed earlier in Round 3 are greyed out, and users have to push a button that has not yet been pushed before the 10 second countdown timer runs out. When they are successful, they see the fox animation; when they are not, Round 3 begins again from the untimed task. As in the other rounds, instructions are provided visually and by automated speech before each of the tasks.

The user receives a score of 1 for each task they complete, resulting in a maximum of 2 points each for Rounds 1 and 2 and 3 points for Round 3. Successfully completing all three rounds of the game thus yields a score of 7. When a subject has to repeat a round, they keep any points already accumulated in that round, but they accumulate additional points in that round only by completing a task they had not already completed.

In all button interactions, clicks are registered by a complete push; that is, touching the button is not sufficient, and the button must be pushed fully. This setting was motivated by our final goal (beyond the scope of this paper) to use AR to help nonspeaking autistic people learn the skills to type on a physical keyboard. Figure 3a shows a screenshot of an unsuccessful press from the untimed task of Round 2, and Figure 3b shows a screenshot of a successful press from the untimed task of Round 2.

For the placement of the holograms, we follow Microsoft's guideline for comfort. 2 All content that requires the user's constant eye gaze is placed slightly below the straight head gaze line to avoid eye fatigue. 3 Also, for reasons discussed in Section 3.1 , we use a tag-along component of MRTK 4 , which makes virtual objects follow user movements and remain in the centre of their field of view, with some flexibility.

To get the best experience from the HoloLens 2, eye calibration is required. 5 However, the calibration process requires the user to keep still and follow a hologram solely with their eyes. Given the sensory-motor and regulatory issues that our target population has, this process could be challenging. HoloLens 2 provides a way to bypass eye calibration by using Auto Eye Positioning 6 , which enables accurate hologram positioning after 20-30 seconds of processing. This is the approach we took. However, this has two implications. First, gaze interactions have to be disabled, and hence we cannot obtain eye-tracking data. Second, users have to wear the device for at least 30 seconds before starting the game.

4 METHODOLOGY

In this section, we describe our subjects. We then outline the materials used in the study and the flow of the test sessions. Finally, we describe the data collection, data analysis, and usability metrics.

4.1 Subjects

The study was approved by the ethics boards of both universities involved. Subjects were 17 adolescents and young adults ( Mean age = 20.82 years; range = 13 − 29 years; 16 males, 1 female), recruited with the assistance of speech-language pathologists, occupational therapists, and educators whose primary caseloads included nonspeaking autistic clients. All subjects had a clinical diagnosis of autism ( mean age of diagnosis of the 14 providing this information = 2.71 years; range = 1.6 − 4.0 years) and scored well above the cut-off on standardized parent-report questionnaires relevant to autism: Social Responsiveness Survey-2 [ 8 ] mean = 26.06 (cut-off for suggested autism evaluation = 15); Social Communication Questionnaire [ 31 ] = 85 (cut-off for suggested autism evaluation = 70).

All but one subject had been in several years of traditional speech therapy ( mean = 14.8 years). None were able to communicate effectively using speech: Parents of 6 subjects indicated that they were unable to talk using short phrases or sentences; parents of 8 subjects indicated that they could say some words but could not have a conversation that involved taking turns or building on what someone had said; parents of the remaining 3 subjects explained that their children could not communicate effectively using speech.

Subjects were seen one at a time at two locations in the U.S.A., (7 at one location and 6 at the other) and one in Canada (with 4 subjects). They were paid 20 USD or CAD via electronic gift card for their participation. Data were collected in July and August of 2022.

4.2 Materials

In addition to Microsoft's HoloLens 2, we used a Fitbit Charge 4 wrist-worn sensor to capture heart rate data, which we had originally intended to use as a proxy for stress levels during the game [ 27 ]. Ultimately, however, only 12 of 17 participants wore the Fitbit, and the data collected were not sufficient for us to use for this purpose. Thus, the heart rate data will not be described further. We also used a physical button of 7.5 cm diameter during test sessions for practice, which resembled the virtual buttons displayed during the game.

4.3 Test Sessions

As with the design of the game, the sessions were designed with input from professionals, parents, and nonspeaking autistic people who have learned to communicate by typing. Per their suggestions, parents and caregivers were present during the session. We followed the principle of presuming competence of our autistic subjects, speaking directly to them rather than to their caregivers, using language that was appropriate for adolescents and young adults, and providing multiple ways for them to become familiar with the device and the game even before agreeing to participate.

First, we made available in advance to potential subjects a 2-minute captioned video that explained what AR is and some future plans for how it could be used to support nonspeaking autistic people. Second, we created a brief written description (with photos) of the history of AR, an introduction to the HoloLens 2, and an explanation of what individuals would do if they agreed to participate. Several subjects had this content delivered to them as a lesson by a therapist prior to the session. Finally, we prepared a second 3-minute captioned video that showed and explained what subjects would see and do in the study–how a research assistant would help them to put on a Fitbit and the HoloLens 2, what they would see when the game began, how to press the virtual buttons, and the goal of the game (e.g., how increasing numbers of buttons would appear in each round; that in the timed task of each round, subjects should try to press a button before the countdown timer ran out).

Figure 4 shows the steps involved in each session. Subjects were seen one at a time. In the introduction phase, subjects were greeted by at least two researchers. A researcher reminded subjects about the goals of the study (which, as noted above, had been described in the materials provided in advance of the session) and reviewed the consent forms with subjects and their parent(s). The researcher emphasized that subjects could take a break whenever they liked and that they could stop at any time. The researcher answered any questions, and the subject and their parent each signed a consent form. In the meantime, another researcher set up the equipment.

The researcher then showed subjects (on a laptop) the 3-minute video described above, introducing the game. Next, the researcher showed subjects the Fitbit, reminded them (as also explained in the video) that it was intended to measure their heart rate, and asked for permission to affix it to their wrist. At the same time, the other researcher set up the HoloLens 2 and the external cameras. The introductory period lasted between 5 and 10 minutes.

In the practice phase (section B in Figure 4 ), a researcher offered a physical button that resembled the virtual buttons they would see in the game. This was intended to allow subjects to practice in the real world the action they would be asked to carry out in the game. Next, the subject was asked whether they wanted to see a live demonstration of the game played by the caregiver or the researcher. For the 8 subjects who requested it, the caregiver or the researcher put on the device, and the subject was shown a live stream of the first-person view of the HoloLens 2 as that person played the first round of the game. Next, Fitbit was offered again to those subjects that declined it in the introductory phase.

Next, we offered subjects the opportunity to wear the HoloLens 2 (section C in Figure 4 ). Importantly, the device was not placed on the subjects’ heads by the researchers, which our informants had explained had the potential to be intrusive. Instead, subjects put it on themselves or had their caregiver put it on for them, and the researchers helped to guide and adjust it, if necessary. Some subjects immediately removed it, in which case the researchers explained that this was not a problem and offered it to them again after a break. Details about subjects’ device tolerability are provided in Section 5.1 .

As illustrated in section D of Figure 4 , after subjects wore the device for at least 30 consecutive seconds (enough time to trigger the auto eye positioning feature of the HoloLens 2), a researcher launched the game, which involved the three rounds explained in Section 3.3 and shown in Figure 1 . If a subject removed the device, if desired, they would take a break; researchers invited them to continue if she or he wished. They were offered the opportunity to put the device on again, using the same procedure described above, and the game continued at the same round they had left off.

Finally, at the end of the game, as shown in section E of Figure 4 , the researchers removed the HoloLens 2 and the Fitbit. They then solicited feedback from the subjects and caregivers.

4.4 Data and Metrics

During each session, we collected raw data consisting of game events, first-person video collected from HoloLens 2, head and hand tracking data from the device, third-person video from an external camera, and Fitbit data (as noted in Section 4.2 , Fitbit data will not be reported). Two types of game event data were collected: a) events recorded by the game script, e.g., button pressed with the timestamp, and b) manually recorded events, e.g., time at which the device was put on. Researchers conducting the study recorded the manual events using a digital form. Timestamps of manually recorded events were assessed according to the first- and third-person video files and revised if necessary.

The rate at which head and hand tracking data are collected is approximately 30 frames per second. When the game is launched, the device sets the reference 3D coordinates. The head and hand tracking data are in the form of coordinates relative to this reference. We do not discuss head and hand tracking data in this paper; however, the raw data is made publicly available 7 to enable future research.

Our analysis focuses on four key metrics: a) how well the device was tolerated, b) subjects’ overall performance in playing the game, c) how performance changed on successive rounds, and d) why some attempted button presses were not successful. We describe these metrics next.

Tolerability : To investigate subjects’ ability to tolerate wearing the HoloLens 2, we report three primary pieces of data. Wear time is defined as the cumulative amount of time the subject wore the device during the session. We also report the Longest Consecutive Time (LCT) a subject wore the device. Finally, we report the number of breaks they took in a session.

Overall Performance : To provide a measure of subjects’ overall performance, we report their final score. As described in Section 3.3 , subjects who successfully completed all three rounds of the game received a score of 7 (i.e., they received 1 point for each untimed and timed task they completed in a round). Because subjects could receive the maximum score of 7 even if they had to repeat one or more rounds, we also report the number of times the subject had to repeat a round along with the total number of successful button presses.

Response Time : To investigate how a user's ability to interact with the virtual buttons changed over the course of the game, we calculated for each round (that a subject successfully completed) a measure called Time to Press (TTP): The mean time in that round between when a button became visible and when the subject successfully pressed it. If a subject took a break between button presses in a successful round, the break duration was deducted.

Characterization of Unsuccessful Attempts : We anticipated that there would be some difficulty interacting with virtual objects due to technology limitations [ 35 ] and motor challenges of our target population; hence we aimed to characterize unsuccessful button press attempts in order to better understand error patterns. The categories were based on researcher notes and analyzing first- and third-person videos of subjects. Due to experimenter or technical error, however, complete videos from both first- and third-person perspectives were available only for 11 participants. A research assistant used these videos to code each unsuccessful button press attempt into one of four categories: a) Genuine misses (if a subject did not point to the button accurately); b) User Interface (the subject appeared to touch an already pressed button that was greyed out); c) Sensitivity (subject appeared to touch the button, but it did not register since they did not push it completely); d) Idiosyncratic error. A second coder independently coded 5 randomly selected videos of the 11 that were available ( $45\%$ ); agreement between the coders on the four types of unsuccessful attempts was $86\%$ . Coders had to decipher what hand motions count as distinct attempts in addition to categorizing such attempts. Since certain participants moved their hands rapidly, some possible attempts were difficult to distinguish. The disagreement was on the basis of what counted as a distinct attempt and not on the basis of categorization.

5 RESULTS

We first present results related to the HoloLens 2 tolerability. Results pertaining to game performance and response time are presented next. Finally, we present a characterization of unsuccessful button press attempts to consider ways the AR interface could be improved.

5.1 Tolerability

The device was well tolerated by most of our nonspeaking autistic subjects. All 17 subjects tried it on, and 15 of 17 ( $88\%$ ) wore it long enough to begin the game. One of the subjects who did not begin the game (Subject 6) removed the device immediately and declined to continue. However, the other subject who ultimately did not start the game (Subject 1) wore the device for increasingly longer periods (6 seconds, followed by 20 seconds, followed by 75 seconds, followed by 80 seconds, for a cumulative wear time of 2 minutes and 55 seconds) before declining to continue.

Figure 5 shows the tolerability data from each of the 15 subjects who began the game, arranged by the number of breaks they took in ascending order. As the figure shows, 10 ( $67\%$ ) played the entire game without taking a break. For these 10 subjects, the total amount of time they wore the device ("wear time") was the same as their single longest time wearing the device ("LCT"): On average, they wore the device for 5 minutes and 4 seconds (range: 03: 05 − 07: 18). The other 5 subjects removed the device to take a break between 1 and 18 times. Their mean total wear time was 13 minutes and 47 seconds (range: 06: 40 − 23: 35), and their mean LCT was 4 minutes and 14 seconds (range: 2: 40 − 5: 40).

5.2 Game Performance

Of the 15 subjects who started the game, 14 were able to interact with at least one button, and 1 (Subject 2) decided to end the game after a few unsuccessful press attempts in the first round. As the top panel of Figure 6 shows, most of the 14 subjects who pressed at least one button obtained a perfect or near-perfect score on the game. Their mean final score was 6.71 (range: 5 − 7). 11 ( $79\%$ ) of these individuals completed both the untimed and timed tasks of all three rounds of the game, pushing 7 buttons and achieving the maximum score of 7; 2 subjects achieved a score of 6; 1 achieved a score of 5.

Another way to measure how well subjects performed on the game is to consider how many times the 14 subjects who pressed at least one button successfully had to repeat a round because they failed to push a button in the timed task of a round before the 10-second countdown timer ran out (depicted on the secondary y-axis of the top panel of Figure 6 ). As the figure shows, out of those who achieved the maximum score of 7, 6 subjects never had to repeat a round, 4 repeated a given round one or two times, and 1 subject finished the game after repeating rounds 16 times. Subjects 10, 17, and 8 repeated rounds several times (range: 5 − 8) and did not complete the game.

On average, the 14 subjects who pushed at least one button in the game repeated Round 1 0.07 times (range: 0 − 1), Round 2 0.64 times (range: 0 − 2), and Round 3 2.43 times (range: 0 − 15). Clearly, Round 3 (involving 3 buttons of the smallest size) was the most difficult for subjects.

Finally, we can also consider the number of successful button presses each subject made over the course of the game. As the lower panel of Figure 6 shows, most subjects, who completed the game pressed between 7 (the minimum number to achieve a perfect score) and 9 buttons (i.e., they pressed more than the minimum because they had to repeat a round). Subject 11, who also completed the game, pressed 32 buttons, another indication of his persistence. The 3 subjects who did not obtain the full score pressed between 9 and 14 buttons. This is important because it demonstrates that even though they did not complete all rounds of the game, they did not have difficulty interacting with the virtual buttons–the challenge, as will be illustrated further in Section 5.3 , was with pushing a button before the countdown timer ran out in the timed tasks.

5.3 Response Time

The 11 subjects who completed the game were faster, on average, to push buttons at the end of the game than at the beginning: Their mean Time to Press (TTP) was 13.40 seconds in Round 1, 6.07 seconds in Round 2, and 5.59 seconds in Round 3. A one-way repeated measures ANOVA on subjects’ mean TTP in each round yielded a significant effect of round, F (2,20) = 4.51, p = 0.02. Post-hoc Bonferroni-adjusted tests showed that subjects were faster to push a button in Round 3 than in Round 1, t (20) = 2.68, p = 0.04, but Rounds 1 and 2 and Rounds 2 and 3 did not differ from each other, p > 0.06.

As Figure 7 shows, at the individual level, the mean TTP for 10 of the 11 subjects who completed the game (green data points, inside the violin plots) was faster in Round 3 than in Round 1 (all subjects except Subject 2). This is noteworthy because the buttons in Round 3 were smaller than the button in Round 1, requiring more precise motor control to press. Clearly, most of the participants in our study were able to generalize what they learned about how to successfully activate a large button in Round 1 to smaller buttons in Round 3.

Figure 7 also shows the mean TTP for Rounds 1 and 2 for each of the 3 subjects who did not complete the game (red data points outside the violin plots; no data points are shown for Round 3 because these 3 subjects did not complete that round). As the figure shows, the mean TTP for one subject improved from Round 1 to Round 2, but the other two subjects were slower in Round 2 than Round 1 8 . As noted in Section 5.2 , even the subjects who did not complete the game demonstrated they could successfully interact with buttons; their challenge was in completing the timed tasks of the rounds.

5.4 Characterization of Unsuccessful Attempts

As mentioned in Section 4.4 , complete first- and third-person videos were available for 11 subjects, 9 of whom completed all three rounds of the game. A research assistant coded these videos to investigate why some attempts at pushing a holographic button were not successful, categorizing each unsuccessful press into one of four categories (Genuine Misses, User Interface, Sensitivity, and Idiosyncratic Error). Figure 8 shows the distribution of these four categories of unsuccessful attempts for each subject for whom video data was complete.

The first thing to note from the figure is that there seem to be two subgroups of subjects. Eight of these 11 subjects had between 0 (Subject 9) and 14 unsuccessful attempts (Subject 13). The reason for those unsuccessful attempts was primarily because of sensitivity issues (where they touched the button but did not push it to the required depth) or because of user interface issues (in Rounds 2 or 3, they attempted to push a previously pressed, greyed-out button). 2 of these subjects made idiosyncratic errors (e.g., attempting to press the physical button still sitting on the table rather than the holographic button).

The other subgroup consisted of 3 subjects who made at least twice as many unsuccessful attempts as the maximum of the other sub-group (range: 28 − 30); for them, too, sensitivity and user interface issues were common. These 3 subjects also genuinely missed the holographic button on some occasions (i.e., they pointed with their index finger extended but did not make contact with the holographic button), and 1 also made idiosyncratic errors.

Overall, $53.3\%$ of the unsuccessful attempts were sensitivity issues, $20.0\%$ involved user interface issues, $20.0\%$ were genuine misses, and $6.7\%$ were idiosyncratic errors. We will defer a discussion about insights from these analyses of unsuccessful presses until Section 6 . However, we note that the fact that only $20.0\%$ of unsuccessful presses were genuine misses suggests that the holographic buttons were perceived as intended–as buttons that afforded being pressed. Additionally, this finding demonstrates that subjects had the perceptuo-motor skills necessary to locate a target button, isolate their index finger, and attempt to press the button.

6 DISCUSSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

The goal of this study was to investigate nonspeaking autistic adolescents’ and young adults’ ability to wear a modern head-mounted AR device and to learn to interact with 3D virtual objects, which is an essential first step toward creating and testing assistive software tailored for this population. Our findings were clear: $88\%$ of subjects wore the device long enough to start the game, and of those who wore the device, $67\%$ completed the game without taking the device off or requesting a break. Performance metrics, including the time to press measure (TTP) and our analysis of unsuccessful presses, showed that most subjects quickly learned the affordances of the virtual buttons used in this study.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to report empirical evidence that nonspeaking autistic people can tolerate a state-of-the-art head-mounted AR device. As noted in Section 2 , the only previous studies investigating the tolerability of an AR device for autistic participants used the Google Glass [ 20 , 32 ]. In neither of these previous studies were the two nonspeaking subjects (both five-year-olds) able to wear the Google Glass at all or for more than a minute. In our study, 16 of the 17 nonspeaking autistic individuals wore the HoloLens 2 for at least 2 minutes and 55 seconds, and 1 wore it for a total of 25 minutes and 35 seconds.

That said, 1 subject could not tolerate the device, 1 wore the device but never pushed a button, and 3 wore the device but did not complete the game. Below, we offer several design recommendations that could have been helpful to these subjects and which may also have been useful to subjects who did successfully complete the game. We then describe some process recommendations based on our experience.

6.1 Design Recommendations

Customizing AR session duration : A key insight from this study is that there was a good deal of variability in the longest consecutive time (LCT) subjects wore the device in a single stretch, ranging from 0 to about 7 minutes. (We note that for those subjects who completed the game very quickly, even though their LCT was short, they could have potentially worn the device for longer but did not need to in the context of our game.) Thus, AR software targeting the nonspeaking autistic population must provide caregivers and clinicians with the ability to carefully control session duration based on empirical observations of their subject's comfort levels.

Rethinking timed interactions : Another key insight is that timed interactions are not effective at improving nonspeaking autistic people's skills in interacting with AR objects. Most subjects learned to press the virtual buttons, as demonstrated by their success in the untimed task of each round. But our requirement that they demonstrate their skill in a timed task in each round was extremely challenging for a few subjects. If they could not press the button before the 10-second countdown timer expired, they had to restart the round from the untimed task. Round 3 (involving the smallest 3 buttons and 2 timed tasks) was clearly the most difficult. Three of the subjects who began Round 3 declined to continue after having to repeat the round several times. We suggest the design of a more forgiving task flow, e.g., allowing subjects to progress to the next round even if they fail in the timed interaction. Repeated practice using untimed interactions on their own could be used as a means to improve subject response times if that were a goal or necessary for a particular application. If for any reason timed interactions are deemed essential, the time limit can gradually increase or decrease based on the observed improvements in a subject's response time.

Designing UI to reduce unsuccessful attempts : Our analysis of reasons for unsuccessful attempts also yielded several important insights. First, the challenges with button sensitivity we observed suggest that requiring the button to be pressed to a certain depth for activation may be too stringent a criterion; it might be possible to start with a more sensitive button (e.g., simply registering clicks on making contact with the button) and then changing it to register on push to reduce accidental presses. A second insight is that the user interface errors were an artifact of our assumption that all users would intuitively know that a greyed-out button was disabled. In hindsight, our video instructions shared in advance of the session with subjects should have been more explicit about what the greyed-out button was. Third, the idiosyncratic errors made by four subjects seemed to reflect some very occasional genuine confusion about what the task was. For example, some subjects reached out to the physical button they had used previously for practice instead of interacting with the virtual button in their field of view. A few tried to touch the HoloLens 2 visor. One subject touched the screen of the laptop that was live streaming the first-person view of the session. Such attempts stopped once we reiterated to these subjects the objective of pressing the virtual buttons in their field of view. In summary, most unsuccessful attempts (including the sensitivity, user interface, and even the idiosyncratic category) could have been eliminated with simple changes to the design of the game and the instructions given.

Customizing hologram placement : We also discovered that the default HoloLens 2 object placement recommendations were effective for most subjects, and most subjects benefited from the use of the tag-along component where the holograms moved with the user. However, there were exceptions. Subject 14 had difficulties reaching the buttons in the game. Although they completed the game successfully, the placement distance and orientation of objects were challenging. Furthermore, this subject preferred to look at the objects using their peripheral vision. This was not possible with the tag-along component; any attempt by the subject to move their head to look at the objects via peripheral vision resulted in the objects quickly being relocated in front of them. Similarly, Subject 8 (who completed only Rounds 1 and 2) tried to walk towards the buttons, which again was not possible due to our configuration. Hence, the placement and orientation of the buttons, as well as the sensitivity of the tag-along component, need to be customized based on user and caregiver input and pilot testing with each subject.

6.2 Process Recommendations

Our extensive consultations with the community were helpful in ensuring user engagement during test sessions. Specifically, the use of age-appropriate language and the strategy of addressing subjects directly (instead of through a caregiver) were critical. One parent remarked that researchers were "kind and communicative directly to (subject name), which is enlightened in and of itself." We avoided the use of infantilizing language in our instructional materials and in the videos shared with subjects before the session and additionally ensured that these materials in themselves had educational value to them (e.g., providing some of the history of the development of AR). Explaining that subjects could take breaks whenever they liked also seemed to help with engagement. For example, Subject 11 took 10 breaks but succeeded in completing the game.

Our efforts to mitigate anxiety were also appreciated. Feedback from caregivers suggested that users found the instructional materials provided in advance particularly helpful. For example, many parents remarked that their children watched the preparatory videos multiple times before their sessions so that they knew exactly what to expect. Some suggested incorporating practice sessions wearing an object similar to the HoloLens 2 to build more tolerance. As an additional method to manage anxiety, most sessions were conducted in a physical space that the subjects were familiar with, in the presence of trusted therapists and family. In many cases, encouraging words and regulatory help from these individuals seemed to reduce anxiety, as did the opportunity before testing began to have someone play Round 1 while the subjects watched.

Finally, based on the analysis of unsuccessful button press attempts, we recommend creating a distraction-free interaction environment. In our case, one free of the physical button and researcher's laptop, which some subjects found distracting. Additionally, in our study, on rare occasions, subjects received additional encouragement that caused mild confusion. For example, in one instance, a subject had successfully pressed a button, but a caregiver was still encouraging them to press the button based on the delayed live feed the caregiver was watching on the researcher's laptop. To address such situations, researchers should provide precise language to caregivers about giving instructions and encouragement during the session.

7 LIMITATIONS

Our study has several limitations. First, our findings that almost all of our nonspeaking autistic users could tolerate the AR device and interact with virtual objects is, of course, limited to the HoloLens 2. Further studies are needed to generalize our findings to other devices. Second, our study had a limited sample size of 17. Note, however, that from a usability perspective, 17 participants is not unusual. Indeed, Nielsen argued that five subjects could identify up to $75\%$ of usability issues, and 15 subjects can identify all usability issues [ 26 ].

Third, our study was conducted in three different locations and involved seven researchers. While researchers were highly trained and the test sessions and protocol were standardized, it is possible that there were differences that influenced subjects’ performance. Fourth, our sample included 16 males and just 1 female. This imbalance occurred because autism is four times more likely to be diagnosed in males than females [ 42 ] and because ours was a convenience sample. It will be important to deliberately recruit more female autistic individuals to participate in any future work.

Finally, we recruited and tested only adolescents and young adults. This decision was based on the minimum age limit recommendation of 13 provided by Microsoft. Sampling a younger or older population might reveal different trends than what we report here.

8 CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK

In this study, we showed that most nonspeaking autistic adolescent and young adult participants could wear the HoloLens 2 and could learn to interact with virtual buttons. Our results contradict conventional intuitions about the suitability of AR for this population. As noted in Section 1 , to the extent that comments of grant reviewers and human subject review board members represent informed positions, nonspeaking autistic adolescents and young adults would not have been expected to be able to wear the device, let alone to interact with holograms as well as they did. Our findings open up exciting, new possibilities for the design of wearable AR for this understudied and under-served population, especially the design of AR to build skills that underlie alternative forms of communication.

Motivated by testimony from nonspeaking autistic people who have learned to type, future work will develop and evaluate modules that allow nonspeaking autistic people to practice their motor skills within an AR environment that delivers engaging, age-appropriate learning materials. CR modules that allow therapists to train their subjects to interact with virtual objects will also be explored. Future work will also investigate how other features supported by modern AR devices and accessories, such as eye tracking, spatial awareness, and tactile feedback, can be leveraged for the nonspeaking autistic population.
