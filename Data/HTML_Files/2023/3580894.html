Recent developments in Virtual Reality (VR) applications, such as hand gesture tracking, provide new opportunities to create embodied user experiences. Numerous gesture elicitation studies have been conducted. However, in most instances they lack validation of implemented gestures, as well diversity of participant groups. Our research explores the digitalization of intangible cultural heritage in collaboration with one of the San tribes in Southern Africa. The focus is on particular gestures as embodied interactions of a VR implementation of a traditional San hunting story. In this paper, we present a gesture study, which entails an in-situ elicitation of natural gestures, a co-designed integration, a VR story implementation with grasping and three mid-air gestures, and a user evaluation. Based on our findings, we discuss the anthropological value of gesture implementations determined by an indigenous community, the local usability of a grasping gesture, and in-VR gesture elicitation, as an extension of existing methods.

1 INTRODUCTION

VR has gained greater popularity, especially with current technical developments, such as hand-tracking, which allows for gesture controlled applications. A recent review, examining HCI gesture vocabularies and underlying factors for their design, found that: "despite the widespread adoption of devices with gesture sensing capabilities, the design of gesture vocabularies that suit the various needs and constraints of an application, it's context, and it's users, is still a challenging task."[ 46 , p.36]. While there is an increasing number of gesture elicitation studies, research on actual implementations and use in diverse contexts remain scarce [ 42 , 45 ]. In 2018, a systematic review of mid-air gesture studies by Vogiatzidakis and Koutsabasis [ 42 ] reported that 45 out of 47 studies did not implement gestures into working prototypes, but used a Wizard of Oz method instead for evaluation. Since then, only a few studies have reported on implemented gestures [ 1 , 10 , 23 , 34 , 39 ], and are emphasizing the significance of user-created gesture validation within a functional VR application [ 4 , 35 ]. Another shortcoming expressed by Villarreal-Narvaez et al. [ 41 ], having reviewed 216 articles reporting on gesture elicitation studies conducted between 2009 and 2019, is the lack of repeatability, replicability, or reproducibility, due to the omission of descriptive details in regard to context of use and the experiment itself.

A further concern of current gesture studies has been the lack of diversity in the recruitment of participants, with a majority of studies conducted with WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich and Democratic) participants only [ 8 , 45 , 47 ]. Nearly half of the gesture studies reviewed by Vogiatzidakis and Koutsabasis [ 42 ] were conducted with academics, either staff or students, and no representation of Global South users. Many populations, especially from the Global South, have been disregarded and marginalized in HCI research [ 20 ] thereby leading to biases and further fostering of the margins [ 12 ]. It has to be noted, that the inclusion of under-represented groups in HCI research can not be justified by the aim of finding "differences" but rather serves the purpose of ensuring equal contributions to the research agenda, process and knowledge creation [ 20 , 32 ]. Thus gesture studies with under-represented participants expand the diversity of participating user groups and the gesture taxonomy in new contexts, as well as provide methodological and theoretical contributions. For example, a recent gesture elicitation study with novice non-Western participants has yielded in the extension of Morris et al. [ 28 ]’s natural gesture elicitation technique in an effort to reduce legacy bias [ 4 ].

In our research we are particularly interested in the possibilities that gesture-controlled VR offers in terms of recreations of embodied cultural experiences, as part of an attempt to safeguard intangible cultural heritage. Navarro-Newball et al. [ 30 ] used mid-air gestures to afford engaging visitor experiences of cultural practices in a museum. They concluded that much more research needs to be done in order to determine the appropriateness and accuracy of gesture types within a given context. In our study, we have explored the digitalization of an oral traditional hunting story told by an elder of the Ju’/hoansi San tribe. We have used a community-based co-design approach [ 44 ] framed in the 7C-model [ 22 ] to transform the story step by step into a VR narrative. In this paper, we focus on the gestures, derived from natural cultural practices, as a significant interaction contributing to an embodied cultural experience. The implemented user interactions are three distinct mid-air gestures, as extracted during a hunting demo in the field, and a grasping gesture. Thus, we present a complete user-driven gesture study including the elicitation of in-situ natural gestures, an integration of gestures in co-designed scenarios, and an evaluation of implemented gesture use during the interactive VR narrative play (see Figure 1 ). We confirm the importance of an iterative and collaborative approach to the co-creation of a digitalized cultural experience, and in particular gestures. Thus, our contribution is an empirical gesture study, which entails the elicitation, curation, implementation and evaluation of gestures. Besides adding to the still scarce body of implemented gesture studies it also add to the even more scarce body of work at CHI, which includes so far under-represented participants. In this study the process of eliciting and evaluating particular cultural gestures is replicable. While on the other hand the evaluation of gestures such as grasping, serves to confirm its usability by local users within the context of a digitalized cultural practice.

In the following section we present related work before we present the research context, the elicitation of the gestures as well as their integration and implementation within the VR hunting narrative. We present the in-VR play evaluation of the gestures and detail the findings. We then discuss important considerations in regards to the elicitation, validation and curation of gestures used as part of cultural practices.

2 RELATED WORK

We first define mid-air gestures, before we present the grasping gesture, and then review methods to counter systemic biases in gesture design.

2.1 A Classification of Mid-Air Gesture Types

A gesture is a bodily motion that contains information, as facial expressions or motion of limbs [ 29 ]. The information conveyed depends on the movement itself and accompanying speech. However, some gestures can be understood in isolation  [ 29 ], such as a flat hand with the palm facing towards another person meaning “stop”. Gestures can further be categorized into hand gestures, specifically, where in this paper these are further distinguished to be mid-air and grab gestures. Mid-air gestures are to be understood as gestures that are performed in the air, without anything touching the hand. This could be a ’thumbs-up’ gesture, a wave, pointing etc. Grab gestures are grouped with the natural interactions of grab, push, drag and release [ 6 ]. With recent hand tracking experiences, it is possible to grab an object from a distance with a mid-air gesture using a pinch gesture [ 5 ], within this study grab will strictly be concerning actual, digital, reachable objects.

In this paper, the focus is on hand gestures without accompanying speech. As such, Aigner et al. [ 2 ] taxonomy of mid-air hand gestures is used. Aigner et al. [ 2 ] define five gesture classifications: pointing, semaphoric (static, dynamic, stroke), pantomimic, iconic (static, dynamic) , and manipulation . Semaphoric (static and dynamic) and manipulation gestures were implemented and evaluated in this study. Static gestures are where the hand performs one non-moving gesture, while dynamic is a moving hand gesture conveying a specific meaning. The meaning of both is learned through experience, such as a thumbs up meaning "yes" or "okay", or a dynamic shake of the hand meaning "no". The meaning of semaphoric gesture is not directly connected to a shape or a specific object, it is strictly connected to the learned meaning of the gestures.

Manipulation gestures include translation, rotation, and scale, e.g. grabbing into the air and moving the hand sideways to demonstrate moving an object to the side. More specifically, when performing manipulation gestures, users expect immediate feedback.

2.2 Grasping Gestures

Grasping gestures were previously implemented using controllers and only recently introduced in gesture controlled applications. Conducting different studies, Adkins et al. [ 1 ] found that users preferred hand-tracked grab gestures over controllers, while Masurovsky et al. [ 23 ] found the opposite. The later states, that the reason might be due to a lack of appropriate hand-tracking solutions thereby having made the comparison uneven.

When implementing grab gestures, the application needs to differentiate between grab, rotation, movement and release [ 21 ]. Including press and drag provides the user with a natural object interaction [ 6 ]. For instance, it allows a user to push an object without the need to physically grab it first. The pinch gesture has been the most frequently used method for grabbing items placed near the user and far away (implemented as the standard within the Oculus Quest 2 [ 36 ] and the Hololens 2 [ 27 ]), functioning as a point and click interaction. When novice users are experiencing hand-tracked VR for the first time it is likely that they will interact based on their real life experiences, using natural interaction. In the real world there are different ways to pick up an item. Depending on the item and its placement, people adjust their aim and movement towards the item. In this way the gesture is not necessarily the same - neither is the click of a button on a controller representative of the object characteristics, or representative of the situation in which grabbing occurs. Natural interaction most logically, is the interplay between these factors, not only leading to immersion in VR, but also predictability for the user who has developed embodied cognition for grabbing objects since early age.

2.3 Mid-Air and Grab Gestures in Research

Research on mid-air gesture design and evaluation is still sporadic, and slowly gaining momentum. Aigner et al. [ 2 ] and Morris et al. [ 28 ] based their work on categorizing gestures and develop elicitation methods, Danielescu and Piorkowski [ 8 ] and Wu et al. [ 45 ] highlight the need for inclusion of the user in the design of gestures, and, Arendttorp et al. [ 4 ], Pei et al. [ 35 ], Yu and Bowman [ 47 ] implement gestures in prototypes to evaluate them in context. While mid-air gesture interaction has been increasing over recent years, research concerning grab with hand tracking is still largely unexplored. Three recent studies have explored grab gestures [ 1 , 10 , 23 ]. Masurovsky et al. [ 23 ] and Adkins et al. [ 1 ] arrive at two different conclusions, comparing grab with a controller versus hand tracking grab. The findings indicate that more efforts need to go in the implementation of grab gestures, resolving technical issue and better informing research. Fang and Harrison [ 10 ] addressed the issue of having no haptic feedback when the controller is put aside. They proved that using the non-active hand within the area of interaction effectively provided a sense of touch feedback. This heightened the sense of immersion and realism for the player, while maintaining presence. Alternatively, Pei et al. [ 35 ] have recently been working on avoiding actual grab and use mid-air static gestures to form physical objects in a hand interface. The hand interface was found to be effective, expressive, and fun to use for their participants. Nine out of the 17 participants had experience with VR, which may have been beneficial in their understanding of the system. It required to remember or associate the gestures with the objects to use them.

2.4 Study Biases and Inclusion of Diverse Participants

Interactions are often designed by developers [ 45 ] focusing on technological affordances rather than human naturalness and comfort. Developers and designers have a tendency to decide on preferred interactions, based on prior experience and without input from end users [ 45 ]. Morris et al. [ 28 ], pointed out, that when technical experienced developers and users design for new interactions the design is based on prior technological experiences causing a legacy bias. In order to imagine new gesture interactions and counter the biases produced, when working with WEIRD participants, Morris et al. [ 28 ] devised the three P's methods (Production of gestures aimed at one specific task, Priming the participants before producing gestures, and Partners performing gestures to one another). To overcome the potential bias Danielescu and Piorkowski [ 8 ] is making use of the production method by Morris et al. [ 28 ], where the participants have to produce as many gestures as possible for the same interaction. Generating many gesture ideas has been found to be challenging, as participants tend to prefer their first and best ideas[ 28 ]. This has resulted in researchers asking for three gestures [ 7 , 14 , 43 ], thereby limiting the production. However, Danielescu and Piorkowski [ 8 ] kept prompting the participants to generate more ideas, having the participants produce between two and ten gestures per referent. Resulting in some participants still preferring the first or second gesture, while others benefited from the extra time and preferred some of their last ideas.

An other method to counter systemic biases is the inclusion of non-WEIRD users into gesture studies, as suggested by Arendttorp et al. [ 4 ]. The so far limited research and inclusion of non-WEIRD participants in gesture research and VR design, leaves the results one-sided and exclusive to populations in the Western world, and raises “...concerns about sample diversity and the generalization of findings from one population to another.” [ 3 , p.1]. It will inevitably lead to the production of western value loaded technologies, being exported to the Global South, when they eventually have the necessary infrastructure. Not only would this mean that users lose their local cultures through interactive experiences with technologies from elsewhere [ 25 ] but equally the technology would be of low ecological validity [ 9 ]. We maintain that especially in embodied technologies, the inclusion of local researchers and participants is a necessity to design for diversity.

3 NATURAL GESTURE ELICITATION AND IMPLEMENTATION IN A RURAL NAMIBIAN COMMUNITY

Under the umbrella of a long-term collaboration between a Namibian rural community and the Namibia University of Science and Technology, in partnership with Aalborg University, the rural partner community selected a traditional story to be created as a VR application, during one of the community meetings. Thus on the first field trip, of this project, gestures were uncovered in a naturalistic situation through a community-led story telling session and a demonstration of a simulated hunt in the bush. In a dedicated design session, community members further demonstrated hunting equipment and gestures used for non-verbal, human-human interaction. The hunting story was then implemented with hand gestures and narrative elements from the traditional hunting story as a VR prototype based on the field elicitation [ 17 ]. On the second field trip, community members were recorded while playing the hunting VR story to observe and analyze the usage and detailed performance of mid-air and grab gestures to further investigate performance, variances and application of gestures naturally performed in a VR context.

3.1 The Ju/’Hoansi Community Partners

The Ju/’Hoansi community, located in eastern Namibia, near the border to Botswana, is one of many marginalized San communities [ 11 ]. Spread across southern Africa, with approximately 30.000 individuals living in Namibia, San people make up roughly 1.18% of the population [ 16 ]. The indigenous San people are considered to be part of the oldest tribes on the African continent [ 24 , 33 ]. The San tribes were traditionally hunter-gathering, nomadic people, with many culturally relevant stories e.g. hunting, in relation to their cosmology [ 26 ]. Our partner community consists of approximately 200 people in 30 households and is part of an established research collaboration with a local university since 2017. Over the years, many emerging technologies have been jointly explored by different community members and researchers, such as VR with controllers [ 37 , 38 ] and gesture control through hand-tracking [ 4 ] within a scope of culturally meaningful VR applications for intangible cultural heritage safeguarding.

Visiting the community has been long drives in 4x4 vehicles into the Kalahari bushland on sandy, often invisible paths. It requires a couple of overnightings in tents on the community-run campsite. The community has released a binding communication protocol [ 19 ], which prescribes a starting and ending community meeting of each trip, creating a forum in which all can present, discuss and reflect past, intended or future activities. Field trips consists of numerous research activities, part of different projects, as well as computer training, and any other support or socialization in the community space. The community decides which projects they would like to pursue and volunteering community members become active participants or co-researchers. In regards to the exploitation of VR, the community has unanimously expressed a desire to see more of their own culture in VR, wishing to use VR as a tool to help teach the younger members of their community about their culture and traditions, but has also often commented that the outside World should know more about their traditions. Thus it was agreed to implement a hunting narrative as told by one of the village Elders, in an attempt to re-create a cultural practice.

3.2 A Ju/’Hoansi Hunting Story Told

After the welcoming community meeting on the first day of field trip 1, the Elder, who was once a hunter, began telling a traditional story of hunting a kudu (a large African Antelope). The Elder first told the story in the local language to a larger community crowd (see Figure 2 ), as few in the community have knowledge of their own past traditions in terms of hunting. After this session, the story was specifically told to us through a translator. In the following we share a shortened version of the story.

The story is about a man that goes hunting with his younger sons. While hunting they find a kudu, which they injure with a poisoned arrow, where-after which it runs away. The hunters head back to their village, not pursuing the kudu any further for the day. The following day, the man and his sons venture back out to find the wounded kudu, tracking its prints and trails of blood. Once they find it, it is weakened by the poison. They proceed to kill it with a spear and cut off its head. The story ends with the hunters gathering the tribe around the dead kudu where they delegate the meat, set up a new village (as done in their former nomadic lifestyle) and celebrate their successful hunt.

3.3 Demonstration of a Traditional Hunt

In order to not speculate on the intricate details of the hunting itself, which we could misunderstand, the community suggested to demonstrate a hunting simulation. With no aim of actually hunting an animal and with the options to pause and ask questions, a hunting trip with novice computer scientist hunters, and knowledgeable community hunters began. The purpose was to expand the understanding of the intangible heritage and to identify gestures used.

Three members of the community (one Elder, one younger adult and the community's coordinator) began by instructing in finding animal tracks in the bush, the gestures they used for silent communication and explained the hunting equipment that they brought. Furthermore, they showed different kinds of bushes they gather berries from, a type of root they find in the ground (which they can both drink and eat) as well as how they prepare their gatherings. Lastly, they demonstrated how they would prepare the poisoned arrows by applying pasted worms to string that is wrapped around the arrowhead. This trip informed the gestures and interactions that were to be implemented in the VR story. Through discussion of the story three mid-air gestures and one grab gesture were specified. A static semaphoric mid-air gesture describing the kudu, and a dynamic semaphoric to guide fellow hunters to crouch. A manipulation grab gesture was specified to be used before approaching the animal. The hunters pick up a small portion of sand and watch which direction the sand is blowing, as it slowly leaves the hand. This tells the hunters which direction to walk to avoid the animal catching their scent and thereby to remain hidden. These three gestures are illustrated in Figure 3 .

3.4 Co-Design Sessions

A total of 12 community members participated in co-design workshop sessions in groups of four. Each group detailed the different steps of the hunt, explaining the hunt preparation, tracking and injuring the kudu, returning home, tracking injured kudu and killing, hanging meat up and building new home. During the sessions parts of the cultural practices were clarified, such as details on the production of poison on the arrows, gestures such as checking the wind direction, refinements of 3D models and selection of scene elements. Each scene was sketched out.

3.5 The Hunting Story Implemented as a VR Prototype

Following the narrative of the hunting story as told by the Elder, and the scenario design session with the community, the VR prototype was divided into four scenes in which the player must go through (see figure 4 ). The developers chose to replace the returning home scene with a fading in and out darkening to indicate that one night had passed. The deployment platform being the Oculus Quest 2 necessitates a rather low-polygonal workflow, thus the 3D objects to be used were also evaluated for their visual characteristics with the community members on a separate occasion. This was to ensure that working in a non-realistic graphical expression did not override any culturally significant details, which could be missed. The community approved the models and provided feedback where changes were necessary. The increased insights about the hunting methods, positioning of hunters, physical movement and gestures used, which were contextualised during the imitated hunt, were likewise implemented. An overview of the scenes is visible as. The application was created in the game engine Unity (version 2020.3.17f1) and installed on an Oculus Quest 2 headset. Only objects to be used in the gameplay were assigned physical properties using the Unity Physics engine thereby allowing collision detection between the objects and the tracked, virtual hands.

In the following sections the story is presented as it is implemented in the technical prototype.

3.5.1 Scene 1 - Preparing for the hunt. The first scene invites the player to prepare for the hunt. The player is offered a poisoned arrow and a root, which both are to be grabbed. When having collected both objects, the player is offered a bag by another Non-playable-character (NPC) to indicate to have prepared for the hunt. Grabbing the bag will take the player to the next scene. The minimum required grab interactions in scene one are three: picking up the arrow, the root and the bag.

3.5.2 Scene 2 - Finding the kudu. Scene two is divided into two sequences, a short sequence where the player first has to track the footprints of a kudu, and a longer sequence where the player must guide a group of three NPCs to hunt the kudu. The player must first gesticulate that there is a kudu, then requesting the NPCs to crouch down, and finally check the wind direction by drizzling sand through one hand. Completing all three gestures will have the NPCs move; crouch down, find their weapons, and finally sneak and shoot the kudu with a poisoned arrow. The kudu gets hit and runs past the player and away into the distance. While passing the player, the arrow falls out of the kudu. Picking up the arrow changes the scene lights indicating a transition from day to night and back to day, with a change of scene. If the user does not perform all three gestures (signal animal, crouch, sand gesture) the story will continue automatically after three minutes. The minimum interactions are thereby three gestures, or none after three minutes.

3.5.3 Scene 3 - Killing the kudu. The third scene is also divided into two sequences. Firstly, the player must track the kudu once more, this time among multiple different, animal tracks. The kudu tracks show an added blood trail to them. Following the correct tracks will lead to the second sequence, finding the dying kudu. The kudu is wounded but not dead, so the player must grab a spear and stab it. Hereafter, an NPC offers the player a knife to cut the meat from the kudu. Placing the knife in the kudu changes the environment to the last scene. Four interactions are needed: Grabbing the spear, stabbing the kudu, grabbing the knife, and cut the kudu.

3.5.4 Scene 4 - Hanging the meat and building a village. The fourth and final scene reveals the dead kudu, cut in three pieces and placed on a small stick. Each piece must be picked up and hung on a branch. Each meat piece placed activates NPCs to set up a new village. When all three pieces are placed, the VR player will see a completed new village and NPCs around a fire. The story is completed when all three pieces have been grabbed and placed in the tree. The minimum interaction is three, as grab and release goes together.

4 EVALUATION OF VR HUNTING PROTOTYPE

Returning for the second field trip, one and a half months later, the implemented hunting prototype was evaluated with the community.

4.1 Procedure of Evaluation

The participants were invited to try the prototype, and afterwards to participate in two post play-through interviews. The first interview was a semi-structured, individual one with a focus on getting the immediate experiences from the participants as they left the gazebo (play-area). The second interview was a semi-structured interview focused on the implemented gestures and grabbing of virtual objects. The participants were interviewed in pairs of two to encourage conversation and discussion of the interactions. The participants were presented with images of the in-game interactable 3D objects to evaluate how grab gestures functioned, and whether the prototype responded as expected with respect to the gestures they made. The interviews were video-recorded to capture potential hand movements performed by the participants.

The interviews were conducted by one facilitator and a translator interviewing the participants. The setup can be seen in Figure 5

4.2 Setup of VR Evaluations and Data Capture

The VR evaluation was conducted in a gazebo, used to shelter from the harsh sun as direct sunlight or darkness hinders the Oculus Quest 2 headset's tracking of participants’ hands. The play-area was defined by the VR guardian and the physical space of the gazebo. The evaluation was video-recorded, both with an outside stationary camera and from inside the application using the headset's ability to do in-game recording. The interviews were video-recorded and the audio was recorded using a standard memo app on a smartphone.

The gazebo was open for all community members and potential participants to observe the current player. Partially because all community members were familiar with the story as it was told on field trip 1, and the fact that the audience could not see inside VR but only the movement of the current player. As the participants wore the headset they did not see the audience. Furthermore, newcomers might be more hesitant to try VR in general and need to ease into it by observing others play first [ 18 ]. The narrative and resulting game play was co-designed with the community, thus the participants already knew which items to pick up in order to prepare for a hunt. As the participants knew the story there was no need to hide the playing participants, the gestures had been agreed upon by the community and was therefore evaluated based on personal approach and potential varieties rather than discoverability.

Two facilitators were present throughout all the sessions, one from the San community guiding the participants, and one researcher for technical support and follow-up questions.

4.3 Participants

12 people (four women and eight men) from the Ju/’Hoansi community participated in the VR play-through and the interviews. One woman and three men were community Elders, where most of them had personal knowledge of their traditional ways. The youngest participant was 22 years old, whereas, the eldest did not remember her exact age, only that she receives pension. All participants stated that they were right-handed. Considering that not all community members were equally volunteering and available during previous researchers’ visits, the participants’ level of VR experience varied. Thus some have been part of previous gesture elicitation and evaluation studies. None of the gestures implemented in the current study were explored by community members prior to this sessions.

5 RESULTS

After a section on data processing the general play-through results are presented before unpacking the more intricate details of personal experiences and the various gestures performed throughout the scenes and the participants’ reflections on these.

5.1 Data Processing and Data Analysis

Two participant's in-VR gesture data sets were removed (from one man and one woman), as the recordings were corrupt and could not be analysed for interaction (the prototype worked well). The participant's interview data was included as they both had experienced the prototype like the rest of the participants and could still comment on their experiences.

The recordings of the ten participants added up to a total of 120 minutes and 20 seconds of video footage, forming the basis for the analysis. The inside- and outside-video recordings were synchronized and framed side-by-side in the video editing tool DaVinci Resolve. This provided a holistic overview per participant and made it possible to review in detail, document and compare across the participants. For example, the video reviews allowed us to check if the system missed detecting gestures being performed outside the camera view of the headset or if participants performed any non-implemented gestures, and had difficulties or different approaches to interacting with specific interactable objects.

The first phase of the analysis was noting the gestures performed by the participants on an individual basis. Open-coding [ 15 ] allowed for the inclusion of every single gesture performed as they were observed in the videos, and the gestures were then noted in a comprehensive spreadsheet. Figure 8 demonstrates an example of this more quantitative overview.

The second phase of the analysis had a qualitative approach to the data and examined on a higher level the open-coded data as two categories; grabbing approaches and mid-air gestures.

For the grab category the data allowed an improved understanding of individual grabbing approaches, sequence of object interaction, interaction not recognized, participants’ changing their grabbing approach, behaviors of carrying and shifting objects between hands. For the mid-air category it was possible to inspect expected and unexpected gestures performed, variations of these and system
detection.

The number of interactions made, were compared to the minimum of interactions needed. While also recording if the participants were able to perform all mid-air gestures to trigger the event of the kudu to run away, or if the time limit of three minutes activated the kudu, and in general it provided a holistic overview of the entire play-through. The minimum required interactions can be seen in Figure 6 with a total of 13 interactions, although the participants are free to make other interactions, such as attempting other mid-air gestures or grabbing objects which is not needed to continue the story, e.g picking up a bow in scene 1. Additionally, the interviews were transcribed from the audio and video material in order to conduct a thematic analysis on the participants’ statements on first impressions, immersion, expectations and gesture interactions.

5.2 General Results from the VR Play-Through

The participants were not given a time limit, nor any other constraints regarding the performance of gestures. Due to this openness, a large set of gestures were performed and resulted in time spent hunting in VR to be between 6.02 minutes and 22.35 minutes, with an average time of 12.4. Performed gestures and time per participant can be seen in Figure 7 showing a usage of more than the 13 minimum required interactions. The participants could interact with additional objects (e.g. the bow and spear in the first scene), this was not necessary to progress in the story. All the participants exceeded the minimum required interactions. Some due to performing non-recognized gestures and others because the participants explored the game beyond interacting with the minimum set of required objects. The data analysis made it possible to identify and inspect all play-throughs, regardless of detection error or individual preference. One participant in scene 2 correctly used the three implemented gestures of crouch down, kudu sign and checking wind direction. All ten participants used the crouch gesture, while four made use of the sand gesture to check for wind. Nine participants made a gesture to sign for the kudu, however only one used the implemented version. All ten participants reached scene 4, where they hang meat on a branch. Two did not manage to hang all three pieces of meat, and therefore did not see the newly built village.

5.3 Personal Experiences from the VR Play-Through

The participants generally agreed that the environment looks real and like the Namibian bush, however, they would still like the foliage to be denser to more closely resemble the area that they currently live in. Overall, the participants liked the story and the focus on their culture, especially the in-game voices which made them feel at home and among family. However, one female participant did not feel that the women had a big enough role, and that there was too much focus on the men and hunting, “everything was good, but the only thing happening there is the men who are the only ones during things there” . Despite the focus on the men hunting, all participants still liked the overall story and feel of the application, especially the elder participants and those over 30 years, as these participants all stated that it was like when they were young and remembered how it was like when they or their parents went hunting. The oldest participant, an elder female over 65 stated “it [was] like the history of her parents, because back then, she was a small child [and] didn't know what happens in life. When she saw it she [thought] "wow this is what happened”, it [was] the history of her parents” and the elderly men unanimously agreed that it was like an actual hunt with one stating “the hunting experience, its real as in real life, …, The way you let the animal escape, …,. And when you have hurt the animal, you find the prints and follow them and go back in a day and go for print.” .

5.4 Gesture Interactivity

This section contains an overview of the gestures performed by the participants while playing the hunting story. Figure 8 serves as a quantitative overview of all data-processed gestures with repetitions, giving a total of 1323 gestures performed across all ten included participants while in VR. All 1323 gestures are noted down and can be seen in Figure 8 .

This data serves as a quantitative foundation for the scene-by-scene presentation of results in the following sections.

As mentioned in Section 3.5 the story was implemented in four scenes. All four scenes contain grab gestures, whereas the mid-air gesture was only needed for the second scene. First described in this section, are the grab gestures of scene one to four, following the usage of mid-air gestures only in scene two.

5.4.1 The Use of Grab. The user has to grab in all four scenes of the story, where the first, third and forth scene is only requiring the use of grab gestures. The user can however make use of mid-air gestures whenever they want.

The first scene . Within the first scene, three objects must be picked up. The arrow, the root and the bag. A bow and a spear is also interactable and can be picked up but is not needed to continue the story. The participants decide for themselves when and what they want to pick up. Nine of the participants all started by reaching for arrows, seven reached for the interactable arrow, while two reached for two non-interactive arrows on the ground. Only one participant did not try to grab the two arrows on the ground. The arrow was grabbed in different variations which can be seen in 9 , nine tried grabbing from the top, six from under and one tried using the tips of the index and thumb. The non-interactable arrows showed a higher usage of the pinch interaction with five participants using the gestures, see Figure 10 , only two attempted from under, where 12 attempts of grabbing from the top having participants try multiple ways to grab the arrows. Nine participants collected the root, where seven in total grabbed it using one hand, two attempted grabbing from the top, and seven from the bottom. Furthermore, four participants used both hands.

All participants grabbed the bag reaching for the strap of the bag, three using their left hand, and seven using the right hand. In general nine participants made use of both the left and right hand switching between them. One strictly used the right hand. Eight wanted to switch the item from one hand to another, possible to make the next interaction more natural for the user. Furthermore, did six try to hold two objects in one hand at the same time. Whenever the participant tried to switch hand or hold two items at once, six dropped an item.

The second scene . When the kudu had run past the player, the arrow piece that broke off must be picked up to continue. The arrow piece was the only grabable object in this scene. All participants managed to collect the arrow, from where seven managed in the first attempt. One was very fast in their movement, so even though registered as grabbed, the arrow disappeared under ground. Three had issues getting the arrow as it was partly outside the play-area in the gazebo, or very close to the ground making it difficult to reach the arrow.

The third scene . The participants had to grab the spear placed in front of the wounded kudu. Then they needed to stab it, and cut it with a smaller knife. Nine grabbed the spear with one hand, five using the right hand, four with the left, and one with both hands. After having picked up the spear seven continued to stab the kudu using one hand, while three grabbed the spear with both hands. Three had problems holding and stabbing the kudu as they moved to fast for the system to recognize their movement, so all three killed it by accident. One of the three grabbed the spear but let it go immediately resulting in the spear falling under the kudu. Participant number 8 (P8) tried to pick the spear 36 times, but the spear got pushed and rolls with the tip into the kudu, killing it. All participants grabbed the knife using different approaches, five grapping from the top, six from under, one with both hands, and one from the side. Five managed to get it in the first attempt. The kudu is successfully cut by six participants using the right hand and by four using the left. One made cutting motions with the hand right even though the knife was in the other hand. This one was counted as a cut on using the left, despite the intention was using the right. Furthermore, did five attempt to grab and lift the kudu's leg for a better cut.

The fourth scene . Grab was the only interaction needed for the fourth scene, where the participant had to grab and hang three pieces of cut meat on a branch in the scene. Five did not have any problems grabbing the meat and hang it, where two never completed due to dropping the meat and it falling out of the play-area. The remaining three managed to hang the meat with minor complications. Eight used the right hand to grab and place the meat, six used the left, meanwhile six tried grabbing with both hands. One who grabbed with the left hand used the second hand while placing the meat as if to stabilise the movement and placement, successfully. Whenever the grab with two hands was working, which it did for three participants, it was due to the participant closing the hands into a fist. If they tried lifting the meat by pressing the palm onto the object, they merely pushed the meat away. This was why one participant did not complete nor place a single piece of meat.

Summary of Grab Usage . As demonstrated by Schlicht and Schrater [ 40 ], new users can show a tendency to perform a larger and less precise grip, thereby reflecting an uncertainty of the outcome of their interaction. The data from our study shows no traces of exaggerated grabbing behavior. The data revealed that all participants made use of both hands. Both in reaching and grabbing for objects, but also to reach with their main hand and switch hands to have the main hand free again. When an object was not easily picked up with their first natural approach, they did change grab technique. For instance first reaching for the arrow from the top, and afterwards from below the arrow. None of the participants seemed hindered in their experience despite struggling to pick an object up. All continued and did never stand around asking for help to get an object. They were curious of experiencing the story, making more interactions that the developers had considered, such as reaching for the two arrows on the ground in scene 1, and attempting to lift the leg of the kudu when cutting it with a knife in scene 3.

5.4.2 The Use of Mid-Air Gestures. The three mid-air gestures; animal sign, crouch down, and checking wind direction, were all implemented in the second scene of shooting the kudu. All had to be used to trigger the event of shooting the kudu. The participant must walk to a specific location, no gestures needed, following kudu tracks, before the encounter with the kudu.

Usage of implemented gestures .

Nine participants used a sign to communicate that they saw an animal, however, only one used the implemented version. The remaining used a different version, varying between four variants, see Figure 12 .

All participants used the gesture for “crouch” successfully. Four managed in the first attempt, one in the third and fifth attempt, two in the sixth, and one in the thirteenth attempt. The participants who tried several times either looked away from the gesturing hand or performed very rapid movements.

Four participants used the sand-gesture, whereas three succeeded and one looked away from their hand while using it.

Regardless that none of the participants had clearly used all three gestures, four participants triggered the effect of shooting the kudu. The implemented gestures and their usage can be seen in Figure 11 .

Usage of gestures not implemented . Besides the implemented gestures the participants were observed to make additional gestures. Eight participants used a gesture conveying that the NPC's should “spread out” (see first gesture in Figure 13 . Two variations of the spread out gesture occurred. One was general for all the three NPC's to go and spread out, seen by seven participants, while another was in combination with a point and only one wave with one hand in one direction, also seen by seven participants. The wave functioned as if saying "you, go right" seen by four participants, or as "come on, closer" by seven participants. The waving gesture can be seen in Figure 13 as "Move Closer". To track the kudu prints did eight participants point at the tracks while following them. Furthermore, was the point gesture observed by nine participants who pointed directly at the kudu to indicate, perhaps to the NPC's where it was located. For further communication to the NPC's did nine make a gesture for a bow and arrow, using a pantomimic gesture, to signal that the NPC should shoot the kudu. The bow and arrow gesture came in two variants, where one strictly made the movement of how to shoot with a bow and arrow, where the other made use of a point indicating the direction or point to the target of interest. The two variations can be seen in Figure 13 as "Bow and Arrow".

5.5 Interviews Evaluating Gestures

The observations made from the VR recordings, inside and outside VR, are supported by statements from the participants. All 12 participants have been taken into consideration, as their feedback is still relevant having experienced the VR hunting story and the implemented gestures.

Many participants used a large variety of gestures while confronted with the kudu and the surrounding NPCs. As seen in Section 5.4.2 , many participants had to repeat their gesture, while the NPCs other times responded quickly. This complete opposite experience is supported by the participants in claims such as: (the NPCs seemed)“familiar with the gestures and responded” , while others had statements such as “they took a long time to respond, and did not seem like they could hear them” .

Focusing on how realistic the grab felt, three commented that they “felt like they were really holding it in their hands” . However, three mentioned that they could “see they were holding something, but it was weird that they could not feel it” . Furthermore, did 11 state that “[the objects] fell out of their hands” . The objects in question was the spear, bow, meat and arrow, where the spear, as an example, sometimes fell out of their grasp, when they tried switching it to the other hand. This was also the case with six participants who attempted to grab multiple objects in the same hand (such as the spear, bow and arrow in the first scene). They could not succeed holding two objects, as the system does not allow them to. One participant stated that, because of it, it did not seem real, as “in the real world you can hold multiple objects in the same hand” . This could ultimately have had an effect on their immersion and sense of presence in the virtual world.

6 DISCUSSION

We first discuss our findings within the framing of their anthropological value. The findings of the study revealed a number of issues which need further considerations in gesture research.

6.1 Anthropological Value of Gesture Implementations

Gestures are a significant part of an embodied cultural practice, which if implemented adequately enhance the fidelity of a digitalized cultural experience. Thus their appropriate elicitation, validation and integration are of utmost importance. In our study, the gestures were elicited in a life hunt demo through observation and verbal confirmation and later implemented as per design discussions. In the evaluation of the three gestures we however observe different behaviors in their use. While the crouching gesture was used intuitively and accurately, the kudu gesture varied across participants, and the sand grabbing gesture was only used by few. We will discuss possible explanations for each and derive recommendations for further work.

6.1.1 Intuitive and Accurate use of Gesture. The non-verbal communication of signing to crouch down gesture corroborates the anthropological findings in a similar cultural context undertaken by Hindley [ 13 ]. All participants in the VR used the crouching gesture successfully. We postulate that the gesture was easy to use and the user was provided with an immediate visual feedback by the NPC's actually crouching down.

6.1.2 Variances in Gestures. The signing of the kudu, showed great variances across the participants during the VR play. Looking into the literature, the gesture demonstrated by the hunters, was very dissimilar to what Hindley [ 13 ] found among the Ju/’Hoansi in earlier studies. In both studies the gesture is imitating the salient animal features, in our case, the horns, but other regional communities (also Ju/’Hoansi) prioritize the spoor (track of an animal), or other salient features made visible by one-handed gestures or even using the arms to mimic horns [ 13 ]. Hindley [ 13 ] describes the hunting gestures used by numerous indigenous communities to sign for specific animals as iconic gestures, due to their mimicking of the different traits of the animals. As the gestures do indeed correlate with shapes from the animals, the classification of iconic seems justified. However, the gestures change depending on where the hunters come from or from who they learned the gesture. Thus, according to Aigner et al. [ 2 ], the gesture would be classified as semaphoric, considering that there is different gestures for the same animal, and if the gestures are performed out of context, the meaning will no longer match with the iconic gesture. Therefore, the variance of the gesture across participants could be due to the fact that community members know that animal gestures are based on salient features yet because most of the participants have not used the gesture before, they approximated the gesture while in VR play mode. Consequently, the gesturing of the kudu or any other animal being an important feature of the hunt would need to be fully agreed upon by the community and be taught to the users prior to the VR game, thereby ensuring high fidelity of the cultural experience.

6.1.3 Lack of Gesture Use. The sand gesture was explained as an important gesture, using the wind direction to hide from the animal. It was a co-designed gesture, but only four participants used the gesture in VR. Similarly Arendttorp et al. [ 4 ] elicitated the "thumbs up" gesture in a natural setting, yet it was rarely used in the implemented VR context. We therefore postulate that some gestures are highly contextual and might need external triggers, such as the "wind testing" gesture might have needed an indication of wind (through visuals or sound) in VR. Similarly to the thumbs up which needs an other human or at least an NPC to be triggered. Thus in further implementations we would like to explore whether a curated simulation of wind would stimulate users to perform the sand grabbing gesture.

6.2 Grab Gesture Implementation Requirements

Previous studies have proven that mid-air gestures vary depending on the user [ 4 , 8 , 13 , 28 ]. Throughout the four VR scenes, grab gestures were used as interactive hinges for scene transitions. However, what we found was a nuanced understanding of the grabbing of various virtual objects themselves. It is to be expected to see different strategies for grabbing different kinds of objects, as the grab gesture and the object properties determine the affordance [ 31 ]. Moreover based on certain cultural protocols the receiving of an object from another person should be done using both hands, which would explain that four users attempted to take the root from the woman using two hands. Yet another factor for grabbing variances has been shown by Schlicht and Schrater [ 40 ], where participants used a larger grip when they were uncertain about the outcome of their interaction. Thus the variance of the grabbing gesture can be idiosyncratic, cultural, dependent on the object as well as the user's state. This implies that a high level of tolerance must be implemented. The current implementation allowed single hand grabbing from different angles, as well as pinching but failed to recognize the use of two hands at a time, which will need to be implemented in future versions. Another observation was the attempt by users to hold multiple objects in one hand, which the current implementation did not support. A bow and spear are normally carried in one hand on a hunt thereby having one hand free for gesturing, moving a branch or something else. The attempt of holding more than one item, and trying to switch an object from one hand to the other often resulted in the participants dropping an item and grabbing it again. Thus future implementations of grabbing should allow for holding multiple objects.

6.3 Haptic Feedback

Observing participants trying to grab the same object multiple times, or realizing they had actually grabbed it without knowing it, had a root in lack of haptic feedback. The observation was supported in interviews, with participants expressing a "feeling of nothing", although visually seeing an object in their hand. Adkins et al. [ 1 ] findings support the claim that the need for equipment is considered unnatural and will lead to lower immersion, and emulating haptic feedback is not easy to implement with no additional equipment. Controllers allow for haptic feedback (both kinesthetic and tactile), providing the user with the feeling of holding something just by holding the controller. That form of haptic sensing is perceived uniformly regardless of the properties of the virtual objects, unless they correspond exactly to the physical properties of the controller itself. Within the context of grabbing virtual objects it is relevant to explore further as the participants expect a sense of feedback thereby obstructing the naturalness of the full grab interaction.

6.4 Implemented Gestures as Validation and New Elicitation Technique

Mid-air gestures are becoming an important topic in the development of VR controller-free interaction. Aiming to expand the usage of multiple different gestures [ 4 , 35 , 39 ]. Many gesture elicitation studies do not implement their gestures [ 8 , 42 , 45 ], and can thereby not validate their gestures in an ecologically, valid context. In the sections above, the findings clearly show a need for multiple gestures, not just for mid-air interactions [ 4 ], but for grab interactions as well. Despite having been co-designers and creators of the implemented mid-air gestures, many gestures had never been suggested until observed during play-though of the hunting story. One animal gesture was shown by an Elder of the community to the authors and the entire community, but four variations were observed during the VR evaluation. Furthermore, there were several, missing pre-produced gestures such as spread out used by eight participants, and an indication of shooting using a bow and arrow also observed by eight participants. These gestures were not found until evaluated through a prototype. This finding places a new question at the center of gesture research, about which natural situation, method for elicitation or performance in a naturally mimicked situation enable participants to perform, to them, natural gestures? We maintain that natural gesture elicitated need to be validated in VR context and further gestures are extracted in-VR application, thereby complementing the first gesture elicitation phase.

7 CONCLUSION

This study contributed to the still scarce but slowly growing research in the field of gesture elicitation, implementation and validation. The findings suggest considerations for the implementation of a variety of angle and execution of grab, provision of haptic feedback, as supported by Fang and Harrison [ 10 ], grabbing and holding of multiple objects at the same time, as well as the exploration of contextualisation needed to trigger natural gestures within the VR application. This study further demonstrates how existing user-created gesture elicitation techniques can be expanded through the implementation of some gestures within a VR application, triggering the execution of further gestures not encountered before. In this study, we discovered three new gestures, namely spread out, move closer, and mimicking a bow and arrow shooting. Thus expanding on existing methodological approaches to user-generated gesture elicitations through gesture implementation, we encourage researchers to explore this approach further.

Within the domain of cultural heritage, the digitalization of gestures as demonstrated and validated by the originating cultural group, enables the preservation of gestures that have been used in a natural context. At the same time it allows to renegotiate valid gestures among users. For example, the implemented mid-air gesture to sign for a kudu was used one time only within the VR, whereby the other participants performed variations, supporting Hindley [ 13 ] stating hunting gestures can change even within the same community. Last but not least the study provides rare empirical data of a gesture study with non-WEIRD participants, thereby stressing particular insights and further promoting pluriversality through diversity in HCI research.
