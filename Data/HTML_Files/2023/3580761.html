Media architecture exploits interactive technology to encourage passers-by to engage with an architectural environment. Whereas most media architecture installations focus on visual stimulation, we developed a permanent media facade that rhythmically knocks xylophone blocks embedded beneath 11 window sills, according to the human actions constantly traced via an overhead camera. In an attempt to overcome its apparent limitations in engaging passers-by more enduringly and purposefully, our study investigates the impact of feedforward learning, a constructive interaction method that instructs passers-by about the results of their actions. Based on a comparative (n=25) and a one-month in-the-wild (n=1877) study, we propose how feedforward learning could empower passers-by to understand the interaction of more abstract types of media architecture, and how particular quantitative indicators capturing this learning could predict how enduringly and purposefully a passer-might engage. We believe these contributions could inspire more creative integrations of non-visual modalities in future public interactive interventions.

1 INTRODUCTION

Media architecture focuses on the technological augmentation of public space [ 88 ] to enrich how passers-by interact with and take ownership of their surroundings [ 79 ]. In the urban context, media architecture has been used to encourage people to linger [ 71 ], help discover new places [ 30 ], support moments of mental rest [ 16 ] or build a sense of community via social interaction [ 7 ]. Whereas media architecture predominantly exploits visual stimulation to engage passers-by [ 26 , 82 ], previous research in other contexts has demonstrated the similar capability of sonic modalities [ 32 , 51 ]. Among them, rhythm, i.e. the pattern of musical beats across time, can positively influence the walking behaviour [ 52 ] of passers-by, as it is able to inform walking speed [ 67 , 68 ], augment walking meditation [ 18 ], or make stair-walking therapy more enjoyable [ 54 ].

Our study focused on MUURmelaar (a quirky translation of “a mumbling wall person") 1 , a five-year-old xylophonic media architectural facade resulting from an intensive co-design process between an architectural office and our research group [ 45 ] in Leuven, Belgium. As shown in Figure 2 , MUURmelaar engages with passers-by who walk on an adjacent passageway by rhythmically knocking a set of 11 xylophone blocks, each hidden underneath a separate window sill, forming a ‘xylophonic window’ as shown in Figure 3 . A custom-developed tracking software uses a video feed from an overhead camera to map the location, speed and number of passers-by as real-time input parameters to create an overall xylophonic rhythm. MUURmelaar was thus specifically conceptualised to invite passers-by to physically engage and socially interact, so that it would create a ‘place’ for the local community. Although MUURmelaar was able to attract the attention of many passers-by, the building owners were dissatisfied about how most passers-by did not realise that the rhythm was reacting to them, whereas those who realised its feedback did not understand how they could control the rhythm. This study thus aims to investigate how passers-by can be encouraged to engage with MUURmelaar more enduringly, i.e. by spending more time, and purposefully, i.e. by knowing how to control the rhythm, all without diminishing its uniquely covert interaction modality.

We hypothesised that MUURmelaar could inform passers-by how to engage with it using feedforward, i.e. a cognitive affordance that occurs before the action of a user to ‘teach’ them what the result of their action will be [ 86 ]. We imagined that this feedforward could consist of a rhythm that encourages a passer-by to walk toward its origin. Our research question is therefore: “ How can feedforward learning enable passers-by to engage more enduringly and purposefully with a xylophonic media architecture facade? ". We initially deployed a comparative study that invited 25 participants to engage with MUURmelaar through a feedforward-driven (FF) and a feedback-driven (FB) condition. We then mapped these two conditions unto the three phases of engagement [ 62 ] to guide: (1) ‘passive’ passers-by to (2) ‘actively’ learn how to engage via FF before they (3) ‘discover’ novel rhythms freely via FB. The efficacy of this feedforward engaging strategy was tested during a one-month in-the-wild study, resulting in 1877 engaging instances for a total of 107 hours and 38 minutes, in which we interviewed 21 passers-by to understand their firsthand experience. Our results statistically evidenced that passers-by engaged with our feedforward strategy significantly longer compared to the basic feedback strategies of the previous years. Moreover, passers-by spending more time reading the instruction were more likely to engage more enduringly, and passers-by who unlocked rhythm 2 faster than rhythm 1 were more likely to engage more purposefully. The interview data further clarified that the rhythmic, spatial and social quality of MUURmelaar qualitatively affected the first-hand experience of engaging passers-by.

Our study contributes four design considerations for media architecture. It demonstrates how feedforward can empower passers-by to engage more enduringly and purposefully with a relatively abstract interaction modality in the public space. It also suggests two quantitative indicators that statistically predict (1) if a passer-by might engage enduringly by measuring their time reading a textual instruction; and (2) if a passer-by might engage purposefully by measuring their initial learning rate. We also propose that a sonic media architecture facade could engage passers-by more meaningfully by grounding its interaction modalities on the spatial and social context of its immediate surroundings. Our study thus aims to inspire future efforts of public interactive interventions to incorporate rhythm or sonic modality in general to enrich engagement in public spaces.

2 LITERATURE

Urban Design theorists proposed that public space should fulfil three human needs: (1) passive engagement (P) with the environment to observe others; (2) active engagement (A) with people or intellectual challenges posed by the space; and (3) excitement of novel discoveries (D) [ 14 ]. By purposefully supporting people as they proceed through these PACD phases of engagement [ 62 ], interactive interventions can help public space to fulfil these needs better. This section gives an overview of how previous research and practices of public interactive interventions have attempted to attract passive passers-by to actively engage, and to motivate them to engage enduringly and purposefully.

2.1 Attracting Passers-by

To overcome interaction blindness [ 47 ], i.e. the natural tendency of passers-by to ignore a public interaction intervention because they deem its content irrelevant, a range of interventions have exploited the expressive power of sonic modality in attracting passers-by. Piano Staircase 2 placed a note-playing stairs to encourage passers-by to walk instead of taking the escalator. Similarly, Musical Stairs motivated children with disabilities in stair-climbing therapy by providing audio feedback for each step that enhanced their enjoyable experience [ 54 ]. Musical Swings 3 motivated passers-by to collaborate socially by mapping the speed and height of the swings to the tempo and pitch of the instruments in a generative rhythmic orchestra. BUG was an installation built into an exterior wall that invited passers-by to slow down and listen to the seismic resonances within the building [ 12 ]. Touched Echo 4 enabled visitors to experience the sounds of World War II through bone conduction by placing their elbows on a railing and covering their ears. Solstice LAMP was a large-scale media facade that engaged passers-by in different zones of proxemics by projecting generative shapes on its facade, which responded to the movements of passers-by in an embodied audio-visual interactive zone in front of the building [ 41 ].

In order to attract passers-by socially, public interactive interventions also exploited the honeypot effect , i.e. the tendency of passers-by to engage with an interactive intervention when seeing others already engaging with it [ 13 ]. This was because the presence of physical [ 19 ] or virtual [ 65 ] participants helps reduce the social embarrassment of passive passers-by, motivating them to become active participants [ 35 ]. To optimise the honeypot effect, previous studies proposed to steer the circulation of passers-by [ 11 ], purposefully deploy triggers to motivate them [ 36 , 89 ], or highlight engaging focal points [ 40 ]. Accordingly, SwingScape invited passers-by to exercise using pull-up bars in a scaffolding by highlighting occupied bars with playful sounds and lights [ 38 ]; Night Lights 5 amplified hand movements of participants into projected patterns on its facade to call for a social ‘dance’; and Mirrored 6 captured sound levels of nearby audiences to generate colour patterns across its facade to augment the social atmosphere [ 39 ].

2.2 Engaging Passers-by Enduringly

Public interactive interventions attempted to prolong the engaging time of passers-by by deploying recognisable feedback to their actions, encouraging social interactions. Puzzle Facade allowed passers-by to use a Rubik-cube puzzle to change the facade colours [ 58 ]; Touch Projector let multiple passers-by to ‘paint’ a facade in real-time via a video stream [ 8 ]; Aarhus by Light translated actions of passers-by into digital silhouettes on its concert hall facade [ 20 ]; and SMSlingshot allowed passers-by to ‘shoot’ a text-message onto a facade with a wooden mobile slingshot [ 28 ]. Studies showed that abstracted representations of feedback helped prolong engagement [ 53 ], as anonymised skeleton representations of passers-by encouraged them to engage more enduringly, with more playful actions than with mirror or silhouette representation [ 2 ]. However, short encountering time with abstracted feedback might also cause passers-by not to recognise the interaction and thus also the possibility to engage [ 66 ].

Musical rhythm , meanwhile, is an intuitive interaction modality to engage people enduringly in walking behaviour [ 52 ], as people tend to rely on auditory feedback while walking naturally [ 83 ], insofar that musical rhythm could motivate people to walk faster or slower depending on its tempo [ 67 ]. The clarity of a rhythm, i.e. the perceived distinctiveness between the beats, also influenced people's sensitivity in recognising rhythmic changes [ 68 ]. In walking meditation, carefully-selected rhythm helps people modulate their breathing more effectively[ 18 ]. Rhythm also motivates physical improvisation, as children were observed to dance naturally while listening to rhythmic music [ 50 ]. Despite this body of research, there is little insight into how a media architecture facade can apply rhythms to engage passers-by.

2.3 Engaging Passers-by Purposefully

Public interactive interventions usually follow predefined trajectories to engage passers-by [ 5 ]. However, as passers-by tend to improvise their own trajectories [ 59 ], a public interactive intervention requires some degree of orchestration to inform passers-by how to engage meaningfully [ 72 ]. To this end, feedforward is an applicable concept.

Introduced by Djajadiningrat et al. in the context of tangible interaction [ 22 ] and later re-framed by Vermeulen et al. as a design principle, feedforward is defined as a form of information that ‘ tells a user beforehand what the result of their action will be ’ [ 86 ]. A feedforward can convey inherent, augmented or functional information about a product [ 87 ] to provide concrete guidance of what a user can expect. As such, feedforward is promised to lead to less frustration or irritation [ 69 ], and designers are recommended to look for opportunities to apply feedforward when they can [ 75 ].

Feedforward has been predominately applied in designing gestural interfaces, such as to visualise an array of hand postures necessary in a gesture to control an interactive surface [ 34 ], to indicate where to touch a button by changing its shape [ 81 ], or to instruct the order of filling in a form on a physical paper by illuminating the corresponding fields [ 56 ]. In other contexts, it was manifested as light signals to communicate the driving decisions of an autonomous car to passengers beforehand [ 76 ], as sensory cues of data physicalisations to appeal to people to touch and explore [ 3 ], or as textual dialogues to inform live performers of possible combinations of musical fragments [ 37 ].

‘Feedforward modelling’, meanwhile, is a form of self-modelling where a user learns a skill by watching a simulation of themselves executing such skill in a video or a virtual reality environment [ 24 ]. Feedforward modelling has been shown to improve the training performance of athletes, ranging from swimming [ 17 ] over trampoline [ 77 ] to gymnastics [ 73 ]. Observing feedforward modelling also enhanced the physical skill and enjoyment of novices [ 33 ] in learning dancing [ 29 ], biking [ 63 ] or running [ 48 ]. In education, ‘feedforward evaluation’ refers to regulating input to the learning activity to ensure that students meet the standards necessary for the planned teaching program to occur [ 74 ]. Feedforward evaluation evidently helps instructors make real-time adjustments, and supports students in managing their learning plan [ 15 ].

Building on this work, we applied ‘ feedforward learning ’ into the context of a xylophonic media architecture facade, as a method to guide passer(s)-by iteratively with rhythms to inform them how to engage with it.

3 XYLOPHONIC MEDIA ARCHITECTURE FACADE

The design of MUURmelaar, the xylophonic media architecture facade, was grounded in augmenting the architectural expression of a newly built office building that forms an inviting curve between a busy beltway and a silent passageway.

3.1 Architectural Context

As shown in Figure 2 , MUURmelaar is the embedded facade of a five-storey office building that hosts the headquarters of a local architecture firm 7 . The building is immediately adjacent to a narrow pedestrian passageway connecting a noisy 4-lane beltway and a quiet natural park. Because this passageway also serves as one of the primary connections for passers-by between the city centre and a suburban neighbourhood, the building corner has a ‘welcoming curve’ that invites passers-by to look into and eventually enter the passageway. The building facade is constructed from rustic and natural materials, such as hand-made stamped concrete and exposed chestnut window frames. The 17 glass windows across the three floors not only offer a panoramic view, but also enhance the openness of the passage by reflecting its immediate environment. The colourful flowerbeds and a painted mural at the two opposite sides of the passageway augment the natural atmosphere, as shown in Figure 1 .

Although open to integrating technological means in their facade, the architects dismissed the use of lights, displays or loudspeakers to respect the rustic materiality and the natural context. They expressed a wish to evoke an intriguing musical soundscape [ 51 ] that is subtle yet perceivable enough to augment and enrich the changing urban soundscape in the passageway. A playful rhythm sounded by wooden xylophones, as shown in Figure 3 , was thus chosen to be reminiscent of the materiality of the window frames and the natural atmosphere. Inspired by the physical slope, each xylophone block has a distinctive pitch ranging from C5 to F6, arranged decreasingly from the beltway to the park. The architectural dimensions of the 11 xylophonic windows thus result in a spatially distributed rhythm, as the sounds are emitted both horizontally along the passageway, and vertically from the ground to the second floor. This soundscape was thus intended to encourage passers-by to linger around and investigate the facade, and even commence a conversation between them or with the office workers when the windows are opened. Our design process of MUURmelaar was reported in detail in a previous non-archived publication [ 45 ].

3.2 Technical Implementation

Our technical system includes three elements: an overhead camera for real-time video streaming, a Mac Mini that interprets this video input to track the location of passers-by with an OpenCV-Python computer vision software, and a customised Arduino control module that translates the commands received from the Mac Mini into electrical signals that rhythmically knock specific xylophonic windows. To avoid false positives, the computer vision software purposefully filtered out movements that were not in the tracking area, faster or slower than the average walking or running speed (0.8m/s to 3m/s), or noticeably unnatural, such as prolonged flickering back and forth of shadows of surrounding trees or sizeable brightness changes caused by moving clouds. As shown in Figure 4 , the computer vision software compared the location(s) of the passer(s)-by to five predefined zones in the passageway, each of which corresponded to a column of xylophonic windows, i.e. an array of vertically-aligned windows on the facade.

3.3 Pilot Studies

Using a research methodology similar to our main study, we initially deployed two pilot studies that compared three potential engaging strategies and three rhythmic tempos. Our first pilot study compared how compelling three engaging strategies were in encouraging 12 participants to change their action; including (1) a feedforward strategy that sounded a basic rhythm at a column of windows to nudge a participant to walk towards its direction; (2) a feedback strategy that increased the tempo of a predefined rhythm to motivate a participant as they ‘climb’ the slope of the passageway; and (3) a disrupting strategy that stopped abruptly a continuous rhythm to encourage a participant to stop and turn around. Our second pilot study compared how enjoyable three different rhythmic tempos were for 11 participants while walking, involving the same rhythm sounded by the whole facade at 60, 90 and 120 beats per minute for a walking speed of 1.4m/s. Depending on the measured walking speed, each tempo slightly increased or decreased for a maximum of 20bpm. However, they are still distinguishable from each other. The results of the two pilot studies showed that most participants found the feedforward engaging strategy most compelling, and the rhythmic tempo of 120bpm most enjoyable. These findings helped us focus the comparative study on a feedforward engaging strategy with a tempo of 120bpm.

4 COMPARATIVE STUDY

The comparative study invited 25 participants to engage with a feedforward (FF) and a feedback-driven (FB) condition.

4.1 Design

The feedforward-driven (FF) and the feedback-driven (FB) conditions, shown in Figure 5 , are based on the concept of feedforward [ 86 ]. Both conditions used the rhythmic tempo from the pilot study, i.e. 120bpm for a walking speed of 1.4m/s. In FF , one or multiple windows in the same column sounded a rhythm as feedforward to inform the participant to walk toward its direction. When the participant successfully followed the suggestion, they were rewarded with a cascading rhythm, i.e. the same set of windows playing simultaneously with a delay of 0.2 seconds between them. The feedforward rhythm was always played from a column that was the furthest from the location of the participant to encourage them to walk for a longer distance. In contrast, FB played the same rhythm from the windows that were closely adjacent to the occupied zone of the participant as feedback, as it followed their walking direction. As shown in Figure 6 , these rhythms gradually became more complex every time the participant walked two times the length of the facade (approx. 15 meters). Consequently, the participant had to walk a total of 120 meters to unlock four rhythms and to receive a final reward, consisting of a cascading rhythm sounded by the whole facade.

4.2 Methodology

Approved by the ethical committee of our university, our research methodology aimed to benchmark the difference between FF and FB.

4.2.1 Recruitment. We recruited all participants online via a poster containing summary information of the study, and an offer for a 10 Euro coupon at a multimedia store chain for those willing to invest 45 to 60 minutes. This poster was posted in five Facebook groups, one representing a local neighbourhood, three for local university students and researchers, and one collecting suggestions for city activities. The announcement targeted people between 18 to 70 years old, who had no hearing impairment or heart problem and could engage in moderate physical activity. Following a hyperlink to an online form, interested individuals could select a preferred time slot from 8:30 to 18:00.

4.2.2 Procedure. A researcher greeted the participant at the passageway while MUURmelaar was turned off and explained the research study design. The participant signed the informed consent form and provided demographic information such as age, gender, and profession. The researcher then asked the participant to give their overall impression of the facade and the surroundings. Each participant engaged with FF and FB in a randomised order. Before each condition, the researcher instructed the participant: “ follow the sound suggestion of the facade, and eventually, it will reward you " for FF; or “ move around to make the facade produce a sound, and eventually, it will reward you " for FB. After each condition, the participant completed a System Usability Scale (SUS) questionnaire [ 4 ]. This validated questionnaire was chosen as literature considers it an established evaluation tool for public interactive installations [ 21 , 55 ]. It is also relatively lightweight compared to other questionnaires, requiring less time and effort from the participants. Finally, the researcher conducted a semi-structured interview asking the participant to reflect and compare the conditions.

4.2.3 Data Acquisition and Analysis. Each participant was video-recorded from the permanent overhead camera and a Go-Pro device positioned at eye level. The study thus collected: questionnaire results (as shown in Figure 9 ), two video feeds, audio recordings of the interviews, the digital log from the technical system, and observations of noteworthy events by the researcher.

The digital log helped us extract potential engagement parameters, such as walking time, idle time (when the participant stood still or looked around), number of walking direction reversals, and the maximum spread of zones walked. Following fair statistical communication guidance [ 25 , 27 ], we used Cronbach's alpha coefficient to test for the internal validity of each set of parameters. We evaluated the normality of each parameter using the Shapiro-Wilk test to the 5% level and the residuals’ graphic Q-Q plots. As not all parameters were normally distributed, we applied the non-parametric within-subject Wilcoxon signed-rank test to benchmark the differences between FF and FB. The questionnaire results were also tested for internal validity using Cronbach's alpha coefficient. The System Usability Scale (SUS) [ 4 ] helped us calculate usability scores of FF and FB from each participant. We then used the Wilcoxon signed-rank test to benchmark the differences between FF and FB. For every test, we used a p -value of 0.05 to denote statistical significance. The interview audio was transcribed to derive the reasonings behind questionnaire answers, while the videos and observations proved helpful in tracing back participant behaviours. We analyse these data following the six-phase method of reflexive thematic analysis [ 10 ]. The familiarisation phase occurred as weekly discussions between the three co-authors, before two primary coders inductively coded the transcript into 13 codes that foregrounded the differences between FF and FB. The seven identified themes were iteratively reconsidered, split and merged via group discussions into four themes, as reported below.

4.3 Results

We recruited 25 participants (13 female, 12 male - as self-reported) ranging between 22 and 51 years old ( Mean = 29.2, SD = 6.6, see Appendix A for demographic data), of which five (P06, P12, P15, P21, P23) had already engaged with MUURmelaar before. Taking approximately between 32 and 65 minutes ( Mean = 42, SD = 8.0 minutes) per experiment, the study produced approximately 3 hours of video and 7 hours of audio recordings.

4.3.1 Quantitative Analysis. Figure 8 reports the parameters that captured how participants engaged with FF and FB. The Cronbach's alpha coefficient produced sufficient scores for both conditions ( α = 0.81 for FF and α = 0.79 for FB), showing that the collected data was reliable. As shown in Figure 8 , the Wilcoxon signed-rank tests proved that the participants spent significantly ( p = 0.002) more time walking in FB ( Mean = 120.6, SD = 84.1 seconds) compared to FF ( Mean = 67.8, SD = 32.5 seconds). In FB, they also significantly ( p = 0.027) reversed direction more frequently (FB: Mean = 16.2, SD = 7.5; FF: Mean = 13, SD = 5.4 times), and spread significantly ( p = 0.019) over more zones (FB: Mean = 4.3, SD = 1.1; FF: Mean = 3.4, SD = 1.2 zones). We thus interpreted these results as FF being more understandable than FB, as participants engaged with MUURmelaar via significantly less walking, reversing direction and spreading out.

4.3.2 Questionnaire. Figure 9 reports the SUS scores of 25 participants between FB and FF. After a normalisation due to the opposite scales between the odd and even-indexed measures, the Cronbach's alpha coefficient produced sufficiently reliable scores for both conditions ( α = 0.80 for FF and α = 0.83 for FB). Whereas the mean SUS score of FF ( Mean = 74.9, SD = 15.0) was higher than FB ( Mean = 70.1, SD = 18.1), we did not find any statistical significance between them ( p = 0.20), which was also the case for each SUS measure. Overall, the SUS scores were relatively evenly distributed between the two conditions, insofar that three participants even scored both conditions as “ excellent " (see Appendix A ).

4.3.3 Interview. The interview data about the first-hand participant experience clarified the SUS ratings.

Engaging with the Conditions . 15 participants preferred FF because of its understandable goal-reward structure ( n = 7): “ I know what to do more than in [FB] " (P12); its clear textual instruction ( n = 5): “ the target was already clear from the instruction " (P14); and its perceived immediate reaction to their action ( n = 3): “ it reacted to my movement instantly " (P19), “ it made you feel that the building acknowledge your presence " (P17). Six participants yet reflected that FF was “ too straightforward " (P20), “ easy " (P05) or “ not challenging enough " (P13). Four participants expressed dissatisfaction as they were “ not in control " (P23), having to “ follow the sound precisely to unlock a new [rhythm] " (P24).

10 participants enjoyed FB more than FF as they perceived to be in control of the rhythm ( n = 7): “ it follows me [while walking] " (P05), “ the sound reacted to my actions " (P06); or have freedom to improvise ( n = 3): “ I walked in different ways to change the sound " (P02), “ I tried to run and it made an exciting sound " (P13). 11 participants, in which eight were newcomers, felt that FB was “ unnecessarily complex " (P20), as they were confused by the instruction: “ I did not know what [FB] wanted from me and how I could move forward " (P02), “ the goal was not clear " (P08); or did not realise their influence on the rhythm: “ I felt like I did not get any feedback or change from it " (P13).

Experiencing the Rhythms . 13 participants found the rhythms suitable for FF, as they were “ clear " (P09), “ easy to follow " (P01) and “ fun " (P13). However, 10 participants felt the rhythms to not support FB, as they did not notice any change (n=3): “ I did not recognise that the sound changed at all " (P03); because of the unclear transition between them (n=7): “ I know it became different but it was too seamless " (P25), “ it was a continuous rhythm so you had to focus to feel the change " (P23). Four participants did not recognise the rewarding cascading rhythm, as they “ did not know what to expect " (P03) and thought that it was “ just the continuity [of the rhythm] " (P19).

15 participants enjoyed listening to the sound of the rhythms, saying it was “ interesting " (P11), “ exciting " (P07), or “ melodic " (P14); seven participants particularly liked that the rhythms were “ spatial " (P22), i.e. coming from different directions: “ I like the sounds that came from the other side " (P04); “ it made you notice that different windows were making sounds " (P02). In contrast, ten participants felt that the rhythms were “ uniform " (P06), “ repetitive " (P22) or “ monotonous " (P16).

4.4 Lessons Learned

We concluded with three lessons learned. Firstly , the textual instruction effectively suggested to the participants how to start engaging in each condition. As such, textual instruction on a printed sign can “spark" curiosity and motivate passers-by to engage. Secondly , the FF condition was deemed more understandable than FB but less enjoyable. Therefore, we considered FF potentially effective as an initial ‘learning phase’ that informs a participant how to engage. Because FB was deemed more enjoyable than FB yet required more skill, FB could be suitable for a subsequent phase during which a knowledgeable participant would feel sufficiently empowered to improvise. Thirdly , FF and FB should employ two distinct sets of rhythms to avoid repetition while highlighting spatiality. The rhythms should also have a distinguishable pause between them to emphasise the progress.

5 IN-THE-WILD STUDY

From the insights of the comparative study, we combined the two conditions of FF and FB to design how MUURmelaar should engage passers-by, which was evaluated in-the-wild during 30 days in August 2022.

5.1 Design

As shown in Figure 10 , we mapped FF and FB into three phases of engagement: passive, active and discovery [ 62 ] to design our final feedforward engaging strategy, with a new set of rhythms as shown in Figure 11 .

In the passive engagement phase, a textual instruction printed on a physical sign in the middle of the passageway, accompanied by a basic repetitive rhythm of one window encouraged passers-by to stop and start engaging. The instruction stated " Learn how to play MUURmelaar by following its rhythm several times. After the rewarding "rhythmic wave", you can make rhythms as you like and as long as you want" . The active engagement phase was designed as FF to allow the passer-by to ‘learn’ how to engage with MUURmelaar. Each round of FF employed one different rhythm, as shown in Figure 11 . After the passer-by successfully unlocked four sequential rhythms (0 to 3), they were rewarded with a cascading rhythm of the whole facade, denoting a shift from FF to FB. The discovery engagement, designed as FB, allowed the passer-by to improvise freely with the rhythms (4 to 7) by moving along the passageway. A short delay of 1s indicated when a new rhythm was unlocked by walking the length of MUURmelaar. A cascading rhythm of the whole facade again rewarded the passer-by when they unlocked all four rhythms of FB.

MUURmelaar automatically started with FF every time a passer-by appeared in the tracking area, as in Figure 4 . As a new passer-by might unknowingly unlock a new rhythm of FF by walking by, we considered passers-by who unlocked rhythm 1 still in the phase of passive engagement. When the tracking area was empty for more than 20 seconds, MUURmelaar automatically reset to rhythm 0. Multiple passers-by occupying different zones or moving in different directions were each mapped unto a rhythm that was merged as a single output. Consequently, a group of passers-by was potentially able to unlock FF rhythms faster or create more complex FB rhythms. Multiple passers-by occupying a single zone and moving in an identical direction were interpreted as a single passer-by.

5.2 Methodology

MUURmelaar was turned on from 8:00 to 20:00 during weekdays and 10:00 to 19:00 during weekends to enable the camera to track passers-by using daylight, and maintain silence during resting hours. Any passer-by could engage with MUURmelaar as they pleased. A researcher was occasionally present, inviting engaged passers-by to complete a SUS questionnaire and answer a semi-structured interview if they had time about how they engaged with MUURmelaar.

During the deployment, MUURmelaar started recording with the overhead camera when it noticed a passer-by within its tracking area and stopped recording when they left. The video feed contained textual data, such as date, time, active condition (FF or FB), rhythm (from 0 to 7) and zone(s). The collected data thus included questionnaire results, the video feed, interview audio recordings, the digital log and observations of noteworthy events by the researcher.

From the digital log , we extracted the parameters of each engaging instance, including the number of passers-by (group size), time spent in front of the sign (reading time), the maximum rhythm that they reached (maximum achieved rhythm) and the duration to unlock each rhythm (completion time, rhythm 1 to 7). As the Shapiro-Wilk test and the residuals’ graphic Q-Q plots indicated that not all of these data were normally distributed, we applied non-parametric between-subject tests to evaluate our hypotheses, including the Kendall rank correlation coefficient, the Kruskal-Wallis test, and the Mann-Whitney U test. The questionnaire results were tested for internal validity using Cronbach's alpha coefficient. After calculating the SUS score, we used the Mann-Whitney U test to benchmark the difference between participants who reached the final rhythm of 7 and those who did not. The interview audio was transcribed to derive the reasoning behind questionnaire answers, while the videos and observations proved helpful in tracing back participant behaviour. We then analysed these data following the six-phase method of reflexive thematic analysis [ 10 ]. The first author, already familiar with the data by running the in-the-wild study, carried out the initial open coding phase, accompanied by analytical memo writing and discussions between all co-authors. From the resulting 58 codes, 21 themes were identified, showing a range of the multiple factors affecting how the participants engaged with MUURmelaar. These themes were then iteratively merged and split through group discussions using a constant comparative method, resulting in the 17 dimensions as reported.

5.3 Results

By removing instances that lasted less than 5 seconds or captured unusual movements between zones, the video data was filtered down from 5074 to 1877 ( $37\%$ ) valid engaging instances taking up 107 hours and 38 minutes. We interviewed 21 participants (10 female, 11 male - as self-reported) between 17 and 62 years old ( Mean = 32.6, SD = 13.9, see Appendix A ), who engaged with MUURmelaar in 14 unique groups, ranging from individuals to groups of two or three people; and included four local residents (P11, P13, P14, P17), six past-visitors (P01, P02, P03, P08, P09, P12) and 11 newcomers.

5.3.1 Quantitative Analysis. As shown in Figure 12 , among 1877 passive engaging instances (rhythm 1), 604 ( $32.18\%$ ) reached active engagement (rhythm 2 and 3). Within these 604, 169 ( $30.0\%$ ) arrived at discovery engagement (rhythm 4 to 7). 33 engaging instances ( $19.5\%$ of 169) unlocked to the final 7th rhythm.

Our first hypothesis was that the new feedforward engaging strategy helped MUURmelaar to engage passers-by more enduringly than the basic feedback strategies of the previous years. We first conducted the Kruskal-Wallis test to detect any significant difference between the total engaging time for all recorded engaging instances in August 2022, 2021, 2020 and 2019. The data of the previous years were filtered similarly to that of August 2022. As the results showed significant differences ( p < 0, 001), we conducted pairwise Mann-Whitney U tests to compare the engaging time of instances recorded in August 2022 with those of previous periods. As shown in Figure 13 , the engaging instances in August 2022 were significantly longer ( n = 1876, Mean = 52.94 s , SD = 33.4 s ) than those of August 2021 ( n = 1852, Mean = 8.22 s , SD = 22.9 s , p < 0.001), 2020 ( n = 1539, Mean = 11.31 s , SD = 63.5 s , p < 0.001) and 2019 ( n = 518, Mean = 14.48 s , SD = 61.5 s , p < 0.001), despite each compared month having approximately close numbers of engaging instances ( n ). Thus, our feedforward strategy engaged passers-by significantly more enduringly.

Our second hypothesis was that the duration of reading the textual instruction (reading time) might indicate how long the subsequent engaging instance will eventually last. We calculated the correlation coefficient between the reading time with (test 1) the summed completion time of all rhythms, and (test 2) the maximum achieved rhythm of all engaging instances that finished in the active or discovery phases (rhythm 2 to 7, n = 604). The Kendall rank correlation coefficient resulted in τ = 0.05, p = 0.005 for test 1 and τ = 0.11, p < 0.001 for test 2, indicating that there were small yet significant correlations between reading time with total completion time as well as maximum achieved rhythm. As shown in Figure 12 , the Mann-Whitney U test further proved that engaging instances ending in the passive phase had significantly shorter reading time ( n = 1273, Mean = 7.7, SD = 7.6 s ) than those ending in the active phase ( n = 435, Mean = 20.8, SD = 29.0 s , p < 0.001) or discovery phase ( n = 169, Mean = 20.4, SD = 26.3 s , p < 0.001). Thus, passers-by spending more time reading the instruction were more likely to engage towards a higher phase.

Our third hypothesis was that the effectiveness of learning at the beginning of FF might indicate how far an engaging instance could reach. We tested this by calculating the correlation coefficient between the learning rate (i.e. the division of completion time between rhythm 1 and rhythm 2) and the maximum achieved rhythm of all engaging instances that ended in the active or the discovery phase (round 2 to 7, n = 604). The Kendall rank correlation coefficient resulted in τ = 0.21, p < 0.001, indicating that there was a moderate yet significant correlation between the learning rate and the maximum achieved rhythm. As shown in Figure 12 , a Mann-Whitney U test further showed that engaging instances ended in discovery had significantly ( p < 0.001) higher learning rate between round 1 and 2 ( Mean = 2.57, SD = 1.69) compared to those ended in active phase ( Mean = 1.70, SD = 0.92). Thus, passers-by who unlocked rhythm 2 faster than rhythm 1 were also more likely to unlock the subsequent sequence of rhythms.

5.3.2 Questionnaire. Figure 14 reports the SUS scores of the 21 interviewed participants ( Mean = 86.6, SD = 9.2). After normalisation, the Cronbach's alpha coefficient produced sufficient scores ( α = 0.83) for the collected data to be reliable. Among 21 participants, 11 ( $52.4\%$ ) voted MUURmelaar to be “excellent" ( SUS > 85.5), whereas 4 ( $19.0\%$ ) ranked it to be “good" ( SUS > 80), and 6 ( $28.6\%$ ) thought it was “okay" ( SUS > 50). There is yet no significant difference between the SUS score of passers-by who unlocked rhythm 7 ( n = 8) and those who did not ( n = 13). To evaluate whether the usability of MUURmelaar improved between the comparative and the in-the-wild study, we compared the SUS scores of the two deployments using a Mann-Whitney U test. We found that participants of the in-the-wild study rated MUURmelaar to have significantly higher SUS scores than those in the comparative study for both FF ( p = 0.004) and FB ( p = 0.001).

5.3.3 Engaging with MUURmelaar. This section reports the results from the thematic analysis of the first-hand experience of MUURmelaar.

Encountering . All participants mentioned that the textual instruction encouraged them to engage, as an “ interesting invitation " (P18) that made them “ intrigued " (P05), motivated them to “ stop and see what happens " (P12), “ discuss what it was with [their] friends " (P02) or even “ come back to try after seeing it the other day " (P01). Eight newcomers were already “ curious " (P06) when hearing the rhythm from further away. However, they were “ not aware that MUURmelaar was making the sound " (P10) until reading the sign (P07). Five past-visitors and residents agreed that the sign informed them that “ there was something new that [they] could try out " (P08). When asked to expand about the sign, 11 participants appreciated that it enhanced their skill, i.e. “ I pass by here every day so it would be nice to be able to play it myself " (P11 - resident), “ I wanted to see how I could play it " (P09 - past-visitor). Five newcomers were yet curious to discover how the fuzzy description would unfold, such as “ following its rhythm " (P04) or “ rhythmic wave " (P20).

Learning . All participants agreed that the learning phase (FF) was understandable, as it was “ easier to follow [than the strategies deployed in previous years] " (P11) for past-visitors, and “ straightforward " (P15) or “ intuitive " (P16) for newcomers. Three participants (P10, P13, P14) yet described that FF was difficult to comprehend at first, as they interpreted “ following its sound " as “ pointing [their] finger [to the windows] " (P13) or “ dancing to the beat " (P14). Although all participants assured to understand FF, only 14 described its underlying feedforward correctly to “ walk to the window that was making sound " (P12), because seven participants were convinced that it was informing to “ walk up and down the slope " (P02). All participants recognised the rhythmic changes, in which three felt that it was becoming “ more difficult " (P03). Eleven participants felt uncomfortable having to follow the feedforward. P02 said that she “ never ran back and forth after a thing like this in public before ", whereas P04 felt that she “ looked weird chasing a sound when other people were watching ". P15 and P16, who notably enjoyed FF, compared it to playing a sport, such as a “ running challenge " (P15) or a “ tennis game " (P16), in which they had to “ reach the goal as fast as possible " (P16). P13 and P14, who lived close by and passed by the passageway every day, found FF to be “ fun " but “ too physically demanding " for them (P13 and P14 aged 62 and 56, respectively), giving up after unlocking rhythm 3.

Discovering . Among 19 participants who reached the discovery phase (FB), 17 recognised the transition between FF to FB, thanks to the “ silent gap " (P03) between the rhythm ( n = 9), the “ continuous knocking " (P10) of the rewarding cascading rhythm ( n = 6), or the “ change in interaction " (P16) from feedforward to feedback ( n = 2). Eight participants unlocked the final 7th rhythm, while 11 others dropped out either at rhythm 4 ( n = 2), 5 ( n = 4) or 6 ( n = 5). Eight dropped out because they “ [were] not aware there were more " (P08, rhythm 4), while three others dropped out because the unlocked rhythms were “ not spectacular " (P19, P20, rhythm 6). In contrast, seven of eight participants who unlocked the final rhythm felt that it was “ satisfying " (P01), “ fun " (P15) or “ dance-able " (P16), as it was “ more complex " (P04) compared to FF. P10, however, expressed that the final rhythm was “ not melodic enough " and felt “ disappointed ". Our observations showed that 16 of the interviewed participants immediately applied the feedforward in FF during FB, i.e. walking the length of the passage, while 10 participants combined the walking with gestures such as waving (P02, P03), flapping (P05, P11, P19, P21) or extending their arms horizontally (P06, P07, P10, P15); and three participants skipped (P01), jumped (P04) or walked in a zigzag pattern (P16).

Twelve participants improvised to achieve a desired rhythm in FB. Three individual participants were satisfied as they could “ control the sound " (P09), “ move around as [they] wanted " (P17), or “ be in charge of the facade " (P12). The four groups of P01, P02 and P03 (G1); P04 and P05 (G2); P06 and P07 (G3); and P15 and P16 (G4) negotiated with one another to choreograph their collective walking patterns, such as by moving in a circle (G1), taking turns to walk (G2), running in opposite directions (G3) or moving left and right synchronously (G4). The groups explained that they wanted to “ make the facade ring continuously " (P02, G1), “ play a melody [by moving] on top of a beat [by standing still] " (P05, G2), “ make a lot of sound waves " (P06, G3), or “ mix the two different pitches together " (P16, G4), in which three groups (G1, G3, G4) expressed that they achieved the wanted rhythm.

5.3.4 Experiencing MUURmelaar. This section reports how the rhythmic, spatial and social quality of MUURmelaar affected its first-hand experience.

Rhythmic Quality . The perceived rhythmic quality, as experienced by the participants while listening to the rhythm, varied widely, even among those who engaged together in a group. About timbre , 17 participants felt that the rhythm was “ warm " (P13), “ soft " (P02), “ wooden " (P21), or even identified them correctly as “ xylophones " (P05); while four others thought that it felt “ cold " (P08), “ harsh " (P18) or “ metallic " (P09). For pitch , 19 participants recognised a difference between windows, which motivated them to “ climb the slope to create a melody " (P13), “ find out how to make different pitches at once " (P15), or “ play a song by walking " (P04). Two participants did not recognise the different pitches, as P10 expressed that he “ could not find any melody "; and P09, who rated the lowest SUS score among the participants, SUS = 80), found that the rhythm sounded “ mechanical, without any up or down ". Regarding the materiality , 11 participants believed that the rhythms were made by “ something physical " (P15), as they associated them with a hidden instrument such as a “ wind chimes " (P14), a “ bamboo percussion " (P01), “ wood blocks " (P21) or “ metal tubes " (P10). 10 participants thought that there were “ small speakers " inside each window, explaining: “ speakers would be the first thing I think of, it is obviously easier to make " (P12). All participants were able to recognise the directionality of the rhythms. 13 participants preferred the rhythms from the top window as they felt “ softer " (P02) or “ more gentle " (P13). In contrast, eight others enjoyed the windows on the street level as they were “ louder " (P16) and thus “ more exciting " (P21).

Spatial Quality . For nine participants, the rhythms suited the architectural expression of MUURmelaar: “ it has a simplicity similarly to the building " (P12), timbre: “ it sounds wooden like the window frame " (P21), or materiality: “ it is harsh like the exposed concrete " (P18). 11 participants yet found that the rhythms contrasted with the building: “ the sounds were fun, tropical, traditional, but the building looks serious and modern " (P14) with unrelated timbre: “ it sounds metallic but there is no metal on the facade " (P09), or different materiality: “ the sound reminded me of a rain forest, but the building looks very cold and dry " (P01). Eight participants concluded that the rhythms were more reminiscent to the adjacent mural or flower beds, as they were both “ colourful " (P21), “ fresh and young " (P15), “ funky " (P10) or “ natural " (P01). All participants however agreed that the rhythms brought more value to the built environment, as it “ attracted [their] attention " (P02), motivated them to “ notice the architecture " (P20), making MUURmelaar “ a special building in the neighbourhood " (P13).

The participants mentioned that the physical properties of the passageway influenced their engagement with MUURmelaar. Four participants said that the passageway suggested “ which way to walk ", thanks to its long proportion (P04, P07, P08) and directionality (P20); while five others discovered that its physical boundary corresponded with the tracking area: “ it did not want me to step on the flowers because then it stopped playing " (P19). Four participants commented on the slope, as P15 and P16 (aged 27 and 28) compared it to a “ running hill ", whereas P13 and P14 (aged 62 and 56) saw it as a “ everyday challenge ", with the rhythm as their “ small reward ". Five participants found the passageway an impractical place to engage (P15), as it was frequented by “ many runners and cyclists passing by " (P11) and thus would be “ not safe " (P09). One participant, P21, felt that she was “ taking over the path" , and would not “ be comfortable [engaging] alone " without P20 being there.

Social Quality . As shown in Figure 18 , MUURmelaar facilitated social interaction among passers-by, as it motivated P03 to join the group of P01 and P02: “ I just saw that they were dancing and asked to join " (P03). A young boy joined P20 and P21 before running back to his parents. Nineteen participants agreed that MUURmelaar enhanced the hedonic qualities of the passageway, making it into a “ playground, especially for children " (P02), helping them feel “ happy " (P20), “ young " (P14), “ healthier " (P06) or “ less stress " (P01). Five participants would introduce MUURmelaar to their friends or family, as P17 would “ come back again with [his] children to play ", P13 would“ tell [her] grandchildren about it ", and P09 said “ it would be fun to play again with a friend ". P11, a local resident, mentioned that MUURmelaar was “ famous in the neighbourhood " as a “ fun building ". It also helped the passageway feel “ safer " especially in the late afternoon, as passers-by acknowledged that “ someone was watching them " (P11).

Seven participants were however concerned about negative repercussion, in relation to the building owner: “ it would be annoying to work inside the building " (P04), the neighbouring houses: “ I would not want to live next to this when anybody can make sounds " (P18), or the passers-by: “ it would be pretty distracting if you are on a bike " (P17). These concerns inhibited continuous engagement as three participants paused:“ I waited until the cyclists went away then started playing again " (P08), adapted their behaviour: “ I walked slowly to make less sound because it is late " (P10, at 19:30), or left and then returned: “ I saw that they [the building owners] were having a meeting earlier, so I left " (P17).

6 DISCUSSION

Based on the reported insights, we propose four design considerations for media architecture.

6.1 Feedforward Learning Helped Engage Passers-by Enduringly and Purposefully

Our results show that feedforward learning supported all passers-by, including newcomers and past-visitors, to engage with MUURmelaar more enduringly and purposefully. Passers-by engaged with our feedforward strategy significantly longer than the basic feedback strategies of the previous years (see 5.3.1 . Quantitative Analysis). $30\%$ of actively engaged passers-by reached the discovery phase (see 5.3.1 . Quantitative Analysis), during which they could influence the rhythm individually or socially (see Figure 17 , Figure 18 and 5.3.3 . Discovering). However, our rendition of feedforward learning also inhibited enduring engagement due to its repetitive character (see 4.3.3 . Engaging with the Conditions), unimpressive reward, lack of perceived agency, social embarrassment, and physical exhaustion (see 5.3.3 . Learning).

As shown in our literature review, HCI applied feedforward to guide gestural interfaces [ 56 , 81 , 84 ], convey decisions of autonomous systems [ 76 ], help performers make informed decisions [ 37 ], or improve physical training [ 24 , 29 , 48 , 63 ]. Our study broadens its application for a relatively abstract sonic media architecture installation to convey functional information [ 87 ] to engage with passers-by more purposefully. Informing newcomers about the functionalities by literal demonstration evidently helps ease the transition between the sequence of PACD engagement phases [ 89 ]. However, predefining the order of FF-then-FB also hinders the participant trajectories [ 6 ] of more experienced passers-by, who are obliged to ‘learn’ a skill in FF they have already mastered. We thus propose that future manifestations of FF could integrate a shortcut [ 57 ] that experienced passers-by could exploit to pass the learning phase immediately. While our feedforward engaging strategy is potentially applicable to any deployment of media architecture, including visual-based, the requirement of feedforward learning to convey understandable, simple yet engaging functional information [ 87 ] makes it particularly useful for low-resolution media architecture [ 42 ].

C1. To engage passers-by more enduringly and purposefully, media architecture could support newcomers with an initial phase of feedforward learning.

6.2 Reading Time Indicated Endurance

The time a passer-by spent reading the textual instruction indicated their availability or willingness to engage enduringly. Our quantitative analysis showed that those who spent more time reading the instruction were more likely to engage more enduringly (see 5.3.1 . Quantitative Analysis). This can be caused by the fact that passers-by reading the instruction might become more informed, feel more motivated because they already spent substantial effort reading, or already had more free time as they were able to stop by and read. Our thematic analysis further showed how the textual instruction alerted newcomers of the engagement possibility, and invited past-visitors to try out new rhythms (see 5.3.3 . Encountering). Such instruction, therefore, should be sufficiently informative about how to engage (i.e. ‘ following its rhythm ’, see 5.3.3 . Learning) to achieve a reward (i.e. ‘ make its rhythms as you like ’, see 5.3.3 . Encountering).

Previous studies of public display demonstrated that curiosity-provoking artefacts [ 46 ] help overcome interaction blindness [ 47 ]. Media architecture also deployed tangible interfaces (e.g. [ 28 , 44 , 58 ]) that, however, required a manual demonstration [ 58 ], a performance [ 28 ] or a workshop [ 44 ] to inform passers-by. In contrast, in our case, the combination of the basic rhythm and the textual instruction was sufficient to spark human curiosity (see in Figure 15 ). Knowing that reading the instruction has predictive power, we can imagine that this activity in itself could become a goal that could be rewarded; alternatively, the system could dynamically adapt the difficulty of FF depending on the reading duration (e.g. passers-by who read the instruction briefly would experience easier FF rhythms). Making the space around the instruction visible and unobstructed would also increase its encounters with passers-by [ 66 ] (see 5.3.3 . Encountering), motivating them to stop by and thus enhancing their chance of engaging [ 28 ].

C2. To optimise the endurance of the engagement, media architecture could exploit measuring the duration with which passers-by read the accompanying textual instruction.

6.3 Learning Rate Indicated Purposefulness

The initial learning rate of FF, i.e. how fast a passer-by unlocked rhythm 2 compared to rhythm 1, formed an indicator of how purposeful the subsequent engagement will be. In particular, our quantitative analysis evidenced that passers-by with higher learning rates were more likely to reach higher rhythms subsequently (see 5.3.1 . Quantitative Analysis). This can be because such passers-by understood the textual instruction better, were more agile (see 5.3.3 . Learning), or collaborated better with others (see Figure 16 ). The fact that they unlocked a rhythm quickly might also motivate them to engage more deeply. Our thematic analysis further showed how participants who understood FF could then apply this understanding to improvise with MUURmelaar, insofar that some purposefully choreographed their actions individually or socially (see 5.3.3 . Discovering).

Feedforward has been applied to enhance learning in different contexts. Educational games deployed algorithms that ensured players always received positive responses to their actions [ 60 ] by proactively adapting the difficulty to their learning rate [ 85 ]. In classroom teaching, feedforward evaluation let educators make real-time adjustments [ 15 ] and allowed students to adapt their plans [ 74 ] accordingly to the current learning outcomes. Studies of public interactive interventions, however, instead captured that newcomers were usually familiarised via social learning [ 49 , 89 ], as allowing multiple passers-by to engage simultaneously triggered social interaction that helped enhance the learning rate [ 80 ]. However, as passers-by learn from not only experienced users but also other unknowing newcomers [ 61 ], public interactive interventions should actively inform passers-by how to engage purposefully [ 1 ]. Our results propose that the learning rate with feedforward can be applied to measure the quality of engagement [ 23 ], which would provide a possibility for public interactive interventions to optimise engagement, such as by proactively aligning the trajectories of passers-by [ 5 ] through feedforward or feedback. For example, when a passer-by struggles to pass through the first FF rhythms, the system could provide more direct (instead of intriguing but fuzzy) instruction, or map the rhythms in a much more intuitive way. When a passer-by in a group struggles to catch up, the system could map the simultaneous rhythms to motivate the group to collaborate, enhancing social learning.

C3. To optimise the purposefulness of the engagement, media architecture could exploit measuring the learning rate with which passers-by pass through the initial challenges.

6.4 Grounding the Rhythms on the Context Helped Engage Passers-by Meaningfully

Grounding its rhythms on the spatial and social context, MUURmelaar meaningfully engaged passers-by by enriching how they experience their surroundings. Our thematic analysis showed that the rhythm complimented the surroundings with atmospheric reminiscence through its timbre or materiality, ranging from “ natural " (P21) over “ traditional " (P14) to “ rain forest " (P01) (see 5.3.4 . Rhythmic Quality). In turn, the spatial context of the passage provided suggestions for passers-by to engage with the rhythm, such as a walking direction, a boundary of the tracking area, or a perceived challenge with its slope (see 5.3.4 . Spatial Quality). The social context surrounding MUURmelaar helped it become a ‘landmark’ in the neighbourhood, as it provided a ‘playground’ for the local community, increased the chance of social interactions between passers-by, or attracted newcomers (see 5.3.4 . Social Quality). Moreover, the focus on rhythms as an alternative interaction modality showed to help MUURmelaar to become an example of more-than-human media architecture [ 31 ]. Using mechanical solenoids, MUURmelaar requires less energy, reduces the need for maintenance or replacement of parts compared to LED lights, and minimises the disturbance to the natural environment as it does not emit sound or light during the night. MUURmelaar is also relatively permanent as it is integrated into also the ‘service’ and ‘structure’ layers of the building rather than only the ‘skin’ layer [ 9 ].

Previous studies have already called for visual media architecture to consider its architectural [ 90 ], contextual [ 64 ] or social qualities [ 43 ] to enhance its long-term sustainability. Our study demonstrates an example of how media architecture, using an alternative interaction modality of rhythm, can also benefit from considering its socio-spatial context. In the case of MUURmelaar, social considerations required the rhythms to be relatively quiet not to cause noise pollution, which made it challenging for passers-by from the main street to discover the possibility of engaging with it (see 5.3.3 . Encountering). Meanwhile, unavoidable negative repercussions of the rhythms on the neighbour and the building owner also inhibited passers-by from engaging with MUURmelaar continuously (see 5.3.4 . Social Quality). We thus recognise that there requires consideration to negotiate the tension between the efficiency of media architecture to engage passers-by and the needs of the neighbourhood to augment the surrounding built environment meaningfully [ 70 ].

C4. To optimise the meaningfulness of the engagement, media architecture could ground its interaction modalities on the socio-spatial context.

7 Limitations & Shortcomings

As our study focused on capturing how passers-by engaged with MUURmelaar through rhythms, it has not sufficiently explored the capability of MUURmelaar in creating recognisable melodies using different pitches of the xylophones or the extent to which such pitches might influence the experience of passers-by. Socio-cultural sensitivities might bias some findings, as MUURmelaar not only manifests a rather modernist and western architectural expression, but its rhythms also play at a relatively stable tempo, originating from western musical preference [ 78 ]. Whereas we deployed our in-the-wild study for one month, our results could only capture a short window of time in the lifespan of a media architecture facade, which ranges from years to decades. Some of our findings, therefore, might not be generalisable to other newer or older media architecture. Although the interviewed participants ( n = 21) ranged among different genders, age groups, occupations and nationalities, they did not represent every cohort of passers-by who engaged with MUURmelaar ( n = 1877), as we were not able to interview non-engaged passers-by, children or non-English speakers. As the study took place in the summer of 2022, some of our findings might be slightly biased or exaggerated due to the environmental conditions of warm weather or the subjective preference of passers-by to be outside during this season. Occasional contextual factors such as sun flares, strong winds or moving clouds might have hindered some passers-by from engaging with MUURmelaar and thus affected our results, as the computer vision software was unable to detect their movements on a rapidly changing background.

8 CONCLUSION

As media architecture is becoming increasingly ubiquitous in the urban context, recent deployments have applied non-visual modalities such as rhythm to attract the attention of passers-by. Through a comparative and an in-the-wild study, our study captured empirical evidence of how a xylophonic media architectural facade could engage passers-by more enduringly and purposefully by applying feedforward learning. From quantitative and qualitative insights, we proposed four design considerations for media architecture, as well as any public interactive intervention in general, including (1) deploying feedforward learning to empower passers-by, (2) exploiting measuring the reading time around the instruction to optimise the endurance of the engagement, (3) exploiting measuring the feedforward learning rate to optimise the purposefulness of the engagement, and (4) grounding the interaction modalities in the spatial and social context. Among other reflections, our study aims to inspire future deployments of media architecture to integrate other interaction modalities beyond visual.
