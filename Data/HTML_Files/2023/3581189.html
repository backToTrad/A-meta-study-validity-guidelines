Don't Panic! - Influence of Virtual Stressor Representations from the ICU Context on Perceived Stress Levels

Sebastian Weiß , Society, OFFIS - Institute for Information Technology, Germany, sebastian.weiss@offis.de
Wilko Heuten , Society, OFFIS - Institute for Information Technology, Germany, wilko.heuten@offis.de

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581189
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Intensive care nurses are prone to suffering from chronic stress due to constant exposure to two main profession-related stressors: interruption and time pressure. These stressors have detrimental effects on the well-being of the nursing staff and, by proxy, the patients. To alleviate stress, increase safety, and support the training of stressful scenarios, we investigate the impact these stressors have on subjective and objective stress levels in a virtual environment. We designed an intensive care unit in which participants (n=26, 18 healthcare professionals) perform common tasks, e.g. refilling an infusion pump, whilst being exposed to interruptions and time pressure. Results from our between-subjects study provide data indicating stress increase in both stressor conditions, suggesting that artificially evoking work-related stressors for stress inoculation training (SIT) is a possible extension to simulation training during nursing education. This knowledge is helpful for designing training scenarios of safety-critical situations early in the professional apprenticeship.

1 INTRODUCTION

In the nursing profession, chronic occupational stress is one of the most common reasons for staff leaving the workforce early [ 51 ]. In this work environment, several stimuli and events (called stressors) can be found, including psychological, emotional, and physical factors [ 45 ]. Especially in the intensive care unit (ICU), the amount of acute stress nurses face on a daily basis can lead to chronic stress, which in turn may result in health problems and, in worst cases, damage to the patient [ 12 ]. Stress has been shown to increase the mental workload, thus reducing the capability for decision making [ 14 ] and it has further been associated with lower physical and cognitive performance [ 1 ]. There is an extensive body of research linking occupational stress to reduced job satisfaction and patient care, higher staff turnover, worsening performance on the job, burn-out and absenteeism [ 28 , 37 , 44 ]. It is possible to help nurses acquire coping skills and resilience through training. Nursing schools have long employed a variety of simulation-based teaching methods [ 42 ]. Educators make use of face-to-face lessons, role plays in the class room and skills labs. Further, students have mandatory practical clinical terms in different care facilities. These methods cover a range of varying fidelity from low- to high-fidelity simulations [ 42 ]. All of these approaches come with their own benefits and drawbacks. Using role play may not provide the necessary realism for safety-critical or stressful situations [ 23 , 58 ]. Furthermore, some schools provide access to skills labs for a more realistic environment and the opportunity to learn with real devices and actors. However, these skills labs are expensive to maintain [ 4 ] and are not available around the clock [ 58 ]. Thus, these educational and simulation techniques may be inadequate or not feasible for some institutions to be used as a unified approach for the broad range of skills aspiring nurses need to develop for their profession. Virtual Reality (VR), with its highly customizable artificial environments, comparably low cost, and the possibility to repeat or adjust scenarios and tasks on demand, has been shown to be a useful tool in simulation based training in several disciplines, such as fire and rescue services [ 13 ], spaceflight [ 15 ], or construction [ 21 ]. This is especially true for safety-relevant situations, as these can be trained without risking damage to people or devices [ 5 ]. While this was a rather new development in nursing and not much research has been conducted until 2018 [ 56 ], there has been a recent increase in research activity [ 42 ]. It is especially important to prepare aspiring nurses with respect to the high amount of stress that is inherently connected with their profession of choice, as it is a major reason for the staff turnover. This can be achieved by applying stress inoculation training (SIT), an intervention developed by Meichenbaum et al. in the 1970s. SIT has already been used in conjunction with VR in other fields, but has not yet been applied for ICU nurses [ 3 , 43 ]. Despite the emerging focus on potential applications within the nursing sector, little is known about the impact and effectiveness of our selected VR-based stressor representations from the environment. However, a controlled and effective stressful environment is a requirement for SIT to be fruitful [ 43 ]. There is a gap in research with respect to VR-based SIT in the nursing profession that this work will contribute to. Therefore, the focus of this work is on creating a virtual representation of an ICU and the implementation of typical nursing tasks as well as stressors that are common to this environment. We are particularly interested in the impact that VR representations of real stressors have on participants and the possible applications of this knowledge to training scenarios for the nursing community.

With this work, we contribute human-centered research into how profession-specific stressors from the ICU can be transferred into virtual environments, for which we created a complete apparatus based on a highly presence inducing virtual representation of an ICU. To the best of our knowledge, this work includes the first VR-based task interruption simulation, and further includes "time pressure" as a second stressor. We investigate their effects on the users’ stress level in a user study with n=26 participants to gain an understanding about the stress inducing capabilities, which is a requirement for SIT-based interventions.

The required knowledge to build a convincing image of this environment was collected from literature as well as two focus groups. The stressors include visual and auditory cues (see Figures 4(a) and 4(b)). With VR, we have a controllable and customizable environment for applying SIT [ 38 ], that implicitly does not carry risk for real patients.

2 RELATED WORK

This section will discuss how stress may be defined and how it can be measured, what stress means in a nursing environment, and why it is important to train nurses’ resilience. It also explains why VR can be an effective tool for achieving this.

2.1 Stress and Stress Measurement

In general, stressors are defined as changes in the environment that force the organism to adapt in order to function properly [ 25 ]. More specifically, stress is the result of various unconscious response patterns that consist of psychological, cognitive, and behavioral components [ 2 ]. In short, if the required amount of change is too big, people get stressed. These reaction patterns are controlled by the autonomous nervous system which mediates a stress response by affecting different organs through the release of stress hormones. These hormones alter numerous physiological features that can be used to detect and track the state of stress in an individual. In a technology review, Sharma et al. provided an extensive list of several affected features and the appropriate measuring technologies [ 52 ]. Among others, these include heart rate (HR), respiration frequency and breathing depth (RF/BD), electro-encephalogram (EEG), changes in voice pitch, and changes in electrodermal activity (EDA) [ 52 ]. These physiological markers are used in biological psychology, especially psychophysiology, to study behavior and other cognitive processes. As it is not trivial to design and execute stress induction correctly, stressors may have varying results on participants based on small deviations from the experiment protocol. Hence, the use of subjective stress indices is highly recommended [ 53 ]. This is performed to ensure that the reaction is due to the inducing stressor and not confounding factors such as eustress, i.e. the stress response to positive excitement, or mental workload [ 19 ].

2.2 Stressors in Nursing

For nurses, several studies investigated the factors that contribute to stress, such as the alarm level on wards [ 7 , 10 , 46 ], work overload in the nursing profession [ 18 , 22 , 36 ], equipment usage [ 20 ], and emotional demands when caring for patients and/or their family [ 20 , 22 , 36 ].

The phenomenon of task interruption is ubiquitous in the ICU. While there are several definitions, the critical nature of the ICU fits best into the definition by Li et al.: a secondary activity that requires one's attention and stops interaction with the primary task [ 32 ]. Drews et al. observed a task interruption frequency of about 30% during a time frame of 34h [ 11 ]. Here, the definition by Li et al. was used to describe an interruption. Moreover, the authors calculate the likelihood of step omission and task abandonment, indicating a risk for patients. Coiera et al. assume a correlation of clinical errors and interruptions in concurrence with multiple tasks due to the disruptive effect on memory processes, ultimately resulting in patient risks [ 8 ]. In addition to patient risk, Mark et al. show that while interruptions have a positive effect on completion times (i.e., faster), they induce stress in the person subjected to the interruption [ 33 ]. Fletcher et al. elaborate on this research and show that interruptions in any form (intrusion, breaks, distractions, or discrepancies) increase stress [ 16 ].

In addition, Laurent et al. have compiled a comprehensive overview of stressor questionnaires in the ICU, further elaborating on the amount and diversity of different kinds of stressors [ 29 ]. Given that there are a multitude of stressors in an ICU, it is reasonable for us to limit the choice for our experiment to those stressors that can be represented in VR. Examples from related work are presented below.

2.3 Stress Inoculation in VR

To prevent stress-related health issues specific stress trainings, e.g. the Stress Inoculation Training [ 38 ], can be employed. Research has demonstrated that through gradual exposure to stressors, participants are able to develop coping strategies that help reduce the severity of the stress response and increase stress resilience [ 27 , 47 ]. VR has been shown to be an appropriate tool for usage in SIT-based trainings. Evidently, there is only little previous research on VR-based SIT for nurses, which is why we discuss similar work from other application domains. In the context of space walks for example, Finseth et al. created a stressful VR simulation for adaptive stress training [ 14 ], including several environmental stressors such as smoke, alarms, and flashing lights. Their machine learning-based system evaluated stress levels in real time and triggered stressors to maintain a certain stress level. They compared this adaptive training approach with a fixed increase in stress, as well a constant low level approach. This adaptive training was most successful in inoculating stress resistance.

To investigate the effect of an emotional connection on stress, Prachyabrued et al. [ 43 ] examined the influence of emotional connection during a stressful VR-based scenario. The authors provided a story-driven SIT where the participants take the role of an Emergency Medical Technician (EMT). One group established an emotional connection by means of a conversation with their non-player character (NPC) supervisor at the beginning of the experiment, before being called to an emergency. During triage on site, the superior has an accident and dies. Results showed that both objective and subjective stress levels, measured by heart rate and a Likert item, were significantly higher in those users who established an emotional connection. In a similar experiment, Weiss et al. [ 57 ] developed a scenario where participants deal with emotional distress in an ICU, when the patient's family members’ requests for the continuation of care are clashing with the moral concept of the participant, inciting moral distress. In line with Prachyabrued, they showed that emotional stress can be induced as part of an SIT.

Clifford et al. [ 6 ] compared the degree of realism required to incite stress in their participants. For the training of aerial fire fighters, they created a variety of environments (multi-modal high-fidelity VR, single-modal low-fidelity radio, real world) and had participants run through a firefighting scenario. The comparison of objective (HR variability) and subjective (Dundee Stress State Questionnaire, see [ 35 ]) data did not yield statistically significant results. While it seems that realism does not have influence on the stress reduction, Misztal et al. [ 40 ] show that visual effects applied to the virtual environment (VE) can increase the stress perception in their participants. Chromatic aberration, blur, or image noise have been used to alter the image participants see. The measuring instruments employed in this study are a 7-point Likert item (not at all stressed - very stressed), the IPQ Presence Questionnaire (IPQ by [ 49 ]), and the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ by [ 26 ]). Although the authors report a statistically significant increase in stress levels between no visual effects and those with visual effects, no conclusions are drawn about which effect was most influential.

By providing a graphical environment with concurrent multi-modal stimuli, VR can be of support in education as a (stress-) simulation tool, as well as in the treatment of phobias and even influence memory, meaning it can be supportive of learning [ 60 ]. Min et al. show that even the sound of a beating heart in a multi-modal set-up may increase the perceived stress [ 39 ].

The related work shows that VR is capable of inoculating stress in different contexts. The examples presented make use of the immersive capabilities of VR [ 48 ], emotional bonds and storytelling [ 43 ], multi-modal stressor presentation [ 6 ] and visual effects [ 40 ]. Some of these concepts directly relate to the stressors identified in Section 2.3 . The use case of a VR-based SIT for nursing has not yet been investigated. Within the present related work, we did not find any VR-based task interruption investigations. This is a research gap as it was not yet researched whether users deal with and react to virtual representations of this in a similar fashion as they would in reality, which is why a validation is required.

The training of nurses using VR is at an early stage, and the effectiveness of the VR representation of our chosen stressors (cf. Section 3 ) has yet to be confirmed. As the body of literature on VR-based SIT in this context is very small to date, we base our choice for stressors on focus groups, cf. Section 3.1 . Based on existing work, VR-based SIT that implements stressors for VR nursing simulations could achieve similar results and further lead to more realistic responses to a training situation than role play, in a more cost-effective manner than skills labs. In this work, we investigate the effectiveness of stressors as an initial step towards the VR-based SIT for the ICU nursing staff.

3 MATERIALS AND METHODS

In this study, we address the impact of virtual replicas of two common stressors from the nursing context to investigate its effectiveness for later use in SIT interventions. We utilized a custom-designed VE (cf. Fig. 1 ) to investigate our research question:

”What effect do virtual replicas of the real-life stressors "Task Interruption" and "Time Pressure" from the ICU context have on VR users?”

The VE contains an ICU with nursing tasks and an environment-typical background noise with chatter, a ringing telephone, and the sounds of a ventilation system, see Fig. 1(a) and Fig. 1(b) . We designed this study as a mixed-methods study, using a variety of user context exploration techniques to create a realistic VE. Our investigation into stressors was based on a literature review, the results of which were later verified in a focus group.

3.1 Context Research

We analyzed the literature in Section 2.2 as well as a review on ICU stressor scales [ 29 ] to get an impression about the multifaceted stress-inducing influences in nursing, and particularly in the ICU. From these studies, we collected stressors and the frequency in which they have been mentioned. In an expert focus group (n=6, 4f) consisting of two nurses, two VR developers, and two experts for nursing technology, we used card sorting [ 54 ] to identify umbrella terms for the stressors from literature. These umbrella terms were "high job demands", "professional relationships", "ethics and morale", and "risk management". These terms aggregate several stressors, such as interruption through alarms, patients requiring attention for social reasons, telephone calls from medical staff and care personnel. Further, "high workload", "high availability", and "low error tolerance" were frequently mentioned as stressful factors. In a second step, we tasked our focus group participants with estimating the feasibility of replicating the stressors in a VR setting. The results were then reviewed in terms of how much of an impact the stressor has and whether it would be feasible to implement. Based on expert discussions we chose to replicate the stressors Time Pressure (TP) and Interruption (I) as they were often mentioned in the literature and can be replicated in a high degree of detail.

Having identified several stressors, we conducted a second focus group with teaching staff from a local nursing school (n=5, 4f). All participants were practice instructors, i.e. they had finished their apprenticeship in one of the German nursing professions (e.g. general care, geriatric care, children's nursing), had at least two years of professional experience and completed further training to qualify as practice instructors. The focus group was held online and participants were given a 30 minute introduction into the capabilities of controller-based interaction in VR. We aimed to collect ideas on which tasks are a) typical for the ICU environment and b) can be implemented in VR with reasonable effort. Additionally, we gained further insight through a shadowing session in the ICU of a local hospital during a morning shift. In cooperation with the focus group, we defined two distinct, typical routine tasks: refilling syringes for an infusion device and collecting information from vital signs monitors to document in a patient chart. These are described in detail in the next section.

3.2 Tasks

3.2.1 Filling Syringes. We endeavored to design the virtual version of the "filling syringes" task as realistically as possible. To complete the task, participants had to use the left and right VR controller simultaneously. Using the designated grip button on the controller shaft, items of interest can be picked up. The trigger button is pressed with the index finger and is used to interact with virtual devices. Other buttons are not required for the task.

The syringe task consists of the following steps: participants read the amount of required medication from a screen. Next, they attach a needle to the syringe bottom part and pull air into the syringe. After 50ml air has been collected, participants point the needle into the vial, and then press the trigger continuously to push air mimicking the process of creating pressure inside the vial. Another continuous push of the trigger then draws medication into the syringe. Required amounts of air/medication are displayed in white (air) and blue (medication) numbers next to the syringe. This process is visualized in Figure 2(b) .

Afterwards, the syringe should be filled with the correct amount of liquid. Before it can be inserted into the infusion device, the participant must replace the needle part with a larger diameter slip tip to increase medication flow. The syringe can now be placed into the infusion device which is located approximately three meters away from syringes and medication vial. The participant removes the syringe already placed in the device and replaces it with the one they just prepared, as shown in Figure 2(c) . Activating a button on the infusion device starts the pumping process and participants returned to the task screen where they mark the task as completed by pressing a button on the screen.

3.2.2 Documenting Patient Vitals. The second task was the documentation of patient vitals. The representation of this task is based on information gained from the shadowing session at a local hospital, where nurses either worked in pairs, or walked from the monitor to the desk, where the medical file of size DIN A3 is unfolded and written on. Due to the distance between the desk and monitor, the staff have to memorize the vitals until they can note them down.

For task completion in VR, participants had to step up to a monitor (cf. Figure 3(a)) located on the left side of the bed at an approximate height of 1.9m and remember a blood pressure reading, which would only appear when they were within a distance of 0.5 meters from the monitor. This corresponds to the real situation in the hospital, where we conducted the shadowing session. Participants were supposed to return to the task screen, where possible value pairs were displayed. Of these, only one matched the value displayed on the monitoring device, see Figure 3(b) .

3.3 Stressors

3.3.1 Time Pressure Stressor. In the experiment, both tasks were randomly assigned a time limit of 20 seconds for the documentation task, and 30 seconds for the syringe task respectively, forcing participants to increase the speed of execution. Participants were informed of the time limit for the task at hand by a short sound and the remaining time was indicated in red letters on the task screen within the environment, as illustrated in Figure 4(a) . If the countdown ended before the task was marked as completed, it would end and the next task would start. Incomplete tasks were logged as a mistake.

3.3.2 Interruption Stressor. Often, especially in ICU, lower priority tasks are interrupted (i.e., the tasks we implemented) by higher priority tasks, such as incoming alarms to a critical situation (e.g., drop in HR, low blood oxygen saturation). In our experiment, we realized the task interruption through a heart monitor placed by the foot-end of the bed, as depicted in Figure 4(b) . Participants had to acknowledge the alarm to continue with the current task. While we are aware that the location of the heart monitor does not reflect reality, we implemented it this way to maximize the distance the participant must move to acknowledge the alarm. Handling the interruption (i.e., confirming and subsequently silencing the alarm) was done by using the controller to interact with the device.

3.4 Study Design

This research was conducted to measure the influence virtual portrayals of stressors from the nursing profession have on participants in order to validate their effectivity. This is a prerequisite for an SIT-style intervention for stress handling to work. To investigate the isolated effect of each stressor, we designed our experiment as a between-subject study. The dependent variables are the individual bio-psychological markers and the independent variable is the stressor presence. For each of the stressors, we minimized task order effects by means of Latin square. To answer our research question, we utilize this study design to investigate the following hypotheses:

  • H 1 : The use of the virtual stressors "Task Interruption" and "Time Pressure" in a virtual training environment increases the stress level in a subject significantly, as measured by the applied measuring technologies.
  • H 2 : The virtual stressor "Interruption" increases the stress level more significantly due to its multi-modal presentation.

3.5 Apparatus

The software was implemented in C# with the game engine Unity3D 1 in version 2019.10.f1. Interactions with the environment were implemented using the Virtual Reality Toolkit (VRTK) 2 v3.3 library to allow a broad range of different VR headsets to be used in conjunction with the experiment. The software runs a gaming grade Windows 10 PC, equipped with an nVidia GeForce 1080TI graphic card, an Intel i78700k CPU clocking in at 3.7GHz, and 16 GB of RAM. To experience the VE, participants wore an Oculus Quest head mounted device (HMD), developed and sold by Meta Inc. Teleportation in the VE was disabled to increase immersion.

To collect data on the psycho-physiological markers we employed an integrated device (Zephyr Bioharness 3, Medtronic., Boulder, CO) for the cardio-pulmonary data (HR, RF, BD) and a galvanic skin response sensor (Grove GSR, SEEED Technologies INC., Shenzen, China) that was worn on the upper arm in a sleeve. The sensor measures HR in beats per minute. The BD is measured by the pressure exerted on the sensor when the chest rises when taking a breath, and is returned in a 16 bit unsigned number with a range from 0 to 65534 [ 55 ]. These signals strongly correlate to experienced stress level (cf. [ 30 , 52 ]) and have been used in literature for stress research in combination with VR [ 34 , 52 ].

For the subjective evaluation we used the Perceived Stress Questionnaire (PSQ) developed by Levenstein et al. [ 31 ]. We employed the short version consisting of 20 items [ 17 ]. Since the PSQ is validated for the period of the past four weeks, we asked participants to relate their answers to the time frame of the experiment. In addition, we queried participants to rate their own stress level on a 7-point Likert scale item ("In this very moment, I feel stressed" with (1) not stressed at all - (7) very stressed). Thus, a self-assessment of the subjects at the time of the experiment could be obtained. Questionnaires were answered in VR to avoid any negative influence on the presence of our participants, as suggested by [ 50 ].

Although visual representations of the alarm and time pressure do not exist in the real world, we added each of them for a particular reason. The visual marker for the interruption stressor helped participants to identify the device more easily, as the spatial sound proved to not be a distinct enough cue as per tests in the piloting session. The visual countdown for TP was added so participants were informed about the time left in a more neutral way as opposed to informing participants about possible consequences for the patient. In addition to the reason mentioned above, participants were also not informed whether a task was solved incorrectly or not, to avoid creating an additional source of frustration.

3.6 Participants

We invited 26 participants (11f, 15m) aged 19-50 years (mean=29.46, SD=6.59) to the study in our lab facilities. Participants had different professional backgrounds (nursing: 15, nursing practice instructor: 1, emergency medical technician: 2, HCI: 5, other: 3), and little to no VR experience. After signing informed consent and data privacy forms participants were allocated to either I or TP stressor condition. Each condition group was populated with 13 participants. Our participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Participants in the I group had a mean age of 30.2 years (SD=9.72 years), for the TP group we report a mean of 27.4 years (9.03 years). There was no significant age difference between the groups (t=0.63, p=0.54).

3.7 Experiment Procedure

The experiment procedure followed a five phase design, each of which serves a certain aspect.

  1. A Training session was run in which participants were taught to move around the VE, interact with it, as well as perform the tasks to gain an overall understanding of the interactions (as specified in Section 3 ). Then, sensors were attached as described before.
  2. For the Baseline Recording after the training phase, participants sat down wearing the HMD. They experienced a relaxing scene that included instructions to breathe calmly. This was done for seven minutes to return participants to a neutral stress level, for the duration of which we asked participants to sit still. The last three minutes served as the baseline of the objective measures. Participants were then asked to answer the PSQ as a self-assessment baseline recording (Q1, Fig. 5 ). Subsequently, participants returned to the virtual ICU and began the actual experiment.
  3. In the no-stress Phase (NSP) , participants were asked to finish the tasks given to them. During the NSP, participants did not have any stressors affecting them. After finishing a task, the next task would appear automatically, meaning that there was no artificial pause in between. Again, physiological data was recorded for the whole duration of NSP. The phase ended with the participants answering the second set of questionnaires (Q2, Fig. 5 ) to get an impression of the subjective stress level at this point.
  4. In the Stress Phase (SP) , upon returning to the VE, participants were asked to execute the tasks in a given order again. However, this time they were put either under time pressure or were interrupted during their task with an alerting sound from a machine in 50% of tasks. This was carried out to better reflect reality, since not every procedure is performed under stress. After exposure to the stressor, participants had to complete the PSQ and Likert item again (Q3, Fig. 5 ).
  5. The Last Phase was conducted after the experiment. We asked subjects to fill out the IPQ to get an impression on how present participants felt in the VE. Finally, the NASA-TLX by [ 24 ] was employed to learn about the cognitive load each stressor put on the participants. Furthermore, we collected demographic data as well as information on occupation and experience. Moreover, we queried previous gaming and VR experiences and posed the open-ended question of what participants thought of the VE and the tasks.

A graphical overview of the procedure is illustrated in Figure 5 . The numbers in the visualization correspond to the previously detailed description.

Please note that while phases 1 through 3 are the same for all participants, we only use the data from participants of each group for statistical evaluation (within group). Only for phase 4, we calculate unpaired t-tests to compare the effect of both stressors with one another (between group). The described experiment procedure has been reviewed and approved by the ethic committee of the local university.

3.8 Data Recording and Statistical Evaluation

Over the course of the experiment, we collected physiological data from four different sources. The data was recorded using an in-house developed software. Recorded files were subjected to separation into pre- and during-experiment files and subsequently analyzed for statistically significant changes. We further collected data on successful task executions as well as time to completion for both no-stress and stress phases. The calculations we applied were on means of the objective data collected during each experiment phase. We show results as changes in percent between two phases, e.g. [( StressPhase − No − StressorPhase )*100]/ No − StressPhase . in Table 1 . All data was tested for normality using a Shapiro-Wilk test. Depending on the result, we applied the repeated measures t-test or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, respectively. For the direct comparison of the two stressors, we used independent measures t-tests or Mann-Whitney-U tests. The increases from baseline to the stress phase and from the no-stress phase to the stress phase were of interest to us, which is why we report only these results. For effect sizes, we calculated Cohen's D for within group, and rank-biserial correlation for the between group comparison in Phase 4 (cf. Figure 5 ). Please note that due to errors in the database, all GSR data had to be excluded from the study analyses as it was corrupted.

We recorded the time for task execution for both stressors and the mistakes participants made during it. 4.4 Interview Results

A semi-structured interview was executed after the experiment. Questions asked included what participants generally thought of the experiment, whether there were (technical) problems, and whether they thought this would be a welcomed addition to the nursing school curriculum. As most of our participants were nurses, their feedback is of great importance to us and is reflected upon in this section. Responses were grouped according to the focus of the participant's reply, for instance, quality of the simulation, reasons for frustration, or missing elements.

Most of the healthcare professionals rated the fidelity and amount of graphical detail of the simulation as high, which is further supported by the evaluation of the responses to the IPQ, see Figure 8(a) . Elaborating on their positive comments, P2 stated "I had expected much less, to be honest". Other experts (P6, P8 and P15) were surprised by the high fidelity in the syringe task in terms of the order in which aspects had to be done - especially swapping the top parts of the syringe, and the fact that the medicine vial had to be held upside down for the mechanism to work. P6 further commented on the correct placement and positioning of medical devices within the VE. Besides that, one participant who was a stroke nurse mentioned the room was "too clean" and a few participants asked for more diverse tasks and stressors (P9, P11, P15). P17 noted that "in the back of my mind, I knew it was just a simulation and I didn't really feel responsible". We believe that by increasing the variety of tasks and stressors, and by incorporating NPCs into our design and software, we are able to increase the presence in the teaching material, which is also supported by statements of P12 and P15.

Some participants mentioned having trouble interacting with the controllers and / or using the controllers to interact with the VE. This is further corroborated on by the NASA-TLX Mental Demand scale. P16 stated that they were annoyed that "I was better at grabbing things during training, but it got hectic when the timer showed up". This is also reflected in the NASA-TLX score where the "Time Pressure" stressor is rated nearly twice in the Frustration scale as its "Interruption" counterpart (cf. Fig 8(b) ).

Concerning applicability of a VR tool for educational purpose, besides stress training, P22 rated the experiment as future-oriented for a general teaching tool in simulation based training, as it offers both a visual and audible environment and enables a diverse set of scenarios. By combining the stress-inducing capabilities with the results of [ 57 ] or [ 43 ], it may be possible to deliver these rich, multi-stress scenarios as all of the presented work proved to be stress inducing to some extent.

5 DISCUSSION

With this experiment, we have investigated the effectivity of VR-based representations of real-life stressors from the ICU as a step towards SIT style interventions for the nursing community. Our results indicate the capability to affect stress level, in the case of two measures to a significant amount. We further show a high degree of presence. The NASA-TLX indicates a medium mental demand, but a low overall demand. In this section we discuss our results in detail. We focus on important aspects such as ability to induce stress, sense of presence, and cognitive load, as they are interconnected and help us in answering the research question on the effects of stressors on the VR users. Further, we provide insights on the comments of experts during the post-hoc interviews.

5.1 Ability to Induce Stress

Regarding the ability to induce stress in VR, we note that we can detect significant changes between the baseline and the stress phase in two of the three measures obtained (heart rate and respiration frequency) for both stressors, whereas breathing depth indicates tendencies towards a higher stress level (see Table 1 ). Although only few of the subjective ratings are statistically significant, this does not contradict the stress-inducing abilities per se. This is especially the case for the Likert item, as it showed high percentage changes between baseline, the no-stress phase, and the stress phase of the experiment. The Likert item was significantly higher rated in the stress phase than in the no-stress phase for the participant group exposed to "Time Pressure", as was the Demand sub-scale of the PSQ 20. It is important to keep in mind that the PSQ20 was designed for a longer period of time, so the results may not represent a real change. For this reason, the Likert item was utilized, as it queries the current stress level of our participants. Considering that increase in psycho-physiological stress measures is significant from the baseline to the no-stress phase, but not from the no-stress to the stress phase, it seems that the virtual environment as well as the cognitive load of the tasks were the stress inducing factors here. At the same time, we see a significant increase in the subjective stress level. As such, although we can not clearly accept hypothesis H 1 , we believe that with more preparation and practice with VR the mental load can be decreased so that a ceiling effect is not achieved as quickly and users can focus on the presented stressors. Prachyabrued et al, for example, made barely any use of VR interactions and thus kept cognitive load to a minimum, and instead made extensive use of the emotional connection for their stress induction.

As all measures show a more pronounced percentual increase between baseline and stress phase (cf. Table 1 ), we suspect that the VR experience itself caused a high mental workload and thus led to a ceiling effect, diminishing the effect the stressor had. These results should be confirmed with an only no-stress control group, as suggested by [ 14 ]. Also, this would help to investigate stress that is contributed solely by the tasks. However, the Likert item shows a significant increase for the comparison between no-stress and stress in the "Time Pressure" group. Because of the way the Likert item was phrased, its increase indicates a strong influence of the time pressure. By comparing the stress-inducing abilities of both stressors with each other, we investigated which stressor has a stronger influence on the stress level. Differences in stressor effect intensity could shorten or lengthen the time until individual stress thresholds are exceeded. It could also provide insight on whether the training simulation should include a singular stressor for a more targeted stress inoculation. The results imply no difference and thus we have to reject H 2 . However, this enables future work on more sophisticated replications of stressors, as well as an increased diversity of stressors and the effect of using several stressors in one participant group. This, too, would increase the realism. There is a large body of literature showing that heart rate is the most commonly used measure for stress. In combination with the results of the Likert Item, the results of this study indicate that our approach of replicating stressors can indeed induce stress in our participants, which supports our research question.

5.2 Sense of Presence

For both stressors, the sense of presence is not at its maximum on any scale. Yet, with high mean scores of 5.81 for the General Presence sub-scale and 5.8 for Experienced Realism (out of possible 7) in participant ratings, we demonstrate that both of our approaches induce a sense of presence. Despite many participants complaining about handling virtual syringes using the VR system controllers, we suggest the high mean score in the Experienced Realism sub-scale may be attributed to our high fidelity approach of making the task of refilling medications virtual. At the same time, this explains the high frustration levels, especially in the TP group (see NASA-TLX evaluation below, and Figure 8(b) ). As there is no significant difference between the stressors in terms of Experienced Realism , we conclude that both have been received as very real. These insights can be used to tailor more realistic VEs towards a training-oriented simulation with simultaneous and multi-modal stressor presentation. Weiss et al. have previously shown that the image realism plays a lesser role in the invocation of stress, further suggesting that the combination of virtual environment, tasks, and stressor plays a greater role than the quality or detail of these aforementioned factors [ 59 ].

5.3 Cognitive Load

The cognitive load measured by the NASA-TLX in our participants during the experiment was generally low. On further inspection of the sub-scales, we see low Physical Demand and Temporal Demand scores, showing that participants did not require physical strength or a long time to finish the experiment. We interpret the 10-point difference in the Mental Demand sub-scale as the participants’ need to renew their focus on the task after having dealt with the alarm. Although participants were given ample opportunity to become accustomed to the VE and to understand what was expected of them in the first experiment phase (cf. Fig. 5 ), the low level of prior VR knowledge probably increased the mental workload.

The Performance sub-scale reveals a 2.75-fold higher score for "Interruption" compared to "Time Pressure", indicating that participants in the "Interruption" group were considerably more satisfied with their performance on the task. This is also reflected in the high scores for Frustration sub-scale, showing a negative effect. Visual inspection indicated that the "Time Pressure" frustrated participants almost twice as much, although this difference was not significant. We believe that in the case of this stressor, these scales provide a combined picture of the fact that many participants in the "Time Pressure" group were unable to complete the syringe task within the specified time limit. Participants needed about the same amount of effort to complete their tasks for both stressors, as represented in the Effort sub-scale. Further, the Overall Workload is comparably low in both groups. We ascribe this to the low scores in Physical Demand and Temporal Demand . We attribute the high SDs to the predefined way the NASA-TLX is evaluated. Due to participants’ replies in these sub-scales, they are weighted with zero and are therefore assigned a value of zero. Because of the small number of participants in each group, this has a strong impact on the calculations of the SDs, which thus become large. However, this aspect may cancel out with a larger number of participants.

Considering the amount of mistakes made during the phases, we have shown that during the stress phase significantly more errors have been made by the "Interruption" group, both in step order as well as step execution, which is in line with the work by [ 8 ].

When taking the NASA-TLX, the PSQ20, and the objective results into account, we reject hypothesis H 2 . It becomes evident that the multi-modality of the Interruption stressor did not have a statistically significant larger impact on the induced stress, even if it did require a higher mental demand, as shown by NASA-TLX. However, it did increase the amount of mistakes made. Therefore, further work should include more sensory modalities in the overall experience in order to investigate possible influences. For example, while increasing vibration could conceivably be used to portray "Time Pressure" and create a sense of urgency, it would be perceived as negative and might impair performance [ 9 ].

5.4 Limitations

Due to the design of the tasks in this study, and the fact that the VE and VR itself present a cognitive stressor, the results contain artifacts from the VE. It is possible that interaction with the VE leads to higher cognitive load during task performance than in reality. Further, the participants were not informed of the possible consequences of failure in the tasks. This was decided to prevent the subjects from feeling responsible to the patient, which could have lead to another source of stress. Objective measures are not capable of differentiating sources of stress, which is why we employed subjective stress measures as well.

Although not measured explicitly, we do not have reason to believe that simulator sickness had an effect, as the VR system was calibrated for room tracking and ran at full frame rate. Further, by disabling vection participants had to rely on full physical motion, and no participant complained about any VR-related effects on their well-being. However, to ensure the validity of the overall results during our next studies, we will employ adequate measuring tools for simulator sickness.

Our representations of stressors and tasks were implemented based on user research in cooperation with a local clinic, in the form of literature research and a focus group. The selected replicas have been deemed two of the most often occurring, and they can be represented in VR with reasonable effort. We are aware that processes and ways to complete tasks are manifold and specific from clinic to clinic, but have chosen to consider our participant pool from the local hospital.

As stress is a multi-faceted and complex concept, it is of utmost importance in stress research to ensure that what is induced and measured is indeed stress [ 2 ]. Our experiment procedure did not include any variations in timings to ensure comparability, but missing experience in some participants required experimenters to step in and help. This broke immersion and shows a high degree of mental workload that our measuring hardware could have picked up on.

Lastly, due to the small sample size these results should not be taken as is. The distance and hygiene rules that came into force as a result of COVID-19 made it generally difficult to recruit participants. Considering the especially strong regulations for nurses, we were happy to welcome 18 members of this profession as participants in our study. In our follow up experiments we hope to be able to recruit a greater number of nurses to ensure generalizability and increase informative value.

6 CONCLUSION AND OUTLOOK

In this study, we evaluated the stress-inducing qualities of virtual replications of ICU-related stressors. For this purpose we present a highly presence-inducing virtual ICU with nursing tasks for stress induction training in the nursing profession. Our experiment delivers a first investigation into virtualized versions of two common stressors: "Interruption" and "Time Pressure". These were chosen in close coordination with teaching staff by means of a focus group at a local nursing school, and are further based on a literature analysis, as well as a shadowing session in the ICU of a local hospital. Measures show statistically significant increases (HR, RF, Likert Item) between the baseline and the stressful experimental condition. The remaining measures indicate increases, albeit not significant. We demonstrate successful stress induction through both applied stressors.

Our results show that stress can be inoculated in VR. In future research, we will compare the effect of the virtual stressors to the effect of stressors in real world conditions (i.e. in a skills lab). In doing so, we aim to prevent virtual reality-induced symptoms and effects as a potential impact [ 41 ]. Furthermore, we will develop a concept to enable teaching staff to control and adjust the VR software remotely from a PC or handheld device, allowing for more diverse trainings, adjusted to the trainee.

Taking into account the very valuable suggestions on the diversity of nursing tasks and stressors, we envision this software as a supplement to the usual role playing games and skills labs, which are not available to every school or every student. When used in conjunction with SIT, this tool has the capacity to augment the status quo of role playing style training with greater realism, variety, and repeatability of safety-critical situations to help nursing students enter the profession and be more prepared for the job. The low financial effort enables every school and student to use the technology in their curriculum. Other possible situations were proposed and will be investigated in future studies.
