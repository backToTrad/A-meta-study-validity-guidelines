Public stigma on the Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) affects the physical and psychological wellbeing of those living with the condition in a severe way. There is work around the design of technology for medication adherence and HIV treatment. Yet, there is still a lack of empirical research that investigates how people could cope with stigma more effectively using technology. Thus, we obtained data from co-design workshops conducted remotely from the U.S. with 25 people living with HIV. Our findings foreground key needs and values via the discussion of features and functionality of speculative co-designed technologies that would allow people to leverage key stigma coping strategies. Based on these insights, we forward design implications for social media, which is the most common type of technology that people living with HIV currently use to cope with public stigma.

1 INTRODUCTION

People living with the Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) experience high levels of stigma in our society [ 14 ]. In the U.S., eight out of 10 people living with HIV report HIV-related stigma impacts their lives negatively [ 4 ]. Stigmatizing perceptions around HIV can originate from the individual themselves (internalized stigma), or they can come from others (public stigma) in the form of discrediting sentiments, attitudes, or enactments of discrimination or violence directed at stigmatized individuals. In this paper, we focus on public HIV-related stigma as this type of stigma has been found to affect individuals living with HIV more severely [ 18 ]. In particular, enactments of public HIV-related stigma such as discrimination, social rejection, and physical or verbal violence can have a detrimental impact on the physical and psychological well-being of those living with HIV [ 18 , 44 ]. Furthermore, public HIV-related stigma can hinder access to proper medical care and social support as stigmatized individuals prefer not to disclose their HIV status to others [ 53 , 55 ].

Researchers in the social sciences (e.g., Communication) have already found that people living with HIV use online platforms like forums and blogs to disclose and exchange social support with others because of the perceived anonymity and privacy that such online environments can provide [ 43 , 54 , 57 ]. Disclosure of HIV status and access to social support via the use of online platforms have been found to be key coping strategies to address and ameliorate the negative impact of public stigma [ 19 , 47 , 54 , 57 , 58 ]. In contrast, most Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) research on HIV has focused on the design of technology for treatment management and medication adherence [ 61 ]. Scant research has gone beyond the scope of medication adherence and treatment to cover other challenges related to privacy (e.g., [ 45 ]), access to social support (e.g., [ 51 ]), and HIV-related stigma (e.g., [ 33 ]).

Notably, Maestre et al. investigated how people living with HIV are using current social media to cope with public HIV-related stigma [ 44 ]. The researchers found that people living with HIV adapt social media to cope with public HIV-related stigma. Yet, social media can also replicate – and in some cases even exacerbate – tensions that people living with HIV experience at the moment of coping with HIV-related stigma via disclosing their HIV status or accessing social support. These tensions intersect with concerns related to trust (e.g., who is reading my posts/comments? ), privacy (e.g., could someone take a screenshot of my disclosures and share it with others? ), as well as data safety (e.g., is my disclosed data encrypted? ) and interpersonal safety (e.g., am I safe from physical or verbal violence after I disclose? ). The tensions around disclosure keep people from disclosing their HIV status to others due to fears of potential enactments of public stigma like violence or social rejection [ 44 ]. The tensions around social support relate to the lack of successful ways to match people living with HIV with others who share similar traits or experiences besides an HIV positive status [ 44 ]. Consequently, researchers suggest that social media ought to take into account the tensions around disclosure and social support exchange so it can become an effective tool to help people cope with public HIV-related stigma [ 44 ].

Continuing with this line of research, we forward the research question "How can we re-design social media technology to aid coping strategies for public HIV-related stigma at the individual level?" (RQ1). Particularly, and based on previous work on the use of social media to cope with public HIV-related stigma, we also ask "How can we re-design social media technology to help address HIV disclosure (RQ2) and social support (RQ3) tensions?". To address these research questions, we conducted a speculative co-design study with people living with HIV to explore the design of futuristic technology concepts that could help people living with HIV cope with public HIV-related stigma. We discuss the participants’ values and needs embedded in the features and functionality of their co-designed technologies to inform design implications for social media, which is the type of technology that people living with HIV currently use the most to cope with public HIV-related stigma [ 44 , 65 ].

The contributions of this paper are several. First, it extends and provides design recommendations for social media to help people cope with public HIV-related stigma more directly and effectively. Second, it expands and reflects on the use of novel methodologies to conduct participatory design remotely with a highly stigmatized population. And third, it suggests research directions that could be beneficial for other stigmatized groups, or for those who need to disclose sensitive information in a technology-mediated way.

2 LITERATURE REVIEW

2.1 HIV-related Stigma

Stigma is a social construct built upon the negative perceptions that discredit or devalue individuals based on personal traits or conditions that differ from what is considered to be normal or moral in a social group. For example, individuals could be stigmatized due to their sexual orientation, disability status, occupation, ethnicity, or certain health conditions(e.g., urinary incontinence) [ 23 ]. Stigmatizing views often originate from people who do not share the stigmatizing condition or trait. This is the type of stigma commonly known as public or societal stigma [ 18 ]. There is also internalized or self-stigma which constitutes negative perceptions that individuals may hold towards their stigmatizing traits and that are not necessarily shared by others in society [ 18 ].

Living with the Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) is still a highly stigmatized health condition. In the U.S., there are approximately 1.2 million individuals living with HIV [ 27 ]. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the majority of people living with HIV (8 out of 10) report that HIV-related stigma impacts their lives in a negative way [ 13 ]. Public HIV-related stigma, or the stigma that is enacted or generated by the negative perception of others about HIV, has been identified as the main cause for instances of discrimination, physical and psychological violence, identity devaluation, and prejudice towards people living with HIV [ 7 , 18 , 23 , 53 ]. Thus, public HIV-related stigma has been directly linked to an exacerbation of feelings of depression and anxiety in people living with HIV [ 18 ]. In addition, people diagnosed with HIV often defer or avoid access to proper and timely medical care due to fears of potential enactments of public HIV-related stigma [ 55 , 73 ]. In this sense, the effects of this type of stigma can be devastating -more so than internalized stigma-, especially when public stigma is enacted in the forms of social rejection and discrimination [ 18 , 53 ].

Consequently, much of the research on stigma from the social sciences has investigated the coping strategies that allow individuals to ameliorate the negative effects of stigma in their lives. In particular, Meisenbach developed a theory of stigma-coping strategies used by people living with HIV and other stigmatized populations [ 46 ]. Meisenbach's coping strategies relevant to HIV-related stigma can be reactive or proactive. Reactive strategies are used when individuals react to public stigma: disclosure, non-disclosure or concealment of HIV status, and bonding with others who live with HIV. Proactive strategies allow individuals to challenge public stigma by reframing or reducing the offensiveness of enactments of public stigma [ 46 , 62 ].

2.2 Technology Design for HIV-related Stigma

Most technological interventions for HIV have been designed and documented by researchers in the medical field. These interventions, often mobile phone-based, have mainly focused on antiretroviral medication adherence [ 44 ]. Beyond the scope of treatment management and medication adherence, researchers from the social sciences have explored how people living with HIV use the Internet to cope with HIV-related stigma (e.g., [ 47 , 54 , 58 ]). The key finding of this line of research is that people living with HIV can indeed improve their psychological and physical health via the exchange of social support in online support groups. For instance, individuals with higher levels of participation (i.e., producing more comments) had better health-related outcomes than those with a lower level of participation [ 47 ]. In addition, the two most frequent types of support exchanged on these platforms between those who seek support (support seekers) and those who provide support (support providers) are informational and emotional support [ 19 ]. Informational support consisted of information about symptoms and treatment as well as guidance for coping. Emotional support consisted of messages that communicated affection, sympathy, understanding, and encouragement. Findings also show that people living with HIV prefer to use online support groups mainly due to public stigma and lack of access to resources in offline settings. These online platforms offer a heightened perception of anonymity and privacy [ 54 ].

HCI research involving the design of technological interventions for people living with HIV is still scarce and has focused on treatment management and medication adherence [ 44 ]. As in the medical field, the majority of technological interventions for HIV have focused on antiretroviral medication adherence via the implementation of systems based on text-based and audio-based alarms and reminders on mobile phones [ 44 , 72 ]. There are, however, examples of more recent HCI research that has explored technology design implications from a broader and more holistic perspective that goes beyond medication adherence and treatment management. For example, Claisse et al. also argue for digital tools that could aid in daily self-care and self-management, moving away from the medication-adherence paradigm [ 17 ]. Singh et al. did a review of mobile apps and found that the majority of these apps included stigmatizing language in their content or had visual cues that could lead to an accidental disclosure of HIV status to others [ 63 ]. Natarajan et al.’s work focused on women living with HIV in India, identified how privacy and disclosure control are aspects that could allow them to maintain existing relationships that could otherwise be affected negatively if accidental disclosure of HIV status took place [ 51 ]. The researchers also highlight the importance of peer-to-peer support that allows people living with HIV to receive advice from others more experienced in navigating social relations and intimacy. Similarly, Kumar et al. suggest designing technologies for people living with HIV that would empower social capital by allowing people to connect and bond with others in order to allow a better exchange of social support to help them face stigma [ 33 ]. Hay et al., found that chat-based applications like WhatsApp can help women living with HIV connect with one another to help cope with stigma via the exchange of peer support [ 26 ].

HCI research has also explored how social media could help people cope with and reduce public HIV-related stigma. For instance, Liu et al. found that the use of web video services like YouTube could help educate others in an effort to reduce public HIV-related stigma via information dissemination on social media platforms like YouTube [ 36 ]. Levy et al. conducted a review of social media dating apps for men who have sex with men (MSM) and analyzed how these platforms could impact HIV-related stigma. They found that the use of visual or explicit “stigma-free” pledges on user profiles let other users know what users are against HIV-related stigma. The researchers argue that the use of such visual aids could reduce public HIV-related stigma in the platform and further normalize the interaction with people living with HIV [ 34 , 44 ]. In particular, Maestre et al. found that people living with HIV adapt social media for purposes of coping with public HIV-related stigma. Yet, the researchers also found that social media can replicate and even exacerbate the tensions that people living with HIV may experience at the moment of disclosing their HIV status to others in order to engage in social support exchange [ 44 ]. For instance, a common concern of people living with HIV using social media is not knowing the real identity or intentions of other users, especially in the case of ’ lurkers ’ who are users who just read posts and comments, but who do not engage or generate their own. In particular, the researchers found that tensions make people not disclose their HIV status to others on social media due to fears of being verbally harassed or being blocked abruptly. The tensions around social support include the lack of successful ways for people living with HIV to be matched with others who share similar traits or experiences besides the same HIV status. Consequently, the researchers suggest that social media technologies ought to take into account the tensions around disclosure as well as facilitate access to effective social support exchange [ 44 ]. In this sense, research on how social media technology could improve coping and reduction strategies against public HIV-related stigma needs to be verified and examined more closely.

3 METHODOLOGY

To advance this line of research and address our RQs, we conducted a co-design study following a funnel model based on the participatory design FACIT PD framework [ 69 ]. Based on this framework, study participants, researchers, and designers can all participate in the co-creation of sketches or prototypes of technology design concepts. Additionally, we used a speculative design approach [ 3 ] for the co-design workshop activities. This approach allows participants not to worry about giving ’correct’ or ’logical’ solutions to a problem. Rather, participants foreground and talk more about nuanced aspects related to their needs and values while imagining or speculating about futuristic technology that could tackle complex social problems [ 6 ]. Moreover, speculative design has been used to allow participants from vulnerable populations to talk about technology design and sensitive topics [ 44 , 49 , 52 ]. Following a Future Workshop structure [ 29 ], the co-design sessions consisted of three stages. The first stage (generative) focused on idea generation, the middle stage (iterative) allowed participants to iterate on design concepts produced in the generative stage; and the last stage (evaluative) focused on the evaluation of a final co-designed technology design concept. Table 1 contains a list and brief descriptions of the activities in each stage. The co-design workshops were deployed online synchronously via private Zoom meetings and asynchronously via a private Facebook group using the Asynchronous Remote Communities (ARC) methodology [ 41 ]. The co-design workshops took place between January and March of 2021.

3.1 Recruitment

Participants were recruited from online support groups on Facebook between the months of September and December of 2020 based on recruitment procedures suggested in [ 42 , 43 , 44 ]. We identified online support groups via a search on the social media platform Facebook using the “HIV support” keywords. Administrators from 18 groups were contacted via private message to ask for authorization to recruit participants for the study. Ultimately, administrators from nine groups allowed us to disseminate a recruitment post that included a link to an online survey. The survey included questions related to the impact of HIV-related stigma, the use of social media to cope with HIV-related stigma, as well as demographic information such as race, sexual orientation, level of education, income, etc. At the end of the survey, respondents were asked if they were interested to be part of a participatory design study which was going to be conducted online on either Zoom or Facebook, depending on their preference. Survey respondents entered a drawing of two USD $ 25 Amazon gift cards. Participants who completed the co-design workshops were given either USD $ 50 Amazon gift cards for U.S. participants, or a USD $ 50 transfer via PayPal for participants living abroad.

3.2 Participants

A total of 59 participants completed and submitted the online survey. 46 respondents indicated that they were interested in being part of the co-design workshops and thus were contacted and sent informed consent information via email individually. Participants could contact the first author by responding to the email or via a phone call if they had any questions regarding the study and the consent process. Ultimately, 25 participants (7 women, 18 men) consented via email to be part of the study. They had different sexual orientations (8 straight, 12 gay, 2 bisexual, 3 non-reported), and races/ethnicity (16 White, 4 Black, 3 Asian, 2 Latino). The majority were from the US (16). There were two participants from the UK and the Philippines, respectively. There was one participant from Indonesia, Uganda, Thailand, and South Africa. Five participants were employed full time, while the rest were either working part-time, unemployed, retired, or receiving disability payments. All participants had at least a high school degree. All participants self-reported having access to Internet and a smartphone for personal use. They all were able to write and speak English fluently and the average age was 49.5 (sd=12.9) years. Participants were assigned randomly to a group in the modality of their preference. There were four groups in total (2 asynchronous groups: GFB1, GFB2; 2 synchronous groups: ZG1, ZG2). 3.3 Procedures

In the first stage, after introductions (A1) 1 , we asked participants to imagine and write about futuristic technology that would exist in a future world where people could cope better with public HIV-related stigma with the aid of technology (A2). The main objective of this stage was to generate initial ideas on futuristic technology individually, that later on could be combined to co-create a concept that would integrate and combine the ideas generated by each member of the group. We did it this way to emphasize the co-generative aspect of the workshops where participants would be encouraged to co-create a technology concept in a democratic and dialogic fashion based on their initial individual ideas. Participants then were asked to use a virtual board ( www.mural.com ) to organize and combine their ideas visually via the use of post-it notes (A3). At the end of this stage, participants discussed and drew a combined technology concept (A4, A5) (see Fig. 1 ).

During the second stage, we showed a sketch of the co-designed technology design concept (see Fig. 1 ). The sketch was created by a team of two UX designers and it was strictly based on the drawings and all data collected during the previous stage such as transcripts, virtual board contents, and feedback received from participants via the debrief survey. Upon showing the visual sketch, participants discussed any aspects of the technology that may have not been captured or represented correctly on the sketch. Participants also discussed any aspects of their co-designed technology concept that needed to be iterated or changed. The what-if scenario activity (A7) included showing a storyboard depicting a potential use of the co-designed technology concept based on the discussion of its use during stage 1 (see Fig. 2 a). Then, we showed to participants a set of five envisioning cards (based on a value-sensitive approach [ 21 , 22 ]) that helped encourage discussion around specific participants’ values (see Fig. 2 b). Each card included a negative tension identified by prior work that people living with HIV experience when using key stigma coping strategies like disclosing an HIV positive status to others or when accessing social support (see Fig. 3 ). After showing each card to the participants, they were encouraged to talk more about the topic and think of ways in which the co-designed technology concept could be further iterated or adapted so that it could potentially help address the tension-generating aspect presented on the card.

During the third and final stage, we showed to participants a sketch of the final co-created technology concept which included the feedback, ideas and changes generated by participants during the second stage. Participants engaged in discussion and provided final feedback on their final co-created technology concept (see appendix B for final sketches and more details of the co-designed technology concepts). At the end of each stage, participants were asked to fill out a debrief survey (A6, A9, A11), which included questions regarding their experiences with the study activities (e.g., what was the activity you enjoyed the most/the least, and why?) as well as questions regarding their preferences and importance on features and technology concepts discussed during the workshop session (e.g., Likert-type questions asking how necessary a feature or functionality of the prototype is from "very necessary" to "very unnecessary", and open-ended questions such as "what are the ideas for technology that you like the most from the session, and why?", "how do you think the co-designed technology could help people cope with public HIV-related stigma?")

3.4 Co-design Modalities

Synchronous Modality. This modality was conducted on Zoom. We chose this video conference platform as it had been used successfully for related remote synchronous research studies [ 2 , 25 , 37 , 38 ]. The study was completed after three consecutive 1 hour and 30 minutes sessions. Each session corresponded to each of the three stages explained above and was scheduled two weeks apart from each other. The two weeks separation between sessions gave researchers and the UX design team enough time to create and prepare the technology design concept sketches for the next session based on the data collected from the previous session. Prior to each session, we provided participants with a link and password to the upcoming Zoom session via email. The duration for each of the activities (avg. of 15min) was determined by researchers based on similar participatory and co-design prior work in face-to-face settings (e.g., [ 8 , 66 , 68 ]) as well as in online settings (e.g., [ 69 ]). Participants completed activities listed in Table 1 by joining a private Zoom meeting and using a virtual board for all activities except for the completion of online debrief surveys.

Asynchronous Modality. This modality was conducted on a secret and private Facebook group. We chose Facebook as this is the social media platform commonly used for research studies conducted asynchronously using the ARC method [ 31 , 35 , 42 , 43 , 56 , 67 ]. During a period of nine weeks (the duration of the study was determined so that the study could adapt and replicate all the activities for the synchronous modality), participants were asked to complete weekly activities listed and described in Table 1 . Participants wrote comments and/or uploaded images under the corresponding weekly activity post. Participants also used the virtual board for activities A3 and A4.

Group Moderation. The first author was the lead moderator in both the asynchronous and synchronous modalities. The second and third authors acted as co-moderators in charge of back channel communication (e.g., if a participant asked anything via chat in the synchronous modality, or via chat or comments via the asynchronous modality). Based on guidelines proposed by [ 40 , 43 ], for the asynchronous modality, it was important that support moderators activate notifications so that any questions or comments generated by a participant were read as soon as possible. In the case of the synchronous groups, support moderators also provided technical support to participants in the cases when participants had problems with connectivity. In the asynchronous modality, we posted the prompt for each activity on Mondays asking participants to complete the activity by the end of the week on Sunday. If a participant had not completed the activities by Friday, a reminder via a private chat was sent to the participant.

3.5 Analysis

Study data consisted of pre-study online survey responses, co-design workshop transcripts, data collected in workshop activities, and data collected via debrief surveys. In the case of the video-mediated sessions, all audio data from recordings was transcribed verbatim. The workshop session transcripts also included all text and images generated from the Zoom and Facebook-mediated groups, which were exported manually (copying and pasting) onto plain separate PDF documents. These documents were then imported to a research analysis software (i.e., Dedoose) for subsequent coding and analysis. All data was de-identified by replacing usernames with participants’ codes. We used descriptive statistics and content analysis [ 32 ] to analyze demographic data from pre-study surveys, and responses to debrief surveys, respectively. In addition, content analysis [80] was used to track changes (i.e., addition, deletion, or update) of features and functionality in technology concepts during each session. We used provisional coding [ 60 ] and inductive thematic analysis [ 10 ] for all session transcripts. The first author completed an initial round of provisional coding using codes developed and adapted from Meisenbach's theoretical framework of coping strategies for public stigma [ 46 ]. Then, based on thematic analysis guidelines suggested by [ 10 ], the first, second, and third authors completed an iterative open-coding analysis of the data. The researchers met several times to identify, revise, and group the codes into emerging categories. Based on the codes applied and memos, the first author produced affinity diagrams [ 39 ] and met with all co-authors to triangulate data as well as to identify, verify, and refine salient themes. During the analysis phase, the first, second, and third authors met remotely via Zoom every week within a span of four months (April to July of 2021) for peer debriefing.

3.6 Self-Disclosure and Ethical Considerations

The first author has been a volunteer at a HIV support center in a city located in the Midwest of the U.S. for several years. He has been actively engaging with this community both in offline and online settings to create rapport and trust with community gatekeepers (e.g., group leaders or administrators) as well with community partners (i.e., social workers and others who advocate against HIV-related stigma) who either belong to the population (i.e., people living with HIV) or are allies. The first author has ample experience conducting research with people living with HIV and other marginalized and vulnerable groups. All recruitment materials, protocols and procedures of this study were revised and iterated based on feedback received from an advisory board composed of these community partners as well as a research committee. All research materials were ultimately reviewed and approved by the IRB department (IRB protocol #2005573319). During informed consent, we explained to participants that they could withdraw completely or skip any study activity without affecting their compensation for being part of the study. We also explained that the completion of the activities would be shared with others in the group. To protect participants’ confidentiality, we highlighted that participants could join the study using a fake Facebook account, using pseudonyms, and/or having the video camera turned off at all times in video-mediated sessions. In addition, all study data was de-identified and video recordings were destroyed after analysis to further protect participants’ confidentiality.

4.1 Considerations for Technology Design to Help People Cope with and Reduce Public HIV-related Stigma

Findings fell under one of the following key categories: HIV status disclosure, education, social support exchange, as well as privacy and safety. In the following paragraphs, we go into more detail regarding each category and discuss the salient themes.

4.1.1 Disclosure of HIV Status should be mediated by technology for a safer face-to-face experience. Participants in all groups envisioned technology that would allow them to avoid direct verbal disclosure in FtF settings. As an analogy, a participant compared their implanted microchip technology concept (See Fig. 4 , Table 2 (B) and Fig. 7 in appendix B) with a pregnancy test kit when a partner discloses their pregnancy status via visual language using a mediator device:

“For a lot of people, visual works better than verbal because people get emotional, they start talking, they start crying, they're not prepared for that rush of emotion that's coming when you're starting to tell somebody something that's really important. So, sometimes if someone can read it first and then they can kind of absorb [the information]. And you're just kind of looking for the facial reaction... It's like with the pregnancy tests! You see one line or two lines to show and tell people that you're pregnant.” – PZ6 (White, straight, woman, ZG1, implanted microchip).

Consequently, all the groups envisioned a technology that would help them mediate the disclosure of HIV status (which could also include other relevant values such as viral load) in order to help them start a difficult conversation with the disclosure recipients and consequently assess if they can seek support from them. For example, someone disclosing their status via a visual symbol sent to an augmented reality (AR) or virtual reality (VR) device, or as a text to a smartphone app. Participants mentioned that direct verbal disclosure of HIV status is often a difficult thing to do due to the possibility of emotional turmoil and fears of potential enactments of public HIV-related stigma. For instance, PZ1 mentioned how difficult it is for her to disclose their HIV status in a direct verbal manner in an FtF setting:

“It was just like coming up to the edge of a cliff. Having to say the words out loud to somebody. And it got to the point where I refused to say it to somebody face to face, verbally.” – PZ1 (Latina, straight, woman, ZG2, AR eyeglasses).

Participants in group ZG2 pointed out that their health passport technology (See Table 2 (D) and Fig. 9 in appendix B) could help avoid accidental disclosure at receptionist desks in hospitals. In such settings, people living with HIV are often forced to disclose their status several times to different people for their appointments. Instead, with the use of technologies like their health passport, disclosure of HIV status would occur automatically -and non-verbally- only when strictly needed and to the people to whom such disclosure matters (e.g., an infectious disease doctor or a dentist).

In addition, participants expressed the need for technology to provide the discloser with a proof of disclosure , or a disclosure contract that could protect them from possible legal ramifications or repercussions of non-dislosure in certain cases such as prior to engaging in a sexual act with another person. For instance, PZ6’s quote below highlight that disclosure of HIV status prior to a sexual encounter could be a difficult thing to prove and that a proof of disclosure could help in those situations:

“Regarding the law, someone using [non-disclosure] against you, is really tough. You really have to work hard to prove it if they're trying to slander you! That's tough to prosecute, so if you have a proof that you've shown somebody that you have [HIV], there's no way they can ever come back to you.” – PZ6 (White, straight, woman, ZG1, implanted microchip).

In sum, participants did not want to disclose directly and verbally to recipients in order to minimize enactments of public HIV-related stigma. Instead, they envisioned and co-designed technology that could mediate such disclosure in co-located contexts where automation as well as a proof of such disclosure could be obtained.

4.1.2 Technology should disseminate educational material to improve coping as well as to reduce stigma. Participants highlighted the importance of education and awareness on HIV in the future as an effective way to reduce public HIV-related stigma in a social group and thus makes it easier for people living with HIV to cope. For instance, PFB12 stressed that education is an important aspect to consider in order to fight against HIV-related stigma: “HIV education is a must to defeat HIV stigma. Folks need to be educated from the very beginning.” – PFB12 (Black, gay, man, GFB2, VR eyeglasses). Thus, across all groups, participants included features and functionality in their technology concepts that would allow the dissemination of educational material for both the general population and people living with HIV. On the one hand, the co-designed technology would provide people living with HIV with educational material comprised of information on how to cope with public HIV-related stigma based primarily on lived experiences of other people living with HIV. Across all groups, information on how to react to social rejection and information on when and how to disclose one's HIV status to others (taking into account current HIV status disclosure laws) were considered as key skills that every person living with HIV should learn. For instance, PFB1 suggested that their Meet-a-Peer app technology (See Table 2 (E) and Fig. 10 in appendix B) could advise those wishing to disclose their status through the technology before disclosure takes please: “The app could provide information on when and why one must disclose their status. In other words, it could help them pick and choose their battles.” – PFB1 (White, straight, man, GFB1, Meet-a-Peer app). This is especially the case for someone with a recent diagnosis, who could receive this type of informational support in video based format uploaded by peers who share similar lived experiences. For instance, ZG2’s VR eyeglasses technology (See Fig. 5 b, Table 2 (F) and Fig. 11 in appendix B) could provide role playing and video-based educational material:

“Our technology would have an educational/informative feature as well that would provide video clips of different scenarios giving examples of HIV stigma and show how to navigate those situations.” - PFB12 (Black, gay, man, GFB2, VR eyeglasses).

On the other hand, the co-designed technology would provide the general population with basic information on HIV (e.g., terminology, information about HIV transmission and HIV prevention). Educational information could also include positive or role-model examples of people living with HIV having relationships with others who do not live with HIV in order to minimize their fears of accidental transmission. Participants in three groups discussed features for their futuristic devices (AR eyeglasses, InfoBot, VR eyeglasses) that would allow people to step in the shoes of someone living with HIV via the use of interactive functionality, like role playing, embedded in their technologies. This interactive functionality would further allow people “to understand and share the feelings of another, without having to have necessarily gone through this experience themselves. To see and feel another through their eyes.” - PFB16 (White, pansexual, woman, GFB2, VR eyeglasses). In fact, a common functionality in the technology concepts was the delivery of educational material upon disclosure of HIV status. This material could be accessed by the disclosure recipient right away after disclosure or it could be accessed at a later point whenever the recipient would be ready. Participants also mentioned that the educational material would be based on information produced and published by reliable sources such as the CDC or the World Health Organization (WHO) in order to avoid the spreading of false information.

In sum, participants envisioned technologies that could disseminate educational material to help minimize the negative impact of public HIV-related stigma.

4.1.3 Technology should mediated peer matching for a better social support exchange experience. Participants in all groups generated technology that could connect people living with HIV with healthcare providers like social workers and professional counselors either in person or via video in order to promote mental health and provide advice in navigating and coping with HIV-related stigma. For instance, PFB3 stressed the need for 24/7 access to trained counselors who could provide mental health services to those living with HIV:

“The technology would provide ample access to counselors and psychiatric teams, either in person, or there is always a face at the end of a phone, not just a faceless voice, but a video conference with a trained professional.” - PFB3 (White, gay, man, GFB1, Meet-a-Peer app).

More importantly, across all groups, the technology concepts included the functionality of connecting people living with HIV with others living with HIV for peer support exchange. Peers would engage in emotional (e.g., talking about feelings) and informational support (e.g., advice, information on treatment and medication). In particular, participants noted that peers would teach them how to cope with HIV-related stigma based on similar lived experiences (e.g., “They could learn from each other about how stigma has affected their lives and the ways they have been able to get over it.” – PFB5 (White, straight, woman, GFB1, Meet-a-Peer app; or, “An HIV positive person could create a profile and be able to meet new people that might have similar or shared experiences and get tips on how to cope with HIV-related stigma.” - PFB12 (Black, gay, man, GFB2, VR eyeglasses)). to facilitate the matching of peers, participants suggested that users of their technologies would be able to filter peers by certain parameters, like location, so they can have subsequent FtF interactions. They also suggested adding filters based on time of diagnosis, and personal traits and interests such as gender, sexual orientation, age, and hobbies. For instance, PZ2 explained that when using the AR eyeglasses technology, people living with HIV could find others who share similar interests in co-located contexts:

“A feature to where it shows interests and things that are in common. So, if you have two people that have their glasses activated, I guess you would say, if you're both interested in baseball or theater or whatever it could be. It'll kind of highlight those shared interests. So, then, you build rapport that would help with support [exchange].” – PZ2 (White, gay, man, ZG2, AR eyeglasses).

Finally, participants in all groups also indicated that users of their technologies would need to be vetted and trained. Participants suggested that users ought to be checked by moderators or administrators, especially in the case of individuals who serve as support providers (e.g., “There needs to be some sort of training/vetting of the support providers, so they do not take advantage of people who could be in a very vulnerable position.” – PFB3 (White, gay, man, GFB1, Meet-a-Peer app)).

In sum, participants envisioned technologies that could help people living with HIV be matched with vetted counselors and support providers as well as with others living with HIV that share similar lived experiences. An initial technology-mediated matching could potentially lead to subsequent FtF interactions between support seekers and support providers.

4.1.4 Technology should foster privacy and safety for individuals and their data. Participants in all groups noted that their co-designed technologies would protect the privacy and confidentiality of HIV status disclosures at all times. Participants talked about two ways in which their technologies could achieve this: via normalization and via concealment. Via normalization, technology - in particular novel or new technology - would need to be used widely by the general population for other health conditions not directly related or linked with HIV in order to avoid accidental disclosure:

“If it's something that only HIV people have it, then it's almost like, it would increase stigma instead of reducing it, but if it's something that everybody uses it for all kinds of reasons, then I'm okay with it.” – PZ7 (Latino, bisexual, man, ZG1, InfoBot).

Via concealment, technology would need to be discrete enough or completely hidden/concealed from the sight or awareness of others (e.g., “I think an implanted chip under the skin would be more discreet and confidential.” – PFB16 (White, pansexual, woman, GFB2, VR eyeglasses); or, “It could use like some type of invisible ink so you don't have this...yeah, rather than a visible QR code that someone's, like, tempted to scan on you that you might not want.” - PZ11 (White, straight, woman, ZG1, implanted microchip); or, “The robot would create a shield like a cabin around the user so that no one else would be able to see what went on in the booth.” – PZ7 (Latino, bisexual, man, ZG1, infoBot)) (see Fig. 5 a). Finally, participants also discussed the option of their technologies being embedded in more ubiquitous and normalized technology like a smartphone or a smartwatch. In any case, as denoted by PZ2’s quote below, the technology would not be associated or linked with HIV in any way. Otherwise, the technology could end up stigmatizing the individuals using it even more:

“[The technology] has to be normalized. If it's like a smartphone, where everybody has one, the presence of the eyeglasses is not going to be outside the norm. It'd not be linked with a certain condition. Kind of like a smart watch is now. They're pretty ubiquitous.” – PZ2 (White, gay, man, ZG2, AR eyeglasses).

Regarding safety, participants in all groups highlighted the importance of their technologies protecting the electronic data to avoid breaches in privacy and confidentiality. In particular, participants expressed that their technologies would provide more data safety by providing stronger data encryption, data access control (e.g., use of pin numbers of fingerprint scanning), as well as greater control over privacy settings like location tracking (e.g., “...medical records are becoming electronic anyway. Our technology could set up a strong encryption so that only authorized people can access it.” – PZ5 (White, gay, man, ZG2, health passport); or, “Our app would have to protect the data for privacy and confidentiality reasons in order to avoid accidental disclosure.” - PFB2 (Asian, gay, man, GFB1, Meet-a-Peer app); or, “GPS location would have to require consent and control from the user in our technology.” – PFB1 (White, straight, man, GFB1, Meet-a-Peer app). Interestingly, participants in two groups ultimately decided to remove the HIV status disclosure field from the user profile screens in the meet-a-peer app (FG1) and in the VR eyeglasses (FG2) for disclosure recipients due to data permanency concerns. Instead, the disclosure of HIV status to others would be done more privately in order to not allow other taking ’screenshots’ of their disclosures or accidental disclosure if such information would remain on a platform permanently.

Participants in group ZG2 also noted that their eyeglasses technology could provide support to users in the form of tangible support, in case of emergencies where the physical safety of the person living with HIV may be jeopardized. Participants explained that their eyeglass technology could have a feature that would function like a panic button that they could activate so that emergency services (i.e., 911) could be requested in a concealed way. For example, participants in group ZG2 suggested that their technology could detect if it had been knocked off violently from the user:

“If the eyeglasses are knocked off, the technology would automatically dial 911 and it would use the GPS coordinates; because, if we're talking a situation where a physical attack is possible, you might want to have the device automatically detecting that.” – PZ5 (White, gay, man, ZG2, AR eyeglasses).

Finally, the language used in participants’ technologies would be constantly monitored automatically for offensive and stigmatizing words or expressions such as “ I'm clean ” referring to someone not living with HIV which could be quite a harmful and stigmatizing expression. In addition, there would be a heightened control over the spread of misinformation (e.g., denialists of the existence of HIV, fake cures, etc.): “Technology should put more effort into screening out bogus groups, such as the denialists who claim HIV isn't real, is harmless, or that the medications used to treat it are more harmful than the virus itself.” – PZ1 (Latina, straight, woman, ZG2, AR eyeglasses). In this sense, participants suggested that certain keywords could be tagged and brought to the attention of system administrators: “Admins could add keywords like ‘cure’, ‘faith healers’, etc. that will automatically let admins know in advance. They would be able then to block them.” – PFB5 (White, straight, woman, GFB1, Meet-a-Peer app).

In sum, participants envisioned their technologies to be used not only by people living with HIV, or their visual design linked directly to HIV. Instead, their technologies were embedded or integrated in already ubiquitous technology in order to help assure privacy and confidentiality. Participants also stressed the importance of data safety as well as interpersonal safety while using their co-designed technologies. In particular, tangible support in cases of emergency as well as moderation of stigmatizing language employed by users of their technologies would be important functionalities to integrate.

4.2 Summary of Findings

The co-designed technologies covered aspects that were common and salient across all groups of participants. These aspects were related to disclosure of HIV status, education, social support exchange, privacy as well as data and interpersonal safety. Salient themes shed light on values and needs regarding technology design that could leverage stigma coping and reduction strategies. In this sense, technology meant to aid with public HIV-related stigma ought to 1) mediate disclosure of HIV status, 2) provide interactive educational material for both people living with HIV and the general population, 3) mediate and help assure effective peer support, 4) provide heightened control over disclosure of HIV status information in order to assure confidentiality and privacy, and 5) promote data and interpersonal safety by employing strong encryption for data as well as providing linkage to services that could be used in cases of emergency where physical safety may be jeopardized. 5 DISCUSSION

In this section, we discuss how participants’ key needs and values -embedded in their futuristic co-designed technologies- could be transferred to current existing social media technology, which is the most common type of technology that people living with HIV use to cope with public HIV-related stigma [ 44 , 65 ]. We end this section with reflections on the co-design methodology used, limitations of this study, ethical implications, as well as directions for future research.

5.1 Design Implications for Disclosure of HIV status on Social Media

To answer RQ1 (How can we re-design social media technology to aid coping strategies for public HIV-related stigma at the individual level?), and RQ2 ("How can we re-design social media technology to help address tensions around disclosure?), we suggest designing technologies that 1) mediate disclosure of HIV status in a co-located context where both disclosers and disclosure recipients could engage in a subsequent FtF interaction after disclosure, and 2) avoid accidental disclosure of HIV status via assuring privacy, confidentiality, and safety. In the following paragraphs we explain with more detail these points.

Social media ought to mediate HIV status disclosure by reducing uncertainty between the discloser and disclosure recipient. Our findings show that participants worry about potential enactments of public HIV-related stigma like social rejection or discrimination during or after their disclosures. Researchers in HCI have also reported these tensions around disclosure on social media. Maestre et al. found that social support activation via anonymous disclosure on these platforms may be rather limited if follow-up FtF interactions between support seekers and support providers does not take place [ 44 ]. In this sense, the researchers suggest that using technologies to disclose could be a first step in helping people disclose to access social support, but that it might be necessary to also facilitate a subsequent FtF interaction between support seekers and support providers in order to increase the benefits of the social support exchange [ 44 ]. Aligned with these findings, participants in our study also expressed via their co-designed concepts the need to have a technology-mediated disclosure that could lead to a subsequent FtF interaction.

Transferring these requirements to current social media could mean that there would need to be a way for users to reduce uncertainty regarding disclosure recipients to facilitate a subsequent FtF interaction. For instance, as suggested in [ 44 ], social media could integrate tools such as a browser extension developed by [ 28 ] that could add visual signifiers to users’ profiles of those who are in a close geographical location. Such visual signifiers could be based on the group members’ prior record of participation in the group and whether a user has posted misinforming or toxic content. These types of visual signifiers could help users feel more comfortable in determining who it might be safer to disclose their HIV status to. We also suggest that potential disclosure recipients could be shown or listed based on geographical proximity so that social media could facilitate a potential FtF encounter.

5.2 Design Implications for Privacy and Safety on Social Media

To further answer our RQs, we forward design implications for social media based on the aspects related to privacy, confidentiality and safety captured and emphasized in the co-designed technology concepts. Participants co-designed technology that would be used by the general population (i.e., a normalized technology). In addition, their technology would assure both data and interpersonal safety at all times.

Social media ought to avoid accidental disclosure of HIV status by minimizing data permanency. Not disclosing an HIV status or concealing an HIV status from others is another common strategy used to cope with HIV-related stigma, as it would protect people living with HIV from potential enactments of public stigma [ 1 , 46 , 62 ]. The functionality and features of the co-designed technology concepts also echoed this and addressed participants’ values and needs for privacy and confidentiality. Participants in all groups stressed the need that if their technology was to be used in public, it would not have to be associated to HIV. In addition, the technology would allow stricter control of their data so that it does not remain in the system (data permanency). Social media is a technology used by the general population, so it is already normalized. However, data permanency on social media is still a key challenge that needs to be addressed  [ 5 ]. Text-based disclosures on social media could be seen by other users at different times and users may even take screenshots of such disclosures [ 12 , 44 ]. In fact, Bussone et al. found that 80% of their study participants would prefer to use traditional paper to track their health information in order to maximize privacy and confidentiality. Similarly, the researchers also found that more control over the sharing of sensitive data as well as strong data safety is needed by people living with HIV when using online technology for personal health management purposes [ 12 ].

Consequently, social media could detect disclosure of HIV status automatically and suggest users make such disclosures via private messages. Social media could also offer the option to users to make posts or comments temporary if or when they contain sensitive disclosures. Such posts or comments could also be deleted or hidden automatically after a certain period of time. We also suggest avoiding any feature or affordances on social media that capture HIV status disclosure such as a HIV status disclosure text field, which are often used on dating social media platforms. Related work has already reported problems with the use of such HIV disclosure fields in user profiles for HIV status disclosure on sex social media apps like Grindr [ 70 , 71 , 72 ]. For instance, researchers found that the phenomenon of privacy unraveling occurs amongst users of these platforms [ 70 ]. That is, the users of these platforms may assume an HIV status depending on how others use and populate an HIV status disclosure field. For example, a user not using the HIV status disclosure field at all may be perceived as someone hiding or concealing a positive status and then be the target of potential enactments of stigma (e.g., users ignoring or blocking other users).

Social media ought to monitor stigmatizing language and behavior. Another common aspect from the co-designed concepts was the monitoring and moderation of behavior and language employed by users of the co-designed technologies. The monitoring process could certainly be achieved in an automated fashion. Work in HCI has already been investigating the effectiveness of automated recognition of offensive behavior and language on social media platforms (e.g., [ 16 ]). Monitoring and moderating strategies have been used in social media to detect offensive posts and content, this has helped to flag users who may be harassing and offending others [ 16 ]. Social media platforms like Facebook, Twitter and Instagram implement algorithms (or people who act as moderators monitoring the platform) that could flag potentially harmful content in images and text [ 48 ]. Accordingly, social media technology could implement sophisticated ways to monitor and flag stigmatizing behavior more effectively. One way to do this could be by detecting the use of stigmatizing language in user interactions (e.g., “ are you clean? ”) as well as on user profiles (e.g., “ DDF-UB2 (disease & drug free, you be too) ”). In addition, participants highlighted the need for tangible support that could be available from peers or emergency help providers in order to protect them from possible enactments of public HIV-related stigma after disclosure (e.g., a panic button for physical violence, or a button to help locate near safe spaces). These kinds of features and functionalities could be added to social media platforms as well. Finally, social media (especially in dating or sexual contexts) could provide a proof of disclosure , which could protect disclosers from legal ramifications of alleged non-disclosure of HIV status.

5.3 Design Implications for Social Support Exchange on Social Media

Responding to RQ.3 (How can we re-design social media technology that addresses tensions around social support?), we suggest designing social media that mediates peer-to-peer support exchange with proper filtering and matching functionalities.

Social media ought to mediate peer-to-peer support exchange with advanced filtering and matching functionality. Linkage to peer social support and mediation of support seeking were desired functionalities in the co-designed technology concepts. Desired features would allow users to specify the type of support needed and receive recommendations and linkage to support accordingly. Furthermore, the co-designed technology concepts highlighted the need for a functionality that would allow users to filter potential peer support providers based on shared lived experiences around HIV as well as certain personality traits. In particular, the geographical location of support providers was a key filtering aspect due to the importance of potential subsequent FtF interactions between support seekers and support providers. Furthermore, having access to more detailed personal details about a peer may promote trust between support seekers and support providers, which may in turn make the sharing of experiences more relevant and reciprocal [ 12 ]. Prior research on peer support has found that people living with HIV cannot always match with people that they can relate to as the only common personal trait is that they are living with HIV [ 44 ]. Network closure theories suggest that an intermediary entity could connect people that otherwise remain apart or disconnected from one another [ 11 ]. Consequently, peer matching algorithms on social media could match peers based on personality traits, shared lived experience, location, etc. Additionally, filtering and matching of support peers could be assisted, as suggested earlier, with visual signifiers in user profiles that could indicate support seekers who are available to provide support at any given time. These visual signifiers could also include the rating of the support provider based on other users’ reviews or ratings.

5.4 Design Implications for Education to Cope With and Reduce Public HIV-related Stigma on Social Media

Social media ought to disseminate educational material prior to and right after disclosure has taken place. Based on the importance of educating people about HIV as a key public stigma reduction strategy as well as the use of interactive educational materials to reduce public HIV-related stigma [ 64 ], we suggest that social media intensifies the dissemination of educational material that would help normalize HIV on the platform (e.g., educational ads or educational campaigns). In addition, social media could provide educational material prior to and upon HIV status disclosure to both disclosers (e.g., information on when and how to disclose from role models living with HIV) and to disclosure recipients (e.g., information on how HIV is transmitted, and information on how to be more supportive). In particular, the educational material would highlight scientific advances in infection prevention such as the use of Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP) [ 24 ] and the fact that if someone living with HIV has undetectable levels of virus in their body they can no longer transmit the virus to others (U=U) [ 59 ]. The dissemination of this type of information has already proven to be effective in reducing public HIV-related stigma [ 24 , 59 ] and it could be provided in social media in an automatic fashion when disclosures or conversations about HIV are taking place, akin to the way informational windows pop up when users type words or interact with content related to the Covid-19 virus.

Additionally, research on public HIV-related stigma has already reported that stigma can be reduced if, in addition to being exposed to HIV educational material, individuals also have more direct contact with someone living with HIV that would allow them to experience firsthand their experiences with living with HIV and having to deal with HIV-related stigma [ 15 ]. Thus, subsequent FtF interactions after disclosure, as explained earlier, could also help to further educate, normalize and humanize HIV. Ultimately, disclosure recipients would be the ones who would have to make a decision to review and engage with the educational material and/or reach out to disclosers for further discussion.

Table 4 summarizes design implications for social media discussed above and that could address tensions around the use of key coping strategies against public HIV-related stigma. We argue that the use of social media could indeed be improved to help people living with HIV cope with the effects of public HIV-related stigma more effectively. Tensions around disclosure and peer support could be addressed by allowing more control over the disclosure process and by increasing the levels of trust in disclosure recipients. In addition, social media could also help leverage coping strategies against public HIV-related stigma and minimize tensions by the provision and dissemination of educational material and by addressing issues related with privacy as well as data and interpersonal safety.

5.5 Reflections on the Methodological Approach Used

As previously reported in [ 44 , 49 , 52 ], we also found in our study that the use of a speculative design approach allowed participants from a vulnerable and stigmatized population to discuss sensitive topics in a technology co-design process. It helped participants feel more comfortable with their technology literacy as they were not required to talk about a specific technology or worry about the feasibility or ’logic’ behind their ideas. Our methodological approach allowed us to capture and identify nuances at the intersection of technology ideation and participants’ values and needs around the use of coping strategies against public HIV-related stigma. We argue that this would have been more difficult to accomplish using more traditional methods like focus groups or interviews. Additionally, participation (i.e., number of comments, turns to speak) and engagement (i.e., completion of activities) were high in both the asynchronous and synchronous modalities. The level of participation fluctuated more in the asynchronous modality in comparison to the synchronous counterpart though. On average, there was an upward trend in engagement throughout the study for the asynchronous modality, with a pronounced increase in participation in stage 2. In contrast, engagement in the synchronous modality remained high and constant across the entire duration of the study with a pronounced increase in the level of participation towards the end of the study in session 3. There were a few participants that were more active than others. We moderated this situation by asking participants who were not participating actively to let us know their thoughts about the activity or to let us know if they had any questions. Another difference was that groups in the synchronous modality worked on two concepts despite being asked to work on just one in the activity prompt. This may have been due to having extra time during the workshop sessions and participants naturally wanting to explore additional options. In the asynchronous modality, groups focused and worked on one co-created concept. Participants in ARC-based studies tend to more strictly follow activity prompts and prefer to work on activities that could be completed in less than an hour per week [ 42 ]. Having participants complete activities for two concepts in this modality may have been too much for participants. Regardless, having both synchronous and asynchronous options allowed us to recruit a bigger and more diverse sample of participants. In fact, participants who preferred to join the study via Facebook would not have been able to do so if Zoom had been the only option to be part of the study.

5.6 Limitations, Ethical Implications, and Future Research

The participant sample was majority white, gay, male, of lower socioeconomic status, over 40 years of age and living in the U.S. Participants recruited from developing countries and those living in rural areas reported experiencing more episodes of public HIV-related stigma in the forms of discrimination, social rejection, and enacted verbal and physical violence. Overall, the type of challenges and the use of coping strategies against HIV-related stigma were consistent across all the participants independent of country of origin, gender, or sexual orientation. Nevertheless, due to the fewer number of non-white participants, we could not see evident differences among different race/ethnicity (apart from those related to country of origin). Prior work has reported that people of color (i.e., Black and Latinx) tend to be more affected by HIV-related stigma which hinders access to proper HIV care and social support [ 50 ]. Thus, more efforts should be made to recruit participants of color in future studies.

We argue that the social media design implications proposed and described in this paper could further help people living with HIV as well as with any other stigmatized condition (e.g., substance use disorder, weight disorder, race, social class). Our implications for social media could also help individuals who may need to disclose sensitive information about themselves in a controlled way and get access to social support. Furthermore, issues related to data permanency, proof of disclosure, and peer support matching would be still relevant to other groups. Nevertheless, ethical implications ought to be further assessed. For instance, more work should be done in regard to the use and storage of sensitive disclosed data on social media platforms. In addition, allowing users to search for others using filters -or in an automated fashion- based on shared lived experiences and personal traits might lead to bias and discrimination. Researchers have already advocated for the avoidance of such filtering mechanisms based on sensitive personality traits such as race and ethnicity or age in online-based services to avoid discrimination [ 34 ]. While people living with HIV would benefit from the filtering of potential peers based on personality traits, could any of these filtering parameters promote discrimination among users? In this sense, we encourage HCI researchers to continue exploring ways in which social media could be enhanced taking into account the design implications discussed in this paper and summarized in Table 4 as well as further explore any ethical implications that may apply.

Another potential avenue of future research could be to investigate the feasibility and ethical implications in the short and long term of the actual futuristic technology concepts co-designed by participants in our study. For instance, research around the use of AR and VR eyeglasses in public settings has already raised ethical concerns regarding the use of co-located devices that could invade people's privacy (e.g., [ 9 , 30 ]). Similarly, the use of robots for social support exchange has received criticism regarding a possible ’dehumanization’ of social support exchange [ 20 ]. Therefore, future research in this direction could not only explore the feasibility of these concepts, but it should also strive to make them safer for people, in particular, those from vulnerable and stigmatized groups.

6 CONCLUSION

Based on a speculative design approach with people living with HIV, we have proposed and discussed technology design implications for social media platforms. Researchers and practitioners could further explore these implications to help stigmatized and vulnerable individuals cope with public stigma more directly and effectively. Ultimately, we argue that all technologies for stigmatized conditions, whether they are meant for treatment or not, should empower individuals to cope with public stigma by facilitating a safer disclosure and getting access to more effective peer matching mechanisms. Additionally, technology ought to allow individuals to proactively reduce public stigma via advocacy and education that could challenge stereotypes and promote awareness and normalization of a stigmatized condition.

7.2.1 Zoom Groups. Zoom group 1 (ZG1) co-created a supportive Infobot and an implanted microchip.

InfoBot. In the future, robots wander around the city. The robots have a front panel that sports a touchpad panel which allows people to select from a set of different services related to HIV and sexual health (Fig. 6 ). Services also cover resources for other chronic conditions. For HIV, the main services are information on HIV, instant HIV tests as well as CD4 and viral load count tests (the robot has a small opening through which people can insert a finger so that the robot can take a blood sample). The robot can also dispense condoms as well as preventive HIV care (i.e., PrEP, PEP). For medication that requires a prescription, 24/7 video-mediated consultation with a trained physician is available. The robots also allow access to peer support near the area by allowing users to search for people (filtered by HIV status and other personal traits) who would be willing to provide social support. In addition to informational support, the robots could also provide emotional support themselves by acting like humans (e.g., giving a hug). They could also create a private space for those who need it by creating a booth-like environment with a stool so that people use the services and receive support privately. Implanted Microchip. People in the future have an implanted microchip under the skin of one of their arms (Fig. 7 ). The microchip serves two purposes: firstly, it stores key medical data of the individual (e.g., blood pressure). In the case of people living with HIV, this data would include viral load and CD4 count measurements. Secondly, the microchip would allow users to disclose HIV status in a mediated way. That is, individuals living with HIV would now have to disclose themselves, but rather the disclosed information would be sent to a smartphone app that would act as a mediator in the disclosure process. This HIV information -or any other medical data info, for that matter- would be sent to a smartphone app with previous consent and authorization from the microchip wearer. The app would scan the chip and retrieve HIV status along with education material on HIV. The purpose of this education material would be to inform the recipients of the disclosure about how HIV gets transmitted and how people can prevent such transmission. 7.2.2 Facebook Groups. Facebook group 1 (GFB1) generated the concept of a smartphone app. Peer-to-Peer Support Smartphone Application (Meet-a-Peer App). The concept of the smartphone application would allow people living with HIV filter and find others that could provide peer support (Fig. 10 ). The main component of the app's interface is a GPS map that displays other users interested in support exchange as well as nearby available resources (social workers, clinics, etc). The people connected and using the app could be either people living with HIV or not. In addition to displaying the location of potential peers, the app would also display other type of resources like HIV clinics, testing sites, educational material on HIV and HIV-related stigma coping strategies, and linkage to trained counselors. The app would also have a user profile section for personal information with an HIV status disclosure field that would provide advice and educational material on HIV status disclosure (e.g., when to disclose and when not to disclose) prior to using and setting this field. Facebook group 2 (GFB2) generated the concept of a Stigma Eradication Virtual Reality (VR) Eyeglasses.

Stigma Eradication Virtual Reality (VR) Eyeglasses. People in the future wear VR headsets that allow to detect harmful or threatening behavior from other people nearby (Fig. 11 ). The VR headset is linked to a smartwatch equipped with a microphone and alarm. Upon detecting stigmatizing language or stigmatizing behavior, the technology sends educational material to the stigmatizer's VR eyeglasses. The main idea of this technology is that the stigmatizers would roleplay scenarios with an avatar that would make stigmatizers realize the harm that they can cause to others with their stigmatizing behavior. In addition, the VR eyeglasses would allow search of peers who are located nearby for peer-to-peer support and provide filtering mechanisms based on HIV status as well as personal information such as age, hobbies, etc. The interface of the VR eyeglasses would also allow access to educational audiovisual material intended for people living with HIV that would show them ways to cope against public HIV-related stigma.
