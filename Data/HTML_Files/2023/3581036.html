Producer Conflict Management Approaches in Online Peer Production Communities – Case Study of OpenStreetMap

Youjin Choe , Department of Infrastructure Engineering, Faculty of Engineering and Information Technology, The University of Melbourne, Australia, ychoe@student.unimelb.edu.au
Martin Tomko , Department of Infrastructure Engineering, Faculty of Engineering and Information Technology, The University of Melbourne, Australia, tomkom@unimelb.edu.au

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581036
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Producers of online peer-production communities (OPPCs) regularly experience conflicts with other producers. A deeper understanding of the producer conflict management approaches employed by community members could help OPPCs improve their conflict management tools. In this paper, we analyze the effectiveness of conflict management approaches employed by producers. Using OpenStreetMap (OSM) as a case study, we manually annotate OSM mappers’ conflict management approaches from 384 online discussion threads contained in one of OSM's public communication channels. We investigate the effectiveness of conflict management by verifying whether producer discussions led to a solution. Our results show that online producer conflict management approaches with empathetic and proactive listening behaviors were more likely to reach a productive solution, whereas those approaches with negative online behaviors were less likely to do so. Based on these results, we reflect on how the user interface of OSM's public online communication channels could better support producers to manage conflict behaviors.

1 INTRODUCTION

Online discussion is the predominant producer interaction method in online peer-production communities (OPPCs), an efficient way of communication for producers who live across the globe. However, online discussions are devoid of observable non-verbal behaviors (e.g., tone of voice, body language, and eye movement) [ 4 ]. In online discussions these non-verbal cues that humans are so receptive to and rely on for nuance in conversation are lost. This loss can lead to misunderstandings and possibly to producer conflict. In OPPCs where producers interact exclusively in writing, producer conflict as well as producer conflict management behaviors manifest themselves through written interactions.

Take an example from the OpenStreetMap (OSM), a so-called Wikipedia of maps. OSM changeset discussion in Figure 1 shows an example of producers discussing changes to the collected data. In a set of comments that address a single change (i.e., a changeset), each sentence has a specific function. A mapper (producer in OSM) may be asking a question for clarification or can be arguing against the actions of another mapper. Sometimes, these changesets may include remarks that are deemed derogatory and unproductive by others. Together, a collection of such sentences is combined into a paragraph that manifests the conflict handling style of the mapper. This style can be dominating style, i.e., one that pushes one's argument, or conversely, an integrating style that takes a problem-solving approach to conflict management.

Depending on how producers interact with each other, conflicts can either de-escalate by finding a compromising solution, or escalate without an agreement [ 46 ]. Applying a theoretical model of conflict management behaviors can therefore inform conflict management processes supporting the effective operation of a group [ 45 , 46 ].

However, such an application remains an underexplored topic. In this paper, we showcase an application of a theoretical model of conflict management behavior and assess the effectiveness of different conflict management behaviors of producers in an OPPC. We aim to derive design refinements enabling to support producer conflict management in the community's communication channel. We set our research questions based on a review of organizational management, human-computer interaction (HCI), and computer-supported cooperative work (CSCW) literature (Section 2 ):

  • RQ1: How do the different conflict handling styles identified in OSM mapper discussions contribute to the effectiveness of conflict management?
  • RQ2: How do the different types of sentences identified in OSM mapper discussions contribute to the effectiveness of conflict management?

We use a dataset capturing the content of the OSM changeset discussion feature (i.e., a public online communication channel for managing producer conflict in online map editing activities) as a case study. Conflict in OSM and OSM's conflict management processes have potential implications on how conflict affects OSM data, and thus on major commercial and community mapping services that use OSM data. 1 It could also be useful in effective conflict management for better mapper retention, which has been a topic of research for other similar OPPCs [ 13 , 51 ].

Our results show that producer discussions in OSM with empathetic, analytic, and forward-looking behaviors are more likely to result in the development of productive solutions, in contrast to impolite and affective behaviors. Based on these findings, OSM and other similar OPPCs can improve the design of their producer conflict management channels to encourage desired producer behaviors and conversely suppress undesired behaviors. This could help maximize positive effects of conflict and minimize its negative effects on producer experience and, therefore, on OPPCs’ product quality, project productivity, and producer retention.

In the next sections, we first briefly review previous studies that established and applied theories on conflict management behaviors in face-to-face and online environments (Section 2 ) and introduce our methodology for collecting and analyzing data on producer conflict management approaches in OSM (Section 3 ). We share our results in Section 4 . In Section 5 , we discuss the design implications of our results on OPPCs’ online interpersonal conflict management user interface (UI).

2 RELATED WORKS

2.1 Conflict and Conflict Management Theories

Conflict , broadly defined as a disagreement between involved parties [ 16 , 39 , 45 ], has been a frequent topic of research. Early conflict theory from organizational management literature views conflict as a negative result of human interaction and argues that it should be eliminated and resolved [ 16 ]. More recent theory treats conflict as an inevitable by-product of human social interactions. The underlying assumption here is that conflict can also have positive effects on groups and lead to the development of a solution [ 45 , 46 ]. Hence, conflict should be managed, rather than eliminated and resolved. This focus on conflict management aims at adjusting the amount of conflict and selecting the most suitable management strategy [ 20 , 46 ].

Theory identifies a four-step loop of conflict management: conflict diagnosis which consists of measuring and analyzing conflict, intervention design , intervention implementation , and intervention evaluation . Many approaches were proposed to measure and analyze conflict and its multi-dimensional characteristics: level of conflict (i.e., within an individual, between individuals from the same group or from different group, between groups) [ 46 ], type of conflict (i.e., task, process, relationship, affective) [ 19 ], complexity of conflict [ 20 , 21 ] and conflict handling styles [ 5 , 45 ].

The HCI and CSCW community has applied conflict diagnostic approaches in a number of case studies: a taxonomy model of types of conflict was applied on Wikipedia to measure and analyze effects of affective (i.e., emotional) conflict [ 35 ] and task conflict [ 47 ]. Other studies expanded on this model to add a new type of conflict – normative conflict – that specifically applies to OPPCs [ 11 , 37 ]. A number of studies partially applied a model of conflict handling styles [ 17 , 54 ] (also, see Section 2.2 ).

Such diversity of applications of the theoretical model reflects the complexity of producer conflict in OPPCs as they grow in size and the stage of their life cycle [ 8 , 24 , 62 ]. When OPPCs commence operations, their identity, as a small group of people sharing the same motivations of populating the product (e.g., quantity of Wikipedia article, open source software), is strong and attracts like-minded members to join the community. Conflict in OPPCs in such early stages is dealt by producers themselves, through mailing lists and by private communication [ 22 ]. This is possible due to the small size and relative ease of building personal connections within the community.

As OPPCs grow in size and impact, their goal changes from growth to a mature phase focused on the assurance of product quality (e.g., quality of Wikipedia articles, OSM data, or open source software) [ 62 ]. Members become increasingly likely to manifest variation in motivations and skills. Discussions become lengthy as consensus solutions become more difficult among producers with diverse backgrounds [ 15 , 17 ]. Discussions are usually mediated by volunteering moderators who have lateral authority within OPPC due to their expertise and long-term project experience [ 4 , 28 ]. In extreme cases of conflict, producers rely on community policies [ 29 , 41 ] and administrative groups (e.g., governance board, arbitration committees) [ 18 , 22 ], which could lead to banning problematic producers [ 48 , 57 ]. In a nutshell, OPPCs experience shift from informal to rather formal conflict interventions, with a need to apply conflict management mechanisms [ 8 ]. In particular, OPPCs could benefit form such mechanisms to (1) systematically diagnose conflict and (2) use outputs of conflict diagnosis to design a more effective conflict management intervention [ 8 ].

Motivated and influenced by these studies, we apply the four steps of conflict management theory, and fully apply a model of conflict handling styles as our approach to conflict diagnosis . We first review different models of conflict handling styles, and select one model as a theoretical framework for measuring and analyzing conflict management approaches used by OSM mappers (Sections 2.2 and 2.3 ). We operationalize this framework through categories of conflict handling styles via manual annotation of online discussions on map edits by OSM mappers (Section 3 ). Our results are intended as inputs for the second step of conflict management theory, intervention design , as discussed in Section 5 .

2.2 Conflict Handling Styles

People react to and manage conflict by diverse behaviors dependent on the group environment [ 46 ]. A conflict handling style is a key component of conflict management: “management of conflict not only refers to maintenance of a moderate amount of conflict but also to handling conflict with appropriate behavioral styles” [ 46 , p.1328].

The dual-dimension scheme is a widely accepted approach to categorize conflict handling styles, categorizing two behavioral dimensions of a person and their intensity (i.e., low and high) [ 5 , 14 , 44 , 46 , 52 , 53 ]. For example, Blake and Mouton use concern for people and concern for production as their two behavioral dimensions [ 5 ]. Similarly, Hall uses concern for relationships and concern for personal goals [ 14 ]. Thomas, and Rahim and Bonoma use concern for others (i.e., cooperativeness) and concern for self (i.e., assertiveness) [ 46 , 52 , 53 ], which is slightly modified as concern for other's outcome and concern for one's own outcome by Pruitt. Except for Pruitt's model, all the others result in five styles of handling conflict, with compromising style as medium level on both behavioral dimensions [ 5 , 14 , 46 , 52 , 53 ].

In this paper, we apply Rahim and Bonoma's model [ 46 ]. Compared to other models that interpret people's intentions or personal values towards the conflict, it is constructed to interpret people's selection of conflict handling styles depending on a situation [ 45 ]. This aspect of the model serves the purpose of this paper to assess the type of conflict handling styles used in OSM and their effectiveness in conflict management during online discussion. It is difficult to tell OSM mappers’ intentions or personal values toward conflict management from their online discussion. However, we can understand the context of the mapper discussion and how they react to different contexts by choosing their conflict handling styles.

Rahim and Bonoma's model consisting of five conflict handling styles: (1) integrating, (2) obliging, (3) dominating, (4) avoiding, and (5) compromising [ 46 ]. Each conflict handling style represents different behaviors of group members that are commonly manifested during in-person conflict situations (See Table 1 ).

Table 1: Definition of conflict handling styles [ 46 ]
Conflict handling styles Concern for self Concern for others Definition
Avoiding Low Low Associated with withdrawal or sidestepping situations
Compromising Medium Medium Both parties give up something to make a mutual decision
Dominating High Low Win-lose orientation or forcing behavior to win one's position
Integrating High High Examination of differences to reach a acceptable solution
Obliging Low High Emphasizing commonalities to satisfy the concern of the others

The integrating style consists of behaviors that identify the problem and suggest a solution that should be mutually acceptable [ 45 , 46 ]. The obliging style , characterized by a low level of concern for self and high level of concern for others, manifests empathy to others’ and a preference to accept others’ solutions [ 45 , 46 ]. In contrast, the dominating style manifests opposite behaviors: a push for one's own solution to manage the conflict at hand, rather than listening to others [ 45 , 46 ]. The avoiding style is manifested when a group member does not participate in the conflict or even blames the others without suggesting a solution [ 45 , 46 ]. Finally, the compromising style consists of behaviors that suggest a solution that blends the desires and needs of the participants of the conflict [ 45 , 46 ].

Only a handful of studies from the HCI and linguistics literature applied conflict handling style models to assess online conflict management behaviors in OPPCs. Similar to the aim of this paper, Huang et al. [ 17 ] assessed the effectiveness of two conflict handling styles, problem-solving and smoothing styles from the model of Blake and Mouton [ 5 ] in online discussions on GitHub. They only focused on those two styles, arguing that the remaining styles in Blake and Mouton [ 5 ]’s model (i.e., forcing, withdrawing, compromising) rarely occur in the community studied [ 17 ]. They found that these two conflict handling styles reduce negative effects of conflict (e.g., contributors leaving the project)[ 17 ]. Based on our preliminary observation of online mapper discussions in OSM, we decided to include all five conflict handling styles of Rahim and Bonoma [ 46 ] in our study, as all of them appear to occur, albeit with variable frequency. Whether each style is effective or not is tested through our first research question proposed in Section 1 .

2.3 Dialogic Functions

Another perspective on producer conflict management strategies can be gained by analyzing producers’ written behavior at the sentence level in a task-based dialogue. Such an approach has been based on linguistic theories that categorize a sentence according to its function. For example, Détienne et al. [ 10 ] manually annotated dialogic functions (i.e., a type of writer's action) of each sentence found in a single controversial Wikipedia Talk Page. They investigated the class of act of each sentence as informative, regulative, and argumentative [ 10 ].

In this classification [ 10 ], informative sentences contain information requests and provisions relevant to the topic of discussion. Regulative sentences shepherd the discussion to stay on track or summarize other contributors’ opinions. Argumentative sentences are used to build a critical discussion by either proposing a solution, arguing against or in favour of other members’ opinions, or evaluating a proposed solution. Détienne et al. [ 10 ] found that participants with strong positions dominantly use more argumentative sentences, rather than informative or regulative sentences [ 10 ]. They also identified a sequential development of the discussion, typically starting with sentences that request and provide information to increasingly argumentative sentences (e.g., arguing against/in favor of, suggesting solutions, evaluating solutions) towards the end [ 10 ].

An additional aspect to note here is that of negative written behaviors in producers’ sentences and their effect on conflict management. Multiple research studies on OPPCs report on how negative emotions and actions in online written interactions derail the entire discussion leaving it without a productive result [ 2 , 3 , 11 , 35 , 38 , 55 , 56 ]. Khazraie and Talebzadeh [ 26 ] found that Wikipedia editors who use negative written behaviors (e.g., condescending, dismissive behaviour, ridicule, pointed criticism) received offensive and defensive counter arguments than mitigation, no-opposition, and compromise. However, there is still no study examining which dialogic function is more correlated with effective online conflict management in OPPCs. We address this gap by answering our second research question as suggested in Section 1 .

2.4 Measuring Online Conflict Management Behaviors

Conflict handling styles have been studied using these methods in a wide range of in-person group settings, from hierarchical private companies and government agencies [ 19 , 25 ] to less hierarchical settings, such as university assignment groups [ 7 , 9 ]. These studies use data collection methods that require direct participation from the group members (e.g., self-reported surveys, interviews) and/or a direct observation of group members’ in-person interaction during conflict situations (e.g., observations, journals) [ 7 , 9 , 19 , 25 ]. Although these methods benefit from the ability to directly and in-depth assess conflict management, they are time-consuming and resource intensive.

To avoid these constraints, most studies assessing conflict management in online communities study online written discussions [ 2 , 10 , 15 , 18 , 23 , 26 , 28 , 30 , 31 , 34 , 41 , 50 , 59 ]. Contrary to in-person methods for the study of conflict management approaches, methods assessing online written discussions lack the ability to observe non-verbal cues directly. However, written verbal cues contain valuable information about how people react to each other during conflict situations. Following these methods, we use conversational data from OSM changeset discussion threads.

In the next section, we introduce our data collection method, processing, and analysis methods, which are carefully devised to enable both quantitative and qualitative understanding of OSM producer conflict management behaviors in online discussions.

3 DATA

3.1 Scope of Data

Among many communication channels in OSM, we focus on a corpus of OSM changeset discussions, as it specifically captures task-based communications, and therefore reflects task-based conflict management in OSM. In the early days of the OSM project, OSM had two means of public discussion: mailing lists and official online discussion fora [ 60 ]. However, as the number of geospatial features created in the OSM database increased, the discussion topics became complex to manage, as they captured conversations ranging from a single editing task (i.e., a changeset), through methods of mapping and tagging, to discussions of OSM community goals [ 60 ]. The OSM changeset discussion feature was therefore introduced in 2014 to separate interactions about a single changeset from other discussions [ 60 ].

A changeset in OSM is created when a mapper edits the map database (e.g., addition, modification and deletion of map elements), and can be accompanied by a description (e.g., reason for editing, details of the edits) [ 40 ]. A changeset discussion is a feature available to all OSM mappers per changeset, enabling to provide constructive criticism and feedback [ 40 , 60 ]. An OSM changeset discussion is, for the purpose of this article, structured along three levels, or units: a changeset discussion thread , consisting of one or more mapper comments , each in turn consisting of one or more sentences .

Unlike a real-time chat, an OSM changeset discussion captures an asynchronous conversation. It does not require the real-time online presence of OSM mappers who initially made edits. A conversation captures the social interactions among the participants to identify what they disagree on and how they reach consensus based on interpreting feedback from each other [ 42 ]. An OSM changeset discussion thus captures data enabling one to study how mappers manage conflict in OSM. In this context, we assume that:

  • Members who participate in creating and editing OSM changesets share a collective goal of producing open spatial data via OSM, regardless of their primary motivation to contribute to OSM; and that
  • A member decides to add a discussion entry on a changeset for a clarification and/or change (either back to its original status or in other ways), which implies a seed of disagreement if the discussion continues without a solution and can result in either de-escalation or escalation of conflict among the participating members, depending on how they manage conflict.

Based on these assumptions, we hereby refer to conflict as any disagreement among OSM mappers during a map edit, regardless of its multi-dimensional characteristics. We focus our analysis on mappers’ conflict management approaches in an online discussion.

3.2 Variables

From our research questions, we identify one dependent variable (i.e., effectiveness of conflict management) and two independent variables (i.e., conflict handling styles and dialogic functions). These variables are manually annotated from the OSM changeset discussion (see Section 3.3 ).

To measure the effectiveness of conflict management, we refer to the literature on organizational management and conflict in other common-based online communities. One of the widely used proxy indicators is the producer retention rate. If a certain type of conflict management strategy is effective, the producer retention rate will increase [ 11 , 17 ]. Similarly, producer contribution frequency will increase, if a conflict management strategy is effective in managing disagreements among producers [ 61 ]. However, these indicators are not easily applicable to OSM, due to the participation structure and motivation in OSM. For example, paid mappers who are hired by companies to map on OSM reduce their contribution frequency or stop mapping entirely because their contracted work ends, not because of conflict with other mappers. University students who started mapping on OSM during a university course may not return to OSM as they lose external motivation to continue mapping.

Instead, we use a binary variable, capturing whether or not a solution has been developed at the end of the written discussion as our proxy indicator for measuring the effectiveness of conflict management. Our application of the theory of change here is that OSM mappers develop, through a written online discussion, a solution only if they can manage and reconcile their divergent ideas into a single solution. An agreeable solution can be either an implementable task or a process for OSM mappers, for example, to add a geospatial feature on the map or using a specific mapping method.

As for our first independent variable of conflict handling styles, we categorized each mapper comment within a discussion thread under one of the five conflict handling style categories of Rahim and Bonoma [ 46 ]. For the dialogic function, we categorized each sentence in a comment under one of the eight dialogic functions of Détienne et al. [ 10 ], plus the affective dialogic function to capture negative written behaviors, as proposed by [ 26 ]. A summary of variables with their descriptions is in Tables 2 and 3 . Sentences that did not fall under one of these categories were left unannotated, as there was no dialogic function to associate with. For example, a sentence that only contains hello is signal the other discussants that a member has joined a changeset discussion [ 36 ], but cannot be categorized into any nine dialogic functions in Table 3 .

3.3 Data Collection

We extracted OSM changeset discussions using the OSM Changeset Analyzer (OSMCha) 2 via Python. OSMCha is an online tool enabling to review and validate any suspicious edits (e.g., from honest mistake to intentional vandalism) on the OSM map.

After preliminary examination of raw data, we decided to focus only on changesets with more than five discussion comments. Discussion threads with less than five comments were considered to have not enough information to understand how individual members chose their conflict handling styles. As changeset discussions were only introduced in late 2014, we limited the temporal scope of the dataset to December 2014 – May 2021. We then restricted the changesets to discussions written in English. In total, we extracted 348 changeset discussions ready for annotation.

There are two common ways to annotate data from text: via computational text analysis and through manual annotation. While computational text analysis methods enable fast, large-scale, and increasingly accurate assessments of word frequencies, sentiments, and themes [ 43 , 49 ], they are not yet robust enough to support the analysis of conversational context, and are erroneous when exposed to complex verbal behaviours, such as sarcasm and double negative [ 49 ]. Considering the importance of conflict context in this paper, manual annotation was deemed more appropriate to encode complicated interactions among OSM mappers.

We recruited two external annotators at a university in Australia who are native English speakers whose experience involves (1) manual annotation of online conversation and/or (2) mapping and interacting with OSM mappers. Upon recruiting the annotators, we provided a code book which contains annotations from Tables 2 and 3 and a training session on how to use the online annotation tool Brat 3 , using mock-up text data. After training, we had three rounds of annotation in which the annotators coded one-third of the acquired changeset corpus per round (approximately 120 text files, each capturing an entire changeset discussion thread).

Annotators coded variables identified in Section 3.2 for each text file separately. The corresponding author then compared the annotation results between the annotators. For those annotation results that did not match, we organized a series of resolution meetings with the annotators to reach annotation consensus.

We conducted a robustness test of our manual content analysis by calculating the consistency of coding by the two annotators on the same set of text data. For the first-round, the annotation results had 59% agreement rate, the second-round had 77%, and the third-round had an 83% agreement rate on average across the annotated variables before resolution meetings. 4

From the 348 OSM changesets we annotated, the integrating style is the conflict handling style most used by the OSM mappers, followed by dominating, obliging, avoiding, and compromising styles. There are differences in proportions of each conflict handling style between those discussions that develop into solutions towards the end of a thread (i.e., effective discussions) and those that did not (i.e., ineffective discussions).

Discussions without solution had a higher proportion of sentences that are affective, arguing against, and evaluating than those with one. Discussions with solutions had a higher proportions of sentences that argue in favor, informative, and regulative-manage than those without one. As summarized in Table 8 , comments that were categorized as avoiding conflict handling style had the highest proportion of affective sentences, followed by dominating style. The compromising conflict handling style had a higher proportion of argumentative sentences and regulative-organize than other styles. The proportion of sentences that are informative (both requesting and providing information) and regulative-manage was similar across all five conflict handling styles.

The skewed distributions shown in Tables 6 and 7 are natural and expected in this corpus. It reflects expected behaviors by the community members, that is, engaging with each other in collaborative, productive, and civil behaviors. The purpose of this study is to show the applicability and potential of the conflict handling style model and dialogic functions as categories of user behavior for conflict management. Following best practice, our sample size is deemed sufficient to run multivariate logistic regression [ 6 ].

4.3 Effectiveness of Conflict Handling Styles on Conflict Management

Our first research question addresses the effectiveness of each conflict handling style with respect to the management of conflict in OSM. We established our hypothesis for this question as follows:

  • H1-0 (Null hypothesis): The coefficients of the logistic regression modeling odds ratio of conflict management effectiveness as a function of each conflict handling style are zero.
  • H1-1 (Alternative hypothesis): At least one of the coefficients of the logistic regression modeling odds ratio of conflict management effectiveness as a function of each conflict handling style are non-zero.

Our analysis on the first research question (Table 9 ) shows that we can reject our null hypothesis.One statistically significant coefficient is from the usage of obliging conflict handling style. At 99% confidence level, having an obliging style during an OSM changeset discussion is 57 times more likely to lead to an effective solution than the discussion without obliging style. This is not surprising, given that the key characteristic of an obliging style is to empathize with the situation of the other and accept solutions suggested by others, rather than push one's own. Obliging styles manifested in OSM's context include mappers apologizing for their edits, accepting solution from other mappers without asking for clarification, and accepting OSM local community's practice and guidelines based on references to relevant Wiki pages. This is aligned with findings from studies that assessed in-person conflict management, where individuals tend to choose obliging conflict handling styles in desire to avoid uncertainty[ 12 ]. However, this result should be interpreted cautiously, as the gap between lower and upper confidence interval is substantive (Table 9 ). Compromising and integrating styles are found to be associated with a higher probability of developing a solution, but again not statistically significant.

Our second research question targets the effectiveness of dialogic functions in conflict management in OSM changeset discussions. We test below null and alternative hypotheses to answer this question:

  • H2-0 (Null hypothesis): The coefficients of the logistic regression modeling odds ratio of conflict management effectiveness as a function of each dialogic function are zero.
  • H2-1 (Alternative hypothesis): At least one of the coefficients of the logistic regression modeling odds ratio of conflict management effectiveness as a function of each dialogic function are non-zero.

As shown in Table 10 , we can reject our null hypothesis. We find that having an OSM mapper using an informative sentence does not have statistically significant odds ratio in effective mapper conflict management. From regulative sentences, we find that sentences that manage the course of action are three times more likely to develop a solution (p<0.05). For argumentative sentences, we find that sentences arguing against are associated with an 89% decrease in developing a solution (p<0.01), whereas sentences arguing in favor of other mappers are associated with an 1.5 times increase in likelihood of developing a solution (p<0.05). Sentences that evaluated and suggested a solution were found to be associated with decreased probabilities of developing a solution, although not at statistically significant levels.

Another statistically significant result of the analysis of dialogic functions is the usage of affective sentences. Sentences that are impolite and contain personal attack are associated with 85% decrease in developing a solution during OSM changeset discussion (p<0.01). This result is similar to those of studies on conflict in other OPPCs introduced in Section 2.3 [ 2 , 3 , 11 , 35 , 38 , 55 , 56 ].

5 DISCUSSIONS

Managing conflict is a time-consuming, but necessary work. Conflict management helps organizations to understand the effect of conflict on group outcomes and to develop appropriate interventions [ 46 , 58 ]. Managing producer conflict is critically important for OPPCs as their operations heavily rely on producer participation that may be negatively impacted by conflict [ 8 , 17 ].

Our results indicate that the obliging conflict handling style and sentences that argue in-favor had a higher probability to develop into a solution. They stem from three common behaviors: (1) attempting to understand what others suggest/argue for; (2) being empathetic to other's stance; and (3) being positive. We also found that regulative sentences that manage the course of a discussion and suggest next steps have a higher probability of aiding with the development of a solution. This can be interpreted as being analytic and forward-looking about the discussion: evaluating each other's opinion, assessing why others think their opinion is right, and reflecting on what solution could be acceptable to all involved.

In discussions that did not develop a solution, we see a high association of dominating conflict handling styles and sentences that argue with each other. Both involve behaviors that do not empathize with others and emphasize their own opinions. Affective sentences are also highly associated with discussions that did not result in a solution. Such sentences involve behaviors that are negative in tone (e.g., sarcastic, condescending) and not focused on the current topic of discussion (i.e., arguing about others’ behaviors rather than discussing the problem at hand).

These findings about producer behaviors can inform the design of two main types of conflict management mechanism in OPPCs: (1) self-management of conflict by producers themselves, and (2) third-party interventions. Self-management mechanisms may involve producer peers managing conflict amongst themselves by applying community guidelines, such as a code of conduct [ 1 , 27 , 33 , 62 ]. Third-party intervention mechanisms may involve a producer that is not part of the conflict or the intervention of one or multiple members of an administrative group within an OPPC that assists with the management of conflict (e.g., volunteering discussion moderators or Arbitration Committee in Wikipedia) [ 18 , 22 ]. While these mechanisms differ in who manages the conflict, they have two commonalities in terms of how the conflict is managed: (1) community guidelines and (2) communication methods.

Community guidelines in OPPCs establish ground rules and standard operating procedures for producers to follow and refer upon [ 33 , 62 ]. When arguing with each other, producers may support their arguments by pointing to relevant parts of community guidelines [ 1 , 27 ]. However, such guidelines are often vague, offering room for interpretation [ 29 ]. Producer behaviors identified in our results could be included in such community guidelines to provide detailed examples of how to self-manage conflict amongst peer producers.

Most communication for either conflict management mechanisms in OPPCs occurs via computer-mediated communication channels, in particular online written discussions. Designing online discussion UI for OPPCs based on the producer behaviors reported here could support producers to maximize positive effects of conflicts, i.e., developing better solutions [ 8 ]. One such UI design intervention is embedded text, as seen in the Talk page of any Wikipedia article. It includes bullet points from the Wikipedia code of conduct. 5 The Stack Exchange Network uses a pop-up window UI element for members asking new questions (Figure 2 ), reminding them how to write a good question adhering to the code of conduct. 6

Even carefully designed UIs supporting producers to manifest desired conflict management behaviors could lead to unexpected and sometimes negative outcomes. Producers may feel uncomfortable receiving a reminder (e.g., as a bullet point or a pop-up window). Such UI design, depending on how frequently it was triggered and how interactive it was, could be seen as nuisance and interference to producers, especially those who are aware of and following relevant guidelines. Examples of unexpected fallout from supposedly positive changes in OPPCs have been reported [ 13 , 51 ]. Making any changes in OPPC conflict management mechanisms should involve a user-centered approach where in-depth consultation and understanding of producers, including their backgrounds, activities, and opinions [ 8 ]. This becomes important when OPPCs cannot solely rely on producers’ voluntary compliance with community guidelines or with formal conflict management mechanisms.

In Section 2 we have reviewed how the conflict management strategies and tools employed by OPPSs become more formal and complicated throughout their start-grow-mature-decline/revive life cycle. Even when OPPCs grow into communities with potentially millions of registered members, they retain their vision of open and voluntary online communities that co-produce knowledge products for public good. They remain as a community with lose management hierarchy among community members and lose compliance to community policies [ 8 ]. As a result, conflict management in OPPCs usually remains highly decentralized and ad-hoc [ 24 , 32 ]. While everyone can participate in conflict management in a seemingly autonomous and democratic manner, this also means that no one is obliged to manage persistent conflicts. The identity of OPPCs as open and voluntary organizations makes it particularly challenging to adopt more structured conflict management process, even as the magnitude and impact of conflict becomes comparable to conflicts in formal, hierarchical organizations [ 8 ].

Our results show the potential of conflict management theory as a procedural guideline to assess producer conflict in OPPCs (Also, see [ 8 ]). We have fully applied one of the theoretical conflict handling style models in combination with the analysis of dialogic functions. This allowed for a systematic categorization and interpretation of online written discussions both at the comment and at the sentence level, providing a detailed diagnosis of how conflict is managed and how it can be used to design an intervention. While we used OSM as a case study, our results show the benefit of applying conflict management theory for designing a conflict management intervention in other OPPCs, in particular the one involving changing producers’ interaction behavior.

6 LIMITATIONS

The results here are limited by the scope of our data source – the OSM changeset discussions longer than five comments, contributed between 2014 and 2021. This selection choice, motivated by the need to track the evolution of a discussion over multiple conversational exchanges, might selectively bias our results to a sample of highly active OSM mappers. Therefore, our results may not be fully representative of the entire OSM community. One way of addressing this limitation would be to expand the data scope to other public communication channels of OSM (e.g., public forum, mailing list) to provide more holistic insight into online conflict management behaviors in OSM.

Furthermore, our data collection method relied on manual annotation by two external annotators and three rounds of resolution meetings to address any annotation discrepancies. We implemented measures to minimize bias (e.g., annotation guideline and annotation training sessions). However, there could still be bias in how the annotators viewed each sentence and comment initially and how they changed their views during and after the resolution meetings.

Finally, we note the limitations of our data analysis method. We converted our independent variables from count data to binary data (Section 4 ). Although such a step was necessary to meet the assumptions of logistic regression analysis, our interpretation of the analysis results lost some nuance in the due process.

7 CONCLUSIONS

We analyzed the online producer conflict management behaviors in OSM based on a series of task-based online discussions. Our analysis results are in line with findings from previous studies in other OPPCs, showing that empathetic and forward-looking behaviors are more likely to enable effective conflict management and that negative online behaviors are less likely. We derive design implications from our findings to suggest conflict management intervention design for OSM's consideration. Our paper showcases the potential of applying a conflict handling style model to identify producers’ conflict management behaviors in OPPCs and to design an intervention that reflects producer behaviors. Our future study will involve collecting producers’ opinions about different communication channel interface designs in OPPCs to manage conflict more effectively.
