Supervising Multiple Operating Rooms Using a Head-Worn display: A Longitudinal Evaluation of the Experience of Supervising Anesthesiologists and Their Co-Workers

Research has explored head-worn displays (HWD) in various professional contexts. However, evaluations have been limited by short-term use, a focus on the person using the HWD, and on performance variables. In a field study, we evaluated a monocular, opaque HWD for multi-patient monitoring, which supervising anesthesiologists wore for 8-10 days each. We investigated the effect of prolonged HWD use on the experience of the supervising anesthesiologists and their co-workers using interviews and repeated observations. A reflexive thematic analysis showed (1) interaction and mindset changes over time, (2) information on the HWD is more than numbers, (3) the HWD affects co-workers' collaboration with supervisors, and (4) distraction depends on the point of view. Using activity theory, we discuss the fact that HWD use develops and changes over time and that even a single-user HWD influences the collaboration with co-workers. We conclude with implications for HWD design, implementation, and evaluation.

1 INTRODUCTION

The hardware advances of head-worn displays (HWD), such as Microsoft HoloLens or Vuzix M400, allow for deployment in various professional contexts and continuous use throughout a workday [ 78 ]. However, previous research has primarily evaluated the effects of HWDs in short lab-based experiments [ 17 , 41 , 71 ]. Consequently, little is known about how HWDs affect individual performance over a lengthier period and how use changes over time when HWDs are deployed in a genuine professional environment. Furthermore, lab-based experiments focus on HWD users themselves. They seldom include further co-workers who are frequently present in professional settings. Therefore, little is known about the effects of HWDs on non-verbal communication processes [ 17 ] or teamwork [ 16 ] between the person wearing an HWD and the co-workers.

The purpose of the present study is to address the above research gaps by investigating the experience of prolonged HWD use in anesthesiology. Supervising anesthesiologists used an HWD for 8-10 work days work days displaying patients’ vital signs of six operating rooms (ORs). We observed each supervisor three times and conducted an interview at the end of the deployment period. In addition, we investigated the effects of HWD technology on the supervisors’ co-workers’ experiences by conducting interviews with the co-workers. Finally, we used activity theory to provide an explanation of our results on a more abstract and generalizable level. In the remainder of this section, we survey different forms of HWDs and their application in the healthcare domain. Then, we focus on the effects of HWDs on vital sign monitoring processes and point out gaps in existing research. Finally, we highlight the contributions of the present work.

1.1 Related work

1.1.1 HWDs and their use in clinical environments. Since the conceptualization of HWDs as the “ultimate display” in the 1960s [ 76 , 77 ], multiple forms of HWDs have been developed. Opaque binocular HWDs can display virtual reality software. In contrast, video and optical see-through HWDs enable augmented reality applications [ 10 ]. However, the present research addresses the use of a monocular HWD displaying two-dimensional information. This category of HWD device is often described as “smart glasses” [ 59 ] and it provides continuous and hands-free access to data. In healthcare environments, such monocular HWDs have been used to support a variety of clinical tasks [for a recent review, see 71]. HWDs can support clinicians by providing manual work guidance [ 55 ], enabling remote collaboration [ 48 ], recording and analyzing information in the environment [ 23 ], visualizing complex data [ 75 ] and, finally and most importantly for this study, monitoring continuous data sources such as the vital signs of patients [ 65 ].

1.1.2 HWD-based vital sign monitoring. Vital signs convey information to anesthesiologists that helps them meet the high-level objectives of keeping the patient hemodynamically stable and sedated [ 63 ]. Anesthesia closed-claims cases [ 79 ] and the analysis of incident reports [ 83 ] have underscored the fact that monitoring data helped to detect upcoming issues or that it could have prevented negative outcomes. However, given other tasks such as comforting the patient or intubating, anesthesiologists cannot always look at monitors. The situation is even more challenging for senior anesthesiologists who supervise several ORs because they can only access a specific monitor if they are in the respective OR or in a doctors’ office that is equipped with a central monitoring station for all ORs. Because HWDs enable continuous, mobile, and hands-free access to vital signs, they provide a form of continuous information that was previously unachievable [ 63 ].

Early research proposing monitoring vital signs via HWDs focused on a single patient and was directed at boosting the clinician's detection of deterioration events while enabling continued manual work on the patient [ 44 , 46 , 58 , 67 ]. More recently, Pascale et al. [ 53 , 54 ] explored the possibility of monitoring multiple patients with an HWD in environments such as intensive care units or operating suites. The rationale for HWD-based multiple patient monitoring is to reduce the clinician's reliance on auditory alarms or central monitoring stations, helping them develop and maintain situation awareness (i.e., knowing what is going on [ 20 ]), and, ultimately, allowing them to quickly direct attention to a deteriorating or unstable patient. In the present study—involving a large teaching hospital in Germany—an HWD could allow a supervising anesthesiologist in an operating suite to see the status of individual patients in different ORs where they are treated by junior anesthesiologists. Supervision of junior anesthesiologists, nurse anesthetists, anesthesiologist assistants or physicians’ assistants is common international practice. In the UK, for example, the ratio can vary from one senior anesthesiologist supervising up to four staff members depending on their education level, experience, and patient cases [ 3 ]. Ideally, an HWD with patient data of multiple patients could enable supervising anesthesiologists to tailor their supervision and direct their attention based on the current situation and to do so independently of their location within the unit.

Previous research has investigated different aspects of HWD-based multiple patient monitoring. In a series of lab-based experiments, Pascale et al. [ 53 ] demonstrated that the availability of continuous vital sign information allows for the quicker detection of patient deterioration events than without this information. However, if the same vital sign information is presented on a conventional display and an HWD, it attracts attention slightly faster in case of a deterioration event [ 62 ]. Salient stimuli such as auditory cues or light flashes can accelerate the detection of deterioration events displayed on HWDs [ 40 , 62 ].

Additionally, in clinical environments, the continuous availability of vital signs on HWDs seems to outweigh the aforementioned potential delays in detection. Schlosser et al. [ 69 ] demonstrated that while anesthesiologists are roaming an operating suite, they are prone to miss alarms displayed on a central monitoring station. Monitoring vital signs with an HWD resolved the issue and resulted in a substantially higher number of alarms detected. Moreover, comments from the anesthesiologists participating in Schlosser's study and a follow-up simulation-study [ 43 ], indicated that the HWD enabled the detection of critical situations and generally improved the situation awareness of physicians. Specifically, participants not only reported that they became aware of more alarms (situation awareness level 1) but also indicated that they could integrate the perceived information with their background knowledge of a specific patient (situation awareness level 2) and consider possible future actions (situation awareness level 3, [ 20 , 73 ]). Even though further clinical trials are needed to study the effects on patient outcomes [ 19 , 41 ], previous research generally suggests that HWDs may effectively assist in vital sign monitoring.

1.1.3 Research gaps in the field of clinical HWD use. In addition to the advances outlined above, recent literature reviews have pointed out various gaps remaining in clinical HWD research. Apart from the lack of clinical trials, the research has highlighted the lack of longitudinal studies for augmented reality applications [ 17 ] and smart glasses [ 19 ]. Elrose et al. [ 19 ] stated that only longitudinal studies can reveal the “hidden” effects of HWD-based vital sign monitoring that are suppressed in short experiments because of participants’ initial excitement or frustration about HWD technology. This conjecture aligns with longitudinal studies of other advanced clinical technologies, such as robotic telepresence [ 6 ], revealing that users tend mostly to experiment with technology in the initial hours and days after deployment. However, they follow more structured work patterns later on after being familiar with the device or technology under investigation. In the context of single patient monitoring, Romare et al. [ 58 ] conducted interviews with nurse anesthetists who used an HWD approximately 10 to 20 times in various situations. Romare et al. [ 58 ] reported that an adaptation to the HWD was needed before the nurse anesthetists became accustomed to the HWD but the adaptation process was not part of the research question. Insights about change can be investigated with longitudinal studies which, by definition, include at least two measures of a phenomenon at different times [ 36 ]. We will investigate such changes over time and discuss our results in light of the development concept of activity theory [ 35 ] which, in brief, states that using a tool such as an HWD enables a supervising anesthesiologist to make changes to an objective such as keeping a patient hemodynamically stable but also feeds back to the user who might learn new ways to use the tool.

In addition to the need for longitudinal studies, literature reviews also pointed out that the effects of HWDs on team processes are underexplored and encouraged researchers to focus on collaborative aspects when studying HWDs [ 16 , 37 ]. Previous clinical HWD research explored team processes concerning remote collaboration [ 56 ]. However, studies in other domains reveal that HWDs not enabling communication between individuals can still affect teamwork [ 2 , 57 , 61 ]. For example, in emergency medical response [ 2 ], HWDs indicating the status of the individual team members of a search and rescue unit substantially altered collaboration—when a team member's status changed, their colleagues rushed to assist. As the current research is situated in an operating suite where approximately 50 staff members may interact with the wearer of the HWD, we believe that studying how HWDs affect collaboration and communication in this context is important.

Finally, Dey and colleagues [ 17 ] pointed out that medical augmented reality studies have focused on performance improvement of medical tasks and have not addressed the experience of the users. Dey and colleagues [ 17 ] suggested that qualitative research on using HWD in the professional environment should offer a more holistic account of actual use and the users’ experience (UX). It has also been noted that UX has received limited attention in safety-critical settings in general [ 27 , 50 , 66 ]. Recent research in acute care showed that UX in the form of satisfaction of psychological needs such as competence and autonomy seems to be as important in the interaction with medical technology as it is in consumer technology interaction [ 31 ]. Using activity theory [ 35 ], we will reflect on how the HWD may have contributed or hindered the satisfaction of psychological needs of supervising anesthesiologists and their co-workers.

1.2 The present study

The present study aimed to address the gaps in previous research outlined above. First, we wanted to understand the effects of prolonged HWD use on the experience of the supervising anesthesiologists (referred to as supervisors in the following). Second, we wished to understand how HWD use affected co-workers’ experience with their supervisors. Third, we wanted to stay open to further aspects of HWD use unrelated to the other aims. To this end, seven supervisors wore an HWD with an application displaying simultaneous vital sign information of patients in six ORs for 8 to 10 days and 4.5 to 7 hours per day. Each supervisor was observed using the HWD on three days (an early, mid, and late day in the deployment period), and we interviewed them at the end of the deployment period. We also interviewed eight co-workers (junior anesthesiologists, anesthetic nurses, second supervising anesthesiologist) who had worked with supervisors wearing the HWD during the study period. All observations and interviews were transcribed and analyzed using a reflexive thematic analysis [ 7 , 13 ]. We contribute (1) a detailed description of the four themes of interaction and mindset changes over time, information on the HWD is more than just numbers, HWD affects collaboration with the supervisor , and distraction depends on the point of view derived from the observation and interview corpora, (2) a discussion of prolonged use of HWDs and the social and collaborative implications for co-workers, in which we used activity theory to provide a retrospective explanation for the observed results, (3) implications for HWD design, implementation, and evaluation in acute care and possibly in other domains.

2 METHOD

2.1 Setting

We conducted the study in a large teaching hospital in a sub-section of the operating suite compromising six individual ORs (the whole unit constitutes 14 ORs). Patients of all ages, physical status, and acuity are treated in the operating suite. In the part of the operating suite where the study occurred, 15–25 mostly urology and orthopedics surgeries are performed every day. Patients are transferred from a regular bed to a mobile OR table for surgery. Next, they are anesthetized in the induction room of an OR and moved to the respective OR. After surgery, patients emerge from anesthesia in the adjoining recovery room and later stay in the general recovery before being transferred to a step-down ward.

An anesthesiologist attends to the respective patient at any time in each OR. Anesthesiologists take care of the patient before, during, and immediately after surgery and keep the patient anesthetized, ventilated and hemodynamically stable. Except in the case of exceptional special interventions, anesthesiologists work in a non-sterile environment and do not perform the surgery. In addition to the anesthesiologist in the room, two experienced senior anesthesiologists supervise six operating rooms (a sub-section of the whole operating suite). In addition to supervising and consulting with their colleagues, supervisors conduct administrative work, coordinate patients, organize and coordinate staff, and provide immediate support in an emergency. The two supervisors coordinate their responsibilities at the start of the shift. The supervisors can see patients’ vital signs on a central monitoring station in the doctors’ office. For the present study, one of the two supervisors wore the HWD. Figures 1 and 2 illustrate the layout of the operating suite in the hospital under study, the general setting, and the HWD.

2.2 Participants

A convenience sample of seven supervisors participated (two female, five male; age range 32-49 years; M = 39 years, SD = 6), and work experience ranged from 5 to 18 years (M = 9.4 years, SD = 5.1). All supervisors were specialists in anesthesiology (German Facharzt). Based on a previous HWD study [ 69 ], we had the impression that 10 days with the HWD would be sufficient to understand changes over time as well as to provide co-workers with enough opportunities to interact with supervisors wearing the HWD. Due to staff scheduling, shift changes on short notice, leave, sick leave, and technical issues, it was not possible for a single supervisor to wear the HWD on consecutive days (Table 1 ). Considering these difficulties, we decided that supervisors should wear the HWD for at least eight days, but we would not add more days if any of the aforementioned issues would delay further immediate data collections. Each supervisor received €60 for their participation.

2.3 Material and procedure

We used the technical set-up from a previous study [ 68 , 69 ] but employed the latest Vuzix M400 (Vuzix Corporation, West Henrietta, NY, USA) as the HWD. The Vuzix M400 can be placed on the left or right side of the head, and the external battery can be attached on the opposite side or at the back of the head. The opaque color display can be positioned in the peripheral field of view and has a size comparable to a 40″ screen viewed from a 2 m distance. We extracted the vital sign values from the video feed of the central monitoring station in the doctor's office (Figure 1 ) and sent the data to the custom HWD interface via a secure WiFi network. For a detailed description of the software application design, development, and technical set-up in the hospital, see Schlosser et al. [ 68 , 69 ].

The application includes an overview screen with vital signs and alarms from six operating rooms (Figure 3 ). The data were updated every second. The supervisors could access more detailed screens of individual patients’ data in a single operating room by swiping up and down on the touchpad at the side of the HWD. If an alarm occurred, the HWD would display the alarm, an auditory signal would play, and the screen would turn white for 150 ms, producing a flash to attract attention [ 62 ]. The auditory alarm signal was one second long, and the pitch of the sound indicated whether it was a low priority (yellow alarm, low pitch) or high priority alarm (red alarm, high pitch). Low-priority alarms occur when a vital sign crosses a specific threshold. In contrast, high-priority alarms occur when a vital sign reaches a life-threatening level.

Author OH or a previous participant performed the handover of the HWD for a participant's study period. Participants received the HWD, the headband to attach it, two batteries to power the device, and a quick overview of how to operate the HWD. Participants were instructed in how to start the display, adjust the volume, and access the detail screens. Participants could contact the hospital's technical support staff or the researcher at any time. For the duration of their participation, the supervisors were responsible for attending to the device and charging the batteries. Supervisors wore the HWD after the first patients entered the ORs and wore it until the afternoon.

Author AM conducted participatory observations with each supervisor during their early, mid, and late HWD use periods. She accompanied the supervisors, took handwritten notes, and asked the supervisors questions during periods of low workload about the HWD. At the end of each day, all notes were digitized and extended based on AM's recall of events. In total, we conducted observations on 21 days. When the supervisor finished their participation, they made an appointment with the author AM for the interview.

The study was reviewed by the local ethics committee and conducted from January to June 2022. All participants were recruited by word-of-mouth. All supervisors provided informed consent when starting to wear the HWD or latest before the first observation day Co-workers provided informed consent before the interview. Due to COVID-19 restrictions and scheduling issues, some interviews were conducted via Zoom (https://zoom.us/) and some in person (Table 1 ). All interviews were audio recorded. We followed a narrative interview approach. After the participant was briefed about the procedure, author AM asked an open question (“How did you experience the study?”) and prompted the participant to speak freely about their experience working with the HWD. After the participant's initial explanations and statements, the author AM asked for further details and focused on unique aspects raised by the participant. Generally, we focused on the participant's personal experiences and asked about specific situations on certain observation days and how the participants remembered them. Due to the different experiences and personalities of the interviewees, the interviews varied greatly in length (see Table 1 ). This is to be expected when conducting narrative interviews [ 26 ]. For details of the briefing and general questions, see Supplementary Material.

2.4 Data analysis using reflexive thematic analysis

We performed a reflexive thematic analysis [ 7 , 13 ] to analyze our data. Specifically, we refrained from calculating coding reliability measures or using a codebook but conducted our analysis as an interpretative reflexive process [ 8 ]. The inductive, experiential approach of the thematic analysis fitted our research questions, but also allowed us to investigate further interesting and unexpected content that we discovered in the data. The ontological framework of our data analysis can be considered critical realism: We are aware that observations and interviews do not reveal a universal truth, but rather an individual reality experienced by the observer or interviewee. Because truth can only be seen through this lens, our epistemology is more contextualized. We assume that knowledge is contextual and depends on the perspective; neither we, researchers, nor the supervisors can act separately from the situation. As researchers, we shape the knowledge in the context of our values, too.

We used ATLAS.ti ( https://atlasti.com/ ) to conduct the reflexive thematic analysis. We followed Braun and Clarke's [ 7 ] six-phase process for data engagement, coding, and theme development: (1) data familiarization and writing familiarization notes, (2) systematic data coding, (3) generating initial themes, (4) developing and reviewing themes, (5) refining, defining, and naming themes, and (6) writing the report. We used seven interviews with anesthesiologists, eight interviews with co-workers, and texts of the 21 observations as data. Author AM collected and transcribed the data.

We constructed the analysis bottom-up and tried to stay open to possible results that were unrelated to the aims of the study. Familiarization was performed during data collection by author AM. Initial interpretations and notes were directly integrated into the notes taken during observations. Author AM transcribed the interviews and TG familiarized himself with the data by reading the transcripts and observation protocols. Because it took a long time for each supervisor to finish the 8-10 days of wearing the HWD, author AM started coding with ATLAS.ti before the data set was complete. Authors AM and TG discussed possible codes and insights during the coding process. When author AM finished coding most data, she started to generate initial themes based on existing codes. Themes were restructured multiple times and reviewed during regular video calls between authors AM, TG, and PS. We started the final analysis with 189 codes and reduced or summarized them to 67 codes used for this report. The reflexive thematic analysis resulted in four themes with two to four sub-themes, each illustrated in a thematic map (Figure 4 ). Author AM provided a researcher reflexivity report in the Supplementary Materials.

3 RESULTS

The abbreviations (S, Co, and O) are followed by the participant number (Table 1 ) and, in the case of observations, by a second number indicating the observation session: early (_1), middle (_2), or late (_3). 3.1 Interaction and mindset changes over time

The theme of interaction and mindset changes over time describes that interaction and mindset of supervisors regarding the HWD were not constant but developed over time.

3.1.1 Supervisors’ familiarization with the HWD. Using the HWD required a consolidation phase of a few hours to a few days until the supervisors became fully used to the HWD. Within hours, supervisors were used to the HWD hardware on their heads. However, it took several days to use the device effectively and efficiently. In the following, we will describe how the increased familiarity with the HWD over time manifested in altered behavioral patterns and opinions about the device.

The supervisors demonstrated the fastest development within the first few days. Some supervisors were skeptical about the general idea of the HWD at first, but most reduced their doubts over time. For example, in observations, one participant declared their surprise at how little effort the HWD demands [O1_1] or that the HWD is not distracting while working on a computer [O6_2]. One co-worker observed this development: “One must say the longer they wore this display, the better they got along with it” [Co_2].

We observed increased self-confidence of participants in adjusting the HWD hardware and using the software after a few days. S7 stated that “When I used the menus […] it was no problem at all and in the end, I have [used] […] [the] touchpad, too.” We conclude that supervisors were able to access the information more efficiently over time and use the HWD without spending much conscious thought on it.

Participants developed their way of using the HWD during the familiarization phase. Some participants defined their way of use early and did not develop it further afterward: “[I] have found […] [the] perfect benefit […] I did not change the use in the remaining days” [S2]. Others reduced their use of the device during the study. For example, S5, who had only recently become a supervisor, declared: “I had the feeling at day five or six, that [the HWD] somehow fades into the background […] I realized that the other tasks [coming along with the new position as a supervisor] are more important than monitoring some random vital signs” [S5].

After some time, many participants experienced the HWD as a natural part of their work, not constantly pushing itself into the focus of attention. Supervisors started using the HWD as a regular information source, as explained by S1: “[…] now I have [the HWD] so I look at it, I mean it is a source of information, you use it.” This statement also implies an obligation to integrate the HWD to accomplish the optimum for patients and co-workers . The described effects of familiarization were robust across shorter or longer breaks in continuous use for the HWD. For example, despite a break of seven days, no refamiliarization was required [O2_3].

3.1.2 Development of different preferences and strategies. Supervisors developed preferences for best use of the HWD. For example, some supervisors preferred to receive an acoustic signal when alarms appeared because they did not perceive the visual flash in every situation. S1 stated that “if [the HWD] maybe slipped a bit out of the field of vision or you look in a totally different direction […] the acoustic alarm is simply more noticeable,” while others declared the visual flash was sufficient and they got disrupted by the acoustic signal [O5_1].

The good fit of the headband made it generally easy for the supervisors to wear the HWD while working (Figure 2(A) ). Nevertheless, we observed, and supervisors reported, that the HWD collided with several objects and people while they wore it [O1_1, O6_2, Co4]. These collisions did not affect the work of the supervisors, harm any co-worker or patient, or damage the device. However, these observations demonstrate that in the high-tempo environment of the operating suite, an HWD with a small footprint and good suitable mounting mechanism is required. Finally, some supervisors noted consequences of the HWD on their heads [O4_2, O6_3, O7_1]. S4 expressed it thus: “[The HWD] gets attention because it is uncomfortable on the head,” and S6 took breaks in wearing the HWD because they perceived a slight headache [O6_1].

With a few exceptions, most supervisors and co-workers could imagine the HWD becoming an integral part of their work in the future. For example, S7 stated: “I can imagine that we will wear it all the time, and there were also several questions [from my colleagues] like ‘oh is this standard now?’”. However, two supervisors started with a skeptical attitude toward the HWD and remained skeptical. One participant explained: “I did not see the big benefit in the beginning […] and do not see it now” [S3]. The initial attitude of supervisors toward the HWD may have affected how they used it during the study and how much they benefited from it. Finally, technical issues might have negatively affected supervisors’ opinions of the HWD. A co-worker reported: “[…] [They] complained a little bit about technical issues and said that they were disturbed or annoyed that [the HWD] did not connect to the WIFI” [Co7].

3.2 Information on the HWD is more than just numbers

The theme of information on the HWD is more than just numbers outlines how supervisors process the information on the HWD and what effects it has on work.

3.2.1 Facilitation effects of the HWD on supervisors’ work practices. The information on the HWD was sufficient for supervisors and the stationary monitoring became less critical. S7 reported: “Some [of my colleagues] asked critically: ‘[…] what is the benefit of somebody wearing [the HWD]?’ I answered then ‘[…] I can move everywhere; I see [the vital signs] always, I can work with my hands.’” For example, the HWD allowed supervisors to exit the OR while X-rays were taken without interrupting their vital sign monitoring [O7_3]. The supervisor also delegated more sophisticated patient cases [S7] to less experienced anesthesiologists. Supervisors could intervene if problems occurred because they had constant access to the patient's data. For example, S3 explained: “You have [the vital signs of] young [colleagues’ patients] permanently in your view”. Because supervisors could see the vital signs, they were able to prioritize their visits to different ORs. For example, Co4 reported: “A few times [when I called], [the supervisor] said [after I explained my question] ‘Okay, I do not have to come for this’ or ‘That is okay’ or ‘I have ten minutes, this is not urgent.’”

Finally, organization is one of the main tasks of a supervisor [S3]. If one OR displayed no vital signs on the HWD, either the patient's anesthesia induction was not finished, or the patient was on their way to the recovery room. This knowledge helped the supervisor provide adequate and timely support during an induction [S2]. Interestingly, most supervisors used this additional organizational information, even S5, who decided not to monitor the vital signs on the HWD. They explained: “The most helpful [function of the HWD] actually was to know whether the patient is already in the [operation] room or not […]” [S5]. These episodes illustrate that the HWD made certain work practices of the supervisors more effective or ultimately enabled new ones.

3.2.2 Mediation of situation awareness. The HWD helped supervisors keep an overview of what was going on in the operating suite (i.e., supported situation awareness). The HWD displayed the latest information. This can be an advantage over other systems like the local electronic anesthesia record that is accessible from every computer and smartphone locked into the hospital's secure IT system. However, the record only shows information if it is manually entered or validated (in case of vital signs) by the anesthesiologist in the OR. Supervisors reported that they perceived a high number of alarms (situation awareness level 1), were able to classify most situations (situation awareness level 2), and sometimes drew consequences such as observing, calling, or visiting an OR based on this information (situation awareness level 3). The following episodes support these benefits.

First, recurrent alarms primed the supervisor for upcoming difficulties. Supervisors recognized noncritical alarms and kept track [O2_1]. The accumulation of noncritical alarms prompted them to consider the problem and how they could intervene if the vital signs did not improve. Therefore, supervisors perceived changes better.

Second, vital sign monitoring via the HWD was possible for many supervisors even while inducing anesthesia or being on a phone call [O2_1]. The lack of information and associated decreased situation awareness after such activities might be less severe when wearing the HWD. S6 told us: “Especially in emergency situations when you cannot leave for a long time; you get the impression that you do not notice anything [of what is going on in the other ORs], but [when wearing the HWD] you have the alarm and see everything and can look up [towards the display] […]”. While wearing the HWD, the supervisor had better knowledge than the second supervisor in the operating suite who was not wearing the device. If supervisors were busy themselves, they repeatedly reported that they used their more complete knowledge about the situation in the different ORs to inform the co-supervisor who might be available to visit an OR [S1]. We conclude that while wearing the HWD, supervisors are less likely to have a restricted awareness of what is going on in the single ORs.

Third, the improved overview of the patients’ vital signs and the course of anesthesia made it possible for the supervisors to prevent critical situations. We observed a supervisor recognizing a low arterial blood pressure in an OR but seeing in the electronic anesthesia record that the colleague at the beside was already starting to stabilize the patient with medication [O5_1]. While not in an OR, supervisors could monitor patients’ vital signs for extended periods of time. Supervisors did that, for example, when patients were in a critical state [O2_1, O2_3] or had multiple serious pre-existing diseases [O7_3] or when they just wanted to recurrently check whether everything was fine [O6_2, O5_1]. If an emergency had already happened, the HWD provided the latest information. The supervisor could understand the situation with only brief or even no further explanations. S7 described: “In one situation […] the red alarm occurred [acoustically] and I saw [on the HWD] ‘oh it is there.’ I ran off […] [and] I could see that the heart rate was already recovering […] and so I knew when I arrived in the [operating] room everything is fine again and [the anesthetist in the OR] had to explain almost nothing.” Therefore, supervisors seemed to be able to anticipate better or choose the right actions.

Overall, these episodes demonstrate an improved sense of safety by the supervisors and may suggest an overall enhancement of patient safety. As explained by S5: “It is another safety net in case the junior anesthesiologist does not call or […] the communication channel ‘calling’ does not work for whatever reasons […] the supervisor gets the notification that it is going wrong in the [operation] room.”

Finally, the effects of the HWD on situation awareness also seemed to reduce the supervisors’ stress levels. The HWD informs them about current vital signs and helps them stay calm about less experienced colleagues, keep informed during other tasks, or assess an alleged emergency. We observed, for example, a supervisor who remained calm when their colleague was called to an emergency and exited their OR because the supervisor saw on the HWD that the vital signs of the patient involved in the emergency were at no point life-threatening [O7_2].

3.2.3 Negative effects on the work experience of the supervisors. The HWD mediated situation awareness, but at the same time the HWD increased the supervisors’ workload [O7_1]. The HWD offered additional information that would have otherwise not been brought to the supervisors’ attention because the anesthesiologist in the OR would act as a filter for non-relevant alarms. One supervisor explained that when working without the HWD that “if you do not hear anything, everything is fine” [O2_2]. This pre-sorting was absent with the HWD; in addition to their regular workload, the supervisor's attention got directed to relevant and irrelevant alarms.

We observed that data on the HWD disrupted conversations or computer work [O7_1, O1_1, Co4, S2]. Sometimes these interruptions would not have been necessary because eventually the alarms or changes in vital signs did not require any action [O1_2, O6_1, Co4]; presumably, these events increased the supervisors’ stress levels. While using the HWD, supervisors were constantly aware of what was going on in every single OR but were also experiencing a high workload because even not life-threatening vital sign changes led to an “alert” of the supervisor [O2_3]. The availability of vital signs and alarms is critical because of potential stress but is also of concern because supervisors may develop resistance to the effects of alarms. One co-worker reasoned that a supervisor might become cognitively overloaded and anticipated the development of alarm fatigue concerning the information on the HWD [Co7].

3.3 HWD affects co-workers’ collaboration with supervisor

The theme of HWD affects collaboration with the supervisor describes how using the HWD affects co-workers. We found many effects on social interaction and professional collaboration between co-workers (especially for anesthesiologists in the OR) and supervisors.

3.3.1 Co-workers' initial concerns and the familiarization with the cyborg. Like supervisors, co-workers had to familiarize themselves with the HWD. When the study started, the HWD was often remarked upon, and co-workers were concerned about its effects. They assumed that the HWD would distract the supervisor [O5_3], be a burden for the supervisor [Co5], and a co-worker mentioned to a supervisor that it would be “total control” [S7]. Co-workers were also worried that “everyone just looks at some monitor and […] neglects the patient a little” [Co2]. However, after a while, the co-workers barely noticed the HWD, and it became a self-evident, non-disturbing part of their work environment: “At first, I had concerns, so I really had to get used to [the presence of the HWD], and I really noticed that [the HWD] was there and over the time I actually ignored it, so it became normal” [Co4]. After experiencing the HWD in use, most co-workers did not feel controlled by the supervisor wearing the HWD [Co3, Co2, Co1]. We observed that during the study, questions about the HWD became fewer [O1_2, O2_3], but the HWD was still noticed and commented on humorously (“Cyborg” [O1_3], “Transformer” [O6_2], “Terminator” [O4_2]). We interpret that the HWD was not seen as foreign anymore, but co-workers were still aware of its use.

3.3.2 The perceived increase in safety by co-workers. Many junior OR anesthesiologists reported that their perceived safety was increased if their supervisor wore the HWD. They recognized that constant vital sign monitoring by an experienced supervisor could be helpful and could reduce stress in complicated situations. If the supervisor detected a potential problem, supervisors often visited the OR in such cases and supported the junior anesthesiologist with advice or practical help. This back up for emergencies or upcoming difficulties had a calming effect and reduced pressure on the co-workers. One junior anesthesiologist reported: “This one time, when the patient was already critical during the induction, [the patient] was also [critical] in the [operating] room, and there I knew ‘Okay [the supervisor] watches the whole time and if something would happen [they] would come running’” [Co6]. One nurse described the impact on their safety experience: “Only the thought that when I am called to the [operating] room because there is a problem with the ventilation, then the supervisor will also notice [the problem] and one [self] feels safer” [Co8].

3.3.3 The two sides of optimized communication with co-workers. The HWD could optimize communication between co-workers and the supervisor. For example, one supervisor reported that watching the current vital signs during a telephone call about the state of the patient of concern helped them evaluate the situation better [O2_2, Figure 2(a) ]. One can infer that the HWD positively affected teaching for junior anesthesiologists. Because the HWD made it possible for the supervisor to contact co-workers early, junior anesthesiologists received suggestions on what they could do to conduct better-controlled anesthesia. It was done via telephone or by visiting the respective OR. One supervisor explained: “It is quite beneficial if you can [teach] on a concrete example […]” [S3]. S4 described a situation: “Once I discussed with [a junior anesthesiologist] ‘Okay with this patient we have to keep the blood pressure high,’ and I realized, of course, if I have the HWD, I can better check how the blood pressure is developing [after I had given my instructions]”.

However, the optimization of communication also bears problems. One concern was that junior anesthesiologists might not call the supervisor for help if they could not handle a critical situation because they assumed the supervisor already had the alarm [O3_2]. Furthermore, concern existed that if the supervisor visited the OR only when they perceived potential problems via the HWD, the junior anesthesiologist of well-led anesthesia would have no chance to ask non-urgent but relevant questions (for which they would not call) [Co2]. Co-workers were worried that the contact between supervisors and colleagues could be lost because “at this stage of my training [as an anesthesiologist] personal contact with my [supervisor] would benefit me” [Co2]. In addition, some co-workers felt neglected by the supervisor prioritizing the HWD over conversations with them: “They [the supervisors] were very brief with you […] if they would not wear the display they would not even know about [these situations in other ORs] and maybe would stay longer in [your OR]” [Co2].

3.3.4 The HWD as a source of social pitfalls and misunderstandings. The HWD introduced some social and collaborative problems. Co-workers interpreted the supervisor's distraction while monitoring vital signs on the HWD as less appreciative or even rude. The current conversation faded into the background and faltered when co-workers noticed that. We observed, for example, that one supervisor interrupted conversations to monitor vital signs on the HWD [O7_1], and two co-workers reported with consternation a situation where they were just left standing without an explanation after the supervisor saw vital signs on the HWD catching their attention [Co2, Co5]. Co-workers could sometimes comprehend such a situation if the supervisors left for an emergency [Co5]. Using the HWD also irritated co-workers: “You see how [the supervisor] peers upwards, and you know they have no epileptic seizure but are watching vital signs [of another OR] […]” [Co6, Figure 3(c) ].

As mentioned above, some co-workers had the feeling of being watched or controlled during the beginning of the study. One co-worker reported that they experienced pressure when their monitor alarmed irrelevantly: “[I] was wondering “Oh, does [the supervisor] see that and will immediately stand right behind me?” […]” [Co1]. We had the impression that sometimes junior anesthesiologists even felt belittled when the supervisor visited in a situation they thought was under control: “[In one situation] at some point [the supervisor] stood behind me, but it was not a real critical situation where [I] would have needed help immediately” [Co1].

Another problem of HWD use for co-workers was when supervisors called instead of visiting if they saw potentially critical vital signs on the HWD. Such calls could be disturbing, as stated by Co2: “[I had] a patient […] who […] got unstable quite quickly, and I was just about to start [a medication against bradycardia and hypotension] and do anything possible, and the blood pressure was dropping, then [the supervisor] called me [in order to say] it would be good if the blood pressure were better […]”. However, supervisors were aware of this issue. For example, S2 stated: “If it is a really critical situation […], from my point of view, it makes no sense to call because then the [colleague] in the [operating] room has other things to do right now than take an additional call […]” (Figure 2(d) ).

Misunderstandings arose because the HWD was not explained adequately to co-workers. Co-workers made wrong conclusions about the HWD's purpose or what information it supplied. Some co-workers did not know that induction and recovery room data was not included in the displayed information [Co2], others did not know what the HWD exactly displayed until the interview [Co1], and many co-workers were concerned that the HWD recorded videos [O7_1, O5_2, S6, Co5]. Finally, in a few instances the HWD's acoustic alarms were confused for alarms of the stationary monitor in the actual OR. The co-workers checked their stationary monitoring and wondered which vital sign caused the alarm [O2_1, O2_3].

3.4 Distraction depends on the point of view

The theme of distraction depends on the point of view outlines that the level of distraction experienced due to the HWD was different for the participants. In particular, a large discrepancy existed between supervisors and co-workers.

3.4.1 Discrepancy in the experience of distraction. Many co-workers told us that the HWD might be a burden for the supervisors. A co-worker stated that: “I believe that it was disturbing at the end [of the day] […]” [Co7]. However, most supervisors did not report any burden. Nevertheless, supervisors noticed the possibility that the HWD might be a stimulus overload if one were conscientious but excluded this possibility for themselves [O6_2]. Supervisors thought they were able to avoid distraction with various strategies we outlined below (supervisor's strategies to avoid distraction).

Supervisors reported that monitoring vital signs on the HWD while performing other tasks would not prevent them from fulfilling the task at hand; even during demanding inductions [O2_1]. In contrast, a co-worker observed, “that the supervisors [were] a little bit ‘on fire’ […] that [they were] actually a little bit more under stress […]” [Co2]. These examples depict how subjective the experience of distraction and disruption can be. Although supervisors were convinced that they were attentive and relaxed in most situations, co-workers perceived it differently in some situations.

3.4.2 Distraction due to the HWD but not by the HWD. The HWD is an additional device and the hardware and software had technical and usability issues challenging the supervisors. For example, a frequently observed and time-consuming problem occurred when the HWD did not connect to the WIFI, and the supervisor had to operate the HWD's menu or call the medical informatics department [O2_2, O3_2, O7_3]. Furthermore, most of the supervisors found it difficult to operate the device, especially actuating the touchpad and adjusting the hardware [S7, O4_1, O5_2].

Introducing an additional device, such as the HWD, to an already demanding OR environment bears the risk that supervisors become distracted by the HWD, causing compromised clinical work. However, despite being a very ubiquitous device, no participant reported any compromised clinical work, and we observed no situation where the HWD negatively affected the clinical performance of the participating supervisors. Besides the social pitfalls, the HWD did not adversely influence the supervisors’ working characteristics, as we observed and were told [O1_1, O4_3, O6_3, S3, Co1, Co6, Co8]. We could detect neither a lack of concentration nor lapses in practical clinical work. As summarized by Co6: “In medical terms, when I […] had contact with [the supervisors], I do not believe I could detect [distraction]” [Co6]. The HWD did not distract supervisors when they were using a direct or video laryngoscope, a bronchoscope, or even the ultrasound device when the ceiling light was switched off [O6_2]. All tasks, including teaching junior anesthesiologists, computer or smartphone use, and videoconferencing, were possible as usual without interference from the HWD.

We conjecture that the supervisor was aware of the immediate clinical situation and the HWD did not negatively affect their clinical performance. When the current situation was demanding and did not allow any distractions, the supervisor seemed to have been able to ignore the HWD. In summary, if a situation required concentration, the HWD faded to the background, but supervisors also used strategies proactively to avoid distractions.

3.4.3 Supervisors’ strategies to avoid distraction. As mentioned, supervisors knew that the HWD could distract them from their current work. First, some supervisors noticed that the sound of the alarms distracted or annoyed them, so they muted the HWD's speaker [O3_2, O5_1, S4] to prevent this condition. One supervisor even noticed that the whole HWD annoyed them [O4_2], so they consciously ignored the display most of the time. Others ignored the display when they had to concentrate [S6]. Second, some supervisors removed the HWD when they anticipated a demanding procedure [O1_3, O5_1]. Third, we observed one supervisor using the HWD to monitor a single patient via the detailed screen because they were, at that moment, responsible for this specific patient: “[I] set [the OR vital signs] on the detailed screen, because I was not interested in the other [operating] rooms and looked on the display [for the patient's vital signs] and not at the stationary [monitoring]” [S7]. By enabling the view of a single OR on the HWD, the supervisors removed visual distractions from the other ORs and did not have to turn their heads towards the stationary monitor in the OR.

4 DISCUSSION

In this section, we relate our results to existing empirical research and reflect on why these behaviors and attitudes occurred. To this end, we chose activity theory [ 35 ] because (1) it lets us consider organizational and personal, psychological needs of the supervisors and co-workers as motives that direct behavior (Figure 5 ), and (2) the concepts of development and hierarchy of activity are particularly suitable to describe changes over time. Next, we provide a short introduction to the concepts of motives, hierarchy of activity , and development , that provide us with opportunities for post-hoc reasoning about the observed behaviors and experiences and encourage future theory-based HCI research.

Activity theory [ 35 , 51 ] states that in an activity, a subject (i.e., the supervisor) interacts with an object (i.e., the motive of safe and efficient anesthesia [ 63 ]) and this interaction may be mediated by tools (i.e., the HWD). During the interaction, the tool is used to develop the object, but the use of the tool also feeds back and develops the subject. In other words, supervisors using an HWD may change or discover new actions which are conscious goals that aim at fulfilling the less tangible motive (i.e., make an inquire about an empty OR to achieve efficiency) and operations which are routine processes that form the lowest-level units of an activity (i.e., looking at a vital sign monitor when a junior anesthesiologist asks a question about the blood pressure). New actions and the transitions from actions to operations is particularly interesting for the longitudinal nature of the study. Because we are interested in the experience of the supervisors and co-workers in relation to the HWD, we also include personal motives in addition to the more organizational motive of safe and efficient anesthesia as an object in our analysis. Personal motive in the form of psychological needs have been considered in relation to UX in the sense that psychological need satisfaction of, for example, competence, autonomy, and relatedness result in a good UX [ 29 ]. Recent work acute care has shown that psychological need satisfaction is also a relevant factor in the interaction with technology [ 31 , 32 ]. The organizational and personal motives apply to all individual staff members involved in the activities of the operating suite.

After discussing the prolonged use of HWDs (Section 4.1) and the social and collaborative implications on co-workers (Section 4.2), we formulate design guidelines, aiming to support designers in developing and deploying usable HWD applications for high-tempo work environments. Finally, we consider the strength and limitations of the study and future research and provide a conclusion.

4.1 Effects of prolonged HWD use

We observed various factors related to HWD use evolving throughout the study. Participants usually quickly became used to the presence of the HWD on their heads and did not experience discomfort due to the device's weight or mounting pressure. In addition to participants becoming more familiar with the HWD hardware, we observed that they employed certain strategies to integrate the HWD in their work early in the study but found other individual ways of using the HWD after a long time. Using the vital sign numbers for clinical work was remarkably similar to a previous pilot evaluation in which supervisors used the HWD for only three hours [ 69 ] Schlosser et al. [ 69 ] also reported several instances of the theme of information on the HWD is more than just numbers . Similarities include, for example, checking vital signs due to alarms, or in times of lower workload, taking immediate actions due to critical vital signs in one OR. Moreover, they involved taking organizational actions if no vital signs are displays in an OR (i.e., induction might take long, and operation is being delayed) and removing the HWD to avoid distraction (sub-theme supervisors’ strategies to avoid distractions ). These use actions have similar characteristics to actions that supervisors do during their activities without the HWD. We conclude that the supervisor already knew the contribution of the actions to their motives and did not require repeated practice to understand the benefit. If an individual supervisor decided to use them, these actions could be integrated immediately into one's work practices and were immediately integrated into an operational level of an activity.

The prolonged duration of use in the present study revealed new use patterns and problematic issues that were unavailable in the previous, shorter study by Schlosser et al. [ 69 ]. Specifically, we observed new actions that supervisors consciously conducted to meet a motive. Supervisors used the HWD to educate junior anesthesiologists by making them aware of concerns and discussing potential issues. Supervisors also delegated more advanced patient cases because they could provide supervision with the HWD even if they were not always physically present in the OR. Vallurupalli et al. [ 80 ] discussed delegating advanced procedures to junior physicians wearing an HWD. However, in previous studies, proctoring was always enabled through a video feed that had to be manually initiated in case of a specific event [ 80 ]. The present research suggests that HWDs can provide supervisors with the action of task delegation while meeting the objective of safe anesthesia. At the same time, the action seems to enable junior anesthesiologist to experience autonomy and competence in leading more advanced patient cases.

Regarding vital sign monitoring, supervisors used repeated alarms on a patient's vital signs as monitoring information, not as urgent alarms needing to be attended to. Using alarms as trend information has also been reported when attending to single patients [ 64 ]. Another prominent effect was monitoring of other ORs while being with one patient for a long time or being involved in a demanding task and thus being able to request additional help earlier. Situation awareness declines when no current information can be perceived [ 84 ]. Participants vividly described how the information on the HWD counterbalanced this effect when they were not able to visit the ORs. Observing vital signs of patient for a longer period while being physically remote is a new action which was enabled by the HWD. Usually, the supervisors’ scattered access to monitoring data does not help them consider trends and the prolonged effects of specific drugs and previous research reported that looking at stationary monitoring instead of an HWD is the dominant operation [ 58 ]. We interpreted that this action needed more time (i.e., days) and repeated experience to develop from a conscious action to operations that are fully integrated in the work activities and function on an automatic level.

Another issue that might have been under- or overestimated in short-term studies [ 19 ] is the mixed effects of HWD on stress. An HWD can reduce stress by displaying the vital signs from multiple ORs because supervisors can check on patients whenever they want. Experiencing such stress reduction required supervisors to trust the HWD, which required time. Stress reduction might, therefore, only be observable in longer study periods and the effect may dominate when only a single patient needs to be monitored [ 58 ]. However, the constant presence of vital signs also indirectly implies an obligation to monitor the available information. Unless the information is actively ignored, it seems hard to dismiss because of its importance for achieving safe and efficient anesthesia. Furthermore, the supervisors could—or to some extent had to—apply existing operations of vital sign monitoring that run on an automatic level when using the HWD. Consequently, the “filter function” provided by the anesthesiologist in the OR—pre-sorting and evaluating information for the supervisors and contacting them in important cases—does not exist when using the HWD. The risk of the information displayed on a heads-up display excessively capturing attention has previously been described in driving [ 22 ] and navigation [ 34 ]. The present study accentuates that challenges related to HWD information filtering also exist in the context of multiple patient monitoring. Depending on the individual supervisor and their personal strategies for using the HWD, the constant availability of potentially relevant patient data occasionally resulted in more workload and stress. Even worse, important information, such as a very low heart rate, may alert supervisors, but the information on the HWD did not provide enough context to evaluate and entirely understand the situation. Similar effects were reported in a 20-minute HWD evaluation in a taxing medical simulator where remotely monitored patients deteriorated [ 43 ].

Finally, the prolonged duration of the study enabled us to investigate practical aspects of integrating HWDs in clinical practice, which has been described as an essential research objective for the field [ 5 , 42 ]. For the present study, participants had to set-up and start the HWD independently of the researchers. This phenomenon is different from previous studies in clinical environments in which researchers analyzed work practices of clinical staff and proposed use cases for HWDs and AR technology. However, they were unable to comment on practical aspects of day-to-day use as the proposed technology was never deployed in the intended work environment [ 49 , 70 ]. In the present study, the setup of the HWD technology by the participating anesthesiologists worked very well, even if they had breaks of several days or weeks between wearing the HWD. Nonetheless, it is important to consider that anesthesiology is the most technology-intensive healthcare discipline [ 74 ], and therefore our users might have been more open and confident with using technology and solving problems. We believe that one needs to consider the technical affinity of users in longitudinal studies to prevent potential frustration of users or drop-outs due to the issues with such an independent technology use.

4.2 Effects of HWD use on the experience of the co-workers

The data collected for this study demonstrates that a single-user HWD application affects the co-workers of the wearer, even though the HWD does not implement any communication features. We believe this insight is critical as most studies assessing collaborative aspects of HWD use focus on remote collaborative applications or the patient-physician relationship.

First, co-workers seemed to have more initial concerns about the potential negative effects of the HWD than the supervisors. The main concerns included being controlled by the supervisors and distractions from attending to the patient. We suggest that the concerns directly relate to a thread of autonomy for the junior anesthesiologists and a compromised motive of safe anesthesia for individual cases (i.e., the case that is physically attended at the moment of distraction). However, these concerns changed. Many co-workers stated an increased safety experience, and no co-worker stated any adverse effects of the HWD regarding the clinical work of the supervisors. The initial concerns about distractions were similar to general concerns about smart glasses for the consumer market [ 1 , 30 , 33 ]. However, in the present case, the HWD had a single application related to the work context, and co-workers experienced supervisors using the HWD. As described in the results, co-workers did not make the expected negative experiences and hence did not experience a thread to autonomy; to the contrary, they experienced an increase in safety which is likely responsible for the changed attitude towards the HWD.

Second, the HWD affected communication with co-workers. The increased situation awareness via the HWD required less updating between the supervisors and junior anesthesiologists communicating via the phone or face-to-face. Although only the supervisor wore the HWD, it enabled a common ground for discussion [ 11 , 12 ] and improved communication. In line with this explanation, the continuous and location-independent availability of information on an HWD in a marine transportation simulation also improved so-called closed loop communication [ 61 ]. In our case, the HWD also enabled efficient supervision of the junior anesthesiologists because supervisors could prioritize OR visits and phone calls based on the information on the HWD. The shared information about the vital signs and the perceived trends in vital signs by the supervisors provided a common ground that, to some extent, enabled communication operations as if the supervisor had been present in the specific OR. The HWD enabled a new action that allowed the use of existing operations and directly serves the motive of efficient anesthesia.

However, the co-workers did not frequently appreciate such an efficiency optimization because they valued the face-to-face contact with the supervisor to review the patient and their work and ask questions even when the patient was stable, and no immediate help from the supervisor was required. Similar concerns about the “over-optimization” of communication have been raised in the context of trauma surgery, where HWD could isolate the wearer from their environment [ 72 ]. We interpret that the more organizational motive of efficient anesthesia that drives the behavior of the supervisors contradicted the more personal motive of competency and relatedness of the junior anesthesiologists which may underly the actions of face-to-face contact and joint review of the patient.

Third, particularly in face-to-face conversations, the HWD resulted in the well-known effect of mobile and wearable devices: Glances on the HWD due to notifications during a conversation were perceived similarly as glances onto smartwatches or smartphones and were interpreted as signs of rudely neglecting the current conversation. If the new information on the HWD was important, the supervisors often prioritized this information above the current conversation. For the supervisors, the motive of safe anesthesia is relevant for several patients whereas the junior anesthesiologists take care of a single patient. Therefore, for the junior anesthesiologist, changing priorities away from their patient must also interfere with their motive. However, although co-workers negatively perceived such attention changes, it is worth mentioning that an incoming phone call about a clinical emergency in one of the ORs for which the supervisor is responsible caused similar behavior. However, the frequency of interruptions caused by the pervasiveness of the HWD is probably higher than that caused by phone calls.

Fourth, the perception of the level of distraction due to the HWD differed between the supervisor and co-workers. Similarly to studies with shorter HWD use [ 69 ], most supervisors did not perceive any distractions or burdens due to the HWD even after prolonged use. The co-workers did not share this impression which might be due to the social pitfalls in face-to-face conversations. In previous studies, an HWD increased operators’ responsiveness to information requests by 0.5 seconds [ 61 ] and looking at an HWD resulted in “freezing” in this position for several seconds [ 43 ]. Although operationally unimportant, such short delays may have been negatively perceived by co-workers during telephone conversations. Finally, as in a previous study with nurse anesthetists [ 58 ], for supervisors, distractions due to the HWD were related to usability issues and technical problems. Such distractions interrupted the otherwise good integration into the environment and workflow and an otherwise good UX [ 27 ]. After the first couple of days of use, the HWD seemed on an operational level for supervisors and therefore did not require conscious actions; only problems with the HWD changed the interaction from more automatic operations to conscious actions. However, the perception of the co-workers suggests that they perceived HWD use as conscious actions that may distract from a task at hand and possibly interfere with co-workers‘ safety motive for the individual patient for whom they are caring.

4.3 Reflecting on activity theory as a theoretical lens

Activity theory provides a structure that can be used as theoretical lens to suggest possible interpretations of empirical data [ 35 ]. We provide a reflection on the benefits of using activity theory to address the three HWD research gaps of missing longitudinal studies, effects on co-workers, and a holistic view on HWD use.

First, the concept of hierarchy of activity provided a framework to understand why some use patterns were obvious for the supervisors and immediately used (i.e., operations) and why some user patterns required time to accrue benefits (i.e., actions). Furthermore, the concept provided novel explanations by, for example, suggesting that checking the vital signs is an operation and that the continuous availability of vital signs will increase stress because of the automatic nature of the operations. The concept of development provided a way to describe changes between the hierarchy and how HWD use evolved overtime. The concepts of hierarchy of activity and development make activity theory an excellent framework for longitudinal HCI studies on HWD or any other technology.

Second, the object that represents the reason for the activity can constitute of several motives at the time and from several subjects [ 35 ]. The joint objectives of the activity allows an explanation of behaviors that are directed towards a common need but at the same time allows a consideration of individual needs that also direct behavior. The different motivational components provide, for example, an explanation for why junior anesthesiologist did not appreciate the more effective communication. In our view, the possibility of considering the motives of multiple people in a single joint activity provides a very good opportunity to study the effect of HWD on co-workers who do or do not use the same technology.

Third, Dey and colleagues [ 17 ] pointed out that medical augmented reality research was missing holistic accounts that address performance and experience. Besides enabling multiple motives, activity theory provides a framework that enables the researcher to consider organizational motives (in our case safe and efficient operation) and personal motives that relate to psychological need satisfaction [ 51 ]. Although our study did not include quantitative performance measures, in our view activity theory provides an elegant way of combining performance and experience in a single framework. Overall, we consider activity theory as a useful framework to address the research gaps we identified. As mentioned above, activity theory has been used as a conceptual lens for empirical analyses of results [ 14 , 35 ]; however, recently such post hoc analyses have been cited as a hindrance for advancing theoretically-based designs [ 52 ]. Although we agree with the latter position in general, we see value in our post hoc analysis because it provides us with possible explanations for why we observed the reported results and, as suggested below, may inspire future theoretically-based designs research. In other words, future HCI work should also use theory in an a priori fashion to inform design and the effects of a design. Furthermore, in our post-hoc application of activity theory, we focus on the subject-tool-object triangle and the effects of the HWD on UX. Engeström's activity system model [ 21 ] extends an individual activity to a systems activity. For organizational work, such a model would enable a more comprehensive analysis of a system like an operating suite and hence produce more detailed predictions of how specific aspects such as the division of labor might be affected by a tool such as an HWD.

4.4 Implications for HWD design, implementation, and evaluation

First, an HWD provides information, and designers need to consider the impact of this information on the user. More information may let a user understand a remote situation better; however, more information also requires more time and effort to interpret the information. Designers should, therefore, carefully evaluate how much information they display on the HWD at any given time. One approach is attention-driven HWD and AR interfaces [ 82 ] that promise to assess the workload of an HWD wearer through sensors and machine learning technologies and adjust the user interface and its information density appropriately. Another possibility may be a different approach to the design of the display. For example, ecological interface design [ 9 , 81 ] aims at arranging low-order sensor data in a fashion that supports the monitoring of higher-order integrated information. In the context of intensive care nursing, such displays have reduced mental demand when detecting critical vital sign changes [ 18 ]. We encourage designers to consider technology such as attention-driven interfaces when developing HWD applications for high-tempo work contexts or consider other means such as different design approaches for adjusting the complexity of displayed content.

Second, matching the auditory alerts to conventions in the target context may confuse. Designing auditory alerts for the HWD that resemble alerts from conventional information displays in the environment under study has the benefit that users are already familiar with the sounds (in our context, the alarms from existing vital sign monitors). However, the omnipresence of auditory information can confuse. One may consider systematically changing the sounds on the HWD to keep the sounds’ familiarity but add, for example, a distinct pitch to avoid confusion with the alarms from conventional information displays. Spearcons (time-compressed speech vocalizing physiological variables and values) may be a different auditory possibility [ 40 ]. Alternatively, one may use vibrotactile alerts for alarms [ 15 , 24 ]. Finally, the alarms could be provided via an ear speaker or bone conduction technology exclusively to the supervisor.

Third, hardware matters when HWD users are mobile and active while accessing information. In the present study, users repeatedly collided with co-workers and objects with the rather small HWD device. We believe that it is important for designers not to automatically pick the latest and most advanced HWD models for any work environment (e.g., Microsoft HoloLens 2; see [ 38 ]) but to consider if the advanced capabilities that such devices afford are actually required in the environment of use (e.g., AR). If this is not the case, the lightweight of “simpler” and smaller HWDs might be more beneficial to users [ 38 ]. For a comprehensive guide on picking the right HWD hardware for their target environment, designers should review Syberfeldt et al. [ 78 ]. Furthermore, in fast-paced and demanding work environments such as the OR, it is always possible, that the HWD must be worn for a longer time or removed or mounted quickly. The generally good wearing comfort of the HWD in the present study contrasts with previous shorter studies reporting that participants experienced headaches, neck strain, or fatigue due to wearing an HWD [ 45 , 58 , 69 ]. We believe that the main benefits of the headband compared to any frame mount are that it distributes the heavy weight of the hardware over a large head area, can easily be used with prescription glasses (Figure 2(A) ), and is less prone to moving.

Fourth, efficient interaction design for HWDs is crucial in high-tempo work environments and remains an unsolved problem. For example, the hardware buttons of the HWD in the present study are on the top of the viewer and were accidentally pressed when users removed the HWD or repositioned it. Speech interfaces are unreliable in loud environments, such as ORs, and can distract co-workers from focusing on an important task [ 58 ]. In-air gestures seem to be the most robust technology to interact with an HWD. However, they require obtrusive hardware and are only intuitive to use with AR interfaces. Researchers and designers should therefore consider combining AR control elements—buttons, sliders, and other inputs arranged around the user—with peripheral information displays to enable quick and robust user inputs. However, more research is required to assess the feasibility of such concepts.

Fifth, even HWDs designed for a single user need to consider collaboration with co-workers. One design implication would be to provide co-workers with a sense of the relative urgency of the displayed information. Co-workers may feel less snubbed if they could understand why, for example, the supervisor looked at the display or left immediately. Outward-facing status LEDs on the HWD could be a basic indicator for incoming, time-critical information [ 15 ]. In the context of a hierarchical organization, the HWD designers should also be aware of the effect of the HWD on the experience of the co-workers. If co-workers experience control and belittlement, their psychological need stratification of autonomy, competence, and relatedness may be undermined [ 31 ].

Sixth, it is important to demonstrate and explain the content and features of the HWD to co-workers. We believe that an increased and correct understanding of the HWD would have supported a faster familiarization of the co-workers with the HWD. Understanding that the information on the HWD can be urgent might help mitigate the social pitfalls. In addition, a wrong understanding of the content—such as the wrong understanding that induction and recovery room data were not available on the HWD—might jeopardize safety if a co-worker makes the erroneous assumption that their supervisor is automatically alerted as well. Finally, co-workers’ concerns about video recording could be resolved if the features of the HWD are explained.

Seventh, the length of the evaluation period seems to affect the insights. In the present study, several use patterns arose within hours, but some required days. We had the impression that learning was fast, and, in hindsight, a five-day user period might have been sufficient. In addition, having more observations in the beginning and fewer toward the end might have provided even further insights into the development of use patterns. Of course, the domain and the complexity of the HWD application need to be considered when deciding on the study period and possible observation days. We want to make researchers aware of these issues and suggest an explicit decision when designing the evaluation study. A recent review on longitudinal studies in CHI publications by Kjærup et al. [ 39 ] may help to inform such a decision.

Eighth, practical aspects of integrating HWDs into clinical practice are an essential research objective for the field [ 5 , 42 ] and researchers need to address practical aspects of day-to-day use more frequently. As described in the results, this worked well in the present study. However, anesthesiology is the most technology-intensive healthcare discipline [ 74 ], and therefore our users might have been more open and confident with using technology and solving problems. We believe that one needs to consider the technical affinity of users in longitudinal studies to prevent potential frustration of users or drop-outs due to technical issues that could not be resolved by the users.

Ninth, we encourage the holistic evaluation of HWDs. In particular, when HWD are used in actual, for example, healthcare, education, or industry settings, we consider a UX evaluation as important as a usability evaluation because the experience of users will have a profound effect on actual use. In such settings, our approach of considering activity theory and motives may be suitable. In shorter, single task, or laboratory settings, other UX evaluation methods have been successfully applied [ 17 ] and may also provide insights when using HWD in actual settings.

4.5 Strengths and Limitations

First, to the best of our knowledge, our study is the first longitudinal study on HWD use in a clinical environment. Typically, pilot studies or usability evaluations last only hours. We initially planned ten consecutive use days within 14 days. However, supervisors could not wear the HWD on so many consecutive days. The current use frequencies represent the ad hoc nature of clinical rosters. One may be concerned that 8-10 days (or 10-32 days in case of the co-workers) is not a very long period in comparison to other longitudinal HCI studies [ 39 ] but our study meets the criteria for longitudinal studies (at least two measures of a phenomena at different time points) and the best length of a longitudinal study depends always on the topic [ 36 ]. In hindsight and as discussed above, an even shorter period and observations on the initial days might have been more suitable in relation to our research questions.

Second, conducting evaluations in the field is challenging. We experienced technical issues with the HWD software (no acoustic alarms) and the information systems at the local hospital (server and WIFI problems) We also had problems when recognizing the vital sign values (incorrect recognition of vital signs such as “1” instead of “/”) and incorrect display of vital signs because of overlaps on the video stream of the stationary monitor we used to capture the vital signs. As described, these issues affected the experience of the supervisors.

Third, we used a mix of observations and interviews. We experienced a very good overlap with what author AM observed and considered important and what participants reported during the interviews. The observations were helpful to “verify” statements during the interview expressed in vague terms or starting with “I think I remember” or “it might have” and added a third perspective on the interaction with the HWD and co-workers. This triangulated data collection was beneficial when interpreting communication and distraction issues. In addition, the observation during early, mid, and late HWD use gave us the impression of the changes in the use pattern over time. Finally, we considered quality criteria for qualitative research [ 8 , 25 ].

4.6 Future work

In the present study, users used an opaque, monocular HWD, and we observed communication and social issues with co-workers in a single and very specialized domain. Future work should investigate these or other collaboration issues with different HWDs, such as AR displays. Similarly, research needs to consider other domains or industrial settings and forms of collaboration. The user had the responsibility for the co-workers in the present study. It would be interesting to investigate collaborations with different responsibility levels. Furthermore, research should investigate ways of preventing social pitfalls and misunderstandings between users and co-workers. As discussed in the implications for design, alternative ways of alerting the user may be less disruptive when the user is in a conversation. Alternatively, the research could attempt to inform the co-worker about specific aspects of the information on the HWD, such as the urgency to foster an increased shared mental model between the user and co-workers [ 47 ]. In our case, the primary idea of the HWD was to support multi-patient monitoring; however, the absence of vital sign information (i.e., no patient in OR and operation has not started) was valuable for organizational purposes. HWDs have also improved operators’ mobility in a marine transportation task [ 60 ]. Although we did not inquire about mobility, it is likely that supervisors’ mobility and task frequencies changed while wearing the HWD. Mobility and task frequencies may affect efficiency, and efficient organization is a major issue in operating suits [ 28 ]. Future work might focus on designing, developing and investigating HWD applications specifically supporting OR organizational work. Finally, interaction with HWD remains a big usability issue. More field research is needed to explore whether more advanced HWD such as the HoloLens can address these issues, or whether HCI research needs to come up and explore novel interaction technologies for HWD in general.

5 CONCLUSION

Building on previous research [ 16 , 19 , 71 ], we investigated the experience of prolonged HWD of anesthesiologists and how HWD use affected co-workers’ experience with their supervisors. Prolonged HWD use of 8-10 days provided novel insights compared to previous work investigating much shorter periods of use (e.g., [ 69 ]). The co-workers’ experience provided novel insights into the collaboration, communication, and perception of HWD use for multi-patient monitoring in the OR. Although the HWD was designed for a single user, HWD uses affected communication. It was a source of social pitfalls and was considered distracting by people interacting with the wearer. Activity theory provided a useful framework for understanding in the observed effects, and it helps to describe and understand the effects on a more abstract and general level. Future work on single-user HWDs should consider possible social context and collaborative issues in designing and evaluating HWD applications.
