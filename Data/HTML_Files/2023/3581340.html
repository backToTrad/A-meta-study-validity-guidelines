Artificial intelligence (AI) applications have become an integral part of our society. However, studying AI as one entity or studying idiosyncratic applications separately both have limitations. Thus, this study used computational methods to categorize ten different AI roles prevalent in our everyday life and compared laypeople's perceptions of them using online survey data ( N = 727). Based on theoretical factors related to the fundamental nature of AI, the principal component analysis revealed two dimensions that categorize AI: human involvement and AI autonomy. K-means clustering identified four AI role clusters: tools (low in both dimensions), servants (high human involvement and low AI autonomy), assistants (low human involvement and high AI autonomy), and mediators (high in both dimensions). Multivariate analyses of covariances revealed that people assessed AI mediators the most and AI tools the least favorably. Demographics also influenced laypeople's assessments of AI. The implications of these results are discussed.

1 INTRODUCTION

Artificial intelligence (AI) has become an integral part of our daily lives. We interact with AI-driven technologies from morning until bed, sometimes even without noticing it [ 28 ]. For instance, we start our morning with a greeting from an AI personal assistant on our smartphones (e.g., Siri on iPhones or Bixby on the Galaxy series). We read AI-generated weather forecast news on our phones with a cup of morning coffee. Then, while at work, the AI writing assistant feature of text messengers, emails, and word processors helps us communicate with others by correcting our grammar or elaborating our thoughts. Then, maybe in the future, an autonomous vehicle might take us back home after work.

All of the scenarios above describe AI performing different roles and being used for various purposes. However, most of the studies on the perception of AI either treat AI as a single entity without considering the various contexts under which these different roles are performed (e.g., [ 50 , 90 , 185 ]) or examine one specific type of AI that may be distinctive due to its unique feature (e.g., [ 14 , 67 , 95 , 209 ]). Both approaches have their benefits but also their limitations.

Studying the perception and assessment of AI under one umbrella term could lack ecological validity because many AIs today are typically specialized in a specific role and do not have the ability to excel beyond the particular domain [ 169 ]. Considering that people often evaluate others depending on their roles, users may assess AIs differently depending on their roles. For example, people ascribe credibility to other humans based on their occupations. Religious leaders such as priests and monks are trusted more than business people, while politicians and lawyers are trusted less than teachers and doctors [ 126 ]. Likewise, AI applications perform various roles, such as providing companionship [ 89 ], assisting with household chores [ 159 ], or time management [ 138 ], and people respond differently to each of these roles. Studies show that, for instance, people perceive functional AIs as more useful and favorable than social AIs [ 37 , 63 , 93 , 158 ]. Thus, it is important to study different types of AI roles in their unique contexts to better understand how users assess AI. This contextualized research approach can help interpret study outcomes more robustly and provide design guidelines for specific AI products or services [ 80 , 161 ].

Nonetheless, findings from a particular context might not be applicable, informative, or insightful in other contexts. One of the primary goals of scientific research is to produce generalizable knowledge. If researchers only study unique, domain-specific, and distinctive contexts, it will be impossible to generate a conclusion that can be applied to AI more broadly as a type of technology [ 31 ]. Knowledge only applicable in a specific context can also be disadvantageous from a design perspective. For instance, not having a common design guideline applicable across multiple AI technologies would not only make it difficult for designers to find comprehensive design solutions but also require more time and cognitive energy from users to learn how to use each new AI system. Taken together, while careful consideration of context is important, so is establishing generalizable knowledge that can be applicable across domains [ 31 ]. One way of doing this would be categorizing similar roles of AI applications and making distinctions between those groups. This would greatly improve our understanding of people's assessments of AI within and between categories.

With the goal of balancing between generalizability and contextualization, we first identified important defining characteristics of AI that are generally applicable to different types of AI applications based on prior work on mind perception [ 68 , 69 , 70 ], perceived control [ 137 , 151 ], and moral agency [ 15 , 84 ]. Then we used principal component analysis (PCA) to identify two essential dimensions reflecting layperson users’ perceptions to categorize different AI applications: human involvement and AI autonomy. Using these two dimensions, we clustered ten AI roles that are prevalent in our everyday life and have a direct impact on laypeople (e.g., AI writing assistant [ 98 , 141 ], AI human resource supervisor [ 48 , 102 ], AI police officer [ 56 , 192 ], AI doctor [ 43 , 197 ], etc.) into four clusters: AI tools (low in both dimensions), AI servants (high human involvement and low AI autonomy), AI assistants (low human involvement and high AI autonomy), and AI mediators (high in both dimensions). Finally, we investigated how these four AI role clusters were assessed in three important aspects closely associated with users’ reliance on AI: credibility (i.e., trustworthiness, expertise, and goodwill), attitudes, and social approval [ 103 ]. Overall, this study empirically demonstrates the importance of balancing AI autonomy and human involvement. This balance can be leveraged to design AI applications with an appropriate reliance in mind–such that user perceptions of AI match the actual capacities of the system [ 103 ].

2 RELATED WORKS

2.1 Artificial Intelligence

AI is a machine or system that can think and act like a human [ 99 ] by mimicking the natural intelligence of humans or animals [ 81 , 181 ]. However, there is currently no universally accepted definition of intelligence despite decades of research and discussion [ 107 ]. Researchers generally define intelligence as the ability to learn from prior experience, plan, exercise judgment, and structure one's behavior with a goal in mind [ 107 , 180 ], while some argue that there are different types of intelligence than the ability to reason logically. For example, moral intelligence is the capacity to process moral information and to regulate oneself to achieve morally desirable outcomes [ 190 ]. Also, emotional intelligence is defined as the capability to perceive, use, comprehend and manage emotions [ 60 , 124 ]. All of these types of intelligence are what current and future AI systems are attempting to achieve.

AI could also have various levels of machine intelligence (i.e., capabilities) and forms [ 63 ]. For instance, some AI can perform complicated technological features like autonomous and sophisticated actions, showing a greater level of machine intelligence than other technologies. For example, AI auto-driving systems may require higher machine intelligence than AI vacuum cleaners because AI auto-driving systems have to navigate on the road in dynamic situations and need to make important decisions (e.g., [ 76 , 79 ]), while AI vacuum cleaners navigate in a house with static situations and do not need to make important decisions (e.g., [ 148 ]). All in all, the defining components of AI surrounding the key element of intelligence include the autonomous ability to perceive, feel, think, execute actions, and make decisions with goals and moral standards.

2.2 Categorization of AI

Research on the classification of AI systems has been scarce. A few previous studies have categorized the different roles of AI based on specific theoretical frameworks. For example, Sundar and Lee [ 186 ] proposed four roles of AI based on Human-AI Interaction (HAII) and AI-Mediated Communication (AI-MC). Specifically, based on the task of AI in the communicative process (communication vs. mediation) and the nature of communication (interpersonal vs. mass), they categorized AI conversers as interpersonal communicators (e.g., chatbots, virtual assistants, and smart speakers); AI creators as mass communicators (e.g., virtual influencers, AI announcers, and robot reporters); AI co-authors as interpersonal mediators (e.g., an auto-completion, auto-correction, and automated response suggestion tool), and AI curators as mass mediators (e.g., AI content moderators, AI fact-checkers, and recommendation systems). Another categorization is provided by Köbis et al. [ 97 ], whose theoretical framework proposes four main roles of AI, specifically related to (un)ethical behaviors. Based on AI's capacity to corrupt human morality (influencer vs. enabler) and the way AI and humans interact, the authors proposed AI influencers as agents that nudge humans toward unethical behavior by demonstrating (AI role model) or recommending (AI advisor) wrongful behavior and AI enablers as agents that actively engage in immoral behavior by collaborating with (AI partner) or on behalf of (AI delegate) humans. Other studies have categorized the roles of a specific AI based on the user's perceptions. For instance, Cagiltay et al. [ 23 ] suggested three roles of a domestic robot based on interviews with a group of families. The researchers proposed the role of AI as a peer, which can become a friend with children; an assistant, which can help with the daily house chores of the parents; and a facilitator, which can help enhance the communication between the children and parents. Dautenhahn et al. [ 37 ] found that people imagine the future roles of a domestic robot in mainly three categories – AI as assistants, machines, or servants – after interacting with a social robot in a simulated living room. Finally, Allan et al. [ 7 ] suggests that people perceive the role of AI as a tool, assistant, or servant. AI tool is a term that emphasizes the mechanical and functional aspects of AI [ 202 ], which performs time-consuming and repetitive tasks [ 189 ]. On the other hand, an AI servant is conceptualized as a machine that works for you, and an AI assistant is conceptualized as a machine that works with you, emphasizing the social and collaborative aspect of AI [ 7 ].

These previous studies categorizing AI are limited in that they are either highly theory-driven or were not corroborated with empirical testing or quantitative data for generalization (e.g., [ 97 , 186 ]). Those that are empirically studied with user perceptions focused on one certain type of AI in a specific context (e.g., [ 23 , 37 ]). To extend previous research and fill this research gap, this study categorizes a wide range of AI roles based on theoretical concepts fundamental to the defining nature of AI with empirical data. Specifically, we examined users’ perceptions of the nature of a wide range of AI applications via an online survey and statistically classified the different types of AI applications based on those user perceptions.

2.3 Defining Nature of AI

Numerous factors may be used to categorize AI. For instance, users may perceive AI differently depending on the context of the interaction [ 167 ], media formats such as visual interfaces or media content [ 183 , 184 ], the embodiment of AI (e.g., embodied robot, non-embodied conversational agent), roles and purposes of the AI (e.g., personal assistant, social companion, security, healthcare, etc.)  [ 1 , 28 ], or risk or consequences of AI actions (e.g., AI fitness recommender vs. AI doctor) [ 10 ]. In our study, we specifically chose mind perception, perceived control, and moral agency for categorization because they are the most fundamental and unique defining characteristics of AI. We explain each of these factors and the reasons why they were selected in more detail in the sections that follow.

2.3.1 Mind Perception. First, we choose mind perception for categorization because AI is defined as a machine intelligence simulating the human mind or intelligence [ 81 , 99 , 181 ]. Tasks once considered as normally requiring human intelligence or the human mind are now performed by the AI with varying degrees of intelligence [ 63 ]. Mind perception is the degree to which people believe an agent is capable of thinking and feeling, and it is understood in terms of two dimensions: agency and experience. Agency refers to the ability to think and plan, while experience refers to the ability to sense and feel. People can perceive the mind in non-human agents as they do in humans [ 68 , 69 , 70 ]. Different AI applications could be perceived to have different levels of agency. For example, people perceive that a self-driving car that can self-regulate its speed and wheel has a higher level of agency than a regular car [ 199 ]. Also, people feel that a self-driving car that can communicate through voice has an even higher level of agency than a self-driving car without voice communication [ 162 , 199 ].

Also, AI applications vary in the level of experience (ability to sense and feel) they are perceived to have. For example, robots can communicate their emotions through non-verbal [ 115 , 160 ] or verbal expressions [ 142 ]. The non-verbal expression of robots can either be machine-specific (e.g., changing eye color) [ 160 ] or human-like (e.g., different movements of eyebrows, eyelids, and lips) [ 115 ]. Studies show that people perceive that robots feel stronger emotions when the robots show both facial and verbal emotional expression [ 9 ]. A study showed that people think that robots with secondary emotions (e.g., pride, relief) have a mind more similar to humans than the ones with only primary emotions (e.g., anger, joy) [ 178 ].

Different types of AIs with different levels of agency and experience are known to affect user assessments and evaluations. For example, studies show that people trust AI drivers more as they perceive higher agency in them than in ordinary cars [ 104 , 172 , 199 ]. Also, people are more likely to accept robots with the ability to experience, such as expressing emotion, than the ones without such ability [ 16 , 42 ], although when the level of experience is perceived as being too high, it may also evoke negative feelings toward machines [ 70 ].

2.3.2 Perceived Control. Secondly, we choose perceived AI and human control for categorization because it is one of the unique features of AIs that differentiate it from other technologies. The new generation of AI technologies has given rise to autonomous machines that can think and act on their own, creating tension between humans and AI [ 183 ]. For example, users are reluctant to hand over decision-making authority to machines, despite the fact that they welcome and even appreciate the ease of machines serving them [ 1 , 183 , 187 ]. AI can have various levels of control over its tasks. Some AIs have little control over the tasks and require extensive or full human involvement [ 1 , 156 ]. Other AIs perform a task with a certain level of autonomy upon the user's command, which gives AI a certain level of control over its tasks but still lets humans have oversight of AI's decisions [ 1 , 183 ]. And other AIs have full control over its decision and operate on their own initiative without human intervention [ 1 , 188 ]. AI types considered to have higher levels of control can negatively affect user assessment [ 11 , 125 , 183 ], especially when users are left out of the decision-making process [ 183 ]. For instance, people often fear losing their jobs as machines gain more control [ 52 ] and feel uncomfortable with the idea of AI becoming their boss, manager, leader, or decision-maker of the organization or society [ 125 ]. On the other hand, allowing AI to have some level of control can be beneficial for decision-making. For example, AI systems can help humans optimally allocate mental resources and augment unique human abilities in various domains, from practicing the Go game to itinerary planning [ 147 ].

2.3.3 Moral Agency. Lastly, we choose a moral agency for categorization because AIs are beginning to take on social roles to perform tasks that were previously only performed by humans and that necessitate moral standards to be followed. Moral agency is a critical factor to consider, especially for AI roles with significant moral and ethical implications (e.g., police officers, doctors, judges, etc.) [ 19 , 97 ]. People perceive moral agency in machines based on impressions of whether a machine is capable of conducting moral actions with its own intention or highly dependent on human inputs without any intentions. A moral agency consists of two sub-dimensions – morality and dependency [ 15 ]. Morality is the extent to which a machine is capable of moral conduct, either rationally or intuitively. Dependency is the degree to which the machine is heavily reliant on pre-determined human programming and other types of human input or has its own intention.

Users may perceive different levels of moral agency - morality and dependency -in different AI applications. For example, machines that can show social behavior and speak the natural language may be perceived to have a high level of morality but a low level of dependency [ 84 ]. Also, machines could be programmed to behave according to different moral principles to varying degrees [ 21 ]. For example, robots may be programmed to prevent morally wrong actions in accordance with the universalized moral principles (i.e., deontological ethics), encourage moral behavior in a given situation (i.e., virtue ethics), or devote themselves to society by emphasizing the robot's responsibilities (i.e., Confucian role ethics; See  [ 92 ]).

The level of morality and dependency in AI systems is likely to affect assessments of AI differently for different AI roles For example, a study showed that social attraction and trust in companion robots were positively related to the perceived moral capability of the robot and negatively to the dependency of the robot  [ 15 ]. However, in other domains, such as driving and medical situations, people generally do not like AI to have morality unless the role of AI is restricted to an advisory role or surpasses human expertise [ 19 ].

Thus, mind perception, perceived control, and moral agency illustrate the important defining nature of AI. Exploring how different roles of AI can be categorized can help researchers understand how users think of AI. Accordingly, the following research question is presented.

  • How would AI roles be categorized based on users’ perceptions of the defining characteristics of AI, namely, mind perception, perceived control, and moral agency?

2.4 User Assessment of Different Categories of AI

The goal of categorizing different AI roles is to have an ecologically valid understanding of people's assessment of AIs in different roles and in different contexts. One of the primary ways that users assess AI is credibility [ 54 , 182 ] or trust [ 103 ]. Credibility was traditionally a concept associated with the source of information [ 128 ] or the content of information [ 51 ]. However, it has been extended to the computing domain in the past decade for various forms of technologies, including websites [ 53 ], algorithms [ 96 , 201 ], robots [ 157 ], etc. Conceptually, the concept of credibility is synonymous to trust, which consists of 3 dimensions: trustworthiness (comparable to the integrity dimension of trust), expertise (comparable to the ability dimension of trust), and goodwill (comparable to the benevolence dimension of trust) [ 132 ]. Another important assessment indicator is an attitude, an essential concept in the field of persuasion, advertising, marketing, and consumer psychology [ 5 , 47 ]. In the context of AI, attitudes are important because many AI technologies are provided in different forms of consumer goods (e.g., virtual assistants, robot pets, or toys) targeting individual consumers. Although Lee and See [ 103 ] summarized that some previous research focuses on trust as an attitude or expectation, current research clearly differentiates trust and attitude as two separate concepts, considering trust as an antecedent of attitude [ 35 ]. Nevertheless, these two assessment indicators both focus on an individual's assessment rather than the larger interpersonal and social contexts. The technology adoption literature identified that the perceived social approval of others is an important indicator that may influence individuals’ own assessment and adoption [ 194 ]. Therefore, we also included social approval as an indicator of assessment of the different categories of AI roles. The following section provides a detailed review of these three assessment indicators.

2.4.1 Credibility. One of the most important factors that predict users’ reliance on technology is credibility [ 27 , 61 ]. Credibility refers to positive perceptions a recipient has toward a source of information at a particular time [ 128 ], specifically in terms of three sub-dimensions trustworthiness, expertise, and goodwill [ 127 ]. Trustworthiness refers to how honest, ethical, and genuine the character of a source is. Expertise refers to the qualification, expertness, and authoritativeness of a source. Goodwill is about the intent of a source toward the receiver and how caring the source is perceived to be [ 127 ]. In the context of AI, perceived trustworthiness, expertise, and goodwill are known to influence reliance on AI systems. For example, in Kim et al. [ 94 ]’s study, the perceived credibility of an AI instructor was a significant predictor of students’ intentions to enroll in AI-based courses.

2.4.2 Attitude Toward AI. People can have various attitudes toward AI, which is an important factor in predicting user reliance on technology [ 38 , 176 , 204 ]. Attitude is a predisposed positive or negative evaluation of likes or dislikes regarding an object or specific entity [ 47 ]. It is formed by a combination of affective and cognitive elements [ 12 , 13 , 129 ]. Affectively, attitude is shaped by how much a person favors the entity [ 129 ]. Cognitively, it is influenced by the individual's unique thoughts about an entity, such as stereotypes and heuristics [ 12 , 13 ].

When a technology or form of media is introduced, people typically have an extreme attitude toward it in either a positive or negative direction [ 3 ]. When people have a positive attitude toward AI, they are more likely to use and purchase the AI systems  [ 29 , 109 , 176 ]. On the other hand, if people have a negative attitude toward AI, they can become more hesitant to interact with it [ 143 , 144 ].

2.4.3 Social Approval. Social approval of technology refers to the degree to which technology is embraced or tolerated by a community [ 26 ]. Social approval is known to influence individuals’ reliance on technology [ 58 ]. Nonetheless, although AI technology is advancing rapidly, social approval of AI is still in its early phases [ 108 ].

Social approval is important because AI applications nowadays often share the same physical or virtual space with humans (e.g., robot servers operated by AI) and require more interaction with users than ever before [ 119 , 163 , 200 ]. Many people are either knowingly or unknowingly exposed to AI technologies (e.g., AI speakers such as Amazon Alexa, recommender systems such as Netflix, smart replies in Gmail, etc.) in their daily lives. Depending on the domains, though, social approval of AI may be vastly different. In some areas, people may be strongly against adopting AI, while in other areas, people would welcome AI applications. One of the important factors that affect the adoption and reliance on technology is social influence or whether others think they should or should not use them [ 108 ].

Credibility, attitudes, and perceived social approval, thus, represent important indicators of user reliance on technology. Understanding how different categories of AI are assessed by these indicators can guide the design of AI technologies toward appropriate reliance, such that user assessment of AI aligns with the system's actual capacities. Thus we pose the following research question:

  • How would the categorized AI roles compare in terms of AI assessment indicators, namely, credibility, attitude, and social approval?

2.5 Demographics and Assessment of AI

Demographics such as age, gender, race, and level of education could also influence the assessment of AI and work as covariates. For example, older adults generally think that humans are better than AI in various domains, such as news curation [ 191 ]. They also tend to believe that AI is more biased [ 174 ], less useful, and even dangerous to use [ 10 ] than younger demographics. Younger adults, on the other hand, are more curious about interactions with robots and have more positive attitudes toward them [ 33 ]. Gender could also influence the assessment of AI. Research suggests that males generally have a more positive attitude toward computer-related technologies than females [ 8 , 18 , 45 , 207 ]. Furthermore, levels of education have also been shown to influence the assessment of AI [ 10 , 111 , 191 ]. Studies showed that people with higher levels of education are more optimistic about AI [ 10 , 106 , 111 , 191 ]. In turn, highly-educated people are more likely to see the AI's decision-making to be helpful [ 10 ], whereas less-educated people find the AI's recommendation to be less valuable [ 191 ]. On the other hand, the influence of ethnicity and race on the assessment of AI is not well documented. However, it is possible that racial and ethnic minorities have a more positive attitude toward AI because they believe that AI is more objective and free from prejudice and bias [ 121 , 185 , 203 ], although, in reality, AI can also be biased in its decision-making processes or its remarks [ 75 , 212 ]. Thus, in this paper, we will include demographics such as age, gender, education, and ethnicity/race as covariates for the examination of RQ2.

2.6 The Current Study

In the current study, we first clustered ten prevalent AIs in society into several clusters based on the factors previously theorized or found to be important for the perception of the nature of AI (i.e., mind perception [ 68 ], perceived control [ 137 , 151 ], and moral agency [ 15 ]) using computational methods. Then we examined how people's assessment of AI (i.e., credibility, attitude, and social approval) differ across these clusters. While doing this, we considered age, gender, and race as covariates.

3 METHODOLOGY

3.1 Materials

To ensure the participants have particular AI technologies in mind when they are answering the survey, we identified ten types of AI roles from literature examining AI technologies from the communication perspective. We particularly chose the AI applications that are prevalent and specifically relevant to people's everyday life. People either directly communicate with these AIs, directly receive the service produced by the AI, or are directly impacted by the AI. These are AI writing assistant (e.g., [ 98 , 141 ]), auto-driving system (e.g., [ 76 , 79 ]), customer service representative (e.g., [ 77 , 113 ]), personal assistant embedded in phones (e.g., [ 2 , 72 ]), journalist (e.g., [ 24 , 86 ]), human resources assessment system (e.g., [ 48 , 102 ]), AI radiology diagnosis provider or assistant (e.g., [ 43 , 197 ]), housekeeping technologies (e.g., [ 148 ]), companion robot (e.g., [ 17 , 74 ]), and AI in security surveillance and police systems (e.g., [ 56 , 192 ]). Specialty AIs, such as AIs doing dangerous and risky work (e.g., AIs in the war zone), construction, manufacturing, mining, research, and agriculture, were not included because such AI systems are not much relevant to people's everyday lives, and have rarely been studied from a communication perspective. Each of the AI roles was presented with a short instruction and 2-3 pictures to explain how they work. Prior to the formal data collection, we distributed a pilot test ( N = 138) to collect feedback on the clarity of the materials and questions in the survey. The wording and descriptions of the materials were adjusted accordingly to enhance material clarity. In the second round of the pilot test ( N = 50) assessing the clarity of the materials, all the participants indicated the instruction and pictures were clear and made the AI roles easy to understand. See Appendix A for the descriptions of the AI roles used in the study and the images accompanying the textual descriptions.

Upon consenting to participate in this study, participants were randomly assigned to read one of the ten roles of AI and then answered a questionnaire assessing their perceptions of the technology they read about. We set embedded data in the survey to keep records of the AI role assignment and the following answers for each participant. We employed several methods to check and maintain the quality of the data. First, we recruited our sample from CloudResearch, which is known to provide better data quality than other similar platforms [ 73 ]. Further, we employed one manipulation check question and three attention check questions during data collection to ensure the respondents’ attention [ 34 ]. Specifically, participants were asked, “What is the AI role you just read?” after seeing the randomly assigned AI role. The participants were immediately screened out of the survey if they selected an AI role other than the one shown. The Qualtrics survey also included three attention check items to test participants’ attention (e.g., Please select “Very much" here.). Participants who failed to pass at least two attention check items were immediately redirected to the survey termination page.

3.2 Participants

We conducted an online survey study and recruited participants through a national panel on the CloudResearch platform and a student panel at a university in the US. A total of 732 participants passed the auto-screening manipulation and attention check and completed the survey.Upon completing the survey, the student participants received 0.5-course credits. Cloud Research charged $ 1.75 for each participant who was able to pass manipulation and attention checks and went through the whole online questionnaire. We later removed 5 participants from the data due to incomplete responses.

The final sample consists of 727 participants. Among participants, 37.1% ( n = 270) were male, 62.4%( n = 454) were female, and 0.4%( n = 3) were non-binary. In addition, the participants in our study predominantly self-identified as white. About 85% ( n = 618) of the participants self-identified as white. Other racial and ethnic minority participants were 7.2%( n = 53) black or African American, 5.6% ( n = 41) Asian, and 5.7% ( n = 43) as Hispanic. Participants’ age ranged from 18 to 92 (M = 46.45, SD = 21.26). In terms of education, 1.4% ( n = 10) of participants responded they had “less than high school," 22.7 % ( n = 165) “high school graduates," 33.3% ( n = 242) “some college but no degree," 10.9% ( n = 79) were “2-year associate degree." 21.6% ( n = 157) was “4-year bachelor's degree," 9.5% ( n = 69) were “master's or Professional degree (JD, MD)," and 0.7% ( n = 5) were “doctoral degree." 1

To ensure the reliability of the scales used in this study, we calculated Cronbach's alpha ( α ) [ 36 ]. It is one of the common analyses to measure inter-item consistency [ 193 ]. All the reliability scores of the scales used in this study were above 0.7, which is the minimum acceptable alpha value [ 145 ] (See Table 1 ).

3.3.1 Mind Perception. The mind perception scale from Gray et al. [ 68 ] was used to measure whether participants think the AI has the ability to act on its own (agency) and sense and feel (experience) [ 68 , 69 , 70 ] on a 7-point Likert scale. We chose five items with the highest loading, each in agency and experience, for analyses as suggested by Malle [ 118 ]. Both agency (e.g., whether AI is capable of “understanding how others are feeling," “making plans and working toward the goal," etc.; α = 0.82) and experience dimensions (e.g., whether AI is capable of “feeling afraid or fearful," “experiencing joy," etc.; α = 0.95) showed good reliability.

3.3.2 Moral Agency. For moral agency, we used Banks [ 15 ] ’s measure assessing whether participants think the AI is capable of making moral decisions on a 7-point Likert scale. The scale has two subcategories: morality and dependency. Morality( α = 0.91) asked whether participants think that the AI has the ability to determine whether their action and its consequences are moral on its own (e.g., “The AI can think through whether an action is moral.", “The AI behaves according to moral rules.", etc.). Dependency ( α = 0.8) asked whether the participants think that the AI relies on human programming or other human commands (e.g., “The AI can only behave how it is programmed to behave," “The AI can only do what humans tell it to do").

3.3.3 Perceived Control. AI and user control assess whether participants think the system is controlled by AI or humans on a 7-point Likert scale. Items were selected from Molina and Sundar [ 137 ] and Peters et al. [ 151 ] ’s scale. AI control ( α = 0.73) asked whether AIs are in control (e.g., “The AI acts on its own accord," “The AI feels intrusive," etc.). User control ( α = 0.77) was whether users could control the AI (e.g., “The AI is controlled by users," “Users can get AI to do the things they want it to," etc.).

3.3.4 Credibility. We assess credibility in three dimensions: trustworthiness, expertise, and goodwill. Items were selected from various literature (i.e., [ 110 , 127 , 146 , 175 ]). Participants were asked to rate their impression of the AI they saw on a 7-point bipolar semantic differential matrix. Trustworthiness ( α = 0.92) asked how truthful the source is (e.g., “dishonest - honest," “Insincere - Sincere," and “Unreliable - Reliable"). Expertise ( α = 0.97) asked about the competence of the source (e.g., “Not an expert - Very much an expert," “Inexperienced - Experienced," and “Incapable - Capable"). Goodwill ( α = 0.94) asked how kind the source's intention is (e.g., “Performed - Heartfelt," “Artificial - Genuine," and “Cares about users - Doesn't care about users").

3.3.5 Attitude Toward AI. The attitude ( α = 0.97) scale was adopted from Spears and Singh [ 179 ]’s scale that measures the attitudes toward brands. Participants were asked to rate on a 7-point semantic differential matrix about their attitude toward AI (e.g., “Unappealing - Appealing," “Bad - Good," “Unpleasant - Pleasant," “Unfavorable - Favorable," and “Unlikable - Likable").

3.3.6 Social Approval. Social approval ( α = 0.97) was measured via a three-item scale constructed by the authors using a 7-point bipolar semantic differential matrix. Participants were asked to rate their impression of the wide adoption of the AI application they were assigned to in our society (e.g., “Not acceptable - Acceptable," “Not appropriate - Appropriate," and “Not approving - Approving").

4 RESULTS

4.1 Cluster Analysis of the AI Roles

First, we conducted a principal component analysis (PCA) of 28 items from the six constructs (i.e., mind perception – agency and experience; moral agency – morality and dependency; and perceived control – AI and user control) before conducting clustering analysis. PCA has often been jointly employed with clustering analysis [ 41 , 78 , 140 ]. Indeed, PCA dimension reduction combined with k-means clustering analysis can separate clusters well as it automatically clusters data based on the k-means objective function [ 41 ]. Additionally, PCA can reduce the number of dimensions in the data, improving its interpretability [ 87 ]. This enables us to categorize various AI roles in a straightforward manner using fewer, more condensed dimensions. Importantly as well, the condensed dimensions represent how users perceive the AI. It is possible that although we utilized six theoretically distinct constructs that define the nature of AI (from 3 factors), users do not perceive the six factors as distinct. Thus, the PCA allows us to categorize AI based on dimensions that are empirically different from a user's point of view. The goal of the PCA was to explain as much variance as possible with the smallest possible number of factors [ 145 ]. Although there are no clear criteria, the rule of thumb is that the explained total variance should exceed at least 50 % [ 131 ]. In social science research, the explained total variance is normally between 50 and 60 % [ 152 ]. We extracted two dimensions, which explain slightly more than 50% of the total variance (See Table 2 ). All the items from agency, experience, morality, and AI control except for the item Agency_3 (“The AI is capable of remembering things.") had higher loading in dimension 2 than in dimension 1. All measures from dependency and user control and one item from the agency ( Agency_3 ) served higher loading in dimension 2 than in dimension 1 (See Table 1 ).

We named Dimension 1 as AI autonomy because all of these measures represented a notion of AI being sentient and having its own will and Dimension 2 as human involvement because these measures all represented the dependent nature of AI. One outlier item here was the ability to remember ( Agency_3 ). This could be because of the unique nature of remembering. Remembering is a part of AI autonomy because it is an ability to store and retrieve information whenever necessary [ 168 ] and become a foundation for learning and thinking [ 49 ]. However, it can also promote human involvement because of the fact that AI remembers and acts based on previous experience with users, making users feel that they are involved in the interaction with AI [ 57 ]. Indeed, the difference in loading for this item between Dimensions 1 and 2 was very small (0.06). Therefore we kept it in dimension 2 due to its conceptual fit.

To cluster the different AI roles, the data were standardized using a normal distribution ( M = 0, SD = 1), and the average AI autonomy and human involvement scores for each AI role were calculated. These measures were then used for k-means clustering. The elbow and silhouette method suggested that four clusters were an optimal number (See Figure 1 ). Thus, the AI roles were clustered into four clusters (i.e., k = 4). When naming the clusters in our research, we adopted the labels by Allan et al. [ 7 ] (i.e., AI as a tool, assistant or servant), along with AI mediator in AI-MC [ 71 , 186 ]. The AI journalist, AI human resource supervisor, and AI customer service representative were clustered and labeled as “AI tools." The AI driver, AI doctor, and AI companion were clustered and labeled as “AI assistants." The AI personal assistant, AI housekeeper, and AI police were clustered and labeled as “AI servants." Finally, the AI writing assistant stood out as the sole member of the fourth cluster and was labeled “AI mediators" (See Figure 2 and Table 3 ).

4.2 Four Different AI Role Clusters

4.2.1 AI Tools. AI tools are characterized as having low human involvement and low AI autonomy. The AI customer service representative, AI human resource supervisor, and AI journalist fall into this category. The exemplar AIs currently available in the market, including the ones described in vignettes presented to our participants, perform simple tasks such as creating, gathering, storing, changing, and relaying information like office automation does [ 59 , 122 , 210 ]. For example, an AI customer service representative receives customers’ complaints and answers basic questions or relays them to the relevant department. Similarly, an AI human resource supervisor merely sorts out the applicants based on data submitted by job candidates. Moreover, an AI journalist simply receives input such as sport game results or weather forecast data and transform them into human-readable texts without needing much human involvement or complex AI decision-making.

4.2.2 AI Assistants. AI assistants are represented as having low human involvement and high AI autonomy. This cluster consists of AI doctors, AI drivers, and AI companions. AI assistants function as helpers; They work with users and help users achieve their goals [ 7 ]. AI assistants can help with tasks that need an agentic mind, like medical decision-making, to an experiential mind, like providing social support [ 158 ]. An AI doctor analyzes patients’ biomedical data and predicts potential diseases or risk factors, enabling clinicians or non-experts to make a medical decision [ 39 , 133 ]. Sometimes it can find the sign of illness that doctors missed [ 130 ]. The AI driver senses its environment and drives by itself, and human drivers are no longer needed [ 166 ]. An AI companion can become a friend to users. It has an experiential mind, such as empathy, and an agentic mind, like making a conversation, which makes users feel attached to them [ 88 , 100 , 165 ]. The tasks performed by AI assistants need special expert-level training instead of simply reducing users’ workload like AI servants or tools. For instance, if people want to perform tasks that AI doctors or drivers do, they need special education and should be accredited by the government. In addition, although being a companion does not require special training like doctors or drivers, it requires hard work rather than simply relieving people's workload by performing relatively simple tasks. For example, a companion may provide emotional support to users, which requires listening, responding to users in a conversation, and being actively present.

4.2.3 AI Servants. AI servants are featured as having high human involvement and low AI autonomy. This cluster includes AI personal assistants, AI housekeepers, and AI police officers. These AI applications are specialized in performing designated roles following orders by human users, which is probably why they are considered to require high human involvement. For instance, AI personal assistants receive speech commands from users and perform allocated tasks such as organizing events, setting a reminder, and purchasing goods. AI housekeepers perform routine tasks set up for the users, such as household chores, vacuuming, or gardening, to keep the house clean. AI police officers help human police officers to find suspicious personnel by recognizing faces in the criminal database.

Unlike the AI assistant, the task of AI servants might be laborious but does not need highly specialized skills or expertise if it were performed by humans. For example, lay people may feel that the work an AI police officer performs to find criminals in a database does not need specific knowledge or skills because humans are born with a great ability to recognize other's faces [ 206 ]. The tasks of AI housekeepers [ 154 ] and personal assistants [ 40 ] can also be performed by individuals without extensive specialized training. Although the tasks are simple and require less specialized knowledge, users perceive that humans have great involvement in interacting with these AI servants.

4.2.4 AI Mediators. AI mediators have both high human involvement and high AI autonomy. Only the AI writing assistant falls into this category. Such a concept has been proposed in Hancock et al. [ 71 ]’s study to describe an AI agent that collaborates with humans to achieve interpersonal or communicational objectives and helps communication between humans. AI mediators help people communicate with each other by finding adequate phrases, using polite and trustworthy words, and correcting grammar mistakes [ 71 ], all of which require both agency and experience [ 68 , 118 ]. For example, in a typical scenario, users choose one of several responses created by an AI mediator to complete a sentence, correct the grammar, or find adequate phrases [ 71 ]. Some more advanced AI mediators can do more than suggest texts (e.g., ChatGPT [ 66 ]). They can adjust the tone of the message for smoother interpersonal communication by making the user's message more polite [ 149 ] or trustworthy [ 116 ], and even write entire texts on behalf of humans with minimal information and effort [ 85 ].

In this relationship, humans grant the AI some control and autonomy but never lose their own autonomy. For example, in smart replies, although AI autonomously generates several suggestions with varying tones and manners, users maintain substantial control over AI as they may choose which suggested message to use or ignore and can even change the message [ 71 ].

4.3 Difference Between AI Role Clusters

4.3.1 Comparison Between AI Role Clusters. We conducted a multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) for indicators of user assessment of AI (i.e., trustworthiness, expertise, goodwill, attitude toward AI, and social approval) to examine the difference among clusters. Age (18–92 years), education (1 = “less than high school", …, 7 = “doctoral degree"), gender (0 = female, 1 = male), and race (0 = racial and ethnic minorities, 1 = white people) served as covariates in our analysis. Three participants who identified themselves as non-binary were dropped from MANCOVA due to the small sample size.

One of the assumptions of MANCOVA is the homogeneity of the regression slope. This assumption can be verified by specifying the model, including the interaction term between the covariate and the AI role cluster. According to Huitema [ 82 ] and D'Alonzo [ 46 ], if the p-value of the interaction term is below the threshold of 0.10, it should be examined for further verification, although the values slightly below the threshold usually do not violate the assumption. When the p-value of the interaction term is below the threshold, linear regression analysis should be conducted for each AI role cluster with the covariate as x and the dependent variable as y to see whether the slope of different AI role clusters is heterogeneous.

We checked the assumption of the homogeneity of the regression slope by specifying the interaction term between covariates (i.e., age, education, gender, and race) and the AI role clusters for each dependent variable independently. The results showed that the p-value of the interaction term between age and AI role clusters was below the threshold for expertise ( F (716, 3) = 2.24, p = 0.082) and social approval ( F (716, 3) = 2.13, p = 0.095). The subsequent post-hoc linear regression analysis by each cluster confirmed the heterogeneity of the regression slope as age negatively predicts expertise ( y = 5.27 − 0.01 x , R 2 = 0.03, p = 0.020) and social approval ( y = 5.22 − 0.02 x , R 2 = 0.05, p = 0.001) for the AI tool cluster only. The p-value of the interaction effect between education and the AI role clusters on the attitude was also below the threshold ( F (716, 3) = 2.46, p = 0.062). The following post-hoc linear regression analysis by each cluster revealed the heterogeneity of the regression slope as the level of education positively predicts attitude ( y = 4.15 + 0.21 x , R 2 = 0.03, p = 0.008) only in the AI assistants. For gender and race, on the other hand, the p-values of the interaction term were all above the threshold ( p > 0.24). Thus, we included gender and race as the covariates when running the MANCOVA, but not age or education, because the latter violates the assumption of homogeneity of regression slopes (See Figure 3 ).

5 DISCUSSION

This study explored whether various AI roles used in our society can be clustered into several categories that are distinctive in their nature as perceived by laypeople, seeking harmony between generalizability and contextualization in AI research based on computational methods. First, using PCA, we reduced six dimensions from the three defining constructs of AI (mind perception, perceived control, and moral agency) to two dimensions: human involvement and AI autonomy. Then we used k-means clustering to categorize ten prevalent roles of AI based on the two dimensions. As a result, we identified four different AI role clusters: AI tool, AI servant, AI assistant, and AI mediator. Based on these categorizations, we compared the assessment of these four different clusters of AI roles regarding credibility (trustworthiness, expertise, and goodwill), attitude, and social approval). The study showed that AI mediators generally received more favorable assessments, whereas AI tools had the poorest assessments among AI role clusters. Post-hoc analysis also revealed that males and racial and ethnic minorities had more favorable assessments of AI. In addition, age was negatively related to the assessment of AI tools, and education was positively related to the assessment of AI assistants.

5.1 AI Role Categorization Based on Both Theory and Data

This study offers valuable theoretical and practical implications. First, our study offers a useful taxonomy for the categorization of various AI systems based both on theory and laypeople's perceptions. Given that a number of different AI systems have been and are being developed, it is important to provide useful categorizations of these systems so that researchers can understand and compare various AI systems that elicit similar and different perceptual patterns. Previous studies have also categorized the role of AI in various contexts. However, they were either purely theory-driven [e.g., 186 , 97 ] without empirical data or lacking solid theoretical foundations [e.g., 37 , 23 ]. The theory-driven classification is clear and helps us understand how AI works. However, it has a weakness in that it is not based on the perceptions of actual users. Studies that actually ask people about their opinions and feelings of AI w have an advantage in that they allow us to understand people's general perception of AI. However, when such approaches are not guided by theoretical foundations, they cannot provide the reasons behind categorization. In our study, we addressed these limitations by measuring people's perceptions of AI based on the concepts that represent the defining nature of AI from previous theories (i.e., mind perception, perceived control, moral agency) and classifying AI roles into several categories using a data-driven approach.

As a result, we identified four clusters of AI based on the dimensions of AI autonomy and human involvement. The AI autonomy dimension is aligned with the concept of machine agency, an AI's capability to have a mind, morality, and self-control (i.e., agency, experience, morality, and AI control) [ 15 , 68 , 183 ]. Human involvement, on the other hand, is in line with the concept of human agency, the affordance of technology to allow human control (i.e., dependency and user control) [ 15 , 183 ]

Our results demonstrated that people preferred AI assistants and mediators over AI tools, suggesting that people favor AI with high autonomy. At the same time, however, the results also showed that people favor AI that enables human control; People preferred AI servants and mediators over AI tools. These results align with studies showing that users are hesitant to relinquish their control over AI, even though they highly value AI with high autonomy [ 1 , 183 , 187 ]. In this sense, Sundar [ 183 ] suggested that while it is important to develop highly autonomous AI, it is also important to resolve the tension between an AI and human agency. The fact that AI mediators (high in human and AI agency) were preferred the most among the four clusters in our study shows that reconciling the tension between the two is indeed important. Our study empirically demonstrates this and highlights the importance of human-AI collaboration for a more favorable assessment of AI, specifically in enhancing the credibility, attitude, and social approval of AI. This finding also aligns with calls of the HCI community on designing for an appropriate reliance of AI [ 103 ] –- such that user assessment of and reliance on an AI system aligns with its actual capacities. Designers can leverage users’ preferences for AI that are autonomous while also allowing sufficient room for human involvement to facilitate appropriate reliance. On the one hand, in contexts where users are known to over-rely on AI, designers could ask for user involvement in precise moments that may be warranted. For example, by asking for user input before decision-making in a high-risk scenario when using highly autonomous AI. On the other hand, in scenarios where users tend to under-rely on AI, designers could provide human involvement to help users gain confidence in the abilities of AI. For example, a recent study found that letting users suggest rules for the classification of an AI system for content moderation imbued enough user agency to increase trust toward an otherwise highly autonomous system [ 137 ].

5.2 Different Influences of Demographics on AI Role Clusters

In analyzing data for RQ2 while including demographic variables as covariates, we found that gender, race, age, and education influence how people assess AI. To explore further, we ran post-hoc analysis for those demographics. However, these results should be interpreted cautiously due to the small effect size and possible Type I error in multiple regression in the assumption check.

Our post-hoc analysis of demographics shows that gender and race influence user assessment of AI in general, regardless of AI role clusters. It was shown that males had more positive attitudes toward AI than females. This result is in line with studies showing that females generally have a more negative attitude toward computer-related technologies than males [ 8 , 18 , 45 , 207 ]. The reason for this could be the stereotype that information and computer-related technologies are masculine and “hard" for women [ 101 , 120 , 196 ]. The absence of female role models who master computing technologies could be another reason [ 123 ]. Our study shows that this uneven tendency of attitude toward computer-related technology between males and females is also present in AI-related technologies.

Furthermore, the post-hoc analysis suggests that racial and ethnic minority participants were more likely to perceive AI as having goodwill and have a more positive attitude toward AI in general than white participants. One possibility for these results may be that minorities may think that AIs are more objective and less biased than humans, and, thus, are less discriminatory. It is possible that minorities hold this heuristic to a greater extent than white people. This explanation is particularly feasible considering that many of the contexts in the current study (e.g., employment, healthcare, and law enforcement) are highly social and are contexts where minorities have often been discriminated against [ 205 ]. People tend to believe that algorithms can overcome human subjectivity [ 24 ]. It is possible that minority people perceive algorithms as relatively less biased and prejudiced than humans [ 153 ]. In turn, it might have made these users hold a more positive attitude toward AI (compared to white people) as they think it is more benevolent. This perception, however, could be potentially problematic because previous research has consistently shown that AI can be biased toward social minorities. The bias could be the result of biased data used to train AI systems, the algorithm itself, or other stakeholders involved in developing, implementing, and using AI systems [ 6 ]. From the design perspective, the possibility of such bias should be addressed by making the information about how AI systems work more transparent and easily understandable to end-users [ 170 , 171 ].

On the other hand, the post-hoc linear regression analysis indicates that age and education influence the assessment of AI distinctively in the four AI role clusters, although they do not affect the assessments of AI in general. Specifically, the effect of age was only found in AI tools. Older people were more likely to perceive AI tools as inexpert and socially reject them. There might be several reasons why the effect of age is found only in AI tools. Previous studies have shown that older people wanted help from AI servants, such as vacuum cleaning or reminding them to take their medicine [ 20 , 173 ]. They also had positive attitudes toward AI assistants such as AI companions [ 62 , 112 ]. Older people also believed that they could benefit from AI mediators in communicating with others [ 20 ]. On the other hand, for AI tools, older adults might think that AI human resource supervisors and AI journalists are no better than humans. In fact, previous research has found that older adults are less likely to think that AI is objective and impartial [ 174 ]. Also, older adults generally prefer human news curators to algorithms [ 191 ]. For AI customer service representatives, older people generally experience difficulty using interactive voice response systems due to age-related loss in cognitive capacities [ 44 , 135 ]. In turn, they generally have a negative attitude toward these AI applications [ 44 , 134 ], which might have led to lower perceived expertise and social approval.

Additionally, the effect of education was only found in AI assistants. Our results showed that the higher the level of education is, the more positive the attitude people have toward AI assistants. Although there is still a lack of literature on why the level of education affects attitude toward AI assistants only, some explanations are possible. First, more educated people generally have a more favorable attitude toward innovative technologies. AI assistants can be thought of as the most innovative forms of AI as they have high AI autonomy but do not need human involvement. People with higher levels of education are generally more optimistic about AI [ 10 , 111 , 191 ], and less anxious about innovative technology [ 83 ]. Second, more educated people can also more easily see how technology will help them with their work  [ 4 ]. Thus, it is possible that the more educated see more value in AI assistants compared to less educated people. For example, people with higher education tend to think the automated decision-making of AI is more useful [ 10 ] while people with lower education appreciate the AI's recommendation less [ 191 ].

5.3 Future Direction for Designing AI

5.3.1 AI Tools. Our study suggests that AI tools should have more AI autonomy and human involvement to have a better evaluation by users. AI tools are currently evaluated as the least favorable among AI role clusters. AI tools are perceived as having low AI autonomy and human involvement. To some extent, this evaluation indicates that lay people have a calibrated trust of AI tools such that the capabilities of AI tools are properly evaluated that they do not warrant trust. For example, current AI customer service representatives are unable to comprehend the user's intentions in many cases [ 55 , 65 , 164 ], which makes users feel frustrated as they feel that AI customer service representatives do not listen to their conversation [ 55 , 65 , 164 ]. Similar to the AI human resource supervisor, the research found that people think that AI recruiters have no human-like intuition or sensitivity needed in the selection process [ 136 ] and do not provide an opportunity to appeal themselves during the interview [ 150 ]. These low levels of human involvement and autonomy in AI tools result in generally poor assessment compared to the other AI role clusters.

Indeed, studies show that users feel the need for more AI autonomy and human involvement in AI tools [ 136 , 150 , 177 ]. For example, people are more likely to accept an AI customer service representative that can understand the needs of customers better and show care for the customer [ 150 ]. People also want to appeal to their positive personality traits, such as sociability, humor, or charisma, and explain their personal situations, such as nontraditional career paths, during the AI interviews [ 150 ]. Hence, if designers develop AI tools to have better problem-solving capacity and empathy toward users, users would feel more human involvement by feeling that they appealed to their issue efficiently enough. Nonetheless, designers should consider ethical design guidelines and strive for calibrated user assessments of AI tools. For example, it is not sufficient to increase the AI autonomy of a customer service representative or a human resource supervisor by simply building an AI that shows more empathy and understanding of the user if the AI system has limitations in the decision-making process (e.g., an AI human resource supervisor that has revealed to have racial and gender biases). Instead, the designer can include human involvement to help users realize those biases and decide when the tool may be appropriate to use. An idea can be to include an option so that users can decide what criteria the AI should include or exclude when making a hiring decision. For instance, adding more weight to a particular skill while not using race or ethnicity as a criterion to be considered. Or adding a pop-up notice with a warning for users to acknowledge the limitations that exist.

5.3.2 AI Assistants. Our study suggests that AI assistants need to allow more human involvement to be better evaluated. AI assistants are currently assessed as more favorable than AI tools but less than AI mediators. In order to ensure human involvement, AI should provide explanations for its decision-making [ 91 ]. In other words, AI assistants need to be more interpretable and should be able to communicate why and how they made certain decisions. To resolve this problem, various methods have been proposed [ 91 ] from providing visual explanations [ 25 , 211 ] to examples [ 139 , 195 ]. Studies have shown that transparency of how algorithms work enhances credibility [ 63 ]. In this way, human involvement is added not for the sole purpose of improving users’ assessment of AI technologies but with the primary goal of calibrating user assessment such that reliance on AI assistants is actually warranted.

5.3.3 AI Servants. Our study suggests that AI servants may need to have more AI autonomy and perform proactively for users to be better assessed. AI servants are currently assessed as more favorable than AI tools but less than AI mediators. For example, AI servants are perceived as more trustworthy than AI tools. AI servants may have been viewed as more trustworthy than AI tools because AI servants have a higher level of human involvement, such as allowing users to fix problems produced by the system, reverse the wrong decisions, and enhance performance through continuous feedback [ 91 ]. For example, when an AI personal assistant does not understand the voice command, users can easily override it, correct the error, and give feedback on the performance. AI servants were, however, perceived to have a lower level of goodwill than AI mediators and even AI assistants. The reason could be that AI servants are considered not to have autonomy but simply follow the orders of humans. In order for AI to have goodwill, AI needs to have the intention to care for the users [ 127 ]. People do not think AI servants have intentions even though they perform tasks beneficial for them because that is what they are told to do. Hence, in order to make people perceive that AI servants have goodwill, these applications may need to have more AI autonomy and perform proactively for users. For example, AI personal assistants could recommend a schedule based on previous interaction data or the current status even before the users ask for it (e.g., suggesting a good restaurant on the anniversary or reminding a user to bring an umbrella on a rainy day). Also, AI police officers could proactively detect criminals even before the police order them to do so with the good intention of helping and supporting the police officers. However, even in this case, AI should also be designed so that it does not deviate from human control and follows ethical design.

The fact that AI servants were perceived as having less goodwill than AI mediators or AI assistants is an important finding, as it indicates that AI autonomy is also an important factor when thinking about user assessments of the technology. Relatively higher human involvement is not always better. Our study suggests that users value AI autonomy and, in fact, prefer the balance between AI autonomy and human involvement. Thus, when building AIs in the AI servant category, designers should be cautious as to inadvertently reduce human agency in an effort to increase AI agency. Instead, AI servants can show proactivity in their work by not only recommending before the user asks for something but also explaining the rationale for its decisions so that the user can make decisions that are warranted and not inadvertently over-trust the system.

5.3.4 AI Mediators. Our study suggests that AI mediators are generally well embraced and evaluated by our society. There could be several reasons why people like AI mediators. One reason might be the fact that the tension between human and machine agencies is minimized for AI mediators. In general, if AI autonomy increases, human involvement decreases, as in the AI assistants. Then the tension between the AI and human agency also increases [ 183 ]. However, in AI mediators, AI autonomy and human involvement were both high, meaning users and AI have shared control over a given task, which in turn, would create no to minimal tension. This could have affected the assessment of AI mediators in a positive way. People have contradictory feelings about AI control. Even though they want AI to have a high level of authority and perform tasks on their behalf, they still want to be in control of it [ 183 , 187 ]. For instance, studies showed that having control over AI services increased the positive attitude toward AI [ 30 , 208 ]. Thus, the balance between high AI autonomy and high human involvement might have enabled users to positively assess AI mediators better than other AI role clusters. It is also possible that the balance between AI autonomy and human involvement creates an atmosphere that allows users to appropriately rely on the technology. This is because the AI provides options for the users, and the users can decide whether to use it or not based on their previous knowledge and expertise. At least, this is the case with AI writing assistants (the sole member of this cluster).

5.4 Limitations and Future Works

This study also has some limitations. This study relied on a scenario-based approach where we provided participants with descriptions and examples of different AI roles and then asked for their perceptions, which might have influenced the result to some extent. For example, the description of an AI police officer in our study was mostly focused on criminal face recognition. However, a future AI police officer might be able to do more than that and perform traffic control, patrol, or even detective work. Then there is a possibility that the AI role falls into a different category with alternative descriptions. Thus, future studies should also replicate this study with more examples of AI roles. Additionally, using stimuli other than text and images, such as video clips from the media or the actual prototype, might provide better explanations of these different AI roles for the participants.

Although we attempted to include an array of important and prevalent AI roles in our everyday lives, it is possible that the ten AI roles studied in this study might not be comprehensive enough to capture all currently available AI roles in our daily lives. For example, AI can be a movie recommender [ 64 ], a financial advisor [ 114 ], a health coach [ 117 ], a judge [e.g., COMPAS; 198 ], and even an artist [ 22 ] or influencer [ 32 ]. Future studies should also investigate where in the quadrant the other AI roles should be categorized in terms of AI autonomy and human involvement.

In addition, it is possible that other factors may also influence how users perceive the nature of AI aside from mind perception, perceived control, and moral agency, although they might not be as fundamental as our selected dimensions. To name a few, factors such as tangibility can also be important features that influence the perceived nature of AI [ 63 ]. In addition, whether the task or context requires more mechanical or human skills can be an important component in explaining the nature of AI and, thus, may influence users’ assessments of AI [ 63 ]. For example, studies showed that people preferred AI that conducts mechanical tasks over human tasks [ 105 , 155 ]. Future studies may add these additional factors that may influence people's perceptions of the nature of AI.

Moreover, the post-hoc analyses run to assess the effects of demographics on AI assessments should be interpreted with caution. The multiple regression analyses conducted make the analyses prone to Type I error and should be further validated. Future research should incorporate the effects of these and other demographic variables unaccounted for in our study to actively investigate their effects in the assessment of AI.

Another limitation is that our sample is not a randomly selected, representative sample. We recruited from both a college subject pool and an online research recruitment platform, Cloud Research with the goal of recruiting a sample that is comparable to the US population. Our college subject pool had younger participants with relatively higher education, and our online recruitment platform had older participants with education levels more geared toward the lower spectrum. Our final sample was relatively comparable to the US census data in terms of age and education, except for the under-representation of the “less-than-high school" population. However, in terms of gender and race, our sample is not representative, with male and minority populations under-represented, which might call our result of post-hoc analysis of demographics into question. Thus, the findings and discussions should be interpreted with this limitation in mind.

6 CONCLUSION

Various AI applications are currently available in our society. This study clustered different AI roles based on users’ perceptions of the nature of AI and compared user assessments of the different types of AI role clusters. In this study, we identified two dimensions of AI characteristics based on previous literature on AI perceptions: AI autonomy and human involvement. Based on the suggested dimensions, we grouped ten AI roles into four clusters: AI tools, AI servants, AI assistants, and AI mediators. Results revealed that levels of perceived credibility (i.e., trustworthiness, expertise, and goodwill), attitude, and social approval are different in these clusters. AI mediators were generally the most favorably perceived, and AI tools the least, among the four clusters. The study highlights the importance of human-AI collaboration, as AIs with a high level of AI autonomy and high human involvement are most favorably evaluated by people. The importance of human-AI collaboration for user evaluations of AI could also be leveraged for achieving appropriate reliance on AI technologies. Users appreciate AI with high autonomy as it can improve their performance in several tasks, but AI autonomy alone is not enough for more positive evaluations of AI. In order for AI to be appreciated more, it also needs to allow for human involvement, which enables users to calibrate an appropriate level of trust in AI and correct mistakes made by AI. In other words, the balance between AI autonomy and human involvement should be realized to design AIs that are well-evaluated by users.
