Deep reading fosters text comprehension, memory, and critical thinking. The growing prevalance of digital reading on mobile interfaces raises concerns that deep reading is being replaced by skimming and sifting through information, but this is currently unmeasured. Traditionally, reading quality is assessed using comprehension tests, which require readers to explicitly answer a set of carefully composed questions. To quantify and understand reading behaviour in natural settings and at scale, however, implicit measures are needed of deep versus skim reading across desktop and mobile devices, the most prominent digital reading platforms. In this paper, we present an approach to systematically induce deep and skim reading and subsequently train classifiers to discriminate these two reading styles based on eye movement patterns and interaction data. Based on a user study with 29 participants, we created models that detect deep reading on both devices with up to 0.82 AUC. We present the characteristics of deep reading and discuss how our models can be used to measure the effect of reading UI design and monitor long-term changes in reading behaviours.

1 INTRODUCTION

Over the past five thousand years, humans have developed the ability to read and to read deeply, spending years developing an array of sophisticated cognitive processes that include inferential and deductive reasoning, analogical skills, critical analysis, reflection, and insight [ 54 ]. In contrast with skim reading where readers only process factual and explicit information stated in texts to regurgitate, deep reading requires readers to interpret implicit information, make inferences, analyze, reason, and reflect on it based on prior beliefs [ 42 , 55 ]. These deep reading skills have followed the development of widespread literacy and the ability to produce written documents in astounding quantities, especially since the Industrial Revolution. Today, more than $86\%$ of the world's population is considered literate 1 and this literacy has changed how we can rapidly communicate complex ideas across communities, countries and nations. While reading has never been a static discipline and reading habits have evolved over time (for example from reading aloud to silent reading), with the advent of the digital revolution, where, how, and what we read has been changing rapidly. Chronic information overload, the ability to search and “surf” from one site to another as well as attention-seeking interface design all work against the practice of deeply reading a single, potentially complex piece of writing and drawing inferences and insights from it. Although the ability to distil insights from a document takes mere milliseconds for a trained mind, the skill of deep reading takes years to develop. There is currently increasing concern that as a new generation grows up reading more and more on mobile devices as a primary platform for all types of documents, deep reading skills may become underdeveloped [ 55 ]. While this concern seems well supported, there currently does not exist a practical method for measuring reading modes (deep versus skim) on mobile platforms.

Without being able to measure deep reading at scale, it is impossible to accurately assess the potential decline of deep reading and the factors that might be accelerating it–let alone create an informed strategy to preserve deep reading skills. Deep reading is a type of reading that involves cognitive processes that cannot be measured directly. Therefore other surrogate measures are needed to assess its presence or absence. Traditionally, comprehension tests have been used as a metric. These tests consist of a carefully constructed set of questions that evaluate the reader's literal, inferential, and evaluative understanding of text [ 6 ]. While effective, this type of assessment is not scalable, as tests would need to be developed for every article, and users would need to actually take such tests frequently. For large-scale assessment, only implicit methods that do not require any additional author or user burden can reasonably be employed.

Eye movement data has been widely used to provide implicitly collected insights into cognitive processes [ 52 ]. Eye movements can be used to detect reading [ 23 ] as well as differentiate between different reading goals [ 50 ]. And while eye fixation durations and saccade lengths have been shown to correlate with readers’ comprehension levels [ 50 , 51 ], robust models capable of classifying deep and skim reading are yet to be made work on both desktop and mobile devices, which is where reading increasingly takes place [ 43 ]. With the prevalence of front-facing device cameras, continuous eye tracking has become more and more feasible.

In this paper, we present a novel method for measuring deep reading versus skim reading across both desktop and mobile devices, and we specify how parameters must be adjusted to compensate for the differences between these platforms. We demonstrate the effectiveness of our method through a series of user tests where we recorded eye movement patterns and interaction data during desktop and mobile reading in a lab-based experiment with 29 participants. We extracted low and mid-level eye-movement features to train a machine-learning model to differentiate between deep and skim reading. Our results show a strong correlation between eye-movement patterns and deep reading activities and the resulting models allow us to detect deep reading with 0.82 AUC on a desktop and 0.73 AUC on a mobile device. We present a detailed analysis of the differences in the reading patterns on desktop versus mobile, which paves the way for continuous and unobtrusive tracking and quantification of reading activities.

The contributions of this paper are as follows:

  • We provide a novel, scalable implicit method for measuring deep vs. skim reading on mobile platforms.
  • We conduct a comparison of eye gaze features for recognizing deep vs skim reading on the desktop vs. mobile platform.
  • We validate the effectiveness of our method on longer documents (>300 words), i.e., during extended reading sessions.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Deep and Skim Reading Behaviour

Reading comprehension can be loosely defined as the amount of information or meaning being extracted from text [ 20 , 47 ]. Moreover, comprehension can be further categorized into different levels based on the amount of cognitive demands and interactions [ 31 , 46 ]. Commonly, underlying theory categorized comprehension into three levels – literal, inferential, and evaluative [ 5 , 6 , 10 , 20 ].

  • Literal comprehension (both skim and deep) is when readers can recall explicit information from the texts.
  • Inferential comprehension (deep) is when readers can interpret the authors’ meaning by inferring information that is implicit in the text.
  • Evaluative comprehension (deep) is when readers can analyse the information in the text based on previous knowledge or experiences and connect what is being read to what is known.

Reading behaviour can be categorized into either deep or skim based on these levels of comprehension[ 42 , 55 ]. Identifying deep and skim reading is crucial in many research and applications in psychology, education, and user interface design [ 19 , 42 , 55 ], but evaluating these behaviours at scale is difficult because they require comprehension tests. Our work aims to identify gaze patterns related to skim and deep reading, which can be detected automatically, and to use these to monitor reading modes in long-term studies. Moreover, individual reading behaviours have been shown to be very dynamic [ 2 , 21 ], where skim and deep reading can occur within a single document. Beyond assessing the overall level of skim and deep reading in a document, our method can help identify which parts of a document tend to be associated with deep versus skim reading behaviours.

2.2 Implicit Assessment of Reading Comprehension using Eye Movements

The relationship between eye movements and human cognition has been extensively studied. Many cognitive processes are infeasible to observe or measure directly but are highly correlated with eye movements [ 28 , 36 ]. As a result, previous works have used eye movements data to infer or analyse people's cognitive behaviour, such as people's attention [ 1 , 8 ], reading behaviour [ 7 , 26 ], and reading comprehension [ 3 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 30 , 39 , 48 , 51 ].

Eye-tracking research has shown great potential in classifying reading behaviours and predicting readers’ comprehension from their gaze data. For instance, eye movements are correlated with comprehension [ 23 , 24 , 35 , 44 ], and more specifically, fixation duration and saccade length are known to be correlated with reading behaviour [ 50 ]. The line of work that uses eye movements to classify comprehension was started by Underwood et al. [ 51 ] who studied the relationship between eye fixations and reading comprehension. They identified that fixation duration could be an indicator of comprehension; however, the generalizability of their results was limited as they trained and tested on the same dataset. Copeland et al. [ 14 , 15 , 16 ] continued with the quantitative assessment of using eye gaze features for predicting comprehension. These authors studied the effect of different text difficulty levels and whether or not readers have English as their first language. They found they were able to predict a reader's score on a comprehension test based on eye movements and that people with English as either a first or second language performed similarly. Makowski et al. [ 30 ] additionally addressed the comprehension prediction problem with short texts (158 words) and three comprehension questions per text. Similarly, Ahn et al. [ 3 ] studied predicting peoples’ overall text comprehension level using gaze patterns as well as other reading parameters such as reading difficulty. They were able to infer either high or low comprehension levels for known individuals with $64\%$ accuracy. Meanwhile, Southwell et al. [ 48 ] found eye movements could be used to better predict comprehension in longer connected text. In particular, they showed shorter fixation duration and more fixations tend to link with better comprehension. Most recently, Reich et al. [ 39 ] developed the first neural network predictor for a number of problems including text comprehension. While accounting for comprehension levels, these prior studies did not consider participants’ different reading modes. Additionally, there is a considerable lack of reading comprehension studies that target or are conducted on mobile devices.

2.3 Implicit Assessment of Reading Behaviour using Eye Movements

Our goal is to classify deep reading, during which readers process information in-depth, versus skim reading where the understanding of the text is generally limited to the literal comprehension level. Few studies have been done to relate eye gaze gestures to these reading modes. The two main studies we identified have been undertaken by Biedert et al. [ 7 ] and Kelton et al. [ 26 ]. Biedert et al. [ 7 ] gave participants a number of short articles and asked them to "assume the role of a newspaper editor who had to read various types of articles under time pressure" in order to answer some questions. Some of the articles were relevant to the questions, and others were not. It was assumed that the participants would skim the seemingly irrelevant articles and read the seemingly relevant ones more deeply. These researchers used eye saccade metrics to differentiate between the eye patterns of relevant and irrelevant document reading with $86\%$ accuracy. In another study, Kelton et al. [ 26 ] induced skim reading using time pressure and a summarization task with news articles. In the skim reading condition, participants were given 10 seconds to quickly look through the article and find three keywords to summarize it. In the deep reading condition, on the other hand, participants were given an unlimited amount of time to read the article. A single question aimed at evaluating comprehension was given after each section. The authors reported an accuracy of $82.5\%$ for these two conditions across entire documents and $72\%-95\%$ accuracy for differentiating these modes in different local areas of the document. These studies showed that time-pressured readers skipped the majority of a document as indicated by eye saccades data.

Inspired by these studies, our method uses a set of three questions to induce skim reading behaviour in the context of long documents. As we are interested in general reading behaviour and especially with regard to deeper text engagement, we focus on longer texts in our work as they have also been shown to yield deeper insights into eye movements and text comprehension [ 48 ]. Further, we administer at least 17 post-hoc comprehension questions designed to measure different types of comprehension to better validate the occurrence of skim and deep reading modes. Additionally, our study looks to detect skim and deep reading on both the mobile and desktop platform, where prior studies have been entirely based on desktop presentations. To the best of our knowledge, no comparable research has been conducted on a mobile device yet.

3 STUDY DESIGN

The goal of our study was to determine the cross-platform validity of features that discriminate between deep reading and skim reading. Our design focused specifically on the physical differences between reading on the mobile platform versus the desktop platform (screen and device size, reading posture, methods of navigation) rather than the challenge of mobility itself. Prior work has shown that reading comprehension varies greatly with different levels of mobility  [ 13 ] and with environmental factors like noise  [ 56 ]. It is only in situations where mobile readers were seated in a low noise environment that high levels of concentration were typically achieved [ 56 ]. As mobile phone use increases among younger readers and in developing countries [ 45 ], we believe that the most important use case for measuring deep vs. skim reading on the mobile platform is the case where the phone is the primary digital platform and the person is seated in a quiet environment, attempting to concentrate.

Our study design leveraged extensive prior work on reading studies involving eye tracking  [ 36 ] and inducing skim and deep reading behavior  [ 7 , 26 , 38 ]. In deep reading, the readers’ goal is to comprehend the text, while in skim reading the aim is to get a general idea of a text's content  [ 38 ] or find a particular piece of information  [ 7 ]. These different types of reading are often differently motivated by different methods. For example, Biedert et al. [ 7 ] motivated skim reading by giving participants the hypothetical newspaper editor role so that they would skim through irrelevant paragraphs and focus only on relevant ones. In Kelton et al. [ 26 ], readers were motivated to skim reading with task to summarize the content with three keywords, or were motivated to deep reading with the task of answering a quiz after reading. For our task, we most closely follow Kelton et al [ 26 ] because it allowed for the same text to be used for both skim and deep reading conditions, which we believed allowed a fairer comparison. This was important for us because our key motivation was validating the consistency of certain skim and deep reading characteristics across platforms.

In our study, we wished to examine skim versus deep reading through longer documents (≈ 1500 words) that would require phone readers to navigate through multiple pages, since longer text showed better associations between comprehension and eye movements [ 48 ]. In prior work, the primary length of text analyzed was approximately one paragraph long (≈ 150 words). With this in mind, we slightly adapted the skim reading motivator in Kelton et al's study from finding three keywords to finding simple facts from a set of three questions. We believed that this would induce skim reading throughout the document whereas to find three keywords that summarize an article readers might not look further than the title and topic sentence. Similar to other prior studies [ 48 ], multi-choice questions were used to evaluate participants’ comprehension.

3.1 Tasks

Our study involved four tasks: a desktop deep reading task, a desktop skim reading task, mobile phone deep reading task, and a mobile phone skim reading task. Hence, we used a two-by-two study design (deep vs. skim reading and desktop vs. mobile) where four texts were randomly assigned to each task condition and all participants performed all four tasks. The task order was counterbalanced. We used a quiz with 20 in-depth multiple-choice questions to motivate readers and measure reading comprehension. For the deep reading task participants were not given access to the quiz questions either before reading or during the quiz. They were told to take as much time as they needed to fully understand the text and that their goal was to score as high as they could on quiz. For the skim reading task participants were given three of the 20 quiz questions in advance. For this task, people were told that their goal was to find the answers to these three questions as fast as they could and that they would be scored based on their reading time and correctly answering the three previewed questions. Answers to these three questions were distributed equally throughout the text. Skim readers were also told that a quiz would follow their reading of the text and that they would no longer have access to the text once the quiz started. In the quiz, they will need to answer all 20 questions as if they were deep-reading the article, but that only the three previewed questions matter - their correctness on the 17 non-previewed questions would be ignored. The answers to all 20 questions would be used to help us further understand participants’ comprehension levels in all four reading tasks. The skim reading task was limited to a maximum of two minutes.

3.2 Texts

For our reading tasks, we selected four articles from the easyCBM repository [ 4 ] 2 . This repository was developed by educational researchers in close collaboration with school district partners across the United States and each article comes with a professionally developed 20 question quiz designed to assess student progress. We chose articles that were approximately 1500 words in length and at the 8th-grade reading level. Given that our participants were to be recruited from an English-speaking university, we believed that this level was sufficiently rich to support our reading tasks while not being so difficult that it would prove to be a barrier for any participant. We additionally chose articles where it was unlikely that any participant would have prior knowledge of the material. Specific details for each text can be found in Table 1 and Table 2 .

3.3 Display

We controlled text display parameters to ensure consistent reading experiences between mobile and desktop. Our goal was to show the same number of characters on each line (≈ 55 characters per line) across both devices with the goal of normalizing vertical progress through the document. To achieve this, we used the same font family (an even spaced sans serif font, Arial), line space (double spacing), and justification (left justified). We used a line width of 600 pixels on the desktop and full screen on mobile (see Figure 2 ). These choices are in alignment with previous readability studies [ 17 , 32 , 33 , 40 , 41 ].

3.4 Data Collected

We recorded participants’ eye movement data using the Tobii Pro X3-120 eye tracker and the Tobii Pro Studio software. The eye tracker had a sampling rate of 120Hz and recorded left and right eye gaze coordinates ( x , y ), and pupil diameters in millimeter. Fixation and saccade data was calculated by the Pro Studio. In the desktop setting, the eye-tracker was mounted at the bottom of a 24-inch monitor. For the mobile platform the tracker was placed at the bottom of an iPhone 11 device (see Figure 1 ). Additionally each platform devices kept a log of participants’ navigation behavior, specifically the x and y offset of the current viewing page.

3.5 Procedure

We initially recruited 36 participants (22 women, 14 men) from a convenience sample at an English language university. We required that all readers to be proficient in English: 32 of them were native speakers and 4 of them bilingual with English as the primary language. After signing a consent form and receiving some general explanation about the experiment, participants were seated in a quiet room, optimal for deep reading. We adjusted the seat so that their heads were centered and approximately 60 cm away from the desktop screen and 50 cm from the mobile phone screen to allow for optimal eye tracking. An eye tracking calibration procedure was done before each task. Participants were assigned the four tasks in a counterbalanced order. Participants used a mouse to navigate on the desktop and a touch gesture on the mobile phone. After the reading tasks and comprehension tests, participants were asked to finish a post-study survey about their experience. In total, the study took around approximately 60 minutes to complete. Participants were compensated with a $ 10 USD gift card for their participation. Seven participants’ data were eliminated due to low quality and missing data leaving data from 29 participants for our analysis.

4 FEATURE ANALYSIS ON DESKTOP AND MOBILE PLATFORMS

In this section, we describe our analysis for evaluating features for detecting deep and skim reading on both the desktop and mobile platform 3 . Because we were looking at longer documents, which we believed more closely resembled real world use cases, we first unroll the reading experience and compensate for page scrolling. We then determined a window size for the data and calculated features, such as fixations and saccades from the raw gaze data. We extracted 50 low-level gaze features and 25 mid-level [ 49 ] gaze features from each sliding window of the processed data for both the desktop and mobile platform. We then tested the effectiveness of these features for detecting deep and skim across both platforms using various machine learning classifiers.

4.1 Data Preprocessing

This first step of pre-processing the data was to compensate for scrolling events through our longer documents. We first detected gaze-fixations using the provided software from Tobii [ 34 ]. Our goal was to unroll the reading experience into one continuous longitudinal experience. To accomplish this we first needed to compensate for gaze behavior during page down scroll events. By observing the scroll data in conjunction with the gaze data on both the desktop and mobile platforms, we noted that participants commonly exhibited a gaze fixation during a scroll, both with the mouse down scroll on the desktop and the gesture based scroll up on the mobile platform. We used the "in scroll" gaze fixation as an anchor point to re-align text and gaze events for the next page adjusting next page events by the offset of the prior page. We then generated fixations from the adjusted unrolled gaze data using the R software package Saccades [ 53 ] that uses the velocity-based algorithm for saccade detection proposed by Engbert and Kliegl [ 18 ]. Next, we identified saccades as transitions between consecutive fixations.

We then used a sliding-window technique to segment the time-series gaze data of the unrolled experience into consecutive windows. Previous studies [ 9 , 27 , 29 ] have suggested different window sizes from 20 seconds to 60 seconds. To robustly evaluate our feature set and determine the difference between desktop and mobile performance we chose a variety of windows that both covered and exceeded this range, testing windows from one second to 120 seconds with different degrees of overlap. We found that window size was an important factor for feature performance as detailed in subsection 5.2.2.

4.2 Feature Extraction

Gaze features can be categorized into three levels: low-level, mid-level, and high-level [ 49 ]. Low-level gaze features can be derived directly from the raw data. These include fixation duration, saccade length, and pupil diameter and are the primary features used in most studies of gaze analysis during reading [ 22 , 25 , 48 ]. Mid-level gaze features proposed by Srivastava et al. [ 49 ] build on low-level features and look for patterns in consecutive fixations and saccades such as compare , scan , and line reading . High-level gaze features relate gaze features to specific stimuli, in this case an area of interest (AOI) in the text. For example, the answer to a question in the text can be treated as an AOI and the time spent in this AOI can be considered a high level feature. Since our text did not include any objective features that would be considered AOI stimuli, and since otherwise AOI is highly subjective to a specific readers intent, we did not attempt to analyze this class of features. Instead our analysis consisted of 50 low-level features and 25 mid-level features as shown in Table 3 and Table 4 .

We calculated metrics including: count of fixations/saccades, as well as mean, standard deviation, variance, minimum value, and maximum value for all fixation durations, saccade lengths, and pupil diameters over each window. We also derived the rate, percentage, and slope of all fixations where fixation rate was defined as the ratio between the total fixation duration and the size of the data window; fixation percentage was calculated as the ratio between the count of fixations and the data duration and fixation slope was calculated as the slope of the regression line fitted on all fixations. We classified fixation duration as either short (under 200 ms), medium (between 200 ms and 400 ms) or long (at least 400 ms). For fixation dispersion we defined the dispersion area to be $75\%$ , (i.e., the area containing $75\%$ fixations).

One of our cross-device differences was the threshold for the saccade size . On the desktop , saccades were considered short if their length was less than 200 px, and long if their length was greater than 400 px. On the phone , saccades were short if they were less than 75 px, and long if their length was greater than 400 px. Between these ranges, saccade length was considered medium. The choice of these thresholds was based on the line width: short saccades went through less than 1/4 of the line while long saccades took approximately half of the line. We additionally categorized the direction of the saccades into one of eight directions: up, up-right, right, down-right, down, down-left, left and up-left. Each range spanned 45 degrees with the up-direction being +/- 22.5 degrees from vertical. We then used four categories to describe the patterns of consecutive saccades: follow, neighbor, opposite and opposite-neighbor. Two consecutive saccades were counted as a follow pattern if they had the same direction, opposite if they had opposing directions, for example up followed by down or up-right followed by down left, neighbor if the two regions were adjoining (e.g. up and up-right) and opposite-neighbor if the following saccade was in one of the two regions adjoining the opposite direction.

We additionally calculated four mid-level shape based features from Srivastava et al. [ 49 ]: string, line, comparison and scan patterns. A string is a pattern of four consecutive identically directed saccades, e.g. left-left-left-left. These are classified as up, right, down and left. A comparison is a series of four opposite directed saccades, e.g. right-left-right-left. This would be called a right-left comparison. A scan is a rotational pattern of four saccades, as described in Srivastava et al. [ 49 ], that we classify into 12 patterns named after the directions of the first two saccades of the pattern, for example "right-left" is a pattern that begins with a right directed saccade followed by a counter-clockwise ("left") rotation. The line feature is a series of consecutive right directed saccades of any size (short, medium or long) followed by a long left directed saccade. We classify short line as three right saccades followed by a left long saccade, a medium line as four right saccades followed by a long left and a long line as five right saccades followed by one long left. This differs slightly from Srivastava et al. [ 49 ] who classified a short line as four right saccades and a long line as six long saccades. We made such an adjustment because our study had a narrower screen size and text column width.

4.3 Classification

The task for our model is to classify if a given reading segment is deep or skim reading, which we model as a binary supervised classification task. The input for the model is a feature vector representation of the mid and low-level features described in Section 4.2 derived from the raw gaze data for a timed window. We experimented with several types of classifiers, including: Kernel Support Vector Machines (SVM), Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and XGBoost. We also compared to a majority vote baseline classifier as the data was imbalanced ( $75\%$ deep versus $25\%$ skim) due to different time spent in deep vs. skim reading.

We trained the model with sliding window inputs and set the truth of source as the study condition from the reading session. We treated the window size and step size of the sliding window as hyper-parameters to be optimized for feature extraction as part of the model training.

Model training and hyper parameter tuning is often done with a fixed training and validation set prior to a model being evaluated on a test set. However, this would significantly reduce the available training data in our setting so we instead train and evaluate using a leave-one-participant-out evaluation using nested cross-validation for the hyperparameter tuning [ 12 ]. In nested cross-validation, the dataset is first divided into k parts, such that in each round, one part is picked as the testing set while the rest is picked as the training set. Before evaluating the test set, a nested k ′-cross-validation is performed within the training set for hyper-parameter tuning. Finally, the best hyper-parameters with the best AUC (chosen over accuracy due to data imbalance) during cross validation were chosen to evaluate on the test set. By doing this, the hyper-parameter tuning process does not have access to or is not exposed to the test set, hence the result is less biased. To evaluate the performance of each model, we used the nested cross-validation with k = 29 and k ′ = 10.

During the hyper-parameter tuning, we varied the window size from 5 to 120 seconds with a step of 5 seconds. We also varied the step size from 10, 25, 50, to 100 percentage of the current window size. We set the model hyper-parameters based on default values and previous similar studies so they were not tuned within the cross-validation. For Kernel SVM, we chose RBF kernel with C = 10 and gamma equals 0.01. For Logistic Regression, we set C to be 1 and penalty to be l 2 . For Random Forest, we set the number of estimators to be 1000, the maximum feature parameter to be the square root of feature count, and the minimum number of samples required for internal node splitting as 2, for leaf splitting as 1. For XGBoost, we set the number of estimators to be 100, the maximum depth to be 3, gamma to be 0, subsample and subsample ratio of columns to be 1, regularization alpha to be 0, and learning rate to be 0.1.

We trained and tested models separately for the desktop and mobile phone platforms (device-dependent classification) and also explored models for cross-device reading mode classification. In device-dependent classification, we used leave-one-out evaluation. That is, we trained on all except one participant's data and tested on the unseen participant's data. Hyper-parameter is tuned by a nested validation within the training set as described above. In cross-device classification, we trained our model using all data from one device and tested with the entire dataset of the other device. For instance, we trained one model using all desktop data which was tested against all mobile data.

5 RESULTS

In this section, we first check the validity of our study design, i.e., whether our task design successfully induced deep and skim reading. Then, we present our results for the reading mode classification, where we demonstrate that our classification methods can effectively differentiate between deep reading and skim reading. Furthermore, we analyze the impact of window size, step size, and mid-level gaze features on our classifiers. We also gained more insights by looking into important features for classification and misclassified instances. Lastly, we explored the role of gaze features in the more generic cross-device mode classification.

5.1 Comprehension Score and Reading Speed

Since the deep and skim reading behaviors were induced by our study design, we started by validating it using participants’ comprehension results and reading behaviors. As shown below, during deep reading sessions, most participants read at a speed that prioritized comprehension and were thus able to achieve high comprehension scores for all three comprehension levels (i.e., literal, inferential, and evaluative). When in skim reading, participants read faster (i.e., performing skimming) but still obtained high-level literal comprehension. However, their inferential comprehension was significantly poorer in skim reading. Therefore, we concluded that our study design had successfully induced the expected behavior.

Figure 3a shows the total number of correct answers across all participants. In both devices, participants had significantly better comprehension results in deep reading (desktop M = 14.90, SD = 1.78; mobile M = 14.14, SD = 2.49) than skim reading (desktop M = 12.34, SD = 2.70; mobile M = 11.10, SD = 2.32). Paired t-test revealed statistically significant differences between the conditions for desktop ( t (28) = 3.97, p < 0.001) as well as mobile ( t (28) = 4.77, p < 0.001). When comparing the same reading condition between desktop and mobile, participants did not show significant better comprehension on one than the other (skim reading: paired t-test t (28) = 1.99, p = 0.0570; deep reading t (28) = 1.49, p = 0.1482).

In addition, we investigated the reading speed of each participant, we found participants read at a much faster speed in skim reading (paired t-test t (28) = 11.33, p < 0.001). As shown in Figure 3b, most participants read at the speed normally considered as reading for comprehension (i.e., 300 words per minute [ 11 ]) (desktop M = 289.31, SD = 129.39; mobile M = 306.27, SD = 140.57) during the deep reading session. However, in skim reading, most participants read at the speed normally considered as skimming (i.e., 450 words per minute or more [ 11 ]) (desktop M = 632.23, SD = 226.74; mobile M = 723.49, SD = 341.09). Across devices, participants did not read comparably faster in one than the other during both deep and skim readings. (Note: no aggregation was performed as each participant only read under each condition once)

Furthermore, we looked into the comprehension results for each question type, i.e., literal, inferential, and evaluative. The literal questions were further divided into two sets, the set of questions that were previewed by participants (Previewed Lit Q), and those that were not. Figure 4 shows the comprehension results for each question type. When comparing deep versus skim reading on each device, participants showed significantly better results for non-previewed literal questions (paired t-test: desktop t (28) = 2.39, p = 0.0240; mobile t (28) = 3.74, p < 0.001) and inferential questions (paired t-test: desktop t (28) = 6.25, p < 0.001; mobile t (28) = 3.12, p = 0.0042), but not for previewed literal questions. For evaluative questions, participants showed better comprehension in deep than skim reading when using Mobile (paired t-test t (28) = 2.07, p = 0.04765), but no such trend is observed when using Desktop. When comparing desktop and mobile reading, participants did not demonstrate significant differences in terms of comprehension results for every question type.

5.2 Reading Mode Classification

We used gaze data to classify deep vs. skim reading based on our induction methods. Specifically, all data from the induced deep reading task was labeled as "deep reading" and all data from the skim reading task was classified as "skim reading." Typical examples of deep and skim readings are shown in Figure 5 . We plotted the gaze map (i.e., fixations and saccades) and heatmap overlaid over the reading articles for each condition. As shown in Figure 5 (a) and Figure 5 (b), when reading deeply, there were more fixations and more short saccades, hence the gaze plot looks relatively dense. On the other hand, when reading shallowly (Figure 5 (c) and Figure 5 (d)), there were fewer fixations but longer saccades, also the regressions appeared more frequently. It is worth noting that as reflected by the skim-reading heatmap, participants paid much attention on the AOIs of previewed questions. While in deep readings, participants did have particularly focused regions but the overall distribution was more spread out. Another interesting finding is that for each paragraph in deep reading, many participants put more effort into reading at the start of the paragraph than at the end of the paragraph.

5.2.1 Classification Results. The performances of all models are summarized in Table 5 , where we used bold text to mark the best classifier regarding one evaluation metric. Note that our baseline, a majority guesser, achieved a relatively high accuracy score due to the imbalanced data from larger number of windows from the longer deep reading sessions. This imbalance makes accuracy less reflective of performance than the AUC and F1 metrics.

Under all three metrics, all models significantly outperformed (one-tailed t-test with p-value < 0.05) our baseline approach, i.e., the majority guesser. Among all the models, XGBoost achieved the best performance in desktop with an accuracy of $88.36\%$ , an F1-score of 0.82, and an AUC of 0.82. We first varied the window size from 1 to 120 seconds fixing the step size to be equal to the window size. Next, we took the best window size, and then varied the step size from 1 second to the best window size. For simplicity, we plotted only the accuracy and AUC scores, the F1 score graphs were very similar to the AUC graphs.

Figure 6 summarizes the classification results of the XGBoost model when varying the window size for window extraction. For desktop, the model achieved a relatively good performance with any window size greater than 20 seconds. When we decreased the window size from 10 seconds to 1 second, performance dropped significantly, indicating a small window size failed to capture any meaningful features. Meanwhile, window sizes had a much bigger influence on mobile. Performance increased as we increased the window size from 1 to 120. Hence a bigger window size might be required for effective mobile classification. Moreover, desktop outperformed mobile on window sizes below 80 seconds, and they performed roughly the same with larger window sizes.

According to the result of our window size evaluation, we fixed the window size to be 120 seconds and varied the step size to measure its influence. When increasing the step size from 1 second to 120 seconds, performance of both devices increased. Adding more data via overlapping windows does not help result in a better model.

6 DISCUSSION

To advance reading research we need to be able to track reading behaviours in a scalable and non-intrusive manner. To develop implicit metrics about the quality of reading, we created a method to reliably induce skim and deep reading using three levels of comprehension. Since reading increasingly takes place electronically [ 43 ], we developed classifiers for both desktop and mobile devices.

6.1 Inducing and Validating Deep vs. Skim Reading

Our evaluation showed that inferential comprehension is most prominently linked to deep reading activities regardless of reading device as it requires readers to empathise with the context of a story in order to derive insights not explicitly stated in the text. Inferential comprehension is essential for passage understanding as it requires readers to connect events in a narrative or understand a character's motive for a particular action [ 5 ]. The tasks we developed to induce deep and skim reading were most effective in their discriminative power with regard to this crucial level of comprehension.

Readers’ ability to answer non-previewed literal and inferential questions was further significantly impeded by skim reading on both desktop and mobile. For previewed and evaluative questions we did, however, not see a difference between reading modes, which may imply that skim readers have not just blindly skipped text but applied skim reading as a selective form of text processing. Especially high-level questions like “What kind of person is Kim?" could be answered well during skim reading. This may indicate that readers have successfully adapted their reading ability to quickly determine how new information fits into their existing knowledge network and make use of prior experiences to quickly judge a passage or character.

Despite participants having received no performance incentives, our method has been shown to effectively induce skim and deep reading and thus can be reused by researchers to further the research on these two reading modes.

6.2 Linking Deep Reading to Eye Gaze Patterns

By combining participants’ comprehension results and their reading speed with eye movement data we were able to determine eye gaze patterns that are characteristic of deep reading activities. In general, participants read slowly at a speed for comprehension in deep reading, and relatively fast when doing skim reading. By looking at the gaze plot and heatmap of each participant, we found that participants tend to read line by line carefully in deep reading, i.e., there were much more short saccades and less long saccades, and the overall reading patterns looked more like scanning slowly from left to right, line by line. While in skim reading, we found that participants jump quickly between paragraphs and only spent relatively more time on texts related to previewed questions. Their reading patterns were more like scanning in a zig-zag manner with longer saccades and less fixations. This observation was consistent with prior research [ 50 ] that fixation duration and saccade length reflect reading behavior. In addition, we found the direction of saccades and the reading pattern to also matter. These features played an important role in the later classification. Classifiers identified a task as deep reading when it had fewer upwards and left saccades (i.e., backtracking-like saccades), more and longer fixations, and smaller pupil diameters. The bigger the window size, the more important saccade-related features were. Desktop classifiers utilized saccade features more, as they were easier to pick up on larger screen sizes. Moreover, distance-based mid-level features were also considered by the classifiers as they identified regression patterns.

6.3 Reading Mode Classification

Our results demonstrate the existence of a strong relationship between eye movements and reading mode. This allowed us to build classifiers to distinguish deep from skim reading. One of the central contributions of our work is to provide reliable classifiers for reading on desktop as well as on mobile devices.

Our classifiers achieved high performance in recognizing deep versus skim reading in general settings. In particular, we have obtained better desktop results than prior studies [ 7 , 26 ] and firstly showed that similar results can be obtained on mobile. In desktop classification, among all four classifiers, the XGBoost achieved an accuracy of $88.36\%$ , a F1 score of 0.82 and an AUC of 0.82, which outperformed our baseline measurement, majority guesser (accuracy = $77.33\%$ , F1 score = 0.44 and AUC = 0.50). Note that our baseline approach achieved a high accuracy due to the imbalanced dataset. For mobile, Logistic Regression performed the best with accuracy = $86.18\%$ , F1 score = 0.77 and AUC = 0.73. XGBoost achieved a similar performance with accuracy = $85.51\%$ , F1 score = 0.76 and AUC = 0.73. The majority guesser had accuracy = $76.66\%$ , F1 score = 0.43, and AUC = 0.50.

By looking closer at the important features, we found besides fixation duration and saccade length and direction [ 7 , 26 , 50 ], pupil diameter and pattern-based features also played important roles in the classification. For instance, medium left or up saccades may indicate re-reading which occurs more in deep reading. Moreover, we experimented with various window and step sizes and found that 120 seconds is the most suitable for both. This aligns with previous studies [ 9 , 27 , 29 ] indicating that a large window size is required for such high-level activity recognition. A shorter window size may not be sufficient to capture important movements. When we increased the window size from 5 to 60 seconds, fixation and pupil features were out-weighted by saccades and mid-level features, indicating the presence of movement-related features and their importance to our model. These findings were common across desktop and mobile, suggesting that with appropriate tweaking in the feature extraction (to suit the mobile), study methods for desktops could be migrated to study mobile. However, we also noticed it was harder for mobile to pick up direction or pattern-based features. The performance difference between desktop and mobile may be exacerbated by the smaller screen size and closer proximity to the screen and the eye tracker. Hence the abilities of both our eye-tracking hardware (i.e., eye tracker's accuracy in terms of correctly identifying fixations and saccades) and software (i.e., correctly picking up useful movements by feature extraction) were limited. To further improve performance on mobile, more fine-grained eye-tracking techniques and mobile-tailored feature sets were required.

Misclassifications may be due to the large variance among individuals’ reading behaviors, which has been widely reported [ 37 ], and the natural mix of deep and skim reading in daily reading activities. This was verified by looking at each individual misclassified instance. During deep reading session, some participants (e.g., participant 14 and 32) still performed a “revision” after reading the entire article once, and the “revision” was more like skimming and scanning than deeply reading. Similarly, when reading shallowly, some participants (e.g., participant 22, 25, 36, etc.) were not able to perform skim reading due to their deep engagement with the presented stories, hence they still read slowly and deeply.

6.4 Implications

In this work, we took the first step towards implicitly tracking and distinguishing deep from skim reading behaviour both on desktop and mobile devices, where digital reading predominantly takes place. The presented patterns and classifiers allow us to build implicit data collectors that quantify and track reading behaviour and changes thereof over time. Especially little is known about the effects on younger generations who grow up with and use mobile phones as their primary source of information. Our work enables long-term studies to be conducted to shed light on the characteristics of skim and deep reading, how interface features may impact the choice of reading mode, and the exploration of contextual information, such as which type of reading environments are more favourable to which type of reading behaviour. Insights from such studies may eventually produce guidelines and insights that will allow designers and application builders to create reading experiences that nudge users towards deep reading and in-depth content engagement and hence support readers’ abilities to process more demanding texts as they are found in literature, science, legal, and also political party books.

6.5 Limitations

First of all, as an in-lab study participants read under controlled environmental and device conditions. Additionally, we imposed several soft constraints on participants for accurate data recording (e.g., do not move head too much). Hence, a lot of varying environmental influences (e.g., distractions, differences in lighting, etc.) encountered during everyday reading activities are not reflected in our data. The stationary set-up of using a Tobii eye tracker under controlled condition, as well as the small sample size, was a necessary first step to validate the successful induction of deep and skim reading as well as link deep reading activities to eye gaze patterns. The resulting classifiers are the basis for building more robust and mobile tracking solutions, which we also plan to expand to other sensor types (e.g., eye tracking through the front-facing rgb camera) and validate against larger groups of participants.

Moreover, in our study design we limited the skim reading time to two minutes, which may have caused changes in reading behavior due to the explicit time pressure imposed. The high scores in literal comprehension, however, indicate that participants were able to successfully undertake the reading tasks.

In addition, our findings could be subjective to risking type I error due to a large number of paired t-tests being run. Regarding our feature selection, we suspect that a better set could be obtained if we tailored our features specifically to reading tasks, such as developing more appropriate mid-level and high-level features, which we leave for future work. Moreover, since a large window size was required for accurate classification, our current approach is more appropriate for post-hoc than real-time classification, which we deem feasible considering our goal of tracking reading behaviour changes long-term. In addition, we failed at inter-device mode classification because many of our important features have dependencies on screen sizes. Hence in future studies, features that are independent of devices should be explored.

6.6 Future Directions

In future work, we will explore more accurate and less constrained methods for classifying reading behaviour in-the-wild. By improving our classifiers through richer mid-level features along with collecting larger datasets, we intend to build more robust classifiers that run with alternative sensors, such as an rgb camera or Apple's true-depth camera. This would allow us to take the tracking of deep reading episodes into the field by deploying our models on consumer devices.

By providing a method to induce deep and skim reading activities along with the link between eye gaze patterns and deep reading, our work paves the way towards implicitly tracking reading behaviour over time. Follow-up studies that we can now undertake include investigating the effect of reading content, layout, or style on reading behaviour in natural settings. For example, are users conditioned to various reading behaviours based on the website they visit (i.e. social media vs news feeds vs blogs)? Similarly, with robust classification we can study different settings where reading behaviours may be influenced by contextual factors, such as while at work, home, or commuting.

7 CONCLUSIONS

Understanding reading behaviour on desktop and mobile devices through eye movement is important to foster and understand deep reading in natural settings. Our study is the first to perform a detailed investigation into reading modes for both desktop and mobile devices. This was enabled through a study design we introduced that could successfully induce people's deep and skim reading behavior as evidenced by nuanced comprehension results and reading speed. Moreover, we showed that these two reading modes could be effectively identified with classifiers trained on features derived from eye-movement data. Finally, we discussed key characteristics of deep and skim readings through the analysis of the most important features for our classifiers. Our work paves the way to study long-term changes in reading behaviour through implicit metrics across different device types.
