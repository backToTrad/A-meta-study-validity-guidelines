Cleared for Safe Take-off? Improving the Usability of Mission Preparation to Mitigate the Safety Risks of Drone Operations

Drone operations such as power line inspection, automated deliveries, or crowd control are becoming more widespread. For flights that present serious risks to human safety, operators must conduct safety assessments and get authorizations from the regulators. These preparation tasks are complex and time-consuming but few previous works addressed them. We interviewed 14 professional drone operators, safety study consultants, and 2 regulators to better understand the needs for these tasks. The result is a workable model of the tasks which includes defining the concept of operation, assessing operational risks, and negotiating for authorization. We devised 9 recommendations to inform the design of future mission preparation tools, and consolidated them with a follow-up questionnaire. The recommendations include systematically describing a mission with operational parameters, showing their estimated impact on mission safety, or enabling awareness of the application's status among all stakeholders. We conclude with design concerns and opportunities to inform future research.

1 INTRODUCTION

Drones or Unmanned Aircraft Systems (UAS) are uncrewed aircraft, particularly suitable for performing tasks that are tedious, risky for people on board, or in environments inaccessible or harmful to humans. The technological maturity of these systems has led to new applications such as drug delivery, area surveillance, or search and rescue [ 58 ].

A drone operation generally consists of three steps: the planning phase occurring before the flight, the execution phase during which the flight is performed, and the post-flight phase, which includes data processing if necessary. A study on professional drone pilots’ practices identified that mission planning is one of the most important phases but also one of the most time-consuming [ 42 ]. This phase can be even more challenging for operations presenting a higher level of risks such as operations taking place at higher altitudes or over a longer range called Beyond Visual Line of Sight (BVLOS). For such missions operators must conform to the regulation [ 2 , 7 , 13 , 59 ] and authorization is often necessary [ 1 , 26 ], which can be particularly challenging to obtain [ 5 , 36 ].

Operators shall produce and submit a file containing a concept of operations and an operational risk assessment of the proposed flights to the regulator. The regulator checks that the safety level of the operation is sufficient before granting authorization or requesting changes. In this case, a loop involving the client, the drone operator, the remote pilot, the regulator, and other authorities (Air Traffic Management or Police) is set up. Instrumenting the mission planning process to support the safety of operations is one of the key aspects of future drone operations [ 27 , 66 ].

Unfortunately, most of the contributions supported the flight phase. Few considered the mission planning phase or the post-flight phase. This creates a lack of field data to inform the design of future systems. Ljungblad et al. [ 42 ] emphasized that "more researchers in HCI could engage in safety concerns when developing applications" and "highlighting a variety of situations of use and how a drone might be a safety risk" . These considerations led us to focus on missions requiring an operating authorization (OpAuth) and the realization of risk assessments.

In this paper, we present the findings from 14 interviews with drone operators and safety study consultants as well as interviews with 2 regulators, consolidated with a follow-up questionnaire. We aimed to better understand their work and challenges during the preparation of drone operations presenting risks to humans and goods. The contributions of this paper are threefold. First, we describe drone professionals’ practices and tools used during mission preparation in different industries. Second, we propose a three-phase model of the collaborative process of operating authorization requests along with 9 recommendations to support the design of safe drone mission preparation tools. Third, we identify design concerns and opportunities to inform future research efforts.

2 RELATED WORK

The field of Human-Drone Interaction (HDI) is a growing area of research within the HCI community [ 9 , 12 , 15 ]. Work focused on the use of drones in different application areas such as navigation assistance [ 40 ], art [ 18 ] or photography [ 16 , 38 ], and proposed interaction techniques for piloting [ 31 , 46 , 58 ]. To guide future work in HDI, interviews with professional drone pilots were conducted by Ljungblad et al. [ 42 ]. They identified that safety is the first concern for professional pilots and highlighted that the flight phase is a small part of the activity compared to mission preparation or post-flight data processing. Supporting safety during the entire mission life cycle is an important aspect that has few dedicated tools according to the participants in their study. Despite the growing body of work in this area, they also identified that real-world studies are scarce. This is also consistent with findings from previous literature reviews that identified a lack of empirical results concerning drones flying close to people [ 8 , 58 ]. We thus have relatively little knowledge about piloting procedures or how safety measures and liability protection are implemented.

The realization of missions with higher potential risks, such as missions lasting several kilometers, the proximity of sensitive infrastructures, or the overflight of populated areas, requires an even more important preparation phase [ 48 ].

For such missions, the European regulatory framework requires an operating authorization [ 60 ] and the US Federal Aviation Agency requires a waiver [ 2 ]. In those cases, each mission is defined in a concept of operations whose risks have to be thoroughly assessed and mitigated to an acceptable level. The Single European Sky Air Traffic Management Research Joint Undertaking, or SESAR JU, has several industrial research projects supporting safe and secure drone operations in Europe [ 54 ]. For example, the PODIUM project focuses on providing Air Traffic Controllers with tools to ensure safety with regular aviation during operations. Such projects often demonstrate technical systems but do not seek to understand the people and the processes involved to improve safety.

Several methodologies exist to conduct an operational risk assessment [ 1 , 41 ], usually with a specific application area such as warehouse operations [ 63 ] or construction support [ 39 ] . Unfortunately, due to their specific nature, such methodologies are often not generalizable to other settings such as outdoor operations. A more generic approach, the Specific Operation Risk Assessment methodology (SORA) is gradually being adopted. The SORA is a methodology for the classification of the risk posed by a drone flight in the specific category of operations and for the identification of mitigations and safety objectives. It helps the operator to identify operational limitations, training objectives for the personnel essential for the operation (e.g., remote pilots, observers, maintainers, etc..), technical requirements for the drone and to develop the appropriate operational procedures that will be part of the Operations Manual [ 30 ]. While effective, SORA is a qualitative and complex methodology. Literature documenting applications of SORA for several use cases helped to identify some lacks and caveats, such as its time-consuming nature [ 36 ], or the lack of treatment of multi-UAS operations risks [ 14 ], or the benefits from taking into account not only the spatial component of the data relied upon (e.g., geometry) but also its semantics (e.g., contextual information, attributes, inter-relationships) [ 5 ]. Several other pieces of research focus on the mapping and assessment of ground risks [ 49 ], air risks [ 44 ], cyber risks [ 62 ], or compliance with SORA guidelines [ 57 ]. However, no previous work describes the real tasks and collaboration involved in performing a drone risk assessment and requesting operating authorization.

Safety studies are often complex to perform. Existing approaches to assist with safety studies include nesting small safety cases to form full cases [ 3 , 65 ], automating techniques [ 65 ], creating databases [ 10 ] and standard risk models for drone missions [ 67 ], appraising weather risks [ 43 ] or using formal modeling techniques [ 20 , 21 , 52 ].

Few prior works from the HCI community addressed the preparation phases as identified by a literature review on safety support for drone operations [ 50 ]. Building on one of the design recommendations presented later in this paper, we studied how interactive maps could support operators from early concept definition to mission execution [ 51 ].

We believe that there is a need to study the processes and interactions between stakeholders, operators, and regulators, for conducting drone missions with serious risks. Indeed, such operations will become more frequent, and ensuring safety is a major challenge for this industry [ 4 , 27 ]. More empirical data from the field is required before designing new software able to support the preparation phase and the safety of the operations [ 42 ].

3 METHOD

We conducted an interview study to better understand the process used by professional drone operators when they prepare a drone mission that necessitates authorization from the authorities. We sought to characterize existing practices as well as to identify constraints and opportunities that can inform researchers and designers of systems capable of supporting safe drone operations. After an analysis phase resulting in a descriptive model and the identification of an initial set of 10 recommendations, we consolidated our results using a follow-up questionnaire that was answered by 7 of our participants .

3.1 Interview Study

We conducted 14 interviews with drone operation managers working for operators of various sizes, as well as two complementary interviews with 2 regulators.

The interviews were conducted between January 2020 and October 2021; 4 in face-to-face and 10 by video conference. Each operator had already performed at least one mission requiring an OpAuth with a detailed risk assessment. The tasks related to the safety of drone operations are spread over a long time, and the results are presented in various documents communicated to the authorities. Thus such tasks are difficult to observe directly, and interviewing the people involved seems an adequate method to obtain contextual data, concrete examples of problematic situations, or to explore new approaches with the operators.

We iteratively developed a set of 16 questions for the interviews. We started with discussions among the research team and with drone pilots with whom we were acquainted that had to request authorization for specific drone operations. We also compared to previous surveys and field work in HDI (e.g. [ 42 , 50 , 58 ]). Table 1 presents the final question set covering five primary axes: demographics; mission characteristics; authorization requests; tools used; and the exploration of new concepts when possible.

The questions were defined to explore in detail and context the process of applying for authorization and conducting a safety study. Information on demographics and assignments performed is used to characterize the interview participants, and to build the discussion around real, lived situations. We also used the critical incident technique [ 28 ] during questions 6, 9, and 14, regarding missions, requests for authorization, and tools to perform them, respectively. Previous work has identified that conducting drone operations involves map representations, but few risk analysis tools use these representations directly [ 50 ]. Moreover, applications for OpAuth require collaboration between the operator and the regulator. Therefore, we explored the concept of conducting the safety study on a map and collaborative work with the regulator, with questions 15 and 16. We also conducted an initial interview with a drone operator (OP0) to assess our questions. Since some needed clarifications, we reformulated them and discarded the results.

3.1.1 Participants. Recruitment for these interviews was guided by the desire to maximize the diversity of participants, both in terms of the size of the organization and the range of operations. We contacted operators and pilots in Europe, likely to carry out missions requiring OpAuth applications by using the information on company websites whether their activities suggested either long-range, or in a challenging environment, or atypical operations. Authorities have also suggested some of them after our interviews. All participants were involved in at least one successful authorization request. In total, we conducted 16 interviews. The first 10 concerned drone operators, i.e. companies conducting drone operations requiring safety studies and having to submit operating applications (OP1 to OP10). The interviewees were the managers of the drone missions, sometimes themselves remote pilots or accompanied by remote pilots. Four interviews were safety study consultants (SC1 to SC4), i.e. consulting companies that carry out safety studies on behalf of a client. All the interviewees operated in France. We also interviewed two teams from the French Civil Aviation Safety, also known as regulators (RG1 and RG2). The first one's role is to verify and validate the declared performances of UAS. The second one analyzes, verifies, and validates all French OpAuth requests. These last interviews allowed us to understand regulators’ workflow and their communication with the operators.

3.1.2 Procedure and data analysis. The interviews were all conducted by one of the authors, with one or two of the other authors as observers, who could intervene to clarify or emphasize a particular aspect. We followed our questionnaire for each interview but had to adapt to the discussions with the participants. The interviews lasted between one and two hours. Most of them were recorded and then transcribed for analysis, except for those of OP1, OP2, OP4, and SC4, which were not recorded for confidentiality reasons. We also asked participants to show us the documents and tools used in the development of the missions and the safety study. Unfortunately, because of a non-disclosure agreement signed by some of the operators they were not able to show any, or we were explicitly asked to keep those documents confidential.

We analyzed the data qualitatively using thematic coding techniques derived from grounded theory [ 56 ]. One of the authors performed the initial coding from the transcripts or textual notes. The codes were then shared with a second author and grouped into an initial set of themes within an spreadsheet file from which we derived recommendations. These themes and recommendations were explored in the following interviews to assess them and update them when necessary. We conducted a total of five analyses as the process unfolded to code new data within our themes, and looked for elements in previous interviews when new themes emerged. Similarly, the recommendations were refined and further developed in subsequent interviews when possible.

3.2 Questionnaire Study

To assess our analysis and consolidate the findings from the interviews, we asked the operators and regulators to fill out an online questionnaire. Our goal was to submit to stakeholder critique the described model of the process we proposed from the interviews as well as the recommendations to support the tasks performed during their work process.

3.2.1 Questionnaire Design and Analysis. The questionnaire was separated into two sections: (1) the recommendations and (2) the conceptual model of the process. The first section aimed at assessing the level of agreement of each respondent to the recommendations using a 5-point Likert scale. They could also provide additional thoughts or feedback, including additional recommendations. They also had to rank the recommendations according to their priority, using a Likert type ranking scale with 7 levels in order to gain accuracy for the prioritization with two additional choices: "the most important one if any" and "unnecessary" [ 37 ] . The second section was intended to evaluate the proposed conceptual model of the process with the associated tasks we identified. They had to rate its correctness and representativeness using a 5-points Likert scale and give more details to enhance it. The last question was about their feedback on the whole set of recommendations and the conceptual model to gather additional input on our work. Figure 5 and Figure 6 in appendix B detail the devised questions. We analyzed the qualitative data using the codes and themes developed during the analysis of the interviews.

3.2.2 Respondents. The questionnaire was sent to all operators interviewed including OP0, as well as to the regulators, making 17 possible respondents. We received completed questionnaires from 7 of them which gives a response rate of 41.18%. Two respondents are regulators from the team RG2, including REGHU, the head of the unit in charge of verifying and validating the OpAuth requests, and REGAG, one of the agents. Apart from OP0, who is solely an operator, the remaining respondents are both operators and safety study consultants (OP8, OP10, SC1, SC2).

4 RESULTS

In this section, we report the findings from our interviews and the subsequent questionnaire, focusing on two main objectives: 1) to describe the current practices of professionals using drones in safety-complex missions, and 2) to identify avenues for future system design to improve the authorization process and safety of drone operations. For concision and clarity, we detail and discuss our design recommendations together with the findings.

4.1 Mission types and workflows

Our interviews allowed us to consider many types of drone missions spanning a variety of industries and requiring authorization from authorities. The missions covered industrial inspection (OP3, SC1, SC2, SC4), weather data recovery (SC4), inspection of large transportation and energy networks (OP1, OP2, OP5, SC1), experimental flights of drone application concepts (OP4, OP6, SC2) or new payloads (OP9), acquisition with a LIDAR (Laser Imaging Detection And Ranging), thermal and multispectral data (OP2, OP4, OP8), air shows with several hundred UAVs at night and near urban areas (OP10), and recognition of possible avalanches and search and rescue in hostile environments (OP7). The risk assessment for delivery by UAVs (OP8, SC2, SC4) and surveillance of industrial zones by autonomous UAVs (SC1, SC2, SC3, SC4) were carried out by external safety study consultants except for OP8.

For all of our participants, the three main stages of a drone mission are mission planning, flight execution, and finally post-flight activities.

To carry out a complex mission from a safety point of view, participants explained that a considerable preparatory phase was necessary before the actual flight. This stage involves highly technical tasks, the involvement of several stakeholders, and the production of numerous documents. OP3 explained that: "Today the work of mission preparation, represents, I would be tempted to say, something between 60 and 70% of the technicality of air operations" . Similarly, SC1 stated "The average work time for a SORA is 100 hours, 250 hours for complex missions," while OP10 commented that: "It once took six months of work for twenty minutes of flight" .

The mission preparation phase can be divided into three main steps: 1) establishing a concept of operations; 2) assessing operational risks, and 3) obtaining authorization as summarized in Figure 1 . The concept of operations , or Conops, is the description of what is going to be done with the drone, where and when, with which system, and which participants. It is used to clearly outline the operations that will be performed as part of the mission. The operational risk assessment is an analysis that defines the requirements that an operator will have to meet before flying a drone. It serves to identify and control the risks to guarantee a targeted level of safety. The European regulation recommends the use of the Specific Operation Risk Assessment (SORA) methodology to conduct this analysis [ 25 ]. The SORA methodology has 10 steps including ground, air and adjacent areas risk assessments; determination of required levels of assurance; or operational safety objectives. To obtain an OpAuth, operators must submit a file to the regulator containing the Conops and the risk assessment as well as administrative forms or approvals from the prefecture. During the examination, successive revisions can be made according to the regulator's requests for clarification or more effective mitigation means.

Based on these findings, we created an initial sequence diagram of the process that was presented in the questionnaire interview. The respondents rated its correctness between neutral and "I think the model is correct overall" (mean 0.14, SD=0.83). We used their textual feedback to create an updated version that is presented in Figure 1 and with additional details in Figure 4 in the appendix. The feedback helped us clarify that other stakeholders such as local police authorities can be involved before the formal approval to mitigate some risks. For instance, SC2 added that "it is necessary to add negotiations between the customer and the operator according to the feedback from the regulator". The regulator REGHU also provided clarifications on exchanges between the stakeholder roles: "It seems important to me to clarify that it is never the final customer who asks for the operating authorization. This one is granted to the person (physical or moral) who exploits the drone(s)" . Commenting on our initial model, REGHU added that "the regulator can also intervene in the upstream step of risk assessment before the formal request".

4.2 Establishing a Concept of Operation

Establishing a Conops is an iterative process involving the client and the operator. SC4 stated that "The Conops is the most important, you have to define what you are going to do and where" . It is also "necessary to define Conops that are clear enough and understandable by neophytes" for SC1. Each Conops poses different problems and leads to different solutions. However, we observed common phases in the missions studied that we report below.

4.2.1 Aligning client needs with mission feasibility: All of the operators interviewed communicate with their clients to identify their needs, except for OP7 and OP10 who benefit from the drone missions they perform themselves.

To gather the client's needs, participants reported starting with an exchange by phone, via video conference, or during a physical appointment. Synchronous work between the client and the operator with immediate exchanges allows for a quick sketch of the concept of operations before refining it. SC1 explained that he collected all necessary information in a file to characterize the mission as much as possible for safety purposes but also to reduce asking for clarifications from the client.

Several participants explained that clients "think big" and want to do many things with drones. However, regulations, often unknown to clients, impose limitations for safety reasons. For missions presenting few risks, operators and pilots can usually identify the corresponding regulation thanks to their training. For missions presenting more serious risks, the evolution of the regulation and its complexity often poses difficulties to the operators. OP8 explained that he must negotiate with the customer: "For example, it will not be four kilometers but two kilometers instead to be acceptable for the regulators " . Figure 2 illustrates an automated surveillance scenario inspired by examples from SC2 and OP9. The client wanted a survey of the whole facilities that are outlined in red (Figure 2 .a). This area poses several challenges as there are people working in it, but also because the safety buffers, areas around the flying area, intersect with speedways (Figure 2 .b). These parameters impose reducing the flight area (Figure 2 .c) and operating when workers are not using the facilities, during the night for instance. Otherwise, the regulator will refuse the operation. Participants described several limitations that were imposed for safety reasons such as avoiding a flight during cold weather since it can reduce the battery performance (OP1); limiting the range to two kilometers from the remote pilot (OP8) or flying during working hours above industrial sites (OP2, OP4).

Participants insisted on the ability to align the customer's needs with 1) the technical feasibility, i.e. what the drone is capable of doing, and 2) the feasibility from a regulatory point of view .

Recommendation 1: Establish the concept of operation (mission goal, technical and regulatory feasibility) synchronously with the customer.

In the questionnaire, REGHU commented that "it would reduce the gaps between the client's wishes and the feasibility of the mission in terms of safety. These gaps result in more iterations with the authority" . SC2, who remained neutral in the questionnaire, stated that "This can be interesting in some cases but not always applicable" .

4.2.2 Explore and refine operational parameters. Participants detailed the key points of the operation including the characteristics of the area, both on the ground and in the air, as well as the purpose of the mission and the challenges involved. All emphasized the impact of weather conditions, which can stop or postpone a mission, except when flights take place inside a building (SC4). We identify these mission properties as Operational Parameters (or OpParam). From our interviews, it appears that an operation can be described in terms of its OpParam. We can classify them from the least controlled by the operators to those they can act upon.

OpParam related to the environment include the weather conditions (e.g. visibility, wind, temperature, and solar wind), the geographical environment (e.g. population density, terrain, roads, obstacles, and human constructions/activities), or the portion of airspace (e.g. type of airspace, drone zones, military zones, aerodrome zones). OpParam related to a given UAS include system performance, level of automation, communication link range, maximum speed, maximum take-off weight, and range of the aircraft. OpParam specific to the mission that must be adjusted by the operator include the payload usage, the planned trajectories, the time of the flight, the speed, the altitude, the take-off and landing zones, safety mitigation, or the roles and training of people involved.

This creates a vast ensemble of OpParam to be apprehended by the operator. The goal of the operators is to define the perimeter of the mission, define important operational parameters to realize an initial risk assessment, and identify the applicable regulation . Participants explained that proposing alternatives is often mandatory. For instance, OP8 stated that "there are two interfaces: one with the client and another with the regulator. We are in the middle. We collect the customer's requirements and we try to make the first barrier, before sending any request to the regulator. To keep our credibility, we force them (the client) to lower their expectations. We tell them that we don't even submit this request because if we do, we risk being thrown out."

Recommendation 2: Systematically identify and use Operational Parameters to describe a mission.

From the interviews, the most recurrent OpParam were the distance to the safety pilot which determines the nature of the flight (in visual line of sight, beyond visual line of sight), the trajectories, the altitude of the aircraft, and speed with are used to calculate the safety margins, or the flight's time and place which are used to know the characteristics of the environment, like whether it is populated or not. OP0 commented that "it allows to speak in clear, precise and common terms to everyone (customer, operator, regulator)" . However, it is impossible to make an exhaustive list of all possible parameters of a drone mission because of their large diversities as stated by SC3: "No two missions are the same" and SC2: "it is difficult, during a mission, to predict all the operational parameters" .

4.2.3 Identify the mandatory actions to take. Once the Conops is determined, the operators must decide what type of safety measures will be required to obtain the operating authorization.

Participants reported the use of online tools such as Clearance [ 17 ], Fly By [ 22 ], or Drone Keeper [ 33 ] to identify the drone restrictions and procedures applicable from shapes drawn over a map. SC2 explained that he stopped using it because of poor usability: "I don't find it ergonomic at all, it wastes my time" .

All emphasized that applying for an OpAuth is difficult. They often tried to find other approaches that do not require it if possible. According to OP2, "the regulator recommends to check thoroughly if we can adapt the operation so that we can use a simple declaration matching the national scenarios or the Predefined Risk Assessment 1 before applying for an operating authorization in a specific category" . For SC2, there are sometimes problems due to misinterpretations: "Some operators think they are in OPEN when what they are doing is SPECIFIC, but they don't know that because they misinterpret the regulation".

Participants expressed difficulties in identifying their mission categories with respect to the regulatory framework. They insisted on the required expertise to cope with all these various situations. The combination of European law and national laws, which are evolving and may be amended, makes operators’ work even more complex.

Recommendation 3: From the parameters of a mission, support the identification of the elements of the regulation concerning the operation.

All respondents to the questionnaire agreed to this recommendation. OP0 commented that "it could avoid wasting time on an exhaustive concept that will not pass regulation's barrier" . SC2 emphasized that it would be very useful, "especially if it can guide to the most appropriate application (scenario or SORA)" . The recommendation is partially implemented by cited online tools [ 17 , 22 , 33 ] that give a list of approvals needed in an area such as air traffic control but do not provide the safety requirements for a specific mission.

4.3 Evaluating and mitigating operational risks

Since 2021, all safety studies for OpAuth applications must apply the SORA methodology with the new European drone regulation [ 26 ]. The methodology comprises several steps including assessing ground and air risks and mitigating their impact; determining the level of assurance and integrity that corresponds to the level of risk of the operation; determining the resulting operational safety objectives; investigating adjacent airspace areas. Descriptions and justification must be provided for each element. All operators interviewed in 2020 (OP1, OP2, OP3, OP4) were preparing to use it except OP4 who had not yet heard of it. Operators and safety study consultants interviewed during 2021 were all using it except OP7 who had been assisted by a consultant.

4.3.1 An iterative and creative process. To establish a risk assessment, several operators indicated using an iterative process. For instance, SC1 and SC2 used online tools such as geographic information systems to get a quick approximation of the risks before going into detail by themselves. Others used internally developed tools or specific spreadsheets to assist them during the assessment of the operational risks: to automate calculations, and evaluate ground fallout areas, lines of sight, and elevation profiles (OP1, OP3, OP6, OP8, OP9). SC2 reported using an online tool [ 35 ] to outline and validate the risk level calculation quickly but forced herself to redo it in detail in a second phase.

RG2 emphasized that the search for solutions to control the risks is a creative process. This was problematic for OP9 which explained that "SORA is very factual, but you can answer it in twenty-five different ways. There are no solutions, no numbers, and most importantly, you have to explain why your solution addresses the problem" . Justifying why the proposal is appropriate is one of the challenges of the SORA methodology. OP3 stated that "SORA is a tool that aims at evaluating the level of safety of the operation, and therefore you have to be able to justify it to yourself and then be able to justify it to the authority. These are two different things" .

Mitigation depends on the operator's choices and some small details can change everything in the result. According to OP6, "It is easy for a mission to become unnecessarily complicated after a change of parameters" . Operators must find their optimal mitigation and while doing it, they have to repeat over and over the steps of adjusting OpParam and computing the level of risks for each potential mitigation. All participants explained that it is important to leave open the choice of the order in which the steps of the risk assessment are carried out. For instance, SC3 indicated that he started with what he finds the most difficult: "if the aerial environment is complex and we realize at a glance that at the territorial level we are going to be in an agricultural zone, we will keep the ground aspect (which is easier to tackle) for the end" . Several operators (OP3, OP5, OP8, SC1, SC2, SC4) were convinced that an exploratory approach can help them find the right balance between the level of safety and the work required in terms of procedures.

Recommendation 4: Support exploration of operational parameters and estimate their impact on mission safety.

REGHU expressed a strong agreement with the recommendation and commented that "it would certainly help operators to explore options that could simplify their procedures while improving the safety of their operations. This work is often done during the examination of the application by the Authority, but it increases the time and workload for everyone (operator and authority)" . SC2 added that "It would save a lot of time to be able to test the parameters that can be modified to make an operation fit into a given risk level" . Recent work used a similar approach allowing to compute drone trajectories resistant to weather uncertainties [ 61 ] from formal OpParam declaration. Note that this recommendation requires that OpParam have been identified and used as suggested by RC2.

4.3.2 Available but unreliable data. Operators retrieved and used online or printed maps via various public and private services. These maps mainly concern population density, satellite views of the mission location, terrain, aeronautical information such as airspace classes, and proximity and boundaries of airports. Operators often added additional data layers on top of the cartographic representation such as infrastructure to be inspected for OP5, areas to be monitored for SC3, or airspace for SC2. Participants expressed concerns about the reliability of the data. While OP3 indicated that he often referred to satellite images for distant locations, SC3 insisted on the obligation to visit the site as a safety measure that can be used to negotiate with the regulator. OP5 and SC2 both questioned the data completeness, correctness, and current validity. They explained that most of the online data and tools available are not officially recognized and their editors disclaim responsibility for the use of the services in case of erroneous data. Only the services offered by a state agency are considered reliable by the regulator and the operators. An important issue raised is the access to relevant and reliable data to perform the operational risk assessment. For example, on population density, OP8 explained that "The definition of Sparsely populated is not given anywhere, (...) there is a legislative gap" . This is confirmed by SC2: "Where do we get the figures to decide (population density)? Who provides the figures to be considered?" . As explained by OP5: "What is extremely important is that, in the long run, the authority validates or recognizes certain tools as being accepted tools. And that there is a stamp or a label (from the regulator) and that if we use these tools, we speak the same language, and we know that the limits are well defined in the tool by the authority. Because if every time we use a different tool we have to present it again to the authority, explain it again, redefine the thresholds together, that's complicated".

Recommendation 5: Develop usable tools supporting the production of reliable data from the regulator's perspective or the mitigation of unreliable data.

The regulator must be able to list and/or provide reliable data sources with directly usable formats or tools that are considered reliable. In return, operators will have to rely on the sources and/or tools validated by the regulator to solve the data reliability problem. All respondents to the questionnaire agreed with the recommendation and were highly concerned as indicated by the priority results. However, the head of the regulatory team (REGHU) commented that "it is a worthy ambition, but it is not the vocation of the authority to produce these unified tools. All the tools, although ‘scattered‘, are freely accessible via public sources: ICAO navigation charts, INSEE population density statistics, etc."

4.3.3 Visually thinking with maps. Having described and quantified the OpParam, the participants explained that they produced maps to represent the operation. The map served as a communication tool, with the client or the regulator but also as a visual thinking tool exposing some potential safety issues, like populated areas in the vicinity or buffers overshooting the operation perimeter as illustrated in Figure 2 . Operators were unanimous that the use of maps is essential during the operational risk assessment. Maps can be used to determine the consequences of an operation at a certain location and this practice is widespread among operators. RG2 indicated that they often asked for three-dimensional visualization of the trajectories over a map to better understand the elevation profile in relation to the relief of the area.

The maps used by the participants were either printed and annotated or digital with additional layers of data such as points of interest and polygons. Participants explained using several sources of data, often within different applications to retrieve elements such as the elevation profile, the density of population or to locate pylons or antennas that might be in the path. When we discussed the possibility of performing safety studies directly on a map by sketching trajectories or annotating risks, all the participants responded positively. Several explained that being able to draw the trajectories and visualize safety elements such as the safety buffers automatically or superimposing several layers of data would actively support their process. SC2 explained that "a graphical object that allows us to keep in view all the parameters that we are going to modify slightly, and to see the impact that it has concretely on the calculation of my air risk or the calculation of my ground risk so that I can see the level of risk evolving by saying: ’In fact, all I had to do was to lower my altitude by five meters and I would go to a ’SAIL 2’ and I didn't need to modify all the other parameters’." .

Recommendation 6: Support representation and manipulation of operational parameters on maps.

4.3.4 Acceleration Strategies. All participants agreed that the SORA is time-consuming. SC1 reported working on a SORA for almost a year on a nuclear site inspection mission. He explained that "the average time to do a SORA is between 100 and 150 hours of work" . With experience, the methodology is performed faster. For instance, OP6 stated that it took him between two days and two weeks. OP5 and OP6, who described shorter duration, have over 20 years of experience in helicopter operations. For all operators with experience/expertise in manned aviation (OP1, OP2, OP3, OP5, OP6, OP8, OP9, SC1, SC3, SC4) but one (SC4), the process is considered affordable.

Some operators developed strategies to accelerate operational risk assessment and the production of the requested files. SC1 and OP10 created macros to generate mandatory tables for the SORA from the list of requirements they identified. Instead, OP8 created a generic concept of operations without specifying exact locations but retaining operationally important characteristics. Conducting the SORA involved checking the difference between the generic safety study with the actual situation. Reusing parts of previously completed studies is similar to the concept of Pre-Defined Risks Assessments (PDRA) encouraged by the regulator. OP2 advocated for "a dream tool that would help with a pre-written SORA a bit like a PDRA, simpler and faster to fill in and send to the regulator" .

The several iterations between the operators and the regulator for implementing the required changes create issues. Regulators must be able to recognize the different versions of a file to see if the necessary safety adjustment or requested clarifications have been made. For RG2, it is a matter of "avoiding misunderstandings. ’Your buffer zone, the last time we met, we said it was not good, I don't see that you changed it’" .

Recommendation 7: Provide usable templates and leverage the concepts of editorial chains: enable versioning, automate the inclusion of elements, update links and generate required documents.

The templates must help accelerate the process and repetitive tasks. For instance, RG2 suggested harmonizing SORA presentations: "If all the SORAs looked alike, it would go much faster to evaluate" . SC2 suggested in the questionnaire to "let the possibility to propose a ‘model‘ of mission based on the files already made because an operator often carries out very similar missions" . However, acceleration should not be detrimental to safety. For instance, OP0 commented that "the clarity of the content of the documents must come first" and REGHU commented that "a procedure or a tool is not the answer to every problem: it is quite possible to incorrectly fill out a very well designed document template" . Regarding the versioning, OP10 and OP8 reused previously accepted documents and pointed out differences to the regulator to speed up processing. OP10 explained that "when we send them the file, we describe the difference from the previous transaction" .

4.4 Obtaining an operating authorization

The file for an OpAuth request must be sent to the regulator. It contains various documents, including approvals from other third parties.

4.4.1 Multiple actors and multiple delays. Participants explained that missions involving multiple stakeholders were often requiring a lot of coordination efforts. They needed to communicate with each of the third parties to inform them and also to obtain their approvals to operate. OP8 gave examples requiring approvals from the city's authorities to fly over populated areas, protocols from airport managers to fly over controlled areas, or memorandums of understanding to share the air space from the military authorities in areas where the military may operate at the same time. The tools used are email, telephone and online services such as [ 19 ], Drone Keeper [ 33 ], Clearance [ 17 ], and U-Space Keeper [ 34 ].

Depending on the areas and the risks presented, it is necessary to obtain the agreement of different stakeholders such as landowners, air traffic controllers, or the prefecture. However, obtaining information on the contact points, the elements to be provided and the deadlines remain a major challenge for several operators. Most requests have notice periods and processing times that must be managed. Failure to provide them to the regulator can delay or even cancel an operation. SC2 explained that "it's always when the regulator asks you for the paper that you realize you don't have it" .

Recommendation 8: Support early identification of third parties, protocols, and deadlines for required clearances.

All respondents agreed to the recommendation. REGHU added that "some online tools (perhaps for a fee) offer this and seem to be appreciated by operators. One caveat, however, is that such a tool can only provide useful information to operators if the input parameters (e.g., location, height, time) are correctly filled in by the operators" . SC2 highlighted the possible difficulties to operationalize the recommendation since "the deadlines vary according to the requests or the seasons" .

4.4.2 Communication and negotiation with the regulator. Files must be submitted by email to the regulator three months before the planned date of the operation. All operators experienced difficulties due to the attached files’ size limitations of the regulator's email. OP9 was annoyed that "given the number of documents, there is no way it can fit in 3MB" . The operators relied on external file transfer services (OP8, OP9, OP10, SC3) or private hosting solutions to leave documents available for long periods to the regulator (OP8, SC4).

After receiving an application, the regulator team performs a pre-analysis. The goal is to verify that all the SORA steps are present, as well as key points such as population density, air risk, and buffer zone size calculations. RG2 explained that "the idea is to see, within one to two weeks if there are prohibitive points. If there is nothing that jumps out at us, we tell the operator that we will continue to examine the file and then we will start looking at the details". RG2 went further on his strategies and stated that "we may put a lot of people to work on both sides for nothing. If we know that the operation is not feasible in the end, we have to realize it quickly enough". Then, the regulator checks and authorizes the application, or asks for clarifications.

In most cases, clarification is required. OP8 is the only one that reported being successful in obtaining once an OpAuth without a request for clarification by the regulator. For SC2, it often takes "three months during which we will have exchanges with the regulator to finalize the file" . Sometimes the delay is not met and OP10 explained that he had to cancel a flight because of an authorization that was not issued in time.

The operators (OP9, OP10, SC2, SC3, SC4) explained that they had no information on the status of their application until the regulator replied via email. Some explained that they contacted the regulator by phone to check if the file is valid before sending it (OP9), to inform them that a submission has been made (OP9, SC2), to evaluate the necessary modifications (SC2) after sending it or to inquire about the status of the submission (OP10, SC4). Operators were all interested in knowing the status of their submissions, especially if the deadline for the operation was approaching.

Some operators had specific communication strategies. For instance, SC2 called the regulator to make an oral pitch of the mission to get quick feedback. Some also explained that the work is made easier when the regulator and the operator have worked together in the past. For SC3, "the regulator was very cautious at first but there was a gradual relaxation as we presented well-made SORA files" .

Recommendation 9: Enable awareness of the current status of the application among all stakeholders.

Indeed, the visibility of the status of the document is a concern for all operators, and only those more accustomed to dealing with the regulator dare to call to inquire about it (OP9, OP10, SC3). Providing information such as an acknowledgment of receipt, a number in the queue, and the current status of the request would allow operators to better estimate the progress of their requests. All respondents agreed with the recommendation except SC2 because he was "unsure if the administration could give this type of ‘progress‘ indication" .

4.5 Recommendations agreement and priority assessment

In addition to the agreement with our recommendation, we collected the participants’ prioritization choices and additional feedback to be explored in future systems. All recommendations were positively rated as illustrated in Figure 3 (a). RC5 and RC8 obtained the highest levels of agreement among all respondents with the smallest standard deviation. In an attempt to guide future efforts, we analysed the priority ranking results presented in Figure 3 (b). Sorting the recommendations by priorities gives the following order, from the most important priority to the less: RC4, RC3, RC9, RC1, RC5, RC8, RC6, RC2, RC7, and RC10. These results can indicate which recommendations can bring the most benefit to users.

We initially devised 10 recommendations for better support that were all positively rated and consolidated by some operators and regulators. We merged RC7 and RC10, covering aspects related to acceleration strategies either for safety analysis or for communication with the authority, based on their similarities and the respondents’ feedback. Not only did the previous RC7 and RC10 receive the lowest priority from the respondents, but both dealt with document edition and presentation.

5 IMPLICATIONS FOR DESIGN

The findings from our interviews show that the mission preparation process is much richer and more complex than the prescribed process. Numerous steps, often time-consuming and requiring several iterations, are necessary between the client's needs and the establishment of a risk-minimizing concept of operation that is acceptable to the regulator. This is consistent with previous studies from Ljungblad et al. [ 42 ]. However, while their focus was on implications for Human-Drone Interaction from the pilots’ perspective, we instead focused on the preparatory phases and safety support as a collaborative process involving multiple actors. In this section, we articulate design concerns and discuss design and research opportunities for actively supporting safety and efficiency during drone mission preparation.

5.1 Design concerns

Tension between safety and business goals. Our participants explained being frequently confronted with new requests from their clients. In such cases, finding an optimal balance between safety concerns and business goals required creativity and critical thinking. While the regulator explained welcoming positively any new operations, which was confirmed by the operators, they also indicated that coping with new situations created more work to assess the safety and often increased their safety requirements. OP8 summarized it: "There are still aspects that the client is not aware of, but you have to be clear with her from the beginning to understand what she wants and explain what is possible (and at what price)" .

Tension between safety and automation. Several recommendations we formulated aim at offering solutions to automate parts of the process that were found too long, too complex, and error-prone by both operators and regulators. For instance, systematically using Operational Parameters (RC2) within adequate templates or tools (RC7) can create opportunities to automate some aspects of the safety analysis such as hazard identification or the impact of the OpParam on safety (RC3, RC8). While automation can greatly accelerate the process, it might present risks to safety. As RG2 explained, the risk with online forms facilitating the application of the SORA methodology is that operators might be tempted to "fill the template instead of thinking" . Tools forcing premature commitment and not letting the user try and explore the potential solutions can be detrimental to the quality of the selected solution [ 55 ].

Balance between an individual and a collaborative activity. Our results indicate that the drone mission preparation process is a collaborative endeavor. Even if the operator is responsible for composing this file, its production is shared with the client, and even with the regulator when the operator agrees with the regulator on certain aspects of the file. Collaborative work appears to be improving safety in our context which is consistent with Pan and Hildre's work in the Maritime domain [ 47 ]. These constraints sometimes led our participants to experience stressful situations or feel helpless, because of the long delays in getting responses from other stakeholders but also because of the need to get all the approvals on time.

5.2 Design opportunities

Exploring safety on maps . Maps and cartographic representations were frequently used by our participants during all phases of their missions but remained underutilized during safety analysis. This is mostly due to the need to produce textual documentation in their applications. We argue that fostering systematic identification and exploration of operational parameters [RC2, RC4] with visual analysis on maps [RC6] can support operators exploring the design space and use spatial reasoning [ 6 ]. Interaction with OpParam can also foster creative outcomes as it can "support rapid incremental and reversible exploration that enables users to learn about distributions, gaps, and outliers" [ 55 ].

We used this approach in a longitudinal study with a drone operator to explore how direct manipulation of OpParam on maps could enhance the identification of risks in context and the formulation of adequate mitigation [ 51 ]. Our results suggest that the visual analysis of safety buffers associated with the drone trajectories helped adjust the trajectories to reduce the risks, which strengthens the validity of our proposition. However, more efforts are required to facilitate the identification of OpParam, and to design relevant and manipulable representations on maps for safety. Indeed, very few previous works considered the use of maps to support the safety assessment of drone operations [ 50 ].

Maps can also help bridge the semantic gap due to the interpretation of OpParam and procedures by each stakeholder [ 11 ]. Establishing shared and concrete depictions of the intended operation between the stakeholders was a central preoccupation of our participants. For instance, RG2 explained that they used maps as a matter of "avoiding misunderstandings " .

Automating risk assessment . Our participants explained that multiple regulations, aviation procedures, safety methods, and values of OpParam must be considered. Some used strategies such as macros (OP7) or expert systems (SC1) to help them with the complex entanglement of these pieces of information. Automation must support the user in understanding and mitigating the risks instead of simply trying to accelerate the process. Using the capacity of computers to apply rules [ 64 ], expert systems can accelerate risk assessments by providing information from a regulatory perspective and a safety perspective, according to the Conops. Compared to other artificial intelligence techniques, these systems are more transparent and the reasoning can be traced back [ 53 ].

In our study, we covered several strategies that could be supported by an expert system including: 1) a data-driven approach, where the values of OpParam determine the level of safety (SC1, OP7); 2) a goal-driven approach, where the user selects a safety assurance and integrity level and will be guided by the system on OpParam which could be adjusted to limit the risks under that specific level (SC2); 3) a negative goal-driven approach, where only the area where the flights are possible are highlighted and manipulable, according to a safety level goal (OP8); or 4) a difference-driven approach, which consists in adapting previous successful elements or comparing with similar conops in finding the solution when facing a complex situation (OP10). One of the main challenges is to integrate such a system within the workflow of the operator, rather than introducing an additional tool. We argue that combining map-based exploration with risk assessment automation should be investigated in future work as means for augmenting the decision process [ 24 ] and supporting creative thinking [ 55 ] about the implications of specific OpParam on safety in a unified manner.

Supporting collaboration . Negotiation and information sharing are keys to efficient collaborative work [RC1]. Face-to-face communication is the most effective in a collaborative work context [ 23 ]. It might help alleviate the potential communication difficulties between the client and the operator. Operators also reported that they use online tools and have remote interactions with the regulator. More coordination tools like identification of the involved stakeholders, the current status, the next step, what are the implications, and how much time it should take could compensate for the lack of collaborative activities [RC8, RC9]. As the regulator cannot provide an indication of progress, this could be delegated to a system. It could keep track of each submitted flight intent and automatically send a notification to each operator at each step or event.

Sourcing reliable data was a main challenge for drone operators. Crowd-sourcing approaches may help get more accurate data [ 45 ]. We argue that pooling authorization data and material for requests can strengthen safety for other operations. Operators could leverage both rejected applications to pinpoint remaining risks identified by the authorities and successful requests to reuse risk mitigation that were evaluated as adequate for similar operations. Moreover, as operators may improve existing authorizations with additional or updated data, these data would be worth sharing among the drone operator's community to improve the reliability of the data [RC5]. There will also be fewer data sources to be checked before each mission. As SC1 explained "gathering all the data is already one-third of the work" .

6 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

We conducted 14 interviews with drone operators on their process of preparing missions that require a specific operating authorization application as well as 2 interviews with the authorities responsible for granting the authorization.

A limitation of our study is the location of the participants, who all operate in France. However, some of the operators have prepared files for other countries, or work with international clients. Besides, the SORA methodology is already recognized in several parts of the world such as the USA, Australia, and Japan [ 29 ]. We interviewed a wide variety of operators, doing diverse missions such as data acquisition, drone delivery, or flying swarms of hundreds of drones. We believe that we have adequately covered current drone operations due to the diversity of our participants and their numbers. Indeed, Guest et al. [ 32 ] indicate that 12 participants are needed to reach saturation. We had 14 drone operators and two regulators, one of which is the head of the regulator team that covered all operations requiring operating authorization in France. Another limitation is the 7-level Likert scale that was not sufficient to allow respondents to fully prioritize the 10 recommendations. However, this choice did simplify the questionnaire and the presentation of the results. We might have missed the use of tools that we know are available online to help with flight preparation. These tools were not mentioned during the interviews: they are either unknown to the targeted operators or do not meet their needs. A hypothesis is that these tools might be used for missions that do not require operating authorization.

We hope that the contributions of this paper will be useful to both system designers and human-computer interaction researchers. The model of the mission planning part identifies the tasks to be performed and the concerned stakeholders. Designers can build upon the presented tasks and contexts to devise new interactions. We also introduced a model that allows us to classify a tool used in a mission regarding the steps (does it support thinking? does it accelerate the processing of the request? does it support the groupware aspects?). The notion of Operational Parameters enables a precise characterization of a drone mission and opens research perspectives on how to make them visible and manipulable by users to describe their mission or to evaluate safety. The characterization of the different relationships between the interested parties and the possible identified improvements offer new research perspectives for Computer Supported Collaborative Work (CSCW).

We devised 9 recommendations that were consolidated with a follow-up questionnaire to inform the design of future mission planning tools. The priority reported by the operators can also inform on the possibly more impacting choice of design features. However, given the important deviation between the results and the few number of respondents, we believe that it is an interesting tendency but it does not constitute a strong ordering indication. The model and the recommendations constitute a theoretical contribution that was validated by subjective data but still requires experimentation in the field. Future work will focus on the implementation of these recommendations within interactive prototypes following the design opportunities we discussed and their validation via field studies with drone operators, as most of our participants have already expressed their interest.
