Research Products at Scale
Learnings from Designing Devices in Multiples of Ones, Tens, Hundreds and Thousands

Andy Boucher , Interaction Research Studio, Northumbria University, United Kingdom, andy.boucher@northumbria.ac.uk

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581540
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

In this paper, I argue that scale is an important quality of research products [ 32 ] and reflect upon the lessons learnt from designing and producing research products at scales ranging from one-offs to those reproduced in thousands. I describe details from a body of research completed by our studio over twenty years by examining four research projects that were designed, developed, and manufactured at four distinct levels of scale. I draw out details from these projects that have not previously been reported and discuss the methodological implications of growth not just for design and production, but also for strategies for engaging with participants. In addition, I discuss particular features of research products produced at the four levels of scale and describe the benefits and trade-offs of producing research products at different scales.

1 INTRODUCTION

The term ‘research product’ was first coined by Odom et al. in 2016 [ 32 ] to describe outputs from a growing body of work in HCI concerned with the design and development of bespoke computational devices. These devices were being built to explore complex relationships between people and technology, often via long-term field study of participants integrating them into their day-to-day lives. Odom et al. [ 32 ] were responding to a feeling within the community of HCI design practitioners that the label of ‘prototype’ was insufficient to describe the devices that they were building for research purposes. They argued that a research prototype is a placeholder for something else, a work in progress, or a final concept in transition to a complete version, whereas a research product is the actualisation of an artefact, which can then be evaluated for what is it , rather than what it will become . Importantly, the word ‘product’ is not used to imply commercial motivation but more that these artefacts are (finished) products of research.

Odom et al. [ 32 ] described four key qualities of a research product. First inquiry: that the research product should drive the research inquiry through the process of making and experiencing it, and that the artefact can also embody research questions. Second finish: that it should have a high degree of finish so that it appropriately represents the final version of itself. Third fit: that it should fit into its intended situation, and neither be too familiar nor too strange. Forth independence: that the artefact should be reliable in the field and able to operate independent of the researcher.

While practitioners may debate the finer details of these qualities, the phrase ‘research product’ has gained traction within the community and is widely used to describe new research artefacts in multiple projects [ 6 , 9 , 23 , 25 , 28 , 30 , 31 , 40 ]. It can also be used retrospectively to label work produced prior to the term coming into widespread use, including our studio's work [ 1 , 13 , 14 , 16 , 17 , 19 , 21 , 34 ].

In this paper, I argue that scale is a fifth quality to add to the original list by Odom et al [ 32 ]. Scale is a dimensional quality that defines how many copies of a research product are made and variations in scale have an impact on the other key qualities. I further suggest that the qualities from the original list are also dimensional, as research products can have a higher or lower degree of inquiry, finish, fit and independence and these dimensions can be impacted by scale . For example, a device designed for many will be less specific than a device designed for one, so the fit will be more generic.

To support this argument, I describe key examples by our design research studio from the past two decades to demonstrate how our approach has evolved by producing research products at various scales. I begin by explaining our approach to the production of single artefacts, then our move into the low-volume production of tens, and then hundreds of units, and finally our turn to the design of ‘self-build’ devices that non-experts can make at home. This led to our research products being produced in their thousands. Through these descriptions, I shall discuss the impact on research product qualities when produced at various scales and contribute insights and strategies for those following a similar path.

1.1 Our design research studio's path to producing research products

For our design research studio, the evolution from device prototype to research product was driven by a variety of influences outside the core HCI community, reflecting practices at the department of Computer Related Design at the Royal College of Art in the late ‘90’s and early 00’s. This was a period of rapid progress around what was possible to affordably achieve from an experience prototype and there are a number of touchpoints of note for this time. Firstly, the accessibility of powerful desktop computing, Macromedia's Director software, and microcontrollers such as the BASIC Stamp enabled design students and researchers to build quite sophisticated and fully working prototypes of tangible interfaces for their projects [ 37 ]. This period saw Computer Related Design students shift from the design school tradition of creating non-working models (supported with a visual storyboard of functionality) to building working interfaces that people could experience. Although ‘smoke and mirrors’ played a part in this early work, it pointed to a near future where design prototypes could be produced with a high degree of built-in computation by designers themselves without the support of hard- or software engineering specialists.

Secondly, Computer Related Design's own research studio was working at the time on an EU-funded research project called Presence, which was exploring technology for older people in specific communities within three towns in Europe. One of the outputs designed for the Bijlmer housing estate just outside Amsterdam was Slogan Benches [ 12 ]. This computationally controlled and networked public seating contained scrollable boards of messages produced by the community, which celebrated the diversity and culture of what was known as a notoriously troubled area. The public could scroll through the messages using buttons on the device or remotely via a touch tone telephone that could dial in to the collection of benches. The Slogan Benches were highly finished pieces of computational public furniture that were installed at the Bijlmer housing estate for a week and used by the community they were designed for. They were self-contained, reliable and did not need to be facilitated by the researchers who built them. Observational and ethnographic methods were used to document how people interacted with the benches and analyses of the findings are documented in several books and papers [ 11 , 12 ]. From this point of view, the Slogan Benches can be seen as one of the earliest examples of research products demonstrating qualities of inquiry, finish, fit and independence.

Finally, Dunne and Raby, who were members of the Computer Related Design's research studio around this time, produced a research project called Design Noir: The Secret Life of Electronic Objects, in which they created eight pieces of furniture for participants to adopt and live with at home. The objects were designed as placebos to investigate the leakage of electromagnetic fields from everyday electronics, with the idea that they would encourage participants to consider the unseen emissions of electronic devices and think about the impact of technology on their lives. Participants’ accounts of living with the objects were gathered through interviews and photography, and these stories were told in the book that accompanies the project [ 8 ]. Again, these were highly finished artefacts designed to embody a research inquiry and thus may be seen as research products as well.

Taken together, these trends demonstrated an opportunity for design researchers to create fully functioning tangible interfaces, produce robust and highly finished designs, create devices that challenge or reimagine our relationship with technology, and call on participants to adopt the artefacts and share experiences of living with them. These features have formed the basis of our methodologic approach to producing research products and have been enabled by the accessibility of new technologies such as open-source microprocessor boards, low-cost CNC fabrication and 3D printing. In addition, our design research studio's long running research mission has been to practically demonstrate the alternative uses for computational technology outside the constraints that dictate the design of consumer products. Through multiple projects over the last twenty years, we have worked to achieve this by designing and making research products that have been developed as single artefacts and as multiples in numbers that range through dozens, hundreds, and thousands. The following case studies are taken from four distinct projects, which unpack our approach on a trajectory that begins with the production of a single research product and finishes with a design of a self-build device that has been built by over 3,500 people.

2 THE DRIFT TABLE, 2003

The Drift Table [ 1 , 22 ] is a research product that was designed and built in 2003 as part of our design research studio's involvement with the Equator Project, an Interdisciplinary Research Collaboration (IRC) funded by the UK's Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council. It is an electronic coffee table that allows people to experience floating slowly over the British landscape from the comfort of their own home. The surface of the table is sensitive to the weight of objects placed on it and uses this as an input to control the direction of the slow scroll of aerial photography visible through a central viewport. Adding more objects causes the table to speed up and ‘descend’ towards the landscape below. Progress is slow and travelling from London to Devon may take days. There is a small text display on the side of the table to show the placename of the current location of the imagery and there is a small reset switch that relocates the photography directly above the physical position of the table should one want to start a journey again.

We designed the Drift Table long before Google Maps was ubiquitous on every smartphone and conceptualised it as a crack in the enclosure of the home that would promote daydreaming and imagination. It isn't ‘for’ anything in any utilitarian sense, but it provides a resource that people can use or play with as they wish.

The Equator Project had a remit to investigate the boundaries between the physical and digital worlds there were eight university partners within the IRC. Our studio's research agenda was to promote non-utilitarian ‘ludic’ values in the home and create devices that would demonstrate these values. We were collaborating with colleagues at Lancaster University who had developed a functional prototype of a table using inbuilt load sensors to track objects placed upon it [ 36 ]. This work had been inspired by ethnographic work also carried out in the IRC, which had been investigating how surfaces were used in the home [ 7 ]. We developed design proposals based on load sensing, of which the Drift Table was one and the first to be developed (others being the Key Table [ 10 ] and History Tablecloth [ 19 ]).

As we wrote at the time, there were several considerations that led to the Drift Table becoming our studio's first research product. It is perhaps interesting to note that we commented on the insufficiency of the term ‘prototype’ as a category in reflecting on the design and build of the device:

“…instead of developing a prototype useful only for short-term demonstrations, we would aim to produce a robust and highly finished product that could be tested for extended periods in people's homes. We wanted users to suspend their perception of the Drift Table as a research prototype, and instead encourage them to consider the ideas and concepts it embodied in the same way as any consumer product. This implied building the Drift Table to be utterly reliable, easy and unobtrusive to install, and, above all, at a sophisticated level of detail and finish” [ 1 ]

2.1 Design, development and production

The Drift Table is a complicated artefact based on the straightforward, perhaps simple proposal that a coffee table could contain high-resolution aerial photography of a whole country, which pans around based on how the coffee table is being used. The initial concept was for the entire surface of the table to be a screen, but as we evolved the design, the display moved to be positioned around 75mm below a central viewport, to better support the idea of the device being a window on the world or a crack in the home. An enormous amount of work went into the design and development of the unseen parts of the Drift Table, to elevate it beyond a research prototype and make represent what it is, rather than what it could be .

As mentioned earlier, the device was created prior to Google Maps and so the first challenge was to acquire photography. Following an audacious request by our institution's public relations manager, this was fortuitously donated by a company called Getmapping, which had recently photographed all of England and Wales as a millennium project. This posed a technical challenge, however, as it was a terabyte of data at a time when the largest hard drive available had a capacity of 120GB. This presented us with our first major choice for the design: whether to make the Drift Table a completely self-contained device or have a screen tethered to an external server machine (cloud-based data services were not common at the time). We decided to pursue the (far more difficult) self-contained approach because it would allow us to craft the device with the authenticity of a finished product and allow the concept to be evaluated by the participant unfettered by the compromises of a tethered device (which would scream ‘prototype’). This decision was pivotal in our history of producing research products.

We were then tasked with a series of engineering challenges that included building the load sensing surface, incorporating a display within the device, a server sized computer with a RAID array of eight hard disks and high-performance graphics card, a cooling system, industrial load cells, and an uninterruptable power supply to protect the precious data (of which we had only one copy). It also needed to be quiet, waterproof, and able to run continuously without input from us. Not forgetting to mention the enormous effort of Anthony Steel of UCL (another IRC partner) to write custom software that could stitch together millions of JPGs and render them to smoothly pan across the screen, directed by inputs from the load cell system, without any glitches or errors. These challenges are detailed elsewhere [ 1 ], but it is worth mentioning the unseen effort required to produce a simple looking research product .

2.2 Deployment with one participant and feedback

The Drift Table was deployed for several months in the home of participant SH, who was observed by an ethnographer over multiple visits to the home. SH's interaction with the table was an overwhelmingly social affair. He used it to talk with flatmates and visitors about trips and would set up the table inspired by what others said and did. He encouraged collective use by offering guests the chance to see their homes from the air. SH identified ‘post-pub, pre-bed, table-time’ as a characteristic routine for members of the household to engage with the table, which he claimed had provided a regular alternative to television watching. Further details of these observations are to be found in the archival account [ 22 ]. In addition, we also hired an independent documentary filmmaker to produce a short film where SH talks to an interviewer about his experience of living with the table. This film has travelled widely and has been viewed 12,858 times on YouTube [ 38 ].

We concluded that the Drift Table was a successful example of both designing for ludic pursuits and reimagining our relationship with technology. However, we did not appreciate at the time how successful it would be as a blueprint for the future research products we would later produce, both in how the device had been manifested but also in how it had been deployed and observed with SH, as an example of demonstrating new approaches to technology. At the time, our conclusions centred on a very careful and particular approach to how the device should be categorised:

“The Drift Table is not an artwork. Nor is it a toy or a tool. It is not designed to provide information, entertainment, or communication. But the temptation to interpret the Drift Table in any or all of these ways is key to its understanding. Perhaps it is best thought of as a pre-genre artefact, designed to be easy to use, but difficult to interpret. In deliberately withholding a clear interpretation or narrative of use, we created the opportunity for people to find their own meanings and uses for it. By avoiding suggestions of what people should do with the Drift Table, we created a situation in which they could play around with what they could do. The curiosity, exploration and aesthetic appreciation that this entailed is at the root of ludic engagement.”[22]

2.3 An extended life that revealed problems with the original implementation

The life of the Drift Table continued beyond this initial field trial (it is in fact still fully functioning at the time of writing) and there are numerous unreported twists and turns to mention. After SH, it was deployed with ZB who lived in a house in north London with her partner and young child. ZB's partner, who found its cooling fans too noisy, was less than enthusiastic about the Drift Table, so we collected it after a few weeks. After ZB the Drift Table was deployed with JB, a writer who lived on his own in an apartment in South London. JB was very curious about the device and wrote a short piece [ 26 ] musing about the relentless drive of modern technology to keep people busy and how he appreciated ‘the quiet leisure’ that the Drift Table had enabled him to pursue. During all these field trials, the Drift Table remained utterly reliable, but it had been treated with care. It required at least two people to lift it, and we transported it to and from deployments in taxis, gingerly manoeuvring its not-inconsiderable mass up staircases and through narrow doorways, wearing high grip gloves (see Figure 1 ). However, the next two opportunities to deploy it involved long distance travel, first to the home of a participant in Glasgow and second to an exhibition in Basel, Switzerland.

While the trip to Glasgow was not a complete disaster, it did not arrive in a functioning state. The eight hard disks were mounted inside custom brackets that we had lasercut from acrylic plastic (see figure 2 ) and these had somehow cracked on the journey, so it was returned for repair. We decided for the trip to Basel to use a specialist art handling company to transport our precious cargo. They made a wooden crate that the Drift Table would snuggly and securely fit into and all seemed well when it arrived in good health and performed well through the exhibition. However, the return journey was not so successful. We assume that the crate must have had a mishap somewhere along the journey because what was returned to us could best be described as a box of bits. Every plastic component had shattered, from the hard drive brackets to the cooling fan housings, plus the monitor mounts had sheared off leaving the display floating around. Once we got over the shock, we realised that we had been taught a lesson: our research product was not as robust as we thought it was. We had built something that would work well if handled with kid gloves, but not something that could be transported like any regular product.

2.4 Re-engineered internals and production of a second device

Around this time, the Drift Table was gaining traction in the HCI community and following the event in Basel, also receiving invitations to be exhibited further. This presented us with a quandary. We had designed and built the Drift Table to a specification that allowed it to work if handled very carefully, but that would not allow it to be freighted conventionally. Plus, our current Drift Table was badly broken. Then came the invitation to exhibit at the V&A Museum and Tate Britain over the same six-month period. These opportunities were too good to turn down so we set about re-engineering and building two new Drift Tables, that would be able to withstand ordinary handling. Externally (and functionally) identical to the original, the new design was constructed from a very strong plywood sandwich chassis (see figure 2 ) that each part (including a stainless-steel hard disk bracket) could be bolted to and which had very good torsional rigidity. This greatly improved the accuracy of the load sensing. These two Drift Tables never experienced any reliability issues despite them being widely exhibited and transported frequently. They are still fully functioning today.

2.5 Summary of the Drift Table one-off (eventually a two-off)

What we learnt from this experience is that a research product can have a life far beyond what was originally planned, and in this context, Odom et al's [ 32 ] qualities of finish and independence can be seen as dimensional. Research products can be more or less finished and more or less independent. Our original Drift Table was robust enough for carefully handled deployments, but not robust enough to be freighted to exhibitions.

We could not have expected at the time that twenty years later we would still be receiving interest and still be able to demonstrate the Drift Table. It has travelled so much and been seen by so many people that there are now multiple perspectives on this device. It is this notion of many viewpoints that inspired us to think about ways to bring research products to many more people.

3 INDOOR WEATHER STATIONS, 2011

After the Drift Table project, we continued to produce singular research products, many of which were highly situated and specific [ 13 , 14 , 17 ]. Nearly all these devices were required for engagement activities long after initial participant field trials. This fuelled the idea that it would be interesting to consider ways for our research products to be more widely circulated. If people were showing interest when these research products were presented or exhibited, then could there be opportunities for them to live with these devices as well? In the timeframe of most research projects, it would be impossible to run consecutive longitudinal studies. But what if it was possible to run multiple parallel studies during a project?

This is when the idea of batch-producing research products first emerged. If we could manufacture research products in low volume, then we could run parallel field studies with participants and gather a range of accounts of how people experienced our devices. Our first attempt at batch-production involved a set of research products called the Indoor Weather Stations [ 21 ]. These were designed to reveal the home's microclimate by highlighting small draughts, the colour and intensity of ambient light, and temperature differentials within the home, using three distinct devices (see figure 3 ). Unlike most technologies designed to increase environmental awareness, which are mostly utilitarian and instrumental (energy monitors, shower timers), the Indoor Weather Stations focused on supporting more poetic readings of the home's environment. They sat within a portfolio of designs for ludic devices that we had been developing since the Drift Table several years prior.

3.1 The three Indoor Weather Stations devices

The Wind Tunnel makes noticeable the almost imperceptible wind currents of the home. It consists of a miniature paper ‘forest’, made of paper film cut into delicate shapes, enclosed by a transparent, semi-cylindrical canopy. The forest emerges from a base that has a 3D-printed landscape creating the effect of a model world within the ‘tunnel’. At one end of the device a tall chimney projects upwards and houses a draft sensor that measures tiny gusts of air near the device. This controls the speed of a small fan inside the forest's enclosure, which amplifies any draft to create miniature storms that visibly buffet the ‘trees’ in the forest.

The Temperature Tape is designed for people to explore temperature gradients within the home. Two lengths of 2.5-metre-long fabric ribbon, which contain ribbon cables connected to temperature sensors embedded at each end, are wound around the device's central hub, resembling a wind-up tape measure. On the main body of the device is a needle gauge displaying the relative difference in readings between the sensors. The total span of the ribbons is long enough to measure temperature variations across the vertical span of a room, or from space to space. To make visible temperature variations along the ribbons’ length, the fabric ribbon is also screen printed with stripes of layered thermochromic ink, arranged so that between 15° - 25° C they fade from yellow to orange to red to black.

The Light Collector displays a history of changing ambient light on a small LED screen. The device is formed from two connected bodies consisting of a funnel-shaped dish, that can be articulated through a range of motion, on top of a small bottle-like main body. Lined with copper leaf, the dish has an RGB sensor at its centre. Every five minutes, the device recreates the colour represented by the sensor data as a one-pixel-high horizontal strip at the top of a display screen housed in the main body. At the same time, the earlier readings are scrolled down one pixel. In this way, the device shows a segmented view of the last two hours of ambient light with the oldest readings at the bottom, and the most recent ones at the top. A feature of all the devices is the ability to play back the last 24 hours of data capture by pressing a prominent ‘last day’ button. With the Light Collector, this causes the display to scroll upwards, revealing readings previously ‘hidden’ by the bottom of the screen.

3.2 Low-volume batch-production

We batch-produced 20 sets of Indoor Weather Stations (or 60 research products). We had acquired a Dimension Elite FDM 3D printer and printed each housing from ABS plastic in-house. This took weeks of 24/7 printing. This was our first use of this material and this machine, and we disliked the aesthetic of the rough printed layer lines. We were worried that the look of this material would make the device appear unfinished, like prototypes, and we were concerned that our research products might appear as proxies for what they could become rather than what they are . We tweaked the designs and explored print orientations to minimise the effect, but it wasn't practical to go to the time and expense of sanding and paint-finishing each part. In the end, we decided to embellish each device with hand-crafted elements. The Light Collectors’ funnels were copper leafed by hand. The Temperature Tapes’ ribbons were screen printed with thermochromic ink. The Wind Tunnels’ forests were produced from separate layers of laser-cut (vegan) vellum, inserted into narrow slots in the printed topographical base [ 27 ]. These details were crucial to offsetting the ‘prototype effect’ of bare 3D prints and produced objects that unusually combined the aesthetics of craft and 3D printing. Though it was still time-consuming to produce, it was ultimately more efficient than sanding and painting.

Despite their diminutive size, each was fiendishly complicated to assemble. The devices were built using the Microsoft-originated Gadgeteer platform [ 39 ], a system that enabled rapid prototyping of embedded devices with a range of electronics modules all connected with fine ribbon cables. The modular nature of this hardware accelerated and enabled an experimental design process working with sensors, actuators, and displays, but in hindsight, it was not the most straightforward platform from which to construct 60 finished devices (see figure 3 ). The Gadgeteer system separates each aspect of computing hardware into individual modules. There was a module for the processor, the SD card, the Wi-Fi, the power, each sensor, each button and so on. An individual Indoor Weather Station device has upwards of 11 modules, each securely screwed down in the ABS housing (we had learnt from the Drift Table to engineer enclosures robustly) each connected with what seemed like miles and miles of ribbon cable. Assembly was fiddly and longwinded, but the devices were very robust and reliable.

3.3 Deployment and feedback strategies for low-volume studies

We deployed the Indoor Weather Stations to 20 households around south London who had responded to posters we placed in shop windows and pinned on lamp posts calling for volunteers. However, we knew that the management of the deployment was going to be challenging. Merely visiting 20 households to set up the devices outnumbered the five or six visits we would typically make to a single household during a deployment, so we developed a number of strategies to gather accounts from people's experiences.

We encouraged self-reporting through several methods, which we referred to as deployment probes, including regular email exchanges and telephone calls. We used paper-based methods such as a ‘significant moment’ notepad that consisted of a book of tear-off pages featuring a diagram of each device and fields for time and date, plus space for comments about the moment itself. This proved to be very useful for participants to record points of interest and was also a useful aide memoire for conversations in person or over the phone. We produced paper-based reports of data collected from the Indoor Weather Stations to send to participants monthly, such as paper calendars displaying colour swatches of light collected from the Light Collector, that we sent to participants to reflect on their experience of the Indoor Weather Stations over time.

About halfway through the study, we also launched an Indoor Weather Stations community website for participants who were willing to anonymously share data from their Indoor Weather Stations with others. This included real-time and historic data of each connected device plus comment boxes to encourage interaction with others, such as household 14 asking household 01 “how do you achieve that aubergine colour?”. These materials prompted discussion at group events with participants, which we hosted at the university. We hired ‘cultural commentators’ [ 10 ] as we had in previous projects, commissioning a film about two particular households in the study.

Finally, we asked our participants to draw upon their own skills by inviting them to self-report using the language of their occupation or hobby. One participant, an artist, produced an oil painting of the Light Collector. Another, a journalist, interviewed us and wrote an article for a magazine to which he contributes. A linguist who participated in our trial was given all the returned ‘significant moment’ sheets to analyse. A final group event for the project was organised, where self-reports and other deployment probe materials were used as props for conversations between participants and researchers on emerging practices around the Indoor Weather Stations.

3.4 Polyphonic interpretations across multiple households

By deploying to multiple households at once, we were able to collect a greater range of qualitative data than the deployment of a one-off product could. Deployment probes enabled us to gather the individual voices in our study, rather than trying to capture them all in a summary account. Overall, we concluded that batch-production was useful in gathering polyphonic interpretation:

“… it may seem that we have rediscovered the ability for larger studies to reveal patterns in data that would be lost in the noise of smaller ones. There is undoubtedly truth in this, but we do not believe it is incompatible with our interest in varied orientations. If batch deployments allow us to aggregate data to uncover subtle trends, equally, they allow us to uncover multiple, potentially incompatible forms of engagement as well. This capability is important in assessing batch production and deployment as a methodology for research through design. This project marks the first time that we have batch produced highly finished, fully functioning computational devices for field trials with multiple participants … Our results, which illustrate the potential for batch production and deployment to allow both more nuanced and more varied forms of interaction to become evident, lead us to believe the approach a valuable one. In particular, it allowed us to appreciate the subtle but abiding attachment many of our participants formed to the Weather Stations [ 21 ]. ”

3.5 Summary of low-volume batch-production

There are several takeaways from the Indoor Weather Station project. First, the varied interpretations we received from participants came at the cost of the very deep and rich engagement that can be achieved with a single participant engaging with a one-off research product. Second, deploying research products in parallel to multiple households necessitated the design of strategies that become as important to design as the devices. In effect, the level of finish increases substantially as far more needs to be considered than with the design of a one-off. Third, in our ambition to scale the project to generate varied feedback about environmental matters, we had produced designs that had a lesser fit than our previous one-off research products, which had generally been designed for quite specific situations. Finally, we discovered a great deal about physically building multiple research products, where every step and every process has a big impact on resources. Although this would seem obvious if we were mass producing these devices, we were more surprised to encounter these issues with the relatively small quantities we were building. It taught us to think more about not only optimising designs in the future, but also taking great care in selecting underpinning technology. The modular system we used was far too complex to use at scale. In fact, the Indoor Weather Stations were so difficult to assemble, our team made a pact never to design and build anything as fiddly again.

4 DATACATCHER(S), 2014

Immediately following the Indoor Weather Stations project, we began working on our next investigation into producing research products at scale, with the Datacatcher project. We decided to scale up, reflecting on everything we learned from the assembly issues with the Indoor Weather Stations, and we decided to take the ambitious step of designing and producing our own electronics. We based this on the open-source version of the Microsoft Gadgeteer platform. Although today, the requirements for this project could be easily met by off-the-shelf boards such as those produced by ESP, at the time the only way to optimise the device both for production and operation was to design and produce PCB's ourselves. By doing so, we believed we could produce research products in greater quantities than was achievable on the Indoor Weather Stations project and so reach a larger audience of participants.

4.1 The Datacatcher mobile handheld device

Datacatchers are handheld mobile location-aware devices that stream short messages about the area they are in (see figure 4 ). Derived from a large number of ‘big data’ sources, the messages simultaneously draw attention to the socio-political topology of the lived environment and to the nature of big data itself [ 16 ]. The intention of the design is both to embody our conjectures about certain issues and to act as a tool in helping us to further investigate people's orientations around those issues. Technically, they are similar to a 2G GSM phone. Each device has a screen, battery, physical interface, and a GSM module. It uses a network-hopping machine-to-machine (M2M) SIM card for maximum coverage, and approximate location is deduced from the exact location of the cell tower it connects to (coarse grain location being fine for most big data searches). Data is streamed downwards from a server that does the work of scraping websites and databases for location-specific data from which to construct the messages. The Datacatcher can also upload the user's opinions about a place from an onboard multiple-choice questionnaire that feeds back into the data pool for that location.

Each device consists of: a two-part casing made from nylon, printed using the selective laser sintering (SLS) method and dyed various colours post-production; an SLS printed dial; a 2.3-inch monochrome e-ink screen; a rotary encoder; a custom designed PCB, containing all the electronic components and ancillary connectors; a lithium-ion battery; a SIM card; and a single screw to secure the casing together [ 2 ].

The Datacatcher is intended to be always on and harvests (or catches) data from every location it moves through, building a dense, linear history of messages over time. It has three basic modes of operation. First, a live mode displays fresh messages about its local surroundings every eight seconds. Second, turning the dial anticlockwise scrolls through all previous messages, and adds a footnote to the message detailing the data source (e.g. ‘UK Census’) and the location and time they were displayed (e.g. ‘Greenwich, 14 November, 15:42’). Third, turning the dial clockwise accesses a series of poll questions that can be answered by scrolling through multiple-choice options and selecting a response by clicking the dial.

4.2 High-volume batch production

In contrast to the Indoor Weather Stations, we spent a great deal of time developing and optimising the design of the Datacatcher for rapid assembly. This included making good use of the strength and elasticity of the SLS printed material, to produce clips and snap fittings for the internal parts, as well as the hole cut through the case where the dial sits. These details would be impossible to form using plastic injection moulding techniques and exploit the possibilities additive manufacturing affords. The screen, encoder, battery, and PCB all snapped into place and connected with only three short ribbon cables, significantly less than the Indoor Weather Stations. The assembled device was incredibly robust and was extensively drop tested – I do not believe we managed to even lightly damage, let alone break a single device. With one screw to hold the enclosure together, each Datacatcher could be assembled in less than 10 minutes. We outsourced the production of the enclosures and PCBs to companies that could produce these components in a fraction of the time that we could make them (though we had prototyped every component in-house). We estimated that we could economically batch-produce 250 units within our budget. This ambition however was curtailed when we discovered that the e-ink screen was in short supply [ 4 ].

We had chosen the Sharp LS027B7DH01 screen because of its high resolution, legibility and – most importantly – its low power requirements. When we came to order the planned 250 units, however, we discovered that Sharp had sold out and was not planning to produce more for months. This led to a worldwide search to find the last remaining units, but despite some success, our planned production of Datacatchers was reduced from 250 to an eventual 130. This didn't affect the realisation of the device itself, but it did impact the scale and plans of the field trial.

4.3 A deployment strategy requiring external support

Although we were unable to produce as many Datacatchers as we hoped, 130 units is still a sizable quantity. In readiness for deployment, each was boxed along with a charger, instructions, and participant consent forms. Stacked up, the boxed devices formed a considerable mountain in our studio (see figure 4 ), which made us somewhat thankful that we were unable to produce the full 250. With many participants to find, the approach we took to recruitment was different from anything we had done on previous projects.

Typically, we had found volunteers either by advertising through small ads and posters [ 5 ], or by being serendipitously matched with a group or situation [ 13 , 14 ] or through the conditions of funding [ 20 ]. With Datacatcher, we had been planning distribution from day one, but as production commenced we found it hard to agree on a strategy to find participants. It was of great importance to the team that we recruit a wider demographic than is usually targeted by new technologies. So, we invited three design consultants, who frame their practice through engagements with the public, to pitch ideas for how they would find a diverse range of people. They each proposed ideas ranging from pop-up shops at the Post Office, community dinner and distribution parties, and a proposition to distribute devices from a converted milk float that could be taken to various events. The most promising proposal came from a team who suggested finding volunteers at street markets and boot sales around London by simply asking passers-by if they would like to be involved in a design research project by adopting a device. This team had experience of working on improving services for the NHS and was used to working with a diverse range of people. The simplicity of the plan and enthusiasm from the team reassured us that this would be an interesting, if slightly risky, approach, and so we commissioned them. They would set up events at four locations where their team would be rousing the public to become involved in the project.

We went along to the first day of recruitment and distribution at a busy street market in Hackney, London and were surprised at some of their design decisions for branding the event. This included wheelbarrows full of Datacatchers and brightly coloured balloons printed with messages taken from the Datacatchers’ content. This boldness didn't really match our usual approach, but we had decided to sub-contract this stage of the project exactly because we wanted to try something different and their strategy worked. After speaking to thousands of people over four days of events, their team was successful in distributing all 130 devices to interested participants, despite our concerns about the design consultancy's ostentatious approach. Although distributing research products made with great effort and expense on the street was high risk, only 5-10 of the 130 remain unaccounted for. Each participant who signed up supplied personal details, signed a consent form, and, most importantly, agreed to be filmed as part of a documentary about their experiences with a Datacatcher. The success of this flamboyant recruitment process was important for us to witness and helped shape our approach, albeit in a very different way, in our next project.

4.4 Gathering feedback with documentary film teams

Although we struggled to decide upon a distribution strategy, we decided early in the project that we wanted to gather feedback through documentary film. Unlike the Indoor Weather Stations, which included many deployment tasks, we wanted Datacatcher participants to focus on the devices rather than on data collection tasks. Capturing the lived experience would be difficult, and the scale of the study meant that ethnographic observations or interviews, which have provided a foundation for previous studies, would be pragmatically impossible. The choice of documentary film had advantages over other methods. First, they would capture a record of participants’ comments in their own words, which we could review over time. Second, we could hire external filmmakers to produce them. Not only would this cut our team's workload and allow us to deal with the logistics of running the deployment, but it would also build on previous work on documentary film for user research, as well as our prior work with cultural commentators [ 10 ]. We had previously used filmmakers, journalists and poets, in order to provide a richer understanding of how the artefacts we make might be understood. Working with outside commentators also allows evaluation to be relatively independent of the research team, which often means that participants can be more candid than they would be with us.

4.5 Thoughts about the feedback

Of the 130 Datacatchers distributed, the filmmakers managed to record just over 60 documentaries capturing participants’ experiences. The remaining participants either decided against being filmed or we were unable to schedule a recording time during the two-month study window. Below are some thoughts from the archival paper on the project:

“ We suggest that our field trial of the Datacatchers successfully demonstrates the potential of batch production and deployment as a methodology for revealing multiple narratives about research devices… Our experiences raised many challenges for the approach, however. First, it was difficult to construct and maintain a coherent ‘world’ for the Datacatchers across the network of partners and participants we worked with, and while the explicitness of this forced may appear preferable to the implicit negotiations that occur in smaller studies, it may be easier to achieve a sense of ‘naturalness’ in the latter than the former, allowing the focus to be on the research devices rather than the oddity of the trial itself. Second, running such a large-scale trial is a huge endeavour, and we experienced a level of waste – in terms of nonresponding participants, chiefly – that may be difficult to avoid. Third, capturing data from a large trial of minimally committed participants is a special challenge, arguably militating against many of the methods commonly in use in HCI.

It is here that we believe we have made our largest methodological contribution, in the form of the documentary films we collected. We suggest that the documentary videos worked well to achieve a balance in capturing rich data without producing an unmanageable amount – it is possible to watch all the films in a single sitting, for instance. If it is inescapable that the films reflect their makers’ sense of what is important, this was mitigated by their previous training, our briefing, and the limited amount of editing that they did. Watching the videos, we believe that their high quality, manageable length, and the compelling accounts they portray justify our belief that this is a highly effective approach to capturing large-scale data. ” [ 16 ]

4.6 Summary of high-volume batch-production

The transition to high-volume batch production presented many challenges, but also new opportunities. We were a little disappointed about how some people orientated to the data itself. We hoped and somewhat expected that the messages would be revelatory and insightful for all participants. Instead many were surprised, dismissive, or occasionally doubtful of the facts. For others, the data demonstrated harsh facts that were uncomfortable to process (‘life expectancy in Kensington is 20% higher than in nearby Newham). This was some distance from some of our earlier focus on designing for ludic pursuits and, like the Indoor Weather Stations, speaks to a more uncomfortable fit of the design. While the highly detailed accounts we had collected on past projects with single participants were absent, they were replaced by a breadth of opinions that demonstrated new and unexpected perspectives on the device we had designed.

The quantity of data we collected reflects our achievement in making and distributing 130 fully-working research products to a diverse range of Londoners over that period. Although we were unable to capture every detail of the participant's usage, we were beginning to appreciate the added value of our research having an impact on a wider public with a broader range of engagement. Our long-running research mission to practically demonstrate the alternative uses for computational technology was moving more towards mass engagements.

5 MY NATUREWATCH CAMERA, 2018

The Datacatcher project taught us that producing and distributing research products at scale is difficult. We had been on a trajectory of growth, but it was clear that trying to make a manufacturing step beyond what we had already achieved would be impractical and difficult to resource. However, we were still interested in exploring the wide distribution of our research products. So, we investigated a completely different method of producing devices with a project called ProbeTools [ 3 ]. In this project, rather than building and deploying artefacts ourselves, we created designs that other researchers could build instead. This approach saw us distribute designs from a website that hosted detailed building instructions for multiple designs, software, lists of off-the-shelf components, downloadable 2D & 3D parts to either lasercut or print, and a web store to sell a custom PCB Arduino shield for one of the many camera designs that we had manufactured and sold on at cost. Although aimed directly at other researchers with building skills, this new ‘self-build’ methodology seemed promising as we sold all 150 Arduino shields and received positive feedback. This made us ponder the question: if we can make this work with researchers who have making skills, could we design a simpler research product that anybody could make at home?

The seed of the My Naturewatch project was born during a meeting to explore ideas for collaborative projects between our studio and Robert Phillips of the Royal College of Art's Design Products department. We demonstrated our ‘self-build’ methodology and he showed us a project where he had worked with beekeepers [ 33 ], and we all discussed a general interest in wildlife. During the meeting, we hit upon what seemed like a wildly ambitious idea: what if we designed a wildlife engagement device that people could make at home themselves and then somehow promoted it on BBC Springwatch? (This is a hugely popular British natural history show that is broadcast every day over a two-week period every June.) If we could reach a large television audience with an interesting and easy-to-make device, then maybe some people would want to get involved – this would be a great way to engage with nature and digital making.

5.1 Designing a research product that anybody can make

For brevity, I will skip a description of the intermediate events, which are described elsewhere [ 18 ], but two years later we had designed and developed an inexpensive wildlife camera that people could make themselves at home (see figure 5 ). It differs from other DIY products, or projects aimed at the maker community, by being radically accessible. We designed it explicitly for non-experts who would not normally consider building any kind of product or device. It could be fabricated from simple ingredients: a food storage container and plastic water bottle, a Raspberry Pi Zero computer and camera module, a USB battery, and downable software. Despite the housing being constructed from household materials and designed with flexibility in mind, it has a strong and recognisable aesthetic, more so considering how many have been replicated precisely to our suggested specification (more on this in 5.5).

The My Naturewatch Camera also has many advantages over commercial ‘camera traps’ (which are generally developed for hunting over appreciation of wildlife). For instance, it uses computer vision frame differencing to trigger photographs, eliminating many of the false-positive images that are found with passive infrared (PIR) triggered cameras. The sensitivity can also be tuned to allow the system to see smaller wildlife such as garden birds, mice and spiders, which is very hard to achieve with PIR. Its settings can be accessed via a web interface from a phone, tablet, etc (it has no physical buttons), which also provides a live preview from the camera, allowing for precise subject framing. The quality of the photography is very high, and the overall cost is less than half of any commercially made product.

Instructions to build the camera were hosted on a project website [ 24 ] designed to a high level of finish . It featured a detailed step-by-step guide of how to assemble the hardware, presented like a richly illustrated food recipe. It was written so that people with no digital or physical making experience could follow, thus demonstrating a good fit with the audience. We were very careful to avoid lingo, and any necessary technical words (such as SD card or ribbon connector) had links to further information so that nobody would get lost, as we didn't want anybody following the instructions to get stuck on ‘technical jargon’ that could form a barrier to engagement. The software was also developed to a high standard of finish and great care taken over the design of the web interface, which was used for access not only to the setup but also for retrieving the wildlife photography. To avoid issues with the Raspberry Pi installation, we packaged together our software and Raspberry Pi operating system as a single disk image and wrote detailed guides on how to copy this onto a blank SD card. Once copied, the camera would start and function straight away. The website also had instructions on how to operate and use the camera, and a maker ‘forum’ where people could post questions and share results.

5.2 Deployment via a prime-time television show

We had successfully aligned the wildlife camera development to the interests of the BBC's Natural History Unit and in June 2018 it was featured on its Springwatch programme. The audience that day was almost 2 million viewers and our website received 22,532 hits during the show. It was clear to us that many people began building cameras within days of the show as we counted software downloads and answered questions on the website's forum. As the days progressed software downloads snowballed as the project continued to proliferate [ 29 ]. During this time, questions and feedback to the forum (more on this below) prompted us to tweak and modify the instructions in hundreds of detailed ways as users spotted errors, areas of confusion, or aspects we could improve upon. The Raspberry Pi retailer Pimoroni [ 35 ] began selling the parts in a dedicated My Naturewatch Camera kit and live streamed a camera construction video. A children's TV presenter made a building video for their YouTube channel, and the BBC had the My Naturewatch Camera Springwatch feature pinned in the list of that season's highlights, so that it could be watched again on the programme website. Though the level of interest in the project peaked in the Summer of 2018, it has had a long tail and the website still receives several thousand visitors per month at the time of writing. We conservatively estimate that at least 3,500 cameras have been made so far, based on software downloads from unique IP addresses and sales of kits by Pimoroni.

5.3 How to get feedback from makers

The difficulty we found with the earlier ProbeTools project, is once our designs were published, makers are under no obligation to provide us with any feedback. We do not make anybody sign up or register to participate in either project, to avoid unnecessary barriers or distractions from engagement. With ProbeTools we encouraged makers to email feedback or share builds over social media, and we also reached out to customers who purchased ProbeTool PCBs through our web store. This was only partially successful though. We received some detailed accounts, but most were scant as the relationship was different from those we had in previous projects. The makers were not participants in our research project, they were researchers doing their own research, and had no commitment to offer us any information. For the My Naturewatch Camera, we needed a different strategy.

The maker forum for My Naturewatch Camera was created not only as a venue for people to ask questions (or post problems) but also as a place that could become a hub for a community we wanted to grow, where people could share content from the cameras or alternative builds. After the project launched, the forum quickly become a hive of activity. For moderation purposes, makers must register with an email address and although this step might restrict some potential posters, there are 460 active users who have contributed to the posts. What is perhaps more remarkable is that at the time of writing, the forum still receives around 4,000 unique page views per month, which is about twice the traffic to the main site. With the forum, not only are makers sharing feedback with us, but also with each other. The forum has also become a self-supporting entity with a handful of ‘super-users’ helping newbies to the site. The requirement to register for the forum also provided us with the means to contact individuals for more direct feedback which has enabled us to gather thicker descriptions through interviews. We still believe that it is important not to make registering a requirement to access build instructions though. Beyond the forum, we also received thousands of messages through social media via #mynaturewatch, which people used to share images of their camera builds and wildlife pictures.

5.4 Thoughts about the deployment and feedback

Compared to our efforts with batch production, the My Naturewatch Camera appeared to hit the sweet spot in terms of our efforts to widely circulate a research product as a small team. Here are some thoughts from the archival paper on the project:

“ In addition, the paper contributes a description of our strategy to engage people in making this computational product via an orchestrated effort involving a national primetime TV show, instructional website, and social media outreach. These contributions are mutually dependent: we designed the camera with Springwatch in mind, and our engagement efforts were successful because My Naturewatch Camera is suitable for that audience. Taken together, our methodological contribution is to highlight that the co-development of a research device and engagement strategy allows research products to be circulated at scale. Thus we see our primary research contribution as the description of the overall configuration of the project, including the relationship we forged with the Natural History Unit.

We have had unsolicited feedback from hundreds of makers, and their comments (all publicly available on mynaturewatch.net/forum, pimoroni.com and Twitter) suggest that most enjoyed building and using the cameras. Moreover, for many of them, the experience has had impact on their engagement with digital making and with local wildlife as they have become more aware and concerned with the various birds and animals they photograph. Perhaps our most significant contribution, then, is to all the people who have used the cameras to find new companionship with the creatures who live amongst us—and to the animals who will benefit from greater care as a consequence. ” [18]

5.5 Summary of self-build distribution of a research product

The My Naturewatch Camera is the culmination of our efforts to scale up the production of research products. Although conceptually and visually simple (a dimensionally smaller inquiry in some respects) it was designed to be radically accessible and has several features worth highlighting. First, it empowers non-experts to learn digital making skills without the pre-requisite proficiency required to participate in maker culture. Second, it encourages people to engage with nature in new and playful ways outside of the serious practice of amateur and professional wildlife photography. Third, it demonstrates to a wider audience the possibilities that computing can offer when freed from the constraints that dictate the design of consumer products, reflecting our studio's mission over the past two decades. Finally, the design and development of the website and software have a level of finish that is perhaps higher than any other research product in our portfolio, which may seem surprising given the simplicity of the physical artefact. The accessibility of the instructions, the clarity of the interface, and the downloadable disk image, also speak to a high degree of fit with the audience

The notion of designing research products that would generate impact emerged through the Datacatcher project, but was intentionally explored with the design of the My Naturewatch Camera. For the first time, participants were not just volunteering to adopt a research product and live with it, they were electing to spend their own money and time to buy parts and build their own device. Although we did not get feedback from every maker, it was clear from the maker forum and social media that many people were new to making, constructing cameras and using them quite experimentally to capture wildlife imagery. In addition, people were engaging with them in ways that we didn't anticipate. School teachers were building them in class, other researchers were writing research bids based around building them with communities they were working with, and nature trusts were acquiring kits to construct in wildlife engagement workshops with their members. Although centred in the UK, we were also seeing contributions from a wider global community.

The design of the instructions, website and software has been continuously iterated since we launched. The software, in particular, has seen the most changes, not only with our own developments but also with contributions from a couple of expert makers who have submitted improvements to the GitHub repository. This is the first time that any of our participants have contributed to the development process and the first time we have updated a research product so significantly after it has been deployed. The physical design however has not changed. Despite the hardware affording all manner of configurations, we have noticed that nearly every camera build that has been shared with us has retained our suggested construction of a plastic bottle top glued to a tall, rectangular food storage box. We developed this design over countless iterations because it suggested an aesthetic of a camera as much as for the practical reasons of keeping the camera lens dry, but apart from a handful of variations that we have seen, including one camera built into a log, most have followed our blueprint. Simple as this design is, seeing it replicated almost exactly by many different people adds weight to the strength of this aesthetic. This also suggests to us perhaps that the makers of the My Naturewatch Camera are broadly from communities outside the usual maker groups, who we imagine would be more likely to modify the design.

6 DISCUSSION

As highlighted throughout this paper, our design research studio's long running mission has been to practically demonstrate the alternative uses for computational technology outside the constraints that dictate the design of consumer products. We have been accomplishing this by designing and producing research products that challenge and reimagine our relationships with technology over multiple projects, spanning twenty years, of which four are highlighted in this paper. With the Drift Table, we focused on designing for ludic pursuits in response to concerns that domestic technology was too task orientated. In a similar vein, the Indoor Weather Stations prompted people to explore their relationship with their domestic environment without imposing normative values. The Datacatcher aimed to raise and resource engagement with societal issues through the exploration of geo-tagged data. Finally, the My Naturewatch Camera sought to promote an exploratory engagement with nature through a form of non-expert digital making.

The use of scale in our practice has been a powerful method to explore how research products might reach ever larger audiences not only to gather varied accounts, but to see our research products have a wider impact on the public, and to a small extent enter the mainstream, as in the case of the My Naturewatch Camera whose growth and popularity is now somewhat out of our hands. However, although this paper describes a trajectory towards mass engagements, we would not advocate any of these examples over the other. Our studio is just as likely to design new one-off research products in future work as we are the next self-build project. The benefit of having now worked at different scales is the knowledge of what can be achieved at various levels, and each has its own merits which I will summarise below.

6.1 The features of each level of scale

6.1.1 One-off research product. The one-off is best suited for situations and inquiry that require a thick description of the engagement, demonstrated in this paper by the Drift Table. The one-off is particularly well suited for specific or perhaps unusual sites and environments. From our portfolio, other exemplars of the one-offs include designs for cloistered nuns [ 13 ] and residential care homes for the elderly [ 14 ]. The one-off research product can embody technical complexity that would be difficult to replicate at scale and is well suited to ambitious designs. The fit and the inquiry of a one-off will likely be tuned for the specific situation, but the finish and independence can be at a level that is just enough to make it successfully through a field trial.

6.1.2 Low-volume batch-production. The Indoor Weather Stations are examples of a production run in the 5 – 30 range (another was a device we built for communities tackling energy and sustainability matters [ 20 ]). This volume is well suited for creating research products for a small group or community, and the production is likely to be manageable entirely by a small team of design researchers. Feedback from participants can be captured in broad strokes and through intense fragments gathered from the more occasional contact with participants within a group. Many varied opinions can be captured at this scale. Recruitment of this size of participant group is likely to be quite straightforward and the devices can still incorporate some of the hand-finished and bespoke elements that characterise the one-off. The fit of the low-volume research product will be different to the specificity that the one-off affords, needing instead to appeal to a broader group situation - this variation is slight but worthy of note. The nature of the inquiry also changes as the focus moves towards gathering accounts of variance across multiple participants instead of a thick description from a single participant or household. The finish and independence will need to be higher than a one-off as the research products will need to operate in a variety of situations and with more independence. In addition, more resources will be needed to gather multiple accounts, including tools such as deployment probes.

6.1.3 High-volume batch-production. Although closely related to low-volume production, high-volume batch-production of above 30 or so units will likely require aspects of the production to be outsourced. Care should be taken on the design of the assembly process so that the devices can be constructed quickly and reliably. The design of any computational infrastructure required to support the study will also need careful planning and implementation to handle the number of units that will simultaneously be live. At this scale , the relationship with participants is likely to change. While is still possible to personally know all the people in a low-volume study, this becomes harder if not impossible in a high-volume study. External assistance is almost a given to help gather stories of engagement with the devices, though it is important to consider carefully how these stories are captured so that the feedback is both manageable, high quality and digestible to prevent the project from becoming unwieldy. In a similar way to a low-volume study, the fit of a high-volume project will have a different specificity than a one-off and will need to be designed with a diverse range of participants in mind. The nature of the inquiry changes further as insights and experiences become more fleeting, yet more varied, and the sheer scale of the feedback begins to demonstrate the potential for a research product to have a significant impact on many people. The finish and independence will need to be far higher than a low-volume study or one-off.

6.1.4 Self-build. Self-build is an efficient means to distribute research products to a wide and potentially very large audience. It requires a strategy for publicity to reach the critical mass needed to gather multiple unsolicited accounts of participant experiences. Build instructions need to be very accessible, and the research products need to be designed to fit the making skill of a general audience better than is typical for most DIY or open-source designs. Smaller self-build studies [ 3 , 15 ] can also produce rich accounts but developing mechanisms to encourage makers to get in touch is vital for gathering feedback. Self-build provides the strongest route for research products to have impact on a wide general public. The fit of self-build will also differ from the individual specificity on a one-off. In the case of the My Naturewatch Camera, the design was tuned to have a broad appeal and leans towards the familiar by using the known concept of a wildlife camera as a hook to attract a wide range of participants, though the implementation would be quite unfamiliar to non-expert participants. The ability for makers to customise their devices, however, meant that they could increase the fit of their cameras to their circumstances. The nature of the inquiry also changes from any other level of scale as it will not be possible to capture the experiences of everybody who encounters the device as there is no obligation for participants to provide feedback. The finish and independence will need to be at the highest level. For example, despite the simple appearance of the My Naturewatch Camera, the design of the device, software, instructions, and website had to be developed to a point where non-experts could construct a robust camera themselves from basic electronics and household materials.

6.2 Dimensions of research product qualities and the feedback trade-off

Through this paper I have reflected on our design research studio's practice of producing research products at various scales and argued that scale is one of the defining qualities to add to Odom et al.’s [ 32 ] list of inquiry, finish, fit and independence as research product qualities. I have also sought to demonstrate how, like scale, these qualities are dimensional. For instance, designs can exhibit more or less finish, fit and independence often driven in response to the demand of scale. Inquiry, however, lies on a more qualitative dimension where the nature of the investigation changes with scale . A one-off research product device for example can entirely drive the inquiry , whereas a self-build research product will have inquiry embedded across a collection of research product assets that will include the design of a device, building instructions, software and so forth. Inquiry is embodied in how research products are experienced, but there is a feedback trade-off when working at scale in how these experiences are gathered. Table 1 charts how both the access and the style of accounts changed across the four projects outlined in this paper, which I include to simply illustrate the trade-off of working at higher scales, where higher volume limits access to participants yet potentially accesses a far wider range of perspectives. Note that this trade-off between depth and breadth holds for a small research team. If the team could grow in proportion to the scale of the project then large studies could still permit close observations, though this might imply hundreds of researchers to study thousands of participants.

One-off Individuals Frequent Access Thick Descriptions
Low-Volume Batch Small Community Regular Access Interviews

7 CONCLUSION

I have reflected in this paper upon the lessons learnt from designing and producing research products in quantities ranging from one-offs to those reproduced in thousands, through the descriptions of four case studies. I have demonstrated how our approach to the design, development and production of research products has evolved in response to working at scale , detailed its implications for participant feedback, and discussed strategies for gathering accounts. I have argued scale is one of the qualities [ 32 ] of a research product and that production at different volumes has impacts on the other qualities. In addition, I have highlighted the key features of research products produced at various levels of scale . This is not to single out any level of scale as being better than the other, but to unpack what makes working at particular levels distinct.

There are a number of reasons that our design research studio has been drawn towards working at higher levels of scale . First, we have found it incredibly compelling to create research projects that enable more people to experience our designs. It allows us to demonstrate alternative uses of computational technology to a much wider audience. Moreover, we are proud of how we have developed a practice of crafting high-quality research products and want more people to enjoy them. Second, it demonstrates how practice-based research can integrate impact into a project from the beginning. In the UK, impact is a (growing) measure of quality in the government's Research Excellence Framework evaluation and so it has become an important factor in the evaluation of research funded by the UK taxpayer. Although scoring well on evaluation metrics is not our main motivation, it is worthy of note. Third, we were able to gather more varied accounts of people's experiences with our designs across diverse groups. This was the reason that we pursued scale in the beginning, and we have always found the feedback to be insightful, surprising and meaningful [ 16 , 18 , 21 ]. Finally, participants do not necessarily regard large-scale research products as research at all, due to their high finish and independence , as well as their means of distribution (e.g. online instructions). They are free to encounter them like they would conventional forms of computational technology. This allows us to assume that their feedback reflects a degree of uncoloured honesty that is far harder to achieve with the closer researcher/participant relationship that is present with a one-off.

These then are the contributions that I hope will encourage others to also consider scale as a valuable method to embrace in research projects seeking to have impact on a wider audience, generate impartial feedback on designs, and create situations where the research products do not even have to be framed as research. We have found it invaluable.
