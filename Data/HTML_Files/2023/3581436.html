For decades, research around emerging technologies has been inspired by science fiction and vice versa. While so far almost only the technologies themselves have been considered, we explore the underlying software development and programming approaches. We therefore conduct a detailed media content analysis of twenty-seven movies that examines the role of software development in science fiction by identifying and investigating new approaches to programming and how software development is conceptualized portrayed within science fiction scenes. With the additional analysis of eighteen design fiction stories exploring the scenario “What if everyone is able to program?”, we envision potential impacts of the democratization of software development on business and society. Our study opens new discussions and perspectives, by investigating the current vision of the future of programming and uncovers new approaches to software development which can serve as a starting point for further research in the HCI community.

1 INTRODUCTION

Emerging technologies like Augmented Reality (AR) [ 71 , 87 ], Robotics [ 15 ], and the Metaverse [ 51 ], are significantly inspired by Science Fiction (Sci-Fi) stories, movies and series [ 78 , 79 ]. Researchers and developers [ 38 ] take up the basic concepts and ideas from Sci-Fi authors and movie makers and transform them into actual applications and implemented software systems [ 78 ]. However, the ways in which the software or technology was developed within Sci-Fi, on which programming concepts this development is based, and which interaction modalities are used, remain open questions. In real-world practice, software development usually encompasses a range of practices and technologies, and professional software developers often use powerful integrated development environments (IDEs) and highly specialized programming languages. Recently, more end-user-oriented approaches have emerged that focus on enabling software development for non-programmers.

In our study, we explicitly take a step back from the software or technology as such and examine how software development practices are represented in Sci-Fi by conducting a sequence analysis of Sci-Fi movies. We relate our findings to the research discourse around software development and use design fiction stories to envision the impact and implications around the idea that it is not only the software or the computer that is ubiquitous, but that the software development as such is democratized, and therefore also ubiquitous. Since the vision of the ubiquitous computer, as proclaimed by Mark Weiser [ 88 ], is omnipresent in Sci-Fi, strong cross-references can be found between the interactions, as well as future concepts of software development in Sci-Fi movies, and the End-User Development (EUD) approaches in the real world. Overall, we observe in the selected movies that end-user-oriented approaches to software development dominate, as compared to traditional representations of programming (e.g., writing source code). Therefore, we perform a detailed examination of EUD approaches and of the effects of a possible democratization and ubiquity of software development. Our findings from the movie analysis point to end-user development concepts, such as programming by argumentation or physical interaction, that provide impetus for discussions about EUD. Thus, this study provides an important contribution to anticipating possible paradigm shifts and further developments in EUD. In order to explore the impact of a ubiquity of software development, we create a fictional scenario, “ What if everyone is able to program? ”, and use a design fiction methodology in one of our courses with students studying Human-Computer Interaction (HCI). In contrast to our movie analysis, most students defined programming traditionally as “writing code” and did not even consider EUD approaches. The stories also reveal a variety of different effects on the personal attitudes of non-developers, social aspects in our society, the job market, and security in our society and economy, which should be considered as potential implications in future paradigms and designs. Our study marks a starting point for a comprehensive series of further investigations into the exploration and development of new software development paradigms, tool designs, and design guidelines based on Sci-Fi movies, as well as other promising media, such as series and games.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Chapter 2 covers related work in the fields of HCI research around the discourses of software development and Sci-Fi. We also identify the research gap and describe our overall research approach. Chapter 3 is a detailed sequence analysis of popular Sci-Fi movies that capture the role of software development. In Chapter 4, we describe the related effects and implications around the democratization and ubiquity of software development. Chapter 5 is a discussion of our findings in terms of advancing approaches to software development (Chapter 5). Chapter 6 provides suggestions for future research work.

2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK

Our study has its roots essentially in two research discourses. We considered the field of software development and the research around Sci-Fi and its role in HCI research. In this section, we discuss the evolution of software development and highlight research approaches around Sci-Fi.

2.1 Software Development in a Nutshell

Software development is one of the most important areas of research in computer science, and it has been continuously evolving since the first high-level interpreted programming languages [ 6 ] emerged, to the point now where it is a global industry that touches the lives of nearly everyone [ 25 ]. This evolution has led to a large number of different programming languages [ 19 , 85 ]. Early on, calls for incremental programming and continuous user participation emerged, since these enable program changes in a non-destructive manner [ 62 ] when new requirements and challenges arise [ 54 ]. This led to improved software quality, the prevention of unneeded and increased user satisfaction [ 1 ]. Many approaches attempt to deal with the challenges by abstraction [ 34 ], simplifying, standardizing, and stabilizing development processes, making better use of synergistic effects, and making structural changes, such as outsourcing [ 2 , 76 ]. Agile software development methodologies, such as Extreme Programming (XP) and Scrum, are very important in the evolution of software development, responding as they do to the challenges of our fast and unpredictable world [ 21 , 24 ]. All the different approaches that have emerged over the past decades still exist today and continue to be used regularly, depending on the use case and the underlying corporate structure [ 74 ]. In general, the sustainability of the software development plays an increasingly important role [ 89 ]. In addition to programming languages, practices, and methodologies, a large number different development environments and tools [ 70 , 86 ], such as integrated development environments [ 68 ] and automated tools [ 3 , 80 ], have also emerged over the years. In contrast, the discourse around End-User Development (EUD) focuses on non-developers and thereby opens up the field of software development to almost everyone. This is necessary because programming is very difficult to learn due to the fact that many people apply poor problem-solving strategies, and suffer from a number of emotional issues [ 7 ] as well as varying levels of confidence and motivation [ 83 ]. Programming also requires a variety of skills [ 44 ] and the domain-specific motivation of end users [ 44 ]. Specifically, EUD describes the sub-process of adapting new technologies to users’ practices and the in-situ emergence of new practices [ 22 , 57 , 81 ]. Lieberman et al. [ 52 ] define EUD as a collection of methods, techniques, and tools that enable non-developers to create, adapt, and extend software. More practice-oriented approaches refer to EUD when non-developers have to switch to a more abstract level to perform their specific task, and where end-users perform activities at four different levels of complexity that require diverse skills [ 55 , 58 ]. These four levels are “Use”, “Parameterization/Customization”, “Recomposition/Integration” and “Extension/Altering” [ 55 ]. However, activities performed at the “Use” level do not represent actual EUD activities in the sense of tailoring. The transfer of software development practices to non-developers [ 48 ] opens up new possibilities with regard to the design of development environments and tools, and there is still a great deal of untapped potential in other practices [ 47 ] and strategies [ 26 ] that could inspire future designs. Some approaches focus on understanding and supporting the motivational forces of non-developers [ 11 , 56 ], such as the study by Benzi and Fogli [ 10 ] or the tool FADER [ 48 ], which utilizes the motivational aspects of gamification [ 18 ]. Despite the evolution of software development, many problems persist, meaning there is fundamental need for new ideas and concepts.

2.2 Science Fiction and its Role in HCI

Several studies have shown that Sci-Fi offers a unique approach to reflecting on technology and provides meaning for otherwise disconcerting new technologies [ 4 , 65 ]. Michaud [ 63 ] argues that Sci-Fi plays a role in the innovation process in techno-scientific societies (e.g., through the creativity-enhancing force that it exerts on the imaginations of those engaged in entrepreneurial and scientific practices). Following this Sci-Fi is a vehicle for pushing the boundaries of what is currently possible and has had a great impact on shaping recent inventions. For example, Star Trek served as an inspiration for mobile phones, wireless telemetry, 3-D printed organs, MRIs, and CT scans [ 72 ]. Further examples, include video conferencing systems which were depicted in “2001: A Space Odyssey”, and the video phones and robots depicted in the 1927 film, “Metropolis” [ 38 ]. According to Jordan et al. [ 38 ] HCI and Sci-Fi have the potential to grow in unison.

While some studies highlight positive future scenarios, others argue that fiction influences beliefs about new technologies, particularly an understanding of risk, which affects public support and thus policy and government funding [ 32 ]. Complementing this, Sci-Fi stimulate the theoretical imagination and promote sustainability research [ 29 ]. Related to this perspective, Barik et al. [ 8 ] argues that software engineering research will temper an optimistic bias with pessimistic futures or dystopias and lead to more diverse and stimulating research directions. Moreover, Sci-Fi exerts an influence on public opinion regarding current issues. This is indicated, for example, by Milne et al. [ 66 ] which investigated how online searches and conversations on discussion boards changed after the release of four Sci-Fi movies concerning privacy issues arising from the use of technology. Similarly, a correlation was found between reading Sci-Fi and an acceptance of uploading the mind into a computer [ 49 ], while another study suggests that media portrayal might shape public attitudes towards cyborg technology [ 43 ].

As result of this fostering of creativity and critical thinking, Sci-Fi is often used in educational settings. For example, some studies show how Sci-Fi can be used to promote scientific thinking [ 9 , 59 ], and to enhance creativity, innovation, and critical thinking and problem solving [ 40 ]. Methodological examples include Goldsmith and Mattei [ 31 ] , who asked students to write essays about Sci-Fi and AI, while [ 45 ] challenged students to write short Sci-Fi stories as a way of learning about risk assessment.

The question of whether Sci-Fi can be used to advance HCI research is the subject of extensive investigation. The entanglement of Sci-Fi was analyzed by reflecting on the presence of robots in computing literature [ 67 ], while another study [ 37 ] analyzed how often the term “Star Trek” was mentioned in the ACM library. Jordan et al. [ 39 ] show that the entanglement between the entertainment industry and science is growing, leading to a bi-directional relationship that creates new job opportunities in the integration of Sci-Fi into education and deepens the influence of Sci-Fi on the public's acceptance of new inventions. Likewise, Milburn [ 64 ] notes continuous and productive reciprocal relationship between science fiction and science (see also [ 78 ]), demonstrating a collaboration between movie and HCI visionaries using various movie examples and research prototypes.

One link between research and Sci-Fi is the design fictions method. Mark Weiser's essay “The Computer of the 21 st Century” [ 88 ] can be seen as the first Design Fiction that found critical acceptance in the field of HCI. Since then, this method has been used in multiple studies [ 46 ]. Storytelling is a powerful tool that allows us to regard a topic with a different, more personal and emotional mindset, and broadens the number of factors we can consider, focusing on societal effects in addition to technical ones [ 45 ]. Oliver [ 69 ] asked participants to develop a prototype, but started the process by asking them to write short stories, based on prompts. The considered topics, used to gain a deeper and more nuanced understanding through Sci-Fi, range from broad categories like sensing technology [ 91 ] or tangible interfaces [ 17 ] to more specific ones like speech interaction [ 5 , 16 ] or shape-changing interfaces [ 84 ]. Panda [ 72 ] investigated healthcare in Sci-Fi by focusing on three distinct works of fiction, while [ 46 ] uses quotes from a Sci-Fi book, combined with personal photos provided by the participants, to illustrate entanglement with a fictional world. Brun et al. [ 14 ] prototyped a memory orb that was inspired by Sci-Fi. Jordan and Silva [ 41 ] also examined the role Sci-Fi could have in regard to HCI research and showed that the genre impacted the field on multiple levels. They conclude: “We believe that such an elevation of Science Fiction as a ‘serious research topic’ has strong potential to inform the Computer Science, HCI and Design research, application and education endeavors of the future.” [ 41 ]

All these aspects of Sci-Fi's influence on designers and researchers can be reduced to four basic types: influence through (1) inspiration, (2) creating expectations, (3) creating a social context, and (4) describing new paradigms [ 78 , 79 ].

2.3 Research Gap and Research Approach

As the literature review has shown, there are already numerous approaches and research efforts to incorporate Sci-Fi into research, and vice versa. However, the relationship between Sci-Fi and software development is severely underrepresented in the research discourse, especially from an end-user perspective. Jordan found that the BASIC programming language was, in part, inspired by Star Trek, and argues that “we have long underestimated the important, invisible relationship between Sci-Fi and HCI and computer science research” [ 36 ]. The aforementioned study by Schmitz et al. [ 78 ] addresses software development only briefly from a very limited movie selection. However, in reviewing examples of previous research in the areas of software development, we note that there is still a great deal of untapped potential that could be accessed through the use of novel approaches.

In order to exploit these potentials further, we adopt an exploratory approach, comparable to that used by Schmitz et al. [ 78 ], to investigate the role of software development shown in Sci-Fi movies, focusing on possible inspirations, expectations, and new paradigms. We chose to limit our exploration to Sci-Fi movies, rather than extending into literature, since software development usually plays a subordinate role and is only depicted figuratively in the margins of the actual plot, without the narrative going into the details of the development process. Furthermore, especially abstract approaches used by non-developers are particularly difficult to describe in text, and easier to capture visually. Science fiction series and video games are equally promising opportunities for future studies, though we choose not to engage with them in this study. We aim to extract novel software development concepts from Sci-Fi movies and locate them in the HCI research discourse. Here, the questions arise around how the implementation of such concepts might impact the future, what ethical, economic and social consequences could arise, and how we can create ubiquitous software development approaches. To shed light on these questions, we additionally asked students to create design fiction stories within the scenario “What if everyone is able to program?” as part of a university course on web development.

We take a multi-step methodological approach within our study. First, we conducted a media content sequence analysis of selected scenes from Sci-Fi movies. From this sequence analysis, we extracted novel software development concepts. Since end-user-oriented approaches dominated the sequence analysis, we related the insights we obtained, in particular, to the vision of EUD, to create a fictional scenario in which everyone is able to create or customize software. Finally, we introduced this scenario into a university course and analyzed student-created design fiction stories for diverse implications of this scenario and the vision of EUD.

3 AN EMPIRICAL STUDY OF SOFTWARE DEVELOPMENT IN SCIENCE FICTION

We examined a selection of Sci-Fi movies to get an extensive overview of the role of software development within that genre, focusing on movies, in which software development and abstract end user-oriented approaches are depicted. Due to the sheer volume of available Sci-Fi sources (there are currently just over 220,000 movies labeled with the genre “science fiction”), a complete review is nearly impossible. As an initial filter, we focused on popular mainstream movies, as this is where we expect to see the greatest impact on the general image of software development in society.

3.1 Data Collection

In order to acquire a dataset (Figure 1 ), we searched IMDb, the online rating portal for movies and series, on the 30th of June 2022, using the search term “science fiction”. We then narrowed down the selection by filtering by publication date (after 1 st January 1990), user rating (minimum 7), rating count (at least 75000) and language (English). Then, we excluded all movies without scenes about programming were eliminated through relying on past experiences of watching the particular movie.

Movies that do not deal with software development, such as Minority Report, were excluded, despite having had a high scientific impact on technology. The final limiting factor was availability. We decided to purchase the three most popular streaming services (Netflix, Amazon Prime and Disney+) and disregarded all movies that were not available on one of these platforms. Furthermore, we decided to reduce our exploration to twenty-seven movies, which represent nearly 50 percent of the available material, in order to enable a detailed qualitative analysis. 3.2 Data Analysis

We selected and analyzed, in detail, 207 separated scenes, with a collective runtime of over six hours out of the overall runtime of more than 58 hours for all selected movies. The process of movie analysis was structured as follows: First, at least one researcher watched the full-length movie and documented important scenes to analyze. Then, the scenes were cut and coded using MAXQDA, a previously deductively defined code set was used. To ensure the quality of the deductive category system of the content analysis (i.e. the codebook), we conducted a multi-level intercoder reliability test with MAXQDA, guided by McDonald et al. [ 61 ]. First, we verified the degree of consistency of the scene selection by watching the first hour of the film M8 separately. In order to calculate the intercoder reliability, we used the minimum code overlap rate on the segment level approach with a minimum value of 80%, since an exact match method is not suitable for audio-visual data material, as a minimum variance of the scene selection by a few seconds is unavoidable. This method is based on a Cohens Kappa variant [ 13 ] integrated into MAXQDA. Thereby, an agreement of 85.57% could be determined. We then checked the code system and the coding behavior of the different coders, by checking the scene selection of the movie M25, which resulted in a high consistency level of 84.01%. The selected scenes, the interpretations of the different coders, and the code system were discussed, and the code system was inductively extended at this point and ambiguous codes were removed. As a result, a coding system consisting of 58 individual codes was used for coding all the scenes. We now present the main findings from the qualitative content analysis of the selected video sequences from the twenty-seven movies.

In general, there is a strong disparity in the distribution of scenes among the different movies. While some movies included no scenes (M19, M20, M21, M23, and M27) or only a few scenes (M1, M9, M10, M12, M24, and M25), others extensively presented software development practices, in particular EUD approaches, (M2, M3, M4, M11, M13, M16, and M22). It is interesting to note that software development practices are addressed and portrayed in older movies (before 2015), but only to a very limited extent in more recent ones. This seems to be a contradiction since software development as a whole has become increasingly well-known in society over time and now plays an important role in many areas of life. In most scenes, software development was implied rather than explicitly shown. In many cases, even abstract representations and metaphors were used to convey the underlying programming in a different way. The implicit and abstract representations were especially related to programming by physical interactions in digital worlds (M2, M3 and M22), because here the boundary between a physical interaction with the virtual environment and programming is blurred.

Initially, one could assume that the approaches often only differ in the respective input or interaction form, such as textual, physical, or acoustic input. Nevertheless, the different concepts must be explicitly considered separately, since the design and development of EUD approaches result in a variety of (technical) implications that differ from each other, for example, the use of an intelligent system for disease diagnosis that also has an XAI component and discusses the diagnosis with the specialist (e.g., doctor) is applying the “programming by argumentation” approach. This results in the need for an NLP component and a speech-to-text API in the design and development, as opposed to a traditional code-writing approach where only text input is required.

In most movies, only certain activities, such as programming (even in abstract forms) and hacking, are shown. Often, only the deployment of the resulting artifact is shown as, for example, in M1 and M18, where only the uploading of a virus is shown, and it is only indirectly implied that the virus was developed specifically for the case at hand. In contrast to this, analysis and debugging activities are rarely discussed or shown at all, demonstrating that a rather one-sided picture of software development is presented. In various scenes (e.g., in M2, M3, M4, M13, M17, and M18), characters were simply talking about different development activities and plans, with most exchanges being vague and abstract.

When analyzing the Sci-Fi movies, we found it particularly difficult to distinguish the role of the key characters in the selected scenes, and it was often not obvious from the context whether the protagonist was a non-developer, an amateur or a professional developer. On the other hand, we noticed that professional developers depicted in the movies (e.g., M6 and M13) use end-user-centered approaches, such as programming by natural language and by argumentation in complex situations to perform their work. For the qualitative analysis of the data, we adhered to the thematic analysis approach [ 12 ], and our deductive category system served as a basic structure and supported us in the first step in examining the coded material more deeply. Through multiple reflection loops, with the aid of visualizations of the codes, and selective reexamination of specific sequences, three more abstract concepts emerged, which we now discuss in more detail, illustrated by key examples.

3.2.1 Programming by Physical Interaction. One of the most interesting development concepts we discovered during the analysis is “programming by physical interaction” . This was particularly true of various interactions with intelligent humanoid robots (M4, M13 and M15) or in the context of fully digital worlds (M2, M3, M22 and M24), similar to a metaverse. The physical interactions had completely different characteristics. In M12, Meronym uses a tangible interface that consists of floating rings to send out a distress signal, while in M15, the main character, Hiro, designs a product consisting of a large number of small magnetic metal pieces that he mentally controls through a headset and can arrange in any position. Similarly, in M13, Tony Stark develops a way to transmit complex commands to his Iron Man suit through implants in his extremities, and even accidentally calls the suit in his sleep, implying a subconscious connection between Stark and the suit. There were also examples of programming through the use of gestures and facial expressions, but this was often shown in combination with other approaches such as “programming by natural language”.

For example, in M15, a robot is taught new skills through an explanation similar to the way in which an adult would talk to child, with the twist that Hiro is the child, teaching Baymax to perform a special type of fist bump, using simple spoken words, gestures, and physical touch to transfer his knowledge to the robot (Figure 2a ). Baymax then verbally confirms that the new data is transferred into his database. Here we see programming portrayed as an interaction that closely resembles an approach for teaching skills and knowledge to a human. This is mirrored in the experience of researchers and developers who are working on robotics in different contexts, and who would like to see such EUD concept, especially for programming by end users, such as elderly people or employees in nursing homes. Gestures are also used by Tony Stark in M13, to control his suit and, when in doubt, to disassemble it. The use of facial expressions for programming can be found in M4, in which the humanoid robot, Sonny, is taught to wink by Detective Spooner as a gesture of trust. The examples of robots as intelligent systems show that the line between training intelligent systems and programming them is often blurred.

The concept of “programming by physical interaction” was more common in relation to digital worlds, where almost every physical interaction with the metaverse, objects, and other actors represents programming at the lowest level. In M22, the characters use objects to trigger certain effects within the digital space called the Oasis. For example, solving and throwing a Rubik's cube will turn back time, and compressing a metal artifact can delete the data of everyone currently in the Oasis. There is also a scene in which the character, H, repairs a broken virtual motorcycle in his workshop (Figure 2b ). The actual repair process is not shown, but it is implicitly conveyed that it is a physical process, since H “builds” objects in the Oasis. What is shown here, however, is the process of analysis as part of software development, in that H takes a close look at the damage. The characters navigate through the Oasis by interacting (clicking and swiping) with projected 3D interfaces, using gestures or giving voice commands.

In M22 we see programming being portrayed as similar to performing a physical task. The IOI corporation needs a large number of workers who can pick up and distribute explosives all over a bridge that they plan to destroy. This portrays programming as a new way of interacting with a digital space, just as a human interacts with a real one. To achieve the goal, physical interaction is required. The situation is similar in Justice League (M24), where the protagonist Cyborg acts in digital space through gestures and seemingly physical interactions with virtual and cyber-physical objects. The protagonist moves visualized stacks of money to a visualized fund pool, manipulating the account balance of a third person who is in physical space and able to withdraw physical money immediately after the interaction (Figure 2c ). This is not a metaphor for hacking, though the protagonist has the ability to perceive information systems and the data and codes behind them as three-dimensional quasi-physical objects, and to manipulate them with quasi-physical interactions. The movies M2 and M3 take the concept to its logical limits as the main protagonist, Neo, a professional developer, is the so-called chosen one within the Matrix. He has the ability to shape the Matrix, also a fictional metaverse, to his will. The change is often brought about with the use of gestures, or even concrete physical interactions with the digital world, such as in the scene in which Neo saves the life of his companion, Trinity, by removing the virtual bullet lodged in her chest with his hand (M3). He is also able to see the source code of the fictional metaverse (the Matrix) in the virtual environment itself while everyone else cannot (M2, M3). There are many more examples in these movies that illustrate the concept of programming by physical interactions , and they are too numerous to be discussed in detail here. Related preliminary basic approaches are presented by [ 27 , 77 , 82 ]. However, within real world settings, programming through physical interaction is actually less common and invites further investigation by HCI researchers.

3.2.2 Programming by Natural Language. Another important finding in our study is the concept of “ programming by natural language” . This was especially shown in connection with intelligent systems and humanoid robots (M4, M5, M6, M7, M8, M11, M13, M16, M17 and M25). There are several examples (M4, M6, M11, M13, and M16) in which commands, and complex sequences of commands, are easily conveyed using natural language and thus can be programmed by both professional developers and non-developers. In M16, for example, the main protagonist, Cooper, uses natural language to make humor and honesty adjustments to the robots. This is done in combination with already known EUD concepts like parameterization (as described within the “Tailorability Staircase” [ 31 ]). Comparable scenes in which only instructions for complex digital or physical actions are formulated can be found between Tony Stark and the AI Jarvis (M6 and M13), and between Detective Spooner and Sony (M4), and between Sam Bell and the abstract robot GERTY (M8) (Figure 2d ). This programming approach is particularly interesting, since it opens the field of software development to everyone. There would no longer be any obstacles in terms of the skills required (e.g., programming languages) and the tools needed (e.g., development environments and hardware systems). In this approach, the boundary between the programming and the training of an intelligent system are fluid and can no longer be precisely separated. A specific example of this is the scene in M15, in which Hiro describes the metaphorical meaning of the word “sick” to Baymax, and this knowledge is also contained.

In the area of programming by natural language, a subcategory of particular interest were discussions among protagonists (humans, intelligent systems, or robots) that were then resolved by the different participants building an argument, in what we have summarized as “ programming by argumentation ”. Specifically, for example, Tony Stark interacts with Jarvis as if he was speaking with another human. Jarvis is able to understand Stark's intentions through contextual clues, without being given explicit commands (M6 and M13). In one scene, Stark gives Jarvis the order to check for specific vital functions and Jarvis autonomously decides to run a complete diagnosis, concluding that Stark has suffered from a panic attack. Similar scenes can also be found in M6. Even though it is not completely unambiguous, it is strongly implied that Stark is changing Jarvis's programming through these interactions, allowing Jarvis to alter his own code based on new experiences. In M8, the protagonist discusses with an AI robot why a certain entrance should be opened. The AI robot initially refuses access. After a linguistic input of additional parameters and contextual information, it reverses its decision. In M4, there are various scenes where discussions and arguments that involve opinion and independent thought arise between the human actors and the intelligent system, VIKI, as well as the humanoid robot, Sony, trying to change the programming, in the scenes represented as opinion and ‘own thinking’. There are other examples in the movies that follow the same pattern. In addition, natural language is often used to query data, describe complex issues, or the different functionalities of the systems (M4, M6, M7 and M13). Here, it is also partly implicit that Explainable AI (XAI) components are used to explain to the programmer or non-developer the functionalities, situational decisions, and internal conflicts of the systems using natural language. The developers then have the opportunity to respond and, in some cases, accomplish their task by leading discussions. Initial technological foundations necessary for implementing such concepts can be found in [ 23 , 28 , 33 , 42 ].

We see great potential in the overall approach of “programming through natural language” for the research discourse around EUD approaches, as it involves the full abstraction of programming languages. At this point, the HCI community could pioneer the design of natural language-based adaptation and programming capabilities.

3.2.3 Other Programming Approaches. In addition to these approaches, we detect others in certain specific scenes, including programming by music or acoustic signals , or with the help of holograms. In M11, the anthropomorphic humanoid robot David uses a flute, in combination with abstract keyboard input, to program an alien computer, a technique he first learns through a holographic audio-visual recording. Holograms play an important role in Sci-Fi movies and automatically provide the sense of a futuristic scenario to the audience. There are several scenes in M6 and M13 in which Stark uses holograms to reconfigure his suit on the one hand, and to analyze and adapt data on the other. In M10, hologram-based deployment is at least leaning towards programming, but this still required a physical pen to situate the content.

Emotional programming represents another abstract form of programming. There are various scenes in which human emotions are interpreted as a kind of command input. For example, in M15, Hiro feels sad or hurt, and Baymax responds to this by looking for a solution. In M26, Guy gains consciousness after meeting a girl and falling in love with her, and later motivates other programs to “strike” by triggering emotions in them. Later, he regains his memory after kissing the girl he loves, implying that the emotional connection was able to alter his code. In M25, the intelligent app PAL changes its own programming based on emotions and becomes evil. This scene leads us to the next concept, where intelligent systems reprogram or hack other systems. PAL specifically takes control of the humanoid robots that its creator developed as its replacement. The scenario is similar to M4, where VIKI takes control of the latest generation of robots. The way in which the hacking or reprogramming between the systems actually takes place is never explained. Only the deployment and the connections are shown, in which the robots receive an update, or are permanently connected to the other system via a direct link.

Overall, we find that “traditional software development methods” that involve writing source code, or at least pseudocode, are rarely depicted in the movies. We only found the creation of source code in M17, where the protagonist and professional developer, Caleb, writes code during his working hours in one scene, and reprograms the security system of a house in another. The question then arises: what image of software development is being conveyed to society in these movies?

4 EXPLORING THE IMPACTS OF SOFTWARE DEVELOPMENT BY EVERYONE

As shown in the film analysis, EUD approaches are more prevalent in Sci-Fi movies than depictions of traditional software development. To capture the potential implications of the democratization of software development (everyone is able to program) and describe a practical way to achieve this vision, we used the methodology of design fiction ourselves. Russel and Yarosh [ 75 ] define a design fiction as a text that “seeks to devise and tell a compelling story about what could be believable and what could be implemented in the not-too-distant future.” While design fictions can be used to inform people about technological possibilities [ 53 ], the method is often focused on exploring the societal and social implications of potential new inventions [ 90 ]. While design fictions utilize many different and diverse kinds of media, text is the most common [ 20 ] and was also used here. Specifically, we used our own lectures, where students are introduced to web development, to give the students the additional task of writing a design fiction story. The specific prompt text for the students was: “ Write a design fiction story about the fictitious scenario ‘Democratization of software development – What if everyone is able to program? (e.g. through skills, supporting applications, ...)’. Develop your own point of view on the topic and critically discuss at least four different arguments. Stories should be four to five pages long, with no title page, no table of contents, and using the common formatting standard. ” The course focused on web development using the MEAN stack, and also discussed modern no-code and low-code approaches that can be classified as end-user development in order to give students a comprehensive insight into the possibilities of modern software development.

The course is part of the master's degree program in Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) and thus addresses a broad target group with different scientific backgrounds. The exact details of each participant were not explicitly recorded as part of the course, however, the ages range roughly from 20 to 30 years. The professional backgrounds (previously earned bachelor's degrees) were very diverse, ranging from psychologists to social scientists, to people with backgrounds in design and software developers. Within the course, 19 students took the final exams and, of the 19 final submissions, only 18 design fiction stories could be used in this study, as one submission was more a project report on the student's own development work. The 18 remaining design fiction stories were between 4 and 6 A4 pages long, resulting in a total of 87 pages. Consent was obtained from students at the beginning of the course for verbatim, excerpted, and anonymized processing and publication of the design fiction stories. All students who made the final submissions agreed to this.

We coded and analyzed the design fiction stories using MAXQDA, following the procedures described below. We ran an empirical, in-depth qualitative analysis of the data, guided by the thematic analysis approach. As a first step, we read all the design fictions to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the material. Upon further review of the material, we then inductively categorized all the design fiction stories according to our research gap. In addition, iterative discussions were held to further refine each theme and develop subcategories, resulting in five themes. Based on the guidelines for qualitative analysis methods [ 60 ], key quotes from the material were discussed in more depth. Throughout the following discussion, we label the stories “S1” to “S18” and present the main findings from the analysis of the eighteen design fiction stories prompted by the scenario, “What if everyone is able to program?”.

4.1 Necessary Prerequisites for Programming by Everyone

Most of the texts began by determining the prerequisites that would be necessary to build a world in which everyone could program. Most students focused on their own ideas of an education system, calling for computer science as a mandatory subject in school. There were also many concrete ideas on how this goal might be realized. S10 argues that “competitions could be held to develop games alone or in groups. We now know that computer games have been proven to be good for learning. In addition, however, developing such games would challenge students’ creativity […]” and S18 explains that “there are so many online courses about learning to code that they are affordable for schools. They are even not only affordable but also more helpful as every student can learn at their own speed.” This student also argued that the education could start at kindergarten, suggesting that “younger children would already get toys that introduce programming to them for example during attending kindergarten. That could be something like a construction kit that brings near basic programming logic to the child, for example, if conditions.” Abstract end-user-oriented approaches such as no-code or low-code platforms, which could be assigned to the research discourse around EUD, were not considered in any of the student design fiction stories. Most of them defined programming for themselves as an activity that consists purely of writing code, and thus requires extensive knowledge that can only be acquired in the context of extensive integration into everyday school lessons.

4.2 Attitude Changes of Non-Developers

When it comes to the effects of this fictive scenario, it was often argued that technical knowledge and programming skills would generally improve the logical abilities and change the mindset of many people. S5 said, “It helps people improve their problem-solving skills and teaches them how to break down any big problem into smaller ones that can be tackled easily.” It was also mentioned that research skills (S16), the ability to deal with frustration and setbacks (S14), and memory (S9) will improve. S4 even supposes changes in people's characters, arguing that the way in which situations are handled would be objective and logical, without being so driven by emotion.

Likewise, there was a consensus that exposure to technology would increase people's willingness to accept innovation and change, leading to more possibilities in the area of digitalization and act as a catalyst for restructuring infrastructure to employ new technical breakthroughs. S8 speculated that, “The rate at which technologies will advance would be unimaginable. Things that would take years could be completed 100 times faster. This also means that more ideas will be developed and not just forgotten about due to the lack of resources or technical understanding.” It was proposed that every city would have specific districts that resembled Silicon Valley (S9). S17 concluded that “everyone would have more knowledge and thus trust in computers, a lot more people would benefit from technological inventions, that are already on the market, but not widely distributed.”

4.3 Social Changes in our Society

The proposed societal changes go even further, alluding to an improvement in general equality of opportunity and power in society. S2 wrote, “By learning how to program, people can also learn how re-signify the use or the purpose of computational devices and platforms, to come up with tools meant to inform and to give others the opportunity to create and express their own voices, to recuperate history, and to break the predominant and stereotypical narrative that programming is a skill meant only for some.” S5 even argues that the scenario would lead to a greater understanding of each other within society and explains that the goal should be “to create a common logic vocabulary that can help generating a common understanding”, implying that communication among people would benefit from a shared understanding for programming. S2 also mentioned that the gap in the number of female and male programmers would change, when everyone would be able to program and that developer teams would most likely have a more diverse setup.

In general, the students painted a positive picture of the possible fictive scenario, with 150 codes of positively connotated mentions, with less than 50 negative and 50 mixed instances in our analysis. First, most agreed that new innovations would appear and greatly benefit society, and it was argued that the user-friendliness of products would most likely increase. S5 said that “having more interdisciplinary teams of developers would highly increase the quality of tailoring a product to the target groups’ wishes and needs.” Another aspect that was often mentioned as a positive development was a higher independence from big tech companies, as the wide-spread programming skills would allow for more open-source projects and start-ups. S3 explains that this “could be a win-win solution as there would be free software available for all, and proprietary software companies would need to make their products more appealing to the end-user (e.g., invest to improve the usability, accessibility, and experiential and hedonic dimension of their products).”

4.4 Job Market and New Opportunities

With regard to the job market, it was seen as beneficial that there would be many more applicants for IT positions, but also critically noted that this would most likely lead to a decrease in the pay and appreciation that is nowadays awarded to professional programmers. S1 mentioned that this would disrupt the entire economy in some countries, where many people are specialized programmers. S13 and S17 also worry that the increased ability to choose a programming job would discourage people from pursuing a career path in education or care work, leading to a lack of qualified workers in these areas. Another common theme was the concern about data security as a widespread ability to program would ultimately also allow people to abuse their powers. S4 explains, “the number of hackers would increase exceptionally since common criminals would now be able to program […].” However, the general consent was that this could be compensated through the increased ability of people to defend against such attacks. S10 describes a world with many digital self-defense classes that teach people “how to use firewalls” and much more, and even likens it taking judo classes.

4.5 Potential Threats to our Society and Economy

A variety of threats to our society and economy arise from the scenario, since the potential for misuse increases with the ubiquity of software development. Potential threats mentioned by the students included power outages that would render many aspects of society helpless (S18), the loss of independence and purpose due to the faster advancements of robots and smart machines (S5), and overpopulation due to better living conditions (S18). It is clear that this topic allowed students to explore the future implications and access very personal convictions that they held. S11 wrote that programming “provides everyone a new way of creating beautiful objects and meaningful moments” and concluded the design fiction story by saying that it “is not a privileged tool and it should be accessible for everyone.”

5 DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS

The analysis of Sci-Fi movies has proven to be fruitful for the HCI community. Inspired by [ 78 , 79 ], we reveal three aspects from the films: inspiration, expectations, and new paradigms and, in addition, ethical and social consequences using design fictions. We find several interesting software development concepts, such as “ programming by physical interaction ”, especially for the emerging area of the metaverse [ 51 ], “ programming by argumentation ”, in relation to the use of AI-based systems like robotics in elderly care [ 15 ], and various other concepts like “ programming by acoustic signals ”, which could be interesting in the area of animal-computer interaction and related novel approaches like the “dog internet” [ 35 , 50 ]. Thus, compared to Schmitz et al. [ 78 ], we were able to shed more light on the field of software development in Sci-Fi movies and uncover several novel approaches. This can be attributed to our larger movie sample, systematic selection process, and rule-based analysis. The aforementioned concepts should be considered in future tool and interaction designs, using new design guidelines, and they deserve further investigations from an HCI perspective. Our approach is also suitable for use on Sci-Fi series and games, which often introduce new technological concepts in even more detail, due to the typically longer runtime. One example would be the series Lost in Space in which the programming of alien robots is changed through physical and social interactions and an emotional bond with the young protagonist, Will Robinson. These series also provide an example of “programming through acoustic signals”, which is an extraordinary approach that could find appeal in various areas of HCI such as animal-computer interaction [ 35 , 50 ]. Furthermore, the Stargate series features many instances of more traditional programming, while the series Black Mirror explores a different technological invention in each episode and could contain a plethora of new approaches to software development. We also see great potential in a further study with Sci-Fi literature, as it often serves as the original inspiration for movies, series, and games.

Following Ludwig et al. [ 55 ], our research reveals how hard the differentiation is between using or interacting with a system and actually altering or programming such a system. This is especially true in the interaction with intelligent systems and other AI devices, which is often performed using natural language that can be considered as programming, as AIs typically learn from and adapt to everything they experience, using every available data point to alter their own behavior (code). Similarly, it can be argued that natural language interaction with intelligent systems, which is already possible today, can also be considered programming. For example, when we use a voice assistant like Alexa or Siri to set a timer or an alarm for “tomorrow at 10 o'clock”, it represents an EUD approach since this activity can be classified as a “Parameterization/Customization” activity on the tailorability staircase, according to Ludwig et al. [ 55 ], and therefore counts as tailoring activity. This is clear evidence that we have already partially arrived at the vision of the democratization of programming and ubiquitous software development, which means: everyone is able to program. At the same time, however, our voice input is also used to improve the voice assistant. Nowadays, these activities are usually considered as usage, rather than software development. However, as AI technology advances, the lines between usage and software development might soon blur completely. We see the need to develop a new understanding of programming in relation to AI systems, especially considering the research discourse around EUD [ 52 , 55 ]. However, as the design fiction stories show, not everyone perceives it that way. Of course, the concepts we uncover in the analysis represent even higher levels of software development that require additional investigation in further studies. Within Sci-Fi movies, programming is often presented as something that can be done more intuitively than people would imagine nowadays, like in M16, where the protagonist uses parameterization by natural language to make humor and honesty adjustments to the robots. This leads to the question of whether such approaches are shown in comparison to writing source code through typing just because they are easier to convey, or to create the artificial feeling of a future scenario, which is the entire modus operandi of science fiction. To answer this question, a comparative study with older movies could be conducted to determine if they show more traditional approaches to software development. Nevertheless, several of the movies we analyzed illustrate a vision that could soon become reality. This reality encompasses programming not as typing commands into a keyboard, but rather programming as interaction, as in teaching robots new skills by modelling them, altering algorithms by explaining to them what should be changed, and giving positive or negative feedback to a computer program and causing it to adapt accordingly. What if, instead of teaching people how to communicate with machines through years of study, we found a way for machines to understand and interpret the way humans naturally program? Would that not truly enable anyone to program? The movies we analyzed show interactions with programs that are easy, enjoyable, and do not require training – in short humans are empowered to program machines in a natural way.

The design fictions show how many positive effects were seen as a result of a more broadly distributed ability to program, making strides in this direction even more desirable. The students stressed how a more diverse perspective being included into software development would lead to a better adaption of systems, an in-depth understanding of programming, math and problem-solving skills, and an improved level of equality. On the other hand, the design fiction stories reveal a common understanding of programming as a skill-intensive activity with regard to math and logic that is relatively static in terms of the process of learning and using it. The suggested strategies to get everyone able to program focused on changing the education system and starting earlier with teaching children at kindergarten how to think algorithmically. It seems like the concept of EUD has not yet made its way into the public discourse. This contrasts with the results of our movie analysis, which raises interesting questions around the reasons behind this. Therefore, in a format based on our study, we would like to enter into a conversation with the students in order to determine the background to this contradiction and to foster the dissemination of EUD approaches. In order to gain a comprehensive picture of the implications of the fictitious scenario it is also necessary to consider the potential downsides of enabling everyone to program, and assessing potential risks, which is an important contribution of design fictions [ 45 ]. Many of the students expressed concerns that the ability to program could be misused, foreseeing increasing numbers of hacking attacks, as well as undesirable societal shifts towards an over-reliance on technology. Additionally, a surge in technological advancement also raises the danger of technology itself growing too potent and developing its own interests which may be opposed to ours. This is reflected in scenes from M25 where the mobile phone, PAL, plots to acquire world domination. Whether the goal of enabling everyone to program is reached by developing new ways for EUD, or by strengthening computer science education, these threats must be taken seriously, and society and its laws will have to adapt to the new situation. In the end, the ability to alter and develop socio-technical systems is like any great power: it needs to be used with responsibility and care. Researchers, developers, and designers have a responsibility to consider such negative implications in terms of design ethics when exploring and developing new software development paradigms, development tools, or design guidelines.

During our analysis, it also became apparent that differentiating professional developers from end-user developers is difficult. In the movies M6 and M13, Tony Stark is a world-renowned computer scientist and engineer, yet when developing new cyber-physical systems, he relies on the help of his AI, Jarvis, with whom he communicates through natural language and voice commands. It is necessary to point out that EUD does not only enable non-developers to create, adapt, and extend software, but also gives professional developers the chance to perform their programming task on a more abstract, and therefore more intuitive and less mentally straining level [ 73 ]. In a way, it is apparent that there are many programming tools even now that allow a switch to a more abstract level to write code. Many modern integrated development environments (IDEs) already include features that simplify many aspects of software development, such as providing parameterization using sliders, or allowing code to be written through natural language (e.g. OpenAI codex), or the suggestion of code by an “AI pair programmer” (e.g. GitHub Copilot [ 30 ]). The vision of EUD does not only concern the people who have previously been unable to develop software, it is also interesting to anyone who has extensive experience in the field. This leads us to the question: is the distinction between professional developers and non-developers, in terms of tool design and EUD approaches, still valid, or do we need a new understanding of professional developers as end-user developers?

Our findings have certain limitations. Sci-Fi films are not always intrinsically designed to present feasible future concepts, but rather to provide an entertaining experience. Hence, there could be a tendency in the movie industry to present programming through gestures and voice control, simply because these input types are easier to create on-screen. In this case, technical requirements are not necessarily considered, which makes implementation by developers and designers difficult. As already mentioned in related works, however, this is precisely the strength of this rather inspiring and boundary-expanding reciprocal relationship. Furthermore, it became apparent that many concepts were rather ambiguous when it came to determining whether or not software development was actually performed. This room for interpretation leads to another limitation: despite our satisfactory inter-coder reliability, there is a slight variance in the evaluation and interpretation of the scenes by the individual researchers. More accurate definitions of EUD could be created here in further work to further increase the agreement rate, even though we are aware that exact agreement is almost impossible.

6 CONCLUSION

With the analysis of software development in Sci-Fi scenes, and the design fiction stories based on the scenario, “What if everyone is able to program?”, we generate new insights in the field of software development, in particular EUD, and outline novel approaches to software development such as “ programming by physical interaction ”, “ programming by argumentation” , and various other concepts like “ programming by acoustic signals ”. We also show how much the lines between interaction and development have blurred already in Sci-Fi, and how vital it is to agree on strict and meaningful definitions for the concepts. Overall, we conclude from our study that there is an urgent need to develop new definitions for the unique identification of different EUD approaches, as well as a new understanding of professional developers as end-user developers, and the training of AI systems as a new end-user-oriented form of programming.

In addition to the limitations already mentioned in the discussion, another, as mentioned in the methods section, is the limited selection of movies. There are still plenty of potentially insightful movies that were excluded from our analysis, either because of their unavailability or because of the selection itself. In addition, there is still a lot of potential in other interesting media such as series and games. Therein remains vast potential for future research to provide a richer understanding of how and what modern Sci-Fi movies represent in terms of novel developmental modalities and what inspirations can be drawn from them. In general, the question of how software development might change and improve in the future is less explored, despite the importance of programming for our technology-based society skyrocketing. As some of the design fiction stories describe, the way we program in the future will shape how society will develop, not only in terms of innovation, but also with regard to welfare, social interaction and overall contentment.

We are confident that our analysis reveals new approaches to software development, especially EUD, and demonstrates the importance of thinking about new ways programming could be done in the future. We hope our study inspires not only HCI researchers, but also designers and even software developers to start thinking about new ways to create end-user-oriented tools to foster the democratization of programming that pave the way to ubiquitous software development .
