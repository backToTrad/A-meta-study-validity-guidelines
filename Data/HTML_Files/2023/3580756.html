Challenges and Opportunities for Interactive Technology to Support Parents of HIV-Positive Children in Ethiopia in the Disclosure Process

Fearing stigma, parents often hide their children's HIV diagnosis from them, and postpone disclosure, in turn negatively impacting children's well-being. Our work explores whether interactive technology can support disclosure. In the first study, we examine disclosure experiences and the role of interactive technology from the perspective of HIV-positive children and parents. Through Thematic Analysis, we highlight how disclosure is linked with parents’ own experience of HIV, and that disclosure needs to be viewed as a process. On this basis, we contribute an experience prototype that guides parents through an incremental disclosure process using interactive storytelling. In a second study, we evaluate the prototype through interviews with six parents. Leveraging Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis, we show that the prototype has potential to transform how parents understand and approach disclosure. Based on our results, we present further design directions, and discuss the (limitations of the) role that technology can play in this context.

1 INTRODUCTION

Because of stigma, parents of HIV-positive children in Sub-Saharan Africa are often reluctant to disclose their children's HIV-status to them [ 31 , 32 , 45 ]. Consequently, children remain unaware of what is happening to their health, or receive wrong information because they are told that they have a less stigmatized disease [ 77 ]. As children grow older and begin thinking critically, the lack of awareness of their own HIV-status becomes increasingly challenging, and negatively influences treatment adherence [ 123 ]. Hence, parental non-disclosure of the HIV-positive status is a crucial barrier to disease management among children with HIV, especially in those who are entering adolescence. Therefore, supporting parents in successful disclosure is of central concern.

Generally, there is a consensus that HIV-status disclosure to children should be treated as an ongoing process [ 71 , 90 ], starting in early childhood and continuing until children can take full responsibility in managing their own health. By adopting an ongoing disclosure process – providing little-by-little increments of HIV information, children grow up receiving age-appropriate health information according to their illness understanding ability [ 71 ]. A process-oriented disclosure approach also helps to reduce parents’ concerns about disclosure (e.g., no explicit naming of HIV is required in the beginning). Despite the scientific evidence and clinical relevance of process-oriented disclosure, to date, this is exceptional rather than the norm in regions with a high prevalence of HIV among children and high HIV stigma. Here, information and communication technology (ICT) holds the potential to scaffold and guide parents through continuous disclosure of the child's HIV-status. However, current technology for HIV focuses predominately on treatment adherence (e.g., reminder systems [ 16 ]) [ 13 , 33 , 75 ]. Interactive systems that support disclosure exclusively understand it as self-disclosure and focus on adults (largely in a social media context) [ 6 , 8 , 47 , 78 , 82 , 102 , 132 – 134 , 138 ]. Supporting parents in disclosing their child's illness status is rarely reflected in currently available systems. In the HCI community, prior research exists on parent-child illness management, presenting support for health monitoring that is shared between parents and children [ 53 , 54 , 84 , 97 , 101 , 124 ]. However, little is known regarding the challenges that parents encounter when communicating health-related information to their children, especially sensitive information.

In this work, we aim to address this gap through a two-step examination of how interactive technology can support disclosure. First, we examine how parents and children currently experience disclosure practices, and their perspectives on technology in this space. Second, we design and evaluate a prototype of an interactive storytelling system that provides a virtual disclosure experience to parents of children with HIV.

Thereby, we aim to answer two research questions (RQs):

RQ1: How is HIV-status disclosure to children living in Ethiopia managed by parents, and to what extent can technology support this process?

RQ2: How do parents perceive an interactive storytelling system that aims to support the process of HIV-status disclosure to children?

In the first step of our work, we conducted four focus groups with thirteen participants to explore detailed experiences of HIV-status disclosure for children and the possibility of ICT support. Applying Thematic Analysis [ 20 ], we crafted themes around the impact of non-disclosure on children's identity formation, how parents are lagging behind children in matters of disclosure, which makes children speculative about their condition, in turn leading to accidental disclosure, and the need for technology that supports parents in disclosure processes while recognizing their individual situations.

In the second step, we built on these findings and designed an interactive storytelling system that guides parents through the experience of partial disclosure of children with HIV. The prototype presents a dialog-based game where an HIV-positive non-disclosed child character presents several concerns about his health, and the player in the role of his mother provides explanations mostly through storytelling. The main goals of the prototype are to establish disclosure as an ongoing process, and to use a biological approach to explain HIV. We opted for a storytelling solution because it leverages existing traditions, and simulates discussion between parents and children. While such experiences could also be presented using non-digital means, e.g., using storybooks, interactive systems can be more appealing and increase engagement [ 5 , 28 , 127 ]. Most importantly, these provide the opportunity to alter and control story flow [ 29 , 60 ], allowing for a more dynamic response to user input, and – in our case – adaptation to specific decisions. Moreover, simulated virtual experiences provide vicarious practice and rehearsal opportunities which may facilitate disclosure in daily life [ 67 , 68 ].

To further explore the potential of interactive storytelling, six parents who have HIV-positive children engaged with our system, that served as an experience prototype, to ground conversation about technology and disclosure. Data were analyzed using Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) [ 115 ]. Resulting themes highlight the importance of formulating and using soft words for disclosure, the reframing of disclosure as a continuous process, the need to address HIV-related parental trauma as well as internalized and public HIV stigma, and the limitations of interactive technology in the context of disclosure.

Our work makes three main contributions: (1) We provide an in-depth exploration of disclosure experiences among children with HIV and their parents, (2) we present an experience prototype that highlights opportunities and risks when leveraging interactive technology to support disclosure, and (3) we contribute design implications for technology to support parents in the disclosure of their children's HIV-status to them.

Appropriate and timely disclosure for children is beneficial for HIV self-management. Current work in HCI focuses on self-disclosure of adults, and approaches disclosure as a discrete event. Our work is a first step toward shifting the focus to parental and continuous disclosure to children and adolescents, a group that is severely affected by HIV in sub-Saharan Africa, but often overlooked in technology design.

2 BACKGROUND

Here, we outline relevant related work to situate our research. First, we give an overview of HIV, stigma, and the challenge of disclosure, particularly in the context of children who acquired HIV at or before birth. Then, we examine previous work that has addressed disclosure and technology in the context of HIV.

2.1 HIV, Stigma, and the Challenge of Disclosure to Children

HIV continues to be one of the global health challenges, particularly in highly burdened regions such as sub-Saharan Africa [ 36 ], including Ethiopia which is the most populous country (around 118 million [ 136 ]) among the hotspot regions. Nowadays, treatment can be successful through antiretroviral therapy (ART), which helps people with HIV to live longer and healthy. Unfortunately, on top of managing a chronic disease, people with HIV carry the burden of HIV-related stigma [ 62 ]. HIV stigma negatively affects the health and well-being of people with HIV [ 18 , 50 , 118 , 128 ]. They encounter negative treatment from society simply because of their HIV-status, including avoidance, rejection, and blaming [ 118 ]. Consequently, people often conceal their HIV-positive status, with negative health implications.

A significant number of people living with HIV concerns children and adolescents who acquired HIV mainly perinatally. As of 2020 estimates, 1.8 million children under 15 years, and 1.7 million adolescents (10 – 19 years old) were living with HIV [ 141 ]. In Ethiopia, due to scarcity of data, the reporting of the magnitude of the HIV burden in children appears incomplete. However, a spectrum analysis estimated almost 160,000 children (5-15 years old) to be living with HIV in Ethiopia in 2013, representing 20% of all people living with HIV in Ethiopia [ 98 ]. While at a younger age, children adhere better to ART, their adherence drops as they grow older [ 4 , 41 , 49 , 61 , 79 , 86 , 119 ]. Consequently, they experience poorer HIV-related outcomes during adolescence compared to childhood [ 58 , 112 ].

The most striking reason for this decline is remaining unaware of their HIV-status [ 3 , 22 , 80 ], as stigma concerns motivate parents to withhold children's HIV diagnosis from them [ 89 ]. Despite this secrecy, younger children adhere to treatment following parents’ recommendations, being satisfied with simplistic explanations about their condition. Often, these explanations are plain deceptions [ 77 ] such as telling them they have another less stigmatized disease (e.g., tuberculosis). However, as children age, they seek more genuine explanations for their health condition and treatment purpose. Because of non-disclosure, children become demotivated for treatment, and in the worst-case, self-discover their status [ 80 ]. In sum, non-disclosure of HIV-positive status damages adolescents’ ability to self-manage HIV and overall wellbeing.

2.2 Understanding Disclosure as a Process

While disclosure is most often promoted from a clinical or scientific ground, for its known benefits such as receiving social support, most people initially prefer to hide their own condition, or their loved ones’, fearing stigma. Non-disclosure is the default stigma coping strategy among people with stigmatized conditions [ 88 , 94 , 96 ]. In the case of children living with HIV, parents often decide to keep the diagnosis from their children until an imaginary moment in the future [ 129 ] at which children will be told everything at once. This contrasts with the general clinical recommendation on disclosure of HIV-status to children: disclosure should start early and should be considered a process [ 71 , 90 ].

Prior research has characterized how children understand and respond to illness processes. A prominent body of literature corresponds to the neo-Piagetian approach, which accounts for a developmental orientation, following Piaget's cognitive development theory [ 17 ]. According to this perspective, children's understanding of illness processes is limited at the beginning but gets ever more sophisticated in parallel to their cognitive development [ 17 , 91 , 130 ]. Moreover, prior research also showed that presenting disease information from a biological perspective is particularly effective in health education for children [ 140 ], and mitigating stigmatic beliefs [ 117 ].

This implies that HIV health information can be introduced early in childhood, rather than waiting until adulthood, and needs to be delivered progressively and incrementally following children's cognitive development and illness understandings [ 130 ]. For instance, generic disease fundamentals including implicit explanations about HIV can be provided to younger children. This is also termed as partial disclosure , as the specific name of the disease is not yet given. As children grow older, however, more comprehensive facts on HIV can be offered, including the specific name, leading to full disclosure [ 2 , 131 ].

As such, healthcare professionals favor early and incremental disclosure of HIV-status to children [ 106 ]. Besides the advantages of timely disclosure on adolescents’ health and well-being, the lack of disclosure also implicates them in secrecy during consultations. Yet parental consent is required for disclosure, and as aforementioned, most parents opt for delaying as long as possible.

2.3 Technology to Address HIV Stigma and Disclosure

In the past decade, various interactive technologies have been designed for people living with HIV, predominantly supporting active disease management, and in particular to improve therapy adherence, e.g., augmented pill dispensers [ 46 , 142 ], apps to provide reminders and stats [ 35 , 51 , 69 , 85 , 107 ], or online platforms to deliver informational massages and scheduling with health clinics [ 24 – 26 , 42 ].

Only in the past years, HCI researchers have highlighted the importance of careful understanding of stigma itself before any attempt to design technology, to avoid further harm [ 39 , 82 ]. Therefore, recent work has foregrounded HIV-related stigma [ 82 , 104 , 111 ], and the intricacies of designing around non-disclosure. Such work is on the one hand addressing the needs for self-presentation, emotional guidance, and social support, yet on the other hand, respecting the concerns around privacy and security [ 23 , 59 , 73 , 81 , 85 , 132 – 134 ].

In particular, prior HCI studies have examined the use of online platforms, and mobile apps to offer peer support [ 23 , 59 ]. Karusala et. al. [ 59 ] studied the use of chat apps to offer peer support to youth living with HIV in Kenya, highlighting the need for continuous negotiation around privacy, such as the use of ambiguous wording and the respect for maintaining anonymity. Bussone et. al. [ 23 ] interviewed 26 people living with HIV in the UK during the design of a peer data sharing platform, likewise reporting the need for sophisticated measures of privacy and security, and flexibility in what identity information to share, to fit with their level of trust with specific persons and the larger community. Marcu et. al. [ 85 ] formulated specific design guidelines for empathy when designing technology in this space, for instance highlighting the need for a positive, non-judgmental tone in the messages and interaction with the app, the use of neutral signifiers to respect privacy and avoid accidental disclosure, and the provision of social support through camaraderie yet respecting anonymity. Warner et. al. [ 133 , 134 ] specifically focused on how social platforms design for (non-)disclosure of HIV-status, alerting for the ‘Privacy Unraveling Effect’ [ 99 ]. Affordances to disclose HIV-status, even when optional, lead to information asymmetry, and in this manner still serve as implicit signals of HIV-status. Introne et. al. [ 56 ] approached HIV from a transitional lens, pointing out different phases of living with a stigmatized identity, and the need for interactive technology to address stigma (and the question of disclosure) as a ubiquitous yet dynamic construct. For new members, a peer support platform can offer an acute lifeline to combat internalized stigma, whereas for senior members, who have normalized their identity, it can help them serve as community caretakers and role models. Restoring ‘old normals’ is also the focus of the work by Kumar et al. [ 66 ], presenting a field study of the everyday practices and challenges of HIV-outreach workers in rural India. The authors argue to take on an intersectional lens when working with a stigmatized populations and discuss in particular how health workers cultivate and strengthen community cultural wealth to ‘repair’ identity. Likewise, Natarajan and Parik [ 93 ] take on an intersectional approach in researching information access and disclosure of HIV-positive women in India, and discuss how gender structures complicate access, understanding and use of critical information to cope with the disease.

HCI researchers and game scholars have also investigated the use of games, simulations, and VR to provide HIV education to the general public [ 48 , 105 ]. Here, the aim is primarily on HIV prevention. However, such interactive learning experiences have been shown to be particularly successful in sidestepping cultural taboos [ 117 ], and may also be successful in reducing public stigma, by explaining the underlying biomedical model of HIV. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, fewer games/immersive experiences have been built to generate empathy toward the stigmatized – although stigma theories assert that increasing empathy reduces stigmatic behaviors [ 19 ]. One such notable exception is “I‘m Positive” [ 109 ], a game with an interactive narrative where the player is a young man, who finds out from a former partner that he may be HIV-positive.

In summary, HCI efforts on HIV, stigma and designing for disclosure have provided a wealth of interesting design considerations, so far mainly focusing on the challenges of working around (non-)disclosure of adults, and the support of care workers in this environment.

However, as discussed in our work (see section 2.1 and 2.2), there is another form of disclosure, not yet addressed in the aforementioned examples – disclosure from parents to the child itself, which becomes particularly relevant in cases where HIV is transmitted from the parent to the child. While the above HCI-related studies are informative of the pitfalls and opportunities when leveraging interactive technologies to address stigma and/or individual disclosure, they do not address how to support parents in the disclosure process. Therefore, in this study we set out to explore whether and how technology can support parents in the disclosure of HIV-status to children living in Ethiopia, one of the HIV hotspot regions.

3 STUDY 1: UNDERSTANDING DISCLOSURE OF HIV-STATUS AND ASSOCIATED CHALLENGES, AND OPPORTUNITIES FOR INTERACTIVE TECHNOLOGIES

To answer our first research question, (RQ1: How is the disclosure of HIV-status to children living in Ethiopia managed by their parents, and can technology support this disclosure process), we conducted focus groups with key stakeholders. In this stage of the research, we envisioned understanding the practice of disclosure to then build an understanding of whether technology could play a role in this space.

3.1 Participants and Procedure

Thirteen participants were involved, four adolescents who knew their HIV-status (DA) (three male, one female; age 17 to 19), three mothers (P) having a non-disclosed child (age 26 to 55), three female nurses (HP) working on pediatrics HIV (age 28 to 52), and three non-disclosed boys (NDA) (age 9 to 12). Participants were recruited through nurses using a stratified sampling strategy. All of them were given extensive information about the study, given space to ask questions, and then provided informed consent. For those under eighteen, permission was first obtained from their parents and then they signed assent forms [ 125 ]. Focus groups were structured into two parts: 1) participants’ experiences on disclosure and/or non-disclosure and 2) reflections on the potential of technology to support the process. The sessions lasted for one hour and a half on average, and participants were given room to ask questions. All sessions were held in a local language and were carried out at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH). The hospital provides free HIV-care for children at the pediatric clinic. Accompanied by parents, children visit the clinic on regular appointments (every 2 or 3 months) or without appointments if necessary. During each visit, they receive examinations, medication refills, and adherence counseling. Participation was compensated with cash payments in the local currency equivalent to 15 Euros.

3.2 Research Method and Materials

Our study consists of focus groups that drew from personas, scenarios, and conceptual designs of technology to support disclosure as prompts for conversation. Here, we first describe the focus groups, and then we give an overview of the materials that were used. We opted for focus groups (over other options such as individual interviews) because they do not require one single participant to immediately and consistently engage, something which was important to us, given the emotional nature of our topic of research. In turn, we accept the drawback that focus groups offer less privacy than interviews, which needs to be considered in the context of HIV stigma, but is less problematic in our setting because participants already (passingly) knew each other as they visited the same department of the clinic.

3.2.1 Focus Groups. We developed focus group questions based on literature and in conversation with medical experts. For each group of participants, we developed a dedicated set of questions to account for their specific situations and vulnerabilities. Focus group with parents with non-disclosed children (N=3) : In the first part of the discussion, parents discussed their non-disclosure experiences, i.e., how did they handle hiding the HIV-status of children ( e.g., what do you say to children about their illness in the meantime until disclosure? ). Inquiries also included children's concerns (e.g., what concerns do children often have regarding their illness? ), and how they address these (e.g., how do you handle these concerns? ). During the second part, questions examined participants’ opinions on the potential of technology to support them (e.g., Do you think a story-based playful technology that you play with your children can help you? How do you prefer to play it, at home with a borrowed tablet or here at the clinic? ). Focus group with nurses (N=3) : Questions similar to the parents were raised during the discussion with nurses, with certain modifications (e.g., Do you think such a story-based playful technology can help parents as well as children? What additional concerns do children raise other than those presented in the scenario? How do you see the usage feasibility of the hypothetical technology, at home or here at the clinic? ). Focus group with disclosed adolescents (N=4) : discussion with this group included their experience before the disclosure (e.g., What were your main concerns about your illness before knowing your status? What do you think you should have known before actual disclosure? ), at disclosure (e.g., When, and how did you come to know your status? Who told you, how? ), and their message for non-disclosing parents (e.g., What do you think parents should do regarding disclosure? ). Discussion on the potential of technology was the same as in the other focus groups. Focus group with non-disclosed children (N=3) : The focus group with non-disclosed children was carefully adapted to reduce the risk of accidental disclosure. With minimal prompts, we asked about their condition in general (e.g., H ow are you doing? Do you want to know more about your illness? What do you want to know more about? ). Concerning the conceptual design, we asked questions like: Do you play digital games? What are your thoughts on a story-based game explaining how we get ill?

3.2.2 Materials. We developed personas, scenarios, and a conceptual design to guide discussions during the focus groups. The first stage of the focus groups was supported by personas and scenarios, whereas the second part drew from a conceptual design of technology to support disclosure. All materials were reviewed by medical experts to ensure correctness and appropriateness for the intended audience of our research. Personas and scenarios were based on design implications from previous literature [ 1 , 9 , 10 , 126 ]. Following principles of dyadic persona creation [ 126 ], we developed a child-parent persona: a child girl who does not know her HIV-status and her mother. We provide a detailed overview of the dyadic persona in the supplementary materials. Once the dyadic persona was defined, we identified scenarios representing children's concerns and questions associated with disclosure. On this basis, we created eight scenarios for further discussion within the focus groups. While all participants have their own experiences of (non-)disclosure, these personas and scenarios provided participants with a reference point for conversation that did not necessarily require them to explicitly reveal their personal experiences, helping avoid instances of vulnerability while still facilitating conversation about a very challenging topic. The conceptual design of technology to support disclosure was rooted in our previous work [ 89 ] and others on the HIV-status disclosure process for children (e.g., supporting continuous disclosure and taking a biological approach to addressing the HIV knowledge gap [ 117 , 130 ]). To this end, we imagined a technology that playfully engages parents and children in a shared experience, intentionally remaining vague on the specifics to make room for the participants’ ideas. To better communicate integration of the technology, storyboards demonstrating two possible use contexts were created. The first scenario was a system that could be borrowed from the clinic for home use, whereas the second scenario assumes that children and parents are invited to engage with the system during appointment visits. Figure 1 illustrates both contexts; a full overview of the materials is included in the supplementary materials.

3.3 Positionality

The current study was the second phase of a larger project on adolescents with HIV. The entire research protocol and the ethical proposal were developed under the guidance of a pediatrics professor at Addis Ababa University. She is also a Pediatric Infectious Diseases sub-specialist and chair of the ART service at the Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital. The ethical process went through double reviews on a hierarchical basis. The first presentation was made to a review committee at the Pediatrics Department at TASH. Once approved at the department level, our submission was transferred to the medical-ethical board of the College of Health Sciences. Through careful review of the experts at the IRB and under close supervision of the pediatrics professor, study materials were modified to minimize the likelihood of accidental disclosure among participants who do not know their HIV-status. After a second presentation to the IRB, the medical-ethical board approved.

The first author who is deeply familiar with the local environment conducted the studies. She is an experienced researcher from Ethiopia with a background in HCI. Discussion materials for all sessions were diligently prepared and rehearsed. The other two researchers are female HCI professors at KU Leuven University with extensive research experience in HCI designs for health. The research was jointly funded by the home institutions of the authors; the overarching research topic was proposed by the first author of this paper, and the research presented here was designed in consultation with the other authors.

3.4 Data Analysis

Interviews were audio recorded and next transcribed verbatim. Data were analyzed following reflexive thematic analysis, as outlined by Braun and Clarke [ 20 ]. This framework prescribes a stepwise analysis process that allows researchers to immerse themselves in the data and identify deep experiences, which is appropriate for our context, exploring lived experiences. The same author who conducted the studies transcribed the data and then coded them after multiple reads. The generated codes and associated excerpts were then translated into English. Initially, identified themes were refined after being re-reviewed by the other research members. We generated 28 initial codes out of 252 data points which were further refined into 20 codes, from which three final themes were crafted.

3.5 Results

Analysis led to three themes focusing on (1) the negative impact of non-disclosure on children's identity formation, (2) how parents are lagging behind while children grow speculative about their condition, and (3) the need to design interventions that cannot be ignored by parents, convince them to disclose faster and reduce the barriers that parents experience.

3.5.1 Why Me? Non-disclosure's Impact on Children's Identity Formation . This theme describes how non-disclosed children struggle with a feeling of being different and forming an identity as being ‘discriminatory unique’ over the years because of their apparent illness and the lack of disclosure. It highlights how they always find themselves differing from others, grappling with their own illness, seeking to make sense of the conflicting information they receive due to their non-disclosed status.

Although parents had not disclosed their HIV-status, their children already realized that they live with an unusual disease, e.g., “ Once, I was taking meds for a long time as I had some illness, and my meds came to end. He [the child] said to me: what about mine? If mine do not end, it means that my disease is something unique ” (P3). They make constant comparisons between themselves and other family members, siblings in particular, to better understand their condition. Additionally, many children question why they are uniquely affected by this condition, unlike the rest of the family, e.g., one parent pointed out how “ He says to me: how come this disease caught me alone, why not you ?” (P2). Those children who experience frequent illnesses (perhaps because of a weakened immunity) enquire about their situation and are aware of their increased vulnerability to other diseases, e.g., a parent quoted their child: “ Why do I get colds more often than other people? ” (P3). Furthermore, the fact that they are the only ones at home always under treatment is another source of discomfort – “ Why am I the only one who always goes to the doctor? Why does no one else in the house go? ” (P1). In addition to these observations, special accommodations or additional health checks are often interpreted by children as discriminatory, and hence contribute to the feeling of being singled out. For example, one parent commented, “ They start comparing themselves with other children at home: am I a ‘different’ person at our home? Why am I treated differently? Why is everyone worried about me? Why does everyone feel compassionate toward me? Why can't this be done for [name sibling]? ” (P3). Overall, we see that the ‘why-me’ questions reflect a dominant concern of children, who do not know their HIV-status, but sense that they are different.

However, participants also mentioned other types of children's concerns such as those regarding illness cause and name, or treatment timeline (when will they be cured completely, when will the treatment end), confusion about the purpose of medication when asymptomatic, and fears of becoming ill again once children experienced a severe illness before (P2, P3, DA1). For instance, children are eager to know the name of their illness, and also sense their parents’ emotional strain – a non-disclosed adolescent said: “ I will be very happy when I know the name of my disease ” (NDA2). A parent recollected: “ He [child] says to me: w hat is the name of my disease? Why do the doctors dodge [the questions], why won't they tell me? Why don't you too tell me, why are you afraid to tell ?” (P3).

3.5.2 We Already Knew! Parents are Lagging in Matters of Disclosure . This theme explains parents’ fear of disclosure, which results in poor HIV communication with children, leaving their questions unanswered and leaving space for speculation. Additionally, the theme also highlights children's perceptions that parents are lagging behind in matters of disclosure, not satisfying their children's curiosity about their own condition, who in turn embark on solitary journeys to learn the truth about their condition, often leading to accidental disclosure.

While sharing pre-disclosure experiences, disclosed adolescents stated that parents often fear honest discussions on HIV, and instead use tricks to convince children to take their medication (e.g., “ We were being tricked into taking medicine ” (DA4)). Child-parent discussions about HIV are usually foggy, and sometimes conversations can turn confrontational, especially if children are already suspicious about their HIV-status. Sharing such an experience, one parent said: “ I said to him [child], I had tuberculosis and pneumonia a long time ago. Before I gave birth to you, I survived pneumonia. These diseases can transmit to the fetus. When I said this, he replied: ‘How can this be? Science doesn't say that. I am reading, right! I have a science book, and it doesn't say that’. He said: ‘It is HIV and is contracted through marriage (sex)’. I can't tell you how I ran to the bathroom and burst into tears .” (P2). Disclosed adolescents also discussed that HIV communication with parents comes from a place of fear, and is often limited to medication adherence. One disclosed adolescent stated, “ We are told that if we stop the medicine, we will get sick and die. Not that - this medicine is good for this and that purpose, or it is useful for immunity. We are told [to take the meds] from the negative perspective ” (DA1).

A lack of open discussions leaves children's questions unanswered, which makes them even more curious about their condition. They constantly search for evidence (e.g., “ I heard many saying I googled my meds ” (DA1)) which often ends with accidental disclosure. Out of four disclosed adolescents included in our study, three reported that they discovered their HIV-status on their own, by cross-checking medication on TV shows (DA2, DA3), and by comparing their own medication to those of neighbors known of having HIV (DA4). Having self-discovered their own HIV-status, some children confronted their parents (P2) while others pretended to know nothing (DA3). Recollecting pre- and self-disclosure experiences, disclosed adolescents reflected that parents are lagging in matters of disclosure. Commenting on this, one participant said “ Some of us know our status before they (parents) reach us … While they are hesitating whether to tell or not, we already started thinking about our future having known ourselves .” (DA1).

Considering the benefits of partial disclosure, disclosed adolescents suggested that it would have been a lot easier for them to accept their HIV-status had they been told of HIV little by little, e.g., “ If our families would have told us the essentials, it would not have been completely new to us… ” (DA3). Therefore, they suggested that it should be a priority to train parents on how to do this, e.g., “ First, families should get trained on how to tell step by step …” (DA2), and “ Well, some parents feel guilty… But I recommend something that changes this attitude… like a short TV series that explains this .” (DA4). Interestingly, they also proposed how disclosure could be supported through school lessons, e.g., “ We would have understood the highlights because we were schooled on it .” (DA2), and “ By the time a child reaches grade six, they learn about HIV/AIDS and sexuality. Hence, I suggest they should be disclosed when entering grade six because they get the hints .” (DA3).

3.5.3 Designing Technology to Reduce Rather than Create Additional Barriers for Parents . This theme summarizes participants’ preferences of the context-of-use of the hypothetical playful technology that was proposed to be built for children and parents and their general opinions toward it. Overall, participants were positive about the idea of creating a playful technology for parents and children. They also appreciated its playful nature relating similarities with current practice, e.g., “ It is good that the technology is in the form of a story. We also do it like that, just verbally .” (HP2). Concerning the use context, healthcare professionals feared that parents may not give equal attention to utilizing the technology at home versus when at the clinic, because they may be occupied by daily routines. For example, one participant said, “ I am afraid and question whether parents will sit down together with children and play attentively. They may not have time for that. Because most of them are economically unstable and away for daily labor ” (HP1). Therefore, they strongly recommended inviting parents and children to experience the technology at the clinic, leveraging waiting times while both are waiting for appointments. Commenting on this, one participant said, “ At home, most of the time parents will be engaged with daily commitments. They may just go away, leaving it to the children. But if it is here, they [parents] may follow it attentively. ” (HP2). Finally, participants also commented that in the home scenario, managing the process of lending tablets to families would be difficult because tablets may get lost or broken (HP1, HP2, HP3, DA1). Furthermore, participants proposed other modalities of creating the technology for users with low digital literacy, such as in a form of animation films broadcasted on TV or distributed through CDs or flash disks (HP3, DA2, DA4, NDA3).

4 STUDY 2: DESIGNING AN INTERACTIVE INTERVENTION TO SUPPORT PARENTS IN THE DISCLOSURE PROCESS

The aim of this step was to answer the second research question, RQ2: How do parents perceive an interactive storytelling system that aims to support the process of HIV-status disclosure to children? To this end, we created TeretTeret, an experience prototype aiming to support parents in a continuous HIV-status disclosure process, focusing on partial disclosure. Here we describe the prototype design and present the results from an initial evaluation with six participants.

4.1 Development of TeretTeret : An Experience Prototype to Support Partial Disclosure

Building on the results of the previous study, we set out to develop TeretTeret , which we intended to leverage as conversation starter for an exploration of parents’ perspectives on interactive technology to support disclosure. Experience prototypes have previously been leveraged to communicate ideas for playful systems [ 87 ] and have also served as conversation starters in sensitive settings [ 120 ].

4.1.1 Design Goals. We derived four design goals from the previous study, which – along with insights from literature – guided the design of TeretTeret .

Design Goal 1: Address parents rather than children. While we had initially assumed that we would be developing a technology that addresses both children and parents, results from the first study highlighted that there is a bigger need to support parents in the disclosure process, empowering them to engage in timely and appropriate conversations with their children. Therefore, our prototype should primarily address parents.

Design Goal 2: Support parents in establishing open conversation. Our results show that children routinely ask parents questions about their condition, which is also backed up by literature (e.g., see [ 89 , 139 ]). Here, findings show that parents feel ill-equipped to meet their children's wishes to learn more about their situation, either avoiding conversation altogether, or deceiving their children. Therefore, our prototype should support parents in the development of communication strategies that facilitate honest and evidence-based discussions of HIV (to a level of explicitness that is appropriate for the stage of disclosure of the child).

Design Goal 3: Introduce disclosure as a process. Many parents approached disclosure as a one-time event, where they had to let children know about their HIV-status. However, literature suggests that disclosure is a process that needs to align with the stages of child development (see section 2.2), gradually informing children about diseases, medication, HIV, and their personal situation. Hence, our prototype should not perpetuate the idea of HIV disclosure as simply letting a child know about their HIV-status, but introduce the idea of gradual disclosure throughout childhood and adolescence.

Design Goal 4: Include evidence-based information on HIV. Our study as well as literature suggest that many parents do not have a good understanding of biological process underlying HIV and its treatment. Consequently, they simply focus on enforcing treatment adherence with limited justification on its purpose. Hence, our prototype should provide basic knowledge of biological processes so that parents can support their children's learning about HIV and diseases in general.

Finally, given the emotionally challenging situation that disclosure represents for many parents, our design was also informed by recent considerations for trauma-informed work in HCI [ 30 ]. Here, it is important that technology designers understand the traumatic experience that HIV can be for patients. In particular, our work takes place in the conflicting area of parental trauma, and children's right to timely disclosure. To account for potential trauma, we opted to build a prototype addressing partial rather than full disclosure, focusing on one of the earlier and less explicit steps in discussing HIV with children. Thereby, we hope to give parents a choice in how far they want to push their examination of their views on disclosure as part of this research, and we hope to avoid experiences with the technology that might reinforce existing trauma and fears (e.g., portrayal of challenges in the final stages of disclosure, or addressing negative experiences of full disclosure). Likewise, our system sought to take different educational levels and differences in technology experience into account among parents, by offering straightforward interaction (touch-based) and accessible language.

4.1.2 System Design. TeretTeret is a dialogue-based role-playing game that invites players to engage in conversation with an HIV-positive boy, called Abule, assuming the role of his mother (Figure 2 and 3). Throughout play, the child presents questions and concerns, seeking answers and confirmation from the mother. The difficulty of these questions increases in line with the growth of the child's understanding, which is signified by life events such as kindergarten graduation. To help the child in their understanding of their situation, the player must engage in conversation by choosing from predefined answers.

We created several stories, including a series of three progressive stories focusing on children of cognitive stage II [ 17 , 130 ], section 2.2 (see supplementary material). The themes in these stories include: story 1 – personal hygiene, the importance of visiting a doctor and treatment; story 2 – (skin) immunity, germs, and treatment adherence; story 3 – the need for lifelong treatment adherence, clinical appointments, and the internal immunity system. The prototype also includes side themes such as relieving children's feelings of guilt about getting the disease (e.g., thinking they are ill because they acted badly), asserting that every kid can get sick, establishing strong relationships with health professionals, and developing routines for medication intake and clinical appointments routines (e.g., preparing the day before). The overall design concept and dialog script were reviewed by a medical expert (3rd author).

The system was implemented in Unity [ 121 ] for deployment on Android platforms. One particular challenge throughout development was lack of availability of culturally appropriate graphical assets, as well as the integration of the local language, which is poorly supported by many Western-centric development tools.

4.2 Method

Semi-structured interviews were designed around the experience prototype we developed, to explore the perspectives of parents having children with HIV. Interviews were conducted in two parts. The first step covered participants’ demographic backgrounds and other introductory matters. These included questions regarding their disclosure plans (e.g., “ When, and how do you plan to disclose your child?”, “What do you (plan to) tell your child until disclosure time?”, “Why is the disclosure to your child late?” ) or experiences of disclosure if they already disclosed children (e.g., “How did the disclosure go?” ). In the second step, participants were first invited to experience our story-based disclosure app, followed by semi-structured interviews. The questions focused on a) overall impression of the prototype, b) relevance of the app for parents who have not yet disclosed their children and c) appropriateness of the presented stories for children. The specific questions included: “How did you get the app?”, “What did you learn from it concerning when and how to disclose?”, “Are you comfortable telling such stories to your child, or do you still have concerns?”, and “Do you think these stories are appropriate for children?” .

4.3 Participants and Procedure

A total of six participants (four female, two male, age 26 to 50) were involved. Participants were recruited through health professionals, and all provided informed consent after receiving information about the study. Four of them had not yet disclosed HIV-status to their children, the other two participants did. With fictitious names, here we describe their profiles to situate their perspectives on our prototype. We also slightly adjusted the ages of parents and children and replaced jobs with comparable ones as an extra layer of anonymity. All interviews were conducted physically at the Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital. The research protocol was part of a larger project (see 3.1.2), and amendment approval was provided for this specific study. Further ethical considerations can be consulted from section 3.1.2.

Yosef is a 32-year-old father with a college degree, working in the transport business. He is a father of a three-year-old daughter with HIV who has been enrolled for ART since she was six months old. Yosef does not have HIV, but the daughter's mother does. The couple has separated because of conflicts arising from the HIV-status revelation. His ex-wife still has difficulties in accepting the daughter's HIV-status, and neither wants to receive treatment for herself. In response, Yosef is the sole parent responsible for the child's treatment. He plans to tell his daughter about her status when she turns ten years old but fears the mother might interfere.

Workitu is a 34-year-old mother living with HIV. She never went to school. After learning about her status, she felt hopeless and initially refused treatment. As a result, she suffered from a range of HIV-related health complications. She has two sons: one child is 14 years old and HIV-negative, and the other child is a three-year-old living with HIV. The older son is in 7 th grade, and he knows about the HIV-positive status of his mother and younger brother. He already started taking significant responsibility, supporting them in terms of medication adherence. Workitu is divorced because of disagreements with her husband on issues associated with their HIV-status. She is currently unemployed, and she and her children are dependent on her mother whom they live with. She plans to tell her HIV-positive son about his HIV-status at the age of five or six.

Fatima is a 30-year-old mother who left school after grade five. She works in a grocery as an assistant. She lives with a 16-year-old son, and both are living with HIV. She has survived an HIV-related illness. Though the decision was challenging for her, Fatima disclosed his HIV-status to her son at the age of ten, when he became suspicious of taking medication for a long time, and because lessons at school touched upon the topic. Reflecting on his post-disclosure reactions, she believes that her way of disclosure was incorrect, and that it would have been better if professionals had told him.

Hiwot is a 26-year-old undergrad student, graduating soon. She has a 12-year-old brother living with HIV. She is primarily in charge of managing his treatment. While the mother has died of AIDS after refusing treatment and the father lives with HIV, she and two other older brothers are HIV-negative. Hiwot believes that now is the time to disclose her brother because she perceives he is older now, but the other family members are resisting and propose waiting a while longer.

Legesse is a 50-year-old father with a college degree and works as a bank security officer. He has a 16-year-old son who lives with HIV but does not know about his HIV-status. He and the other older siblings (a son and a daughter) do not have HIV, but the mother has died of AIDS a long time ago. Despite him being HIV-negative, the older son used to accuse him of introducing HIV into the family. This was a disturbing experience for him. Fearing similar experiences, Legesse keeps postponing disclosure to the son with HIV, though he knows he should have initiated the process a while ago. He now plans to disclose soon, hoping to leverage empowering spiritual explanations and support of church clergy.

Sinafkesh is a 40-year-old working in the education sector. She does not have HIV but is raising an orphan nephew living with HIV who recently turned 17 and was disclosed a year ago. She used to explain his condition to him by relying on school biology (e.g., information on germs), and was expecting the final disclosure to become easier. However, it was a difficult experience, and after learning that he got the infection from his parents, her nephew started to question why and how they gave him HIV, and which parent was particularly responsible. He was also highly concerned about his own future, knowing that both parents died of AIDS.

4.4 Data Analysis and Interpretation

We used interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA) [ 115 ] for analyzing the data. IPA resembles other qualitative data analysis methods, particularly Thematic Analysis [ 20 ] in the overall process of data analysis. However, it differs in the approach that emphasizes individual accounts with deep explorations of personal experiences. To capture important details of each participant, individual cases are first analyzed one by one, and then a cross-case analysis is conducted across all participants. Hence, a heterogeneous and small size of participants (usually six) is satisfactory. In addition to its idiographic nature, IPA provides freedom to give interpretations on top of individual experiences. Hence, IPA gave us opportunities to detail individual demographics relevant for contextualization, and present deep personal experiences and perspectives of participants. Our analysis went through several steps, following the procedure described by Smith [ 114 ]. The first author, who also conducted the interviews, transcribed each interview, and re-read it multiple times to get immersed in the content, together with making exploratory notes. Then, transcribed data were analyzed case by case by formulating personal experimental statements (now translated into English) which were further grouped into personal experimental themes. Finally, personal themes across all participants were merged to provide the final themes which were re-reviewed and approved by all the authors. Examples of previous applications of IPA include [ 74 , 87 , 103 ]. As the individual backgrounds of researchers are important in IPA, these details can be found in section 3.1.2.

4.5 Results

Analysis resulted in five themes: (1) technology to help parents find the right words for disclosure, (2) reframing disclosure as a process (3) addressing parental HIV trauma, (4) combating public and internal HIV stigma through storytelling, and (5) limitations of interactive technology in disclosure processes.

4.5.1 Technology to Help Parents Find the Right Words for Disclosure. This theme focuses on the potential of technology to help participants find the right words to address disclosure. Parents perceived the prototype – especially the strategy of discussing HIV through playful stories – as a novel and diplomatic way of speaking about HIV with children. For example, Legesse commented: “ You see, it is hard to talk about it [HIV]. And there is no doubt that this requires such a diplomatic language .” Here, he meant that distancing children's personal HIV health condition through stories and relatable characters relaxes parents’ anxiety over directly discussing the matter. Such techniques of abstraction, through the means of storytelling, make parents more courageous than techniques that are directly presenting children's cases of illness. Other participants similarly expressed that discussing the health condition directly with the child is difficult. Instead, using stories, takes away the fear of engaging with HIV topics, e.g., “ It is difficult to speak directly to children, this way is hard. But this one [pointing to the experience prototype] is awesome. For parents who did not yet disclose, it will be easier for them to tell in this way, if they come and see and learn like me, and tell in this way .” (Fatima). Commenting on the covert manner, Sinafkesh stressed that this tactic is especially relevant for fearful parents: “ It helps a lot for scared parents. Instead of explaining it dryly, this is better…This provides a huge relief, it takes away our anxieties as well as those of children .” She meant that if parents use such methodology, they will no longer be fearful of discussing HIV with children deflecting children's concerns. At the same time, children will not be super sensitive about their condition as such narrations include satisfactory answers to their questions concerning their health condition. As for Sinafkesh, though it was too late for her case because she already disclosed to her nephew, she also commented: “ So much as we were worried about it, so much as we were fearing it, if only we knew there existed such an easy method .”

In addition to the tactics of how to talk about the condition, participants also mentioned that the app inspires them what to talk about, and how to talk about it. For instance, Hiwot said: “ This is better. Instead of being anxious and keeping silent, it is better to tell this story. And he [child] too will relate it to himself .” Participants also stated that the app can further inspire families to be creative and formulate their own similar stories to discuss any HIV topic. “ This is a story of a child you created yourself, right? A family can create something else in their own way to tell something else. The idea of this app is great for me. It is a big inspiration ” (Sinkafesh).

4.5.2 Reframing Disclosure as a Process. This theme summarizes participant responses that highlight how the prototype helped them view disclosure as a process rather than a one-off event. For example, Yosef said: “ I have never thought of telling it in such a way. But now, when I saw this app, especially when I saw Abule's story, it made me realize that there is such a way. I have understood that there is an alternative to talking directly about the disease or medication .” Yosef added: “ I have just changed my mind, right now after seeing this app. What I thought originally was to tell her when she turns 10, not in the way of the app. I was thinking of telling her directly and only when she reaches that age. However, now, after seeing the story, Abule's story, I was immediately thinking of telling her from the age of 6 or maybe 7. They [children] start reading stories from that age [...] now I have changed my mind. Although I will not tell her directly about the disease at the age of six or seven […] I just want to tell her in the form of stories, taking Abule's story as example .” Here, Yosef is explaining that the app helps him to reconsider his original view of disclosure as a one-time event, instead, he considers starting disclosure earlier, revealing HIV info little by little. Reflecting on her own event-based disclosure experience, Fatima compares it with the process-oriented disclosure: “ This [telling little by little] is cool. It is difficult for children to process everything at once. It is difficult for them and for us [parents] too. It was very difficult for me. If such a strategy had existed before, it would have worked for me. In the first place, I shouldn't have done it [disclosing her son] myself. I shouldn't have started it, for I didn't explain it well .”

Parents of toddlers said that the experience with the app helped them realize that as children grow older, they will start searching for themselves. Regarding this, Yosef explained: “ To be honest, until this moment, I never thought that she would ask such questions in the future .” Generally, we have observed that when children are still young, parents can hardly imagine that there comes a time when their child will start questioning their illness, and hence the need for disclosure becomes ever more pressing. For example, Workitu expressed confidence now on how and what she expects when her child gets disclosed at 5: “ He will understand me. My son is a child who easily understands what you say to him. Even now, he is a boy who understands me a lot. So, I hope he understands me by then .”

4.5.3 Addressing Parental HIV Trauma. Our data shows that many parents, and particularly those living with HIV, find it difficult to accept either their own HIV-status, that of their children, or both. This was strongly linked with emotionally challenging experiences they had in the context of HIV. While some parents survived AIDS-related diseases, they only did so after experiencing health complications, because they initially refused treatment (e.g., Workitu). For the same reason, others lost their lives (e.g., Hiwot's mom) which could perhaps have been avoided. Yosef shared a similar family experience and his concern about how this impacted his disclosure plan to his daughter: “Yes, there is a reason that I am afraid concerning disclosure. It is her [my daughter] and her mother who have HIV. There is a disagreement between us, and we have separated. The girl lives with her mother. I am the only one who took the girl to the doctor. The mother has no desire to be treated. She is not about to start meds for herself, she is mentally in trouble. This thing concerns me, I am afraid of what the mother will tell her .” Reflecting on such experiences, participants recommended targeting parents personally to help them improve their own negative attitudes to HIV, in addition to helping them how to deal with children on HIV matters. For instance, here is what Yosef recommended : “As far as the thing [prototype] is addressing parents, what I propose to be included is something dedicated to parents only that provides positive attitudes about HIV. Something that positively hints to them that it is possible to live healthily, that it is possible to achieve higher positions after schooling…”

4.5.4 Combatting Internalized and Public Stigma through Digital Storytelling. This theme focuses on the potential of technology in general and storytelling in particular to address internalized and public stigma, both common in the context of HIV. Here, parents also saw potential in directly addressing children, translating the stories into interactive digital experiences such as animations, cartoons, films, or games. For example, Sinafkesh suggested: “ Not just parents, if children themselves can see this as an animation film, for example, much better outcomes can be achieved.” Participants expressed that digital stories would be effective to capture children's attention, as they sometimes intentionally avoided discussions on medication , “When we discuss this, he changes the subject. When I start talking about meds, he diverts ” (Hiwot). Sinafkesh added further: “ Because, when you tell a fairy tale, such as Cinderella, they listen with much interest. Well, the same will be with these stories, it is a matter of making them attractive and using the words at their level .” Participants also acknowledged that digital stories would be appropriate for children and help them understand better because the stories are designed directly for them. For instance, Legesse commented: “ You are already going in a way that is suitable for children. The story narration is awesome. For example, the personification of the skin that converses with the child in the story is very interesting .”

Furthermore, participants were thoughtful about the potential impact of the intervention on society in a broader sense. With respect to designing a similar intervention for children, they suggested that the design should be open to all children, including those without HIV. They proposed that a proper understanding of HIV among the public would reduce HIV stigma, which in turn would reduce complications associated with disclosure. For instance, Hiwot suggested: “ For example, if this [story] goes on National Television, it works for all children including the healthy ones. It helps healthy children themselves to learn about HIV. Now, the main reason why HIV is feared is because of external (society) fears, because of discrimination. But if others – healthy people are aware, all this will not happen .”

4.5.5 Limitations of Interactive Technology in Disclosure Processes. Participants had generally favorable opinions toward the prototype including its context-of-use at the clinic. However, they also pointed out drawbacks associated with usability issues for parents from lower educational backgrounds, e.g., “ This app will be very helpful, especially for families with good educational awareness, families with at least an elementary or high school education. But for uneducated families, hmm […] it doesn't go with them ” (Legesse). More specifically, they doubted whether such parents would be able to play with the prototype, as well as understand the HIV science presented in it. However, participants did not want such families to be excluded but instead proposed solutions for inclusive design. For example, “ assigning an assistant ” (Yosef) and tailoring to their specific needs: “ The app is good. But, in terms of usability, it should be crafted according to the spectrum of parent's knowledge level .” (Legesse). Interestingly, Workitu – who has not received formal education, proposed that her older son who is educated better can use the app on her behalf: “ My older son, because he is educated, can explain him [younger son]. For example, when he [younger son] refuses medication in the evenings, he uses some tricks to get him to take medication, e.g., he pretends as if he also takes medication. Similarly, he can explain to him this thing [app] as well .”

5 DISCUSSION

This paper investigated disclosure of HIV-status to children with HIV living in Ethiopia, focusing on the experiences of disclosure of children and parents, and the potential and limitations of technology to support disclosure as a process. Here, we start our discussion by revisiting the research questions, reflecting on the role that technology can and should play in this setting. We end with considerations for the design of technology to support disclosure of HIV-status to children.

5.1 RQ1: How is the disclosure of HIV-status to children living in Ethiopia managed by their parents and can technology support this disclosure process?

In line with previous studies [ 83 , 139 ], our findings in the first study confirmed parents’ reluctance to inform children of their HIV-positive status, due to stigmatic fears and personal trauma. Yet, the more parents keep hiding their HIV-positive status, the more children's curiosity increases to understand their condition. Eventually, perceiving parents’ unwillingness to tell, children turn to search information sources (e.g., TV programs on HIV) to self-discover their own condition. Furthermore, the lack of disclosure negatively impacts children's self-identity. They compare themselves with others (especially with siblings), perceiving themselves as ‘different’. Concerning the possibility of technology to support the disclosure process, results indicated how participants were overall positive towards playful technology and appreciated similarities with the local practice of storytelling. However, they expressed concern about the economic instability of parents and the busy schedules, and deploying the interactive technology at home was deemed problematic. Instead, they favored making use of idle time at the health clinic to experience the game.

5.2 RQ2: How do parents perceive an interactive storytelling system that aims to support the process of HIV-status disclosure to children?

Generally, participants were positive about the storytelling system. Particularly, the idea of implicit disclosure i.e., discussing HIV with children indirectly through story characters was found novel and relatable. Most importantly, we observed that the experience with the prototype was transformative for parents, who shifted their viewpoints on disclosure from a one-time event to a gradual process. Participants highlighted that with techniques that support and abstract disclosure (e.g., stories, metaphors) and that at the same time remain easily understandable to children, disclosure may become more achievable. Experiences with the prototype also enabled parents to anticipate how the issue of hiding HIV from children becomes ever more pressing in the future, together with realizations that children grow more inquisitive as they grow older. The experience prototype also raised ideas around addressing HIV stigma more widely, helping parents in their own coping. Finally, the dialogue-based game, even when designed for simple touch interaction on tablet, may still be challenging for users with low educational backgrounds despite its simplistic interaction design, which is an aspect that needs to be addressed in future iterations, examining to which extent technology needs to be adapted for different user groups.

5.3 Considerations for Design

Here, we discuss the main considerations for the design of technology to support parental disclosure of sensitive information to children. First, we reflect on the potential of interactive systems to be transformational technologies. Building on these transformative simulated experiences, we articulate how parents (or disclosers of sensitive information in general) can be further supported. Second, we reflect on the role of trauma in the context of HIV disclosure. Finally, we discuss the challenge of providing sufficient support, while avoiding that disclosure is fully delegated to technology.

5.3.1 The Potential of Interactive Systems to Offer Transformative Experiences. In our work, we approached the role of interactive technology to support disclosure from an open-ended perspective, wanting to investigate whether and how technology could support the disclosure process. By focusing on parents and asking them to engage with partial disclosure through an interactive storytelling experience, we found that such systems have potential to transform how parents think about disclosure. The way the prototype presented disclosure offered parents a safe space to explore communication about HIV, and triggered reflection [ 15 ] in participants. In particular, it broke down prior understandings of disclosure, and expanded parents’ perspectives on disclosure in terms of viewing it as a process and recognizing the importance of correct biomedical understanding of the disease among children, and demonstrating the power of stories to present information. While our work – at its current stage – of course cannot provide conclusive evidence that the insights gained during our study afford changes in parents’ actions, we argue that this still is a promising result in terms of empowering parents to play an active role in the disclosure process: recognizing that while inherently challenging, disclosure can be implemented as a process that is supported by factual information, is an important first step toward parents changing attitudes to postponing disclosure. Here, our work also has potential to apply to other situations in which parents need to disclose sensitive information to children, and where similar challenges have been encountered, e.g., whether to disclose conception through donor insemination [ 92 ] or adoption [ 137 ]. Our findings are in line with other HCI studies that suggest that simulated disclosure experiences (e.g., with virtual agents like chatbots) facilitate self-disclosure [ 67 , 68 ]. In our context, we would hope that transformative experiences will have a positive impact on engaging in disclosure. However, whether and how prior virtual disclosure experiences can facilitate disclosure to a human requires further research.

5.3.2 Providing Structure and Information for Disclosure. Our findings show that disclosure is not only challenging because of stigma and traumatic experiences (see section 5.3.3), but also because it is a complex process that needs to be structured and continuously managed. Here, there is potential in technology that can help parents (or disclosers of sensitive information in general) navigate the disclosure process, allowing them to establish structures that facilitate disclosure, and enabling them to provide the right information at the right time.

Additionally, our findings support previous work [ 89 , 108 ] that highlighted that parents can find it difficult to adopt an appropriate health communication strategy with their children (even in cases where disclosure has already taken place). This implies that parents should be trained in tactics of presenting sensitive (health) information to children. Here, digital storytelling seems a promising strategy. Previously, storytelling and creative art were found to be beneficial for increasing children's awareness about personal hygiene and personal health [ 116 ]. Hence, future HCI research on parent-child health communication should further examine the potential of digital storytelling [ 5 , 65 ], especially in sensitive contexts like HIV.

Beyond parental disclosure, our findings have implications for designing interactive technology for disclosure in general, e.g., self-disclosure in adults on social media. We believe that establishing a process-oriented disclosure approach may apply for self-disclosure as well. This requirement of step-by-step disclosure is hinted at when people share “vague information” using indirect signals and cues [ 134 ] to first “test waters” [ 7 ]. Moreover, research indicated preferences of selective disclosure , differentiating the amount of information to be disclosed based on friendship intimacy [ 100 ]. Therefore, we ask, how might we support people on social media to define and structure the complex and continuous process of disclosure.

5.3.3 Understanding Technology to Support Disclosure as Trauma-Sensitive Technology. Our work shows that technology to support disclosure needs to be approached with care, not just because disclosure can be emotionally difficult for children, but also because of the individual experiences that parents bring into the process. Here, our work highlighted the personal struggles and traumatic experiences parents face in the context of HIV. Participants talked of deceased loved ones, broken households and struggles in employment. Research confirms that traumatic stress is common among the majority of people living with HIV in low- and middle-income countries [ 11 , 21 , 38 , 63 , 70 , 122 ], and its negative impact on their well-being is well-documented [ 44 , 72 , 95 ]. Designers and researchers need to be mindful that traumatic experiences shape their expectations related to disclosure, and in turn towards the experience prototypes. Additionally, this opens up a design space in which technology does not just offer opportunities to address HIV stigma (see section 4.5.4) but can help parents unpack their own experiences with the disease. Ultimately, we believe that successfully addressing parental trauma can be a steppingstone toward better managing disclosure of their children.

5.3.4 The Risk of Delegating Disclosure to Technology. In our work, parents and healthcare workers were enthusiastic about the potential of interactive technology to support disclosure. While this is important for technology adoption, we highlight that – in our specific setting – such systems need to be designed and integrated with care. Designers bear responsibility to ensure that technology supports rather than carries out disclosure. In an ecosystem in which parents delay disclosure due to fear of stigma and/or their own vulnerability, and healthcare workers push for faster disclosure to increase treatment adherence of HIV-positive children, a real risk of delegating disclosure to technology exists (also see [ 64 , 110 ]), allowing parents to remove themselves from the disclosure process. This could lead to prioritizing the result of disclosure over intergenerational implications, removing opportunities for the relationship between parent and child throughout the process, and leaving children with a technology that may not be able to address all their questions and concerns. Therefore, we recommend designing technology for disclosure in a way that – while it may address children in addition to parents – requires participation of an adult at crucial stages of the process and emphasizes the importance of parental involvement in disclosure. Technology should not assume the role of the main actor (for example observed in online therapy [ 64 , 76 ] or care robots for older adults [ 110 ]). Such health technologies have already triggered ethical debates on the (un)desirability of such approaches, as they avoid the human element that can provide the emotional support that is required as part of disclosure experiences.

6 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

Our work has several limitations and presents opportunities for further research. Firstly, the presented playful system only covers the first half of the entire HIV disclosure process (i.e., partial disclosure), leaving the other half of the disclosure journey (i.e., full disclosure) unaddressed. Hence, how parents could explain to children the mechanism of viruses, including mentioning HIV explicitly, is still lacking. Designing for full disclosure again requires identifying what constitutes age-appropriate disclosure mapped with their cognitive advancement, e.g., the neo-Piagetian perspective of children's understanding of illness processes (see section 2.2). Moreover, the Piagetian cognitive development theory has been widely used in research on illness understanding among children, However, it has also been criticized for underestimating children's understanding [ 12 ] when applying it in a specific domain (i.e., health) since it is a domain generic theory [ 27 ]. This implies that designs related to children should also consult other contemporary theories of development [ 14 , 55 , 135 ]. Furthermore, in addition to children's cognitive capability to consume medical information, designs related to children should also consider other aspects of development (social, physical, emotional) [ 40 , 113 ] to obtain a holistic understanding of their needs. The application of child development theories in interactive designs for children is widely acknowledged in HCI [ 37 , 43 , 55 ]. This effort should also be complemented with rigorous user analysis and active involvement of children [ 34 , 57 ] of multiple age groups to understand lived experiences and specific cultural contexts [ 52 ]. Finally, our prototype assumes the player to take the role of a mother, and hence there is a need to include fathers and other parental roles.

7 CONCLUSION

Stigma and fear among parents hinder HIV-status disclosure to HIV-positive children. In this work, we explored how non-disclosure negatively impacts children's wellbeing, how parents are hesitant to disclose despite children growing speculative about their health, and the room for technology to support parents. Considering the sensitivity of this space, we developed an interactive storytelling experience addressing partial disclosure, allowing parents to explore and reflect on disclosure. Through this prototype, we demonstrated the potential of interactive technology to support parents in establishing conversation about disclosure, and reframing disclosure as a process. Thereby, our work serves as a first step toward the design of interactive technology to support parents in disclosing sensitive information to children, as current research predominantly focuses on self-disclosure among adults. Nevertheless, we would like to emphasize that technology design in this space requires great caution, considering with care the traumatic and stigmatic experiences, and the ethics involved in designing technology in this space.
