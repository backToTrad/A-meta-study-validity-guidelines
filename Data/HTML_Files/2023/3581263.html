It has been shown that providing explanations about AI-based systems’ decisions can be an effective way to increase users’ trust and acceptance. The effect of explanation design in smart home systems on users’ acceptance and perceptions is however less known. We therefore explored the effect of different explanation designs on acceptance in the context of the Philips Hue smart home lighting system. We conducted interviews ( N = 10) and an online experiment ( N = 452) using three everyday smart home lighting scenarios with different explanation types. The results showed that although participants indicated a positive attitude towards explanations, receiving an explanation can potentially reduce the perceived control of the lighting system. Furthermore, participants preferred system-based explanations rather than user-based explanations. Our study also provides recommendations for the design of explanations in smart home systems.

1 INTRODUCTION

In 2003, the Department of Trade and Industry (DTI) Smart Homes project, defined a smart home as “a dwelling incorporating a communications network that connects the key electrical appliances and services, and allows them to be remotely controlled, monitored or accessed” [ 6 , 34 ]. They further elaborate that a smart home must have three distinct elements, namely an internal network, intelligent control, and home automation. Most modern smart home systems include networking and automation, while intelligent control remains limited. For example, a case in point is smart lighting systems. As one of the most popular smart home systems [ 15 , 40 ], smart lighting systems include light sources, sensors and user control devices [ 59 ]. However, the term “intelligent” mainly means IoT-based services or applications that allow users to control lights remotely, set simple rule-based automation or use a smartphone for control.

The definition of "intelligent" in smart home systems is being renewed and expanded. It is of benefit for the development of smart home systems to be able to receive and process environmental inputs in order to deliver optimal outcomes without human intervention, hence supporting interaction between the user and the environment [ 18 ]. For example, create adaptive lighting effects by utilizing a set of intelligent, context-aware algorithms in smart home lighting systems [ 7 , 59 ]. In addition to providing straightforward aspects such as illumination and ambiance, smart lighting system could support other use cases such as security (mimicking your presence when not at home)[ 2 ], detecting activities and routines (based on usage of light)[ 18 , 43 ], and monitoring and detecting behavioral abnormalities (changes in the usage pattern)[ 30 , 48 ], etc.

Adaptive and context-aware lighting systems can, however, be challenging for end users to understand. If there is a mismatch between what the user expects and how the system behaves, users may mistrust, misuse, or abandon a system [ 41 , 45 ]. For intelligent systems in general, providing explanations about system decisions can be an effective way to increase users’ trust and enhance the system's transparency [ 1 , 46 ]. There is a lot of discussion on how to deliver clear, precise, and understandable explanations to users, however most of it focuses on recommender systems or professional applications such as healthcare and e-commerce domain [ 5 , 22 , 58 , 63 ]. Some studies have explored the impact of explanation design on acceptance and trust in AI-infused systems, however only a few of these studies are focusing on smart home systems and tailored explanations for end-users [ 5 , 22 , 55 , 56 ]. The explanation design explored in these studies focuses on choosing among alternatives selections such as Top-N lists and word clouds (decision and action selection), whereas explanations for smart home systems emphasis on explaining the current decision (action implementation). Compared to empirical studies focusing on developing explanations for domain experts and developers, there are less studies focused on explanation design in human-AI systems targeting end-user groups [ 50 , 55 , 56 , 64 ]. Therefore, these findings cannot be directly applied to smart home systems.

To address this gap, and provide design recommendations for explanations that can be used in smart home scenarios, we explored the attitude and expectations of end users in lighting decision explanations, and examined the effect of explanation design on adoption intention. To explore this, an interview study and an online experiment were conducted. Three lighting scenarios were designed where explanations are needed in combination with two different formats of explanations (user-centered and system-based explanations). We also studied how users evaluate the explanation design and how explanations are influenced by the user's perception of the system in terms of perceived control, perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, and adoption intention. The research questions are:

  • RQ1: What are the attitudes and expectations of users when receiving an explanation of smart home lighting system decisions and why?
  • RQ2: What aspects of the explanation affect the users’ satisfaction with the smart home lighting system?
  • RQ3: To what extent does receiving an explanation with the proposed lighting scenarios impact users’ perceived control, perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, and adoption intention?

From the thematic analysis of the interviews we identified three themes showing the user's attitude, their expectations, and the differences among explanation types. We have found that system-based explanations lead to higher satisfaction with smart home lighting systems and that explanations in general have a positive effect on perceived ease of use. The results also showed that although users expressed a generally positive attitude towards explanations, they also perceived less control over the system when receiving an explanation.

The contributions of the study are 1) as one of the early attempts to examine the explanation design for end-users in smart home automation systems, we uncovered the needs of explanation for future systems and developed two corresponding explanation formats; 2) we highlighted the complexity and subtleness in providing explanations with controls of smart home lighting systems, which can be applied to related smart home systems; 3) we provided explanation design recommendations for future user interaction with smart home systems.

2 THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

2.1 User's acceptance and perceived control in the home context

In order to successfully implement a smart home lighting system in the home environment, it is important to understand how users accept and trust technology and its decisions. One of the most prevalent and acknowledged models for assessing user acceptance is the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) by Davis et al.[ 19 ]. In the TAM model, user acceptance of information technology is impacted by both perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, whereas perceived usefulness is also influenced by perceived ease of use. [ 19 ]. Based on the original TAM, Venkatesh proposed an extended theoretical framework of technology acceptance with additional anchors and adjustments, such as control and intrinsic motivation [ 69 ]. Several past studies have suggested and validated the technology acceptance model as a crucial framework for offering explanations of technology usage [ 38 , 44 , 54 ].

As intelligent and context-aware as smart homes are, one crucial challenge is the conflicting requirements between the user's active involvement and automation or decisions made by the system [ 73 ]. Hence, it is important to understand how much control users are willing to hand over to the system and how the system's behavior influences user's perceived control [ 61 , 74 ]. Perceived control is defined as how natural and easy it is for users to perform a particular activity [ 42 , 52 ]. As most home lighting used to be control-driven, it is increasingly important to investigate the users’ perceived control of a smart home lighting system.

Several studies have incorporated perceived control into the TAM framework [ 24 , 42 , 52 , 68 ]. Studies explored the relationship between perceived control and TAM constructs, and found that there is a strong positive correlation between perceived control and perceived ease of use [ 24 , 68 ]. These studies mainly focused on the e-commerce context. A study by Park et al. explored the main determinants of user acceptance of IoT technologies in a smart home environment [ 52 ]. They conducted an online survey with more than 1000 participants who had experience with IoT technologies in a smart home environment, and investigated a model suitable for smart home technology adoption. The study found that higher perceived control contributes to a higher score in the perceived ease of use, thus increasing users’ intention to use, which serves as an extension of the original Technology Acceptance Model [ 70 ]. Several aforementioned studies indicate that assessing perceived control can assist in understanding the acceptance of smart lighting systems that are able to make decisions. These studies showed that assessing perceived control with the TAM model is a necessary approach for investigating user acceptance in smart home systems [ 20 , 52 , 75 ].

2.2 Explanation design in smart home context

With a lack of control, users may mistrust, misuse, or abandon a system if there is a mismatch between what the user expects and how the system behaves [ 41 , 45 ]. One of the most applied solutions for the problem is to provide useful information and feedback about system decisions to users [ 57 ]. One type of information, explanations, are created to assist users with understanding how the system works. To be more specific, the reaction upon receiving an explanation represents how well a user can understand system decisions in a given context [ 73 ]. Providing explanations about system decisions can be an effective way to increase the users’ trust and enhance the system's transparency [ 1 , 46 ]. Miller suggested the reason people expect explanations is that they try to understand the system's behaviors by narrowing down the cause for the system's behaviour and forming a model for future predictions.

There are considerable discussions on how to deliver explanations in a clear, precise, and understandable way to users. One challenge about explainability is that the more complex the model grows, the more difficult it is to interpret and explain, and the less accurate the explanation will be [ 1 ]. Explanations should be contextual, timely, experiential, and explorative to increase the interpretability to users [ 5 , 12 , 37 , 49 ]. There are several general perspectives aiming at formulating explanation types, such as focus on global and local information [ 72 ], explanation method such as logical rationales and examples [ 22 , 41 , 63 ], explanation formats such as visualization, texts, or lists [ 1 , 29 , 64 ]. For instance, a study by Alam showed that a combination of the different aforementioned formats has been shown to result in high satisfaction in an AI-driven medical decision-support system [ 5 ]. Their study showed that compared to texts with rationale, users showed higher satisfaction when presented with the integrated rationale with examples or visual content. A study by Ehsan et al. demonstrated how user's mental models differentiate explanation rationales based on short-term and long-term goals [ 23 ]. The study argued that when facing an unexpected behavior of the system, users prefer a transparent yet simple explanation to understand from the perspective of the system. Another interesting approach is the productivity-oriented (or utilitarian) and pleasure-oriented (or hedonic) explanations for the intelligent systems [ 39 , 67 ]. Li and Mao suggested that providing explanations with a sense of human touch can increase social presence for health advisory services [ 39 ]. Compared to the utilitarian approach, which focuses on the effectiveness and functionality of the information, hedonic aspects increase the user's feelings of social presence and system reuse intentions. They argued that systems with a communication style that matches the user's language habits are able to increase the user's trust and enjoyment.

While most studies of explainable systems are focused on technical aspects, such as the algorithm and network structures, insights from the perspective of human-centered approaches are less investigated [ 49 ]. Industry applications, such as health and e-commerce with expert explanations, have received more attention than consumer products in this domain [ 5 , 22 , 58 , 63 ]. Little attention has been paid to the smart home context, especially studies on end users’ perspectives [ 56 ]. There are few studies on smart home systems that address the aspect of explanations of the intelligent decisions in a smart home lighting system. For instance, Das et al. explored explainable algorithms in activity recognition in smart homes, and argued that providing explanations about activity recognition to end users is of great value in smart home automation [ 18 ]. They indicated that further studies should aim to explore the possible impact of receiving an explanation on user satisfaction in a smart home context. The study showed that designing explanations in the context of smart home systems may have different requirements than explanations in other contexts. We therefore aim to add to work in the field, by providing insights on explanations in highly automated smart home systems, especially smart home lighting systems, which lack the necessary research in order for practitioners to give tailored explanations to users.

3 SCENARIO AND EXPLANATION DESIGN

We designed two studies to investigate the explanation design and its impact on users’ acceptance and perceptions. The first interview study aimed to gain an understanding of users’ attitudes and their expectations of explanations of smart home lighting systems in different lighting scenarios ( RQ1 ). Additionally, we explored the aspects that affect users’ satisfaction and probed the reasons of the preference between two predefined explanation types ( RQ2 ). In the online experiment, we assessed the effect of the explanations and scenarios on users’ adoption intention and perceptions ( RQ3 ). The following section demonstrates the design and rationale of scenarios and explanations in both studies.

3.1 Lighting scenario design

Three lighting scenarios were prepared for the study to evoke discussion about explanation design and perceptions. Based on empirical studies and industry practices [ 2 , 7 , 11 , 18 , 66 ], we selected three representative lighting automation scenarios, including remote access and automation, activity identification, and context-aware intelligent assistant in residential lighting environments. The first scenario represents the ability of the system to remotely automate the key functions without human intervention [ 2 ], which requires the least active involvement of users. The process can be done by making use of past usage data. The second scenario adopted the concept of adaptive systems based on activity detection [ 7 , 18 ]. For this scenario, the system requires a combination of current data and previous data locally. In the third scenario, the system demonstrates the ability to learn, adjust, and predict the lighting based on multiple variables, the system requires the usage of personal and environmental data from connected systems. The data and the decision-making process are retrieved through the cloud. From scenario 1 to scenario 3, the complexity of the task performance of the smart home lighting system and the required connectivity of home systems also increases.

  • Scenario 1: Presence mimic. When the presence mimic is activated, the lighting system will make decisions on turning on and off certain lights at home to mimick their presence for their safety. The decision is based on the previous data when users are away from home.
  • Scenario 2: Activity-based mode. Providing activity-based lighting can help improve the quality of life for the occupant [ 18 ]. Based on historical user data and the current activity of the user, the lighting system makes decisions about turning on and off certain lights for users or switching to proper lighting scenes.
  • Scenario 3: Lighting assistant. The system collected not only the sensor data such as exercise logs and health data, but also the contextual data including the weather, humidity, etc. The system can keep learning about the users’ habits and routines. In this way, the system can predict the most suitable lighting.

The design of the hypothetical lighting scenarios follows the principles of scenario-based design and experimental vignettes [ 3 , 13 , 14 ]. Storyboards were used in order to keep participants interested and to avoid fatigue [ 25 ]. Corresponding to each scenario, three stories were designed based on internal studies conducted in the Signify research group and discussed thoroughly among the research team. Additionally, the three lighting scenarios were assessed by experienced experts in the lighting domain and improved accordingly. All lighting scenarios were not existing features and were not used by any of the participants at home. The three scenarios are practical applications of context-aware and adaptive smart home lighting systems based on the Philips Hue lighting system. All three scenarios were presented in the format of vignettes in two studies. 3.2 Explanation design

For each scenario, we designed and formulated three different explanation types (user-centered explanation, system-centered explanation, and no explanation) tailored to the scenarios. Adopting the principles for Human-AI systems, several design guidelines were considered for all variants of explanations. To be more specific, with all explanation designs, we used short and simple language to reduce the complexity [ 50 , 56 ], an icon as visualization with supporting text-based information [ 64 ], and the content is timely[ 5 ], relevant[ 65 ], and contextual[ 22 ]. In addition, we focused on a particular system decision of the system that may be considered unexpected behaviors by users. All explanations involve reasoning for a certain decision in the scenarios [ 46 , 63 ]. For all the options, there was the same icon with the same system status message in the notification. Next, based on the difference between hedonic and utilitarian explanation designs [ 39 , 67 ], we developed three different types of explanation on a notification page for participants as follows (see example in Scenario activity-based mode in Figure 2 ). We consulted with the AI-based algorithm domain expert and confirmed that these explanations are faithful to the underlying AI system performing the actions.

  • User-centered explanation: As suggested by Li and Mao [ 39 ], creating a sense of human touch of a virtual advisor has a positive influence on the user. Systems with a communication style that matches the user's language are able to arouse the user's trust and enjoyment. In addition, adding a pronoun in communication can enhance trust. The use of “you” and “we” pronouns is recommended by customer service communication to emphasize the customer [ 9 ]. Therefore, the message was based on the interest of the user in the first person plural, offering explanations with a personal touch to the participants.
  • System-centered explanation: Compared with the user-centered explanation, the system-centered explanation provides the same amount of information, but in a less social, more matter-of-fact way. Providing participants with explanations in a neutral tone, the explanation focused on the functionality of the system.
  • No explanation: In this notification, only the system status was provided as well as all the other options. This is the control group.

4 STUDY 1: INTERVIEWS

4.1 Method

Design. A qualitative study using semi-structured interviews was performed. The goal of the interviews was to gain insights into people's understanding and expectation of explanations in smart home lighting scenarios ( RQ1 and RQ2 ).

Participants. Ten participants (7 male and 3 female) were recruited through convenience sampling, see Table 1 . Five participants were employees of Signify and the other participants were recruited externally from the Philips Hue beta user community. We recruited participants who 1) have more than 5 smart lighting bulbs or luminaires of Philips Hue; 2) have experience in home sensors or automation usages of Philips Hue lighting system; 3) are without visual, auditory, and motor impairments; 4) are able to communicate fluently in English. We did not aim to recruit a representative sample, we however tried to cover a diverse background in age, households, occupations, and different levels of smart home experience. Participants’ ages ranged between 24 and 64 years old ( M = 36.80 years, SD = 11.38 years). Participants joined voluntarily and did not receive any compensation. All participants resided in the Netherlands at the time of the study.

Procedure. The interviews were semi-structured and conducted by one researcher in either a face-to-face or an online setting with video recording. The duration varied between 50 and 75 minutes. After signing the consent form and agreement to the video recording, participants were given a short introduction at the beginning of the study. In the interview, after a short introduction, they were asked to share 1)the general usage of the Philips Hue lighting system, 2) their setup of smart home services or devices, and 3) their past experience of their smart bulbs and automation at home (e.g. do you consider your current automation sufficient? If not, what can be improved?). Then they were presented with all three scenarios via the shared screen by the researcher. They were asked about their expectations for the situation, their level of confusion or annoyance, and how well they can relate to the scenarios as compared with their actual home experience (e.g. does the scenarios applicable in your case? would you consider the scenario confusing in this case?). Then, three explanations for each scenario were presented to the participants at the same time. They were asked which explanation they preferred and the reasons for their preference (e.g. which explanation would you prefer and why? In your own case, are you satisfied with the explanation or not?). In addition, they were requested to modify the existing explanation in terms of formatting, phrasing, and user interface. All participants were presented with all scenarios with all explanations in the same order, see Figure 3 .

Data analysis.

To understand how the users perceived the different explanation types and scenarios, we followed the inductive thematic analysis procedure suggested by Braun and Clarke [ 10 , 16 ]. The data acquired through the interview were fully transcribed from video recordings and analyzed with an inductive open coding procedure. All data was manually coded. In total, 563 codes were generated. Two coders, one internal researcher and one external researcher, independently coded the data through inductive categorization. For each sub-theme, one or multiple codes were assigned. Themes and sub-themes were developed and presented to the whole research team in peer debriefing during the middle stage and the last stage of the analysis for formative feedback [ 62 ]. The inter-coder reliability (ICR), expressed in the percentage of agreement per theme, was on average 81.8% with a minimum of 77.6% and a maximum of 87.5%.

Attitude towards explanation. There are three major insights indicating the attitude towards the explanation given. First, eight participants were likely to assume the system misbehaves without an explanation accross all the scenarios. As can be seen below, they especially voiced clear frustrations.

“I would be wondering if the system is actually working” (P1, P4, P6, P7) .

“I think the system wasn't robust. It could annoy me. I would want a system that takes away my responsibility, not that I have to babysit it.” (P5)

Second, all participants considered explanations helpful in understanding the system. All participants acknowledged the usefulness of the explanations in assisting them to understand how the system works (P2, P10) , or showed an increase in trusting the system (P1, P7) .

"I want to see the logic, how it works. An explanation could be handy. Otherwise, I might be doubting is it picking the wrong scene.” (P2)

Third, all participants emphasized the importance of having control over the decisions highlighted in the explanations. Some participants clearly paid more attention to how to possibly have an impact on the decision, including switching the system off, switching back to the previous lighting state, or overriding the decision of the system. They mentioned the importance of having sense of control and having the ability to impact the decision (P1, P3, P4, P8, P9) .

"I do trust the system, but I want to control my own life...[]...I think a sense of control is very important. Aside from the explanation, I would like to have the option to change the behavior, turn off the feature or change the settings"(P8)

Expectation for explanation. In terms of the design of the explanation, there were several common expectations mentioned. Eight participants mentioned the explanation needs to be short, simple, and easy to read. The message should be simple to understand, only including the critical information for the decision.

“For me the sentences are complicated, I've been reading the text for the fourth time and for me, fewer lines always helps.” (P6) .

Six participants commented that the icon helped them to attract attention and understand the information, indicating that the visualization together with the text was able to make the explanation easier to understand.

"The icon is good, it catches my attention. It makes sense to me and gives my info. However it needs to be accurate." (P5)

Six participants noted that the availability of additional information would be ideal to get a general understanding of the system. This additional information could be details of how the data was used, what was the general lighting schedule was, or a global explanation of how the system worked in general.

"It could be nice to know more if you click on it. I maybe not always want to know more but I want to have the option. Especially in the beginning when you gain trust." (P4)

Interestingly, five participants addressed the fear of being bothered by the explanations. Some suggested that the message should not be a pop-up message, and several mentioned that the message should disappear automatically after some time.

“I wish the system can learn and only give me explanation when I am confused. I would like to have information accessible somewhere, but not pop-up or pile-up in the notification. That's annoying." (P7)

Explanation types. In most of the stories, participants preferred the user-centered explanation or the system-centered explanation over no explanation. Participants considered the former two similar, and more informative and clear to show the reasoning behind the decision. Some participants preferred the user-centered explanation over the system-centered explanation due to the hedonic aspect of the message.

“It's more humane. There is a personal attitude, which I like. I like it to be less formal, less official." (P4)

Some participants considered the user-centered explanation disturbing due to the tone of the information, stating it is patronizing (P8) or creepy (P6) . The use of pronouns was considered improper by one participant.

“It's too long, and it's creepy. It feels like some of my data is harvested, I wouldn't like having a smart agent at home, I would like to have more control of my own life” (P6)

“I don't like the ‘we’, either there is a fictional person, like Siri, then it makes sense. Now it's like, who is it connected to? it's weird" (P3)

On the other hand, several participants who preferred the system-centered explanation over the user-centered explanation stated that the system-centered explanation is more precise and efficient. While some participants suggested that the system-centered explanation “ is the right amount of information.” (P9) , participant #4 criticized that it is “too robotic and cold” .

“ It's explicit, precise and short. I prefer the sterile way of information. Also it's the right amount of information. I don't need more."(P5)

Three participants preferred the “no explanation” option in some scenarios only when the explanation was still accessible. Participant #10 explains, “If you every time see a very long message, it's not necessary, only when I need it then I can click.” Most participants ignored the no explanation option during the study, stating “it's not informative”(P1) or “there is not enough input” (P5) .

4.3 Summary of Study 1

In Study 1, we conducted online semi-structured interviews with 10 participants to explore their attitudes and perception of the explanation design in a smart home lighting system. The most important insight is that most participants had a positive attitude towards having an explanation of a smart home lighting system decision. The results showed that providing explanations could help them to understand and learn about the system, which is consistent with previous studies [ 46 , 73 ]. However, the timing of the explanation should be decided carefully since a few participants suggested only showing the information when it is necessary [ 65 ]. Another point worth mentioning is the aspects of what users perceive as a good explanation, such as short and simple [ 50 , 56 ], with proper visualization [ 64 ], seamless and less intrusive, with accessibility for complete explanation [ 23 , 36 ]. In general, the explanation design was appreciated by the participants. However, the preference between user-centered explanation and system-centered remained unclear.

5 STUDY 2:ONLINE EXPERIMENT

5.1 Method

Design. The second study was an online experiment where different forms of explanation were tested in three scenarios. The independent variable of the study was the different types of explanation of the smart home lighting system and three scenarios of the smart home lighting system (Explanation type: user-based, system-based, no explanation; Scenario: presence mimic, activity-based mode, lighting assistant). The main dependent variable was the user's adoption intention. Perceived control, perceived ease of use, and perceived usefulness were measured to examine how they mediate the effect of explanation on users’ adoption intention. Both independent variables were manipulated between participants, leading to a 3 by 3 between-subjects design.

Participants. All participants were Philips Hue lighting users who used the related app. All participants were recruited from the Philips Hue beta community, which is a platform for users to give feedback or participate in studies. Participants had to be at least 18 years old. All of them were required to have good English skills and be able to understand the questionnaire in English. In total, 452 participants participated in the study. The sample consisted of 34 females, 413 males and two non-binary participants. Three participants preferred not to state their gender. The reason for this asymmetric distribution was that the Philips Hue beta community is predominantly male in composition. The average age of the participants was 42 ( M = 41.97, SD =10.60, Min = 19, Max = 74) with 16 missing values. The sample descriptive information is listed in Table 3 . Participants were randomly assigned to one of nine conditions using a randomization feature in Qualtrics, in a 3x3 matrix (scenario x explanation type).

Procedure. All participants were invited via email through the Philips Hue user beta community. The experiment was conducted on Qualtrics. The participation was voluntary and participants did not receive compensation. The duration of the whole experiment varied from 5 to 10 minutes. Participants were asked to sign the informed consent of the study, and gave their consent for using their anonymized data in an academic research publication. Then participants were randomly allocated to one scenario with one explanation type. Next, they were asked to read the story attentively and fill out three manipulation check items. After the scenario was finished, they were randomly allocated again to one explanation (or no explanation) corresponding to the scenario. Then participants were asked to read through the notification page. After this phase, they were asked to fill in the explanation satisfaction scale. Participants who received no explanation did not receive the explanation satisfaction scale. At last, participants filled in a questionnaire about their perception of the explanation, acceptance of the features, and demographics.

Data analysis. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) was utilized to assess the relationship between the dependent variable (explanation present/absent) and extended TAM variables (perceived control, self-efficacy, perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, adoption intention). We were interested in identifying whether receiving an explanation for end-users led to differences in the assessment of perceived control, perceived ease of use, and thus possibly placed an indirect effect on adoption intention. Explanation (absent/present) was defined as an exogenous categorical variable. We hypothesized that receiving explanations can lead to a higher score in perceived ease of use (H1). We further assumed that this effect was mediated by perceived control positively (H2, H3), which can be found in several past studies [ 24 , 42 , 52 , 68 ]. In addition, based on previous studies [ 33 , 70 , 71 ], we hypothesized that self-efficacy positively impacts the perceived ease of use (H4). At last, our assumption was that the relationship of perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, and adoption intention supports the original TAM model [ 69 , 70 ]. This means that a higher perceived ease of use leads to a higher perceived usefulness (H5), thus having a positive impact on adoption intention (H6). A structural equation model (SEM) was performed the assess the relationship between explanation and constructs ( RQ3 ).

In addition, differences in explanation satisfaction between two explanation types were examined using two-way between-subject ANOVA ( $\alpha \;=0.05$ ). We conducted the manipulation check, reliability check, and outlier detection on the data obtained. In the manipulation check, participants were asked to indicate their agreement to three statements on a 5-point Likert scale related to their confusion after reading the scenario. All three scenarios showed a decent manipulation to create confusion ( p < .001). Four observations that resulted in the lowest score (strongly disagree) in all manipulation items were dropped. The reliability coefficients for all scales met the expectation with Cronbach's $\alpha \;$ larger than 0.70. Z-score was used for outlier detection. Six observations in the self-efficacy scale were dropped with a z-score smaller than -3.

A two-way between-subjects ANOVA was conducted to compare the effect of scenarios and explanations on satisfaction. In other words, scenarios and explanations independently impact the explanation satisfaction. A Tukey post-hoc test revealed significant pairwise differences between scenario presence mimic and scenario activity-based mode ( p = .01). The result showed explanation satisfaction in scenario presence mimic is significantly higher than in scenario activity-based mode. Explanation satisfaction scores of system-centered explanation group are significantly higher than user-centered explanation group in both scenario presence mimic ( t (104) = -3.44, p < .001) and activity-based mode ( t (98) = -2.17, p = .03). In scenario lighting assistant, there is no statistical significant difference on explanation satisfaction between the user-centered explanation group and the system-centered explanation group with t (99) = -0.74, p = .45.

5.3 Summary of Study 2

In Study 2, we investigated the relationship between explanation presence/absence, self-efficacy, perceived control, perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, and adoption intention through the structural equation model. Most of our assumptions were met and consistent with past studies [ 24 , 33 , 42 , 52 , 68 , 69 , 70 , 71 ]. A positive effect of having an explanation on perceived ease of use was reported. However, we found one exception, which is the negative direct impact of explanation presence on perceived control. Furthermore, with two explanation types we developed (user-centered explanation and system-based explanation), we have identified that system-based explanation leads to higher satisfaction in smart home lighting systems.

6 GENERAL DISCUSSION

6.1 The effect of explanation on constructs of TAM model

Study 1 explored the attitude, expectations, and preference of participants on explanation design via qualitative research ( RQ1 & RQ2 ), whereas Study 2 mainly investigated how receiving an explanation impact user perception of smart home lighting systems ( RQ3 ). Study 2 found that having an explanation implied a minor negative impact on the perceived control of users compared to the group without explanation. One possible reason might be that the current lighting system does not match users’ mental models of a traditional lighting system. As suggested by Study 1, when there is no explanation provided, users may consider there is a system error. Users who received explanations may think the system to be unexpectedly “intelligent” or “smart”, while it is unlikely for users to update their mental model which requires cognitive effort. This mismatch between the mental model and the experience may lead to negative consequences [ 38 ]. An alternative explanation may be that receiving an explanation may activate the notion of control. There is a potential parallel with regard to cognitive processes involved in users’ privacy calculus in terms of system-related perceptions [ 21 ]. Previous studies have shown that when informing users explicitly of the ways in which their data may be used by an app, it paradoxically increases their distrust or concerns with the system [ 35 , 53 ]. This is also consistent with some user remarks in Study 1, with some participants stating that receiving explanations induces a feeling of “my data is harvested” or “being spied on” . Giving an explanation to participants may trigger their awareness of privacy concerns, thus activating notions of potential risk, rather than putting their minds at ease about taking a well-informed decision. However, with the mediation of perceived control, receiving an explanation still contributes to higher perceived ease of use, indicating that although participants feel less in control of the system, the explanation helped them to understand how the system works. The result of Study 2 also supported the original TAM model, suggesting that a higher perceived control and a higher self-efficacy can lead to higher perceived ease of use, through increasing perceived usefulness, leading to an increase in user acceptance [ 33 , 69 , 70 ]. The result of Study 1 strengthened this argument, with several participants stating that their acceptance of the lighting features depended on “the added benefit” (perceived usefulness), “how well I understand this system” (perceived ease of use), or “easy to configure with override option” (perceived control).

6.2 Difference of explanation types on explanation satisfaction

In Study 1, the preference between system-centered explanation and user-centered explanation was not yet clear. However, the result of Study 2 of the explanation satisfaction score illustrated that the system-centered explanation gained a statistically significant higher score than user-centered explanation in presence mimic and activity-based scenarios. Combining some comments from Study 1, this effect on explanation satisfaction could be attributed to several reasons. First, with the same amount of information, compared to the user-centered explanation, the system-centered explanation emphasized on its effectiveness, indicating that utilitarian aspects of the explanation enhance the perceived informativeness of users [ 39 ]. Users may treat the hedonic aspects of the information as noise or barriers to gain information. This may indicate that compared to other systems, users do not expect a smart home lighting system to provide a feeling of social presence [ 39 , 67 ]. Second, the use of pronouns can possibly have a negative impact on the explanation satisfaction. The user-centered explanation used “we” as the subject, whereas the system-centered explanation used “the system” as the main subject. Few users mentioned the confusion of the pronoun in Study 1. Although using first-person plural pronouns indicates intimacy and closeness to the users, there are other studies indicating that using “we” can be harmful to communication when the language is inconsistent with the expectation [ 60 ]. Therefore, the lower satisfaction in user-centered explanations could be attributed to the misuse of pronouns.

6.3 Theoretical and practical implications

Theoretical implications. First, the current study explored the effect of having an explanation for end users in smart home lighting systems. We argued that providing an explanation has both a positive and negative impact on perceived ease of use, which further results in a negative impact on adoption intention. The implication is that an explanation can assist users to understand the decision-making process, whereas the possible harmful effect needs to also be taken into consideration. Second, both studies addressed the importance on providing controls in smart home lighting systems. It is essential in smart home systems to consider the optimal controllability and availability to override the system along with providing an explanation [ 74 ]. Our study addressed the complexity of providing explanations in a highly automated system, and raised questions for future explanation design. Lastly, our study enriched and verified the possible aspects of an explanation with the hedonic and practical communication style, and found that in smart home contexts, users focus more on the effectiveness of the explanation.

Practical implications. Our study indicates different explanation design principles or guidelines focusing on combining user controls with automation in a smart home context for future research. Some recommendations might be helpful for future designs of user interaction with similar systems from a pragmatic perspective:

  • Short and simple explanations with natural language: Considering some user comments in both Study 1 and Study 2, explanations for end users should carefully choose the wording and use plain language, and the length of the information should be short. This is in line with previous studies, showing that the explanation should be simple and short for end users [ 56 , 64 ].
  • Accessibility to global information: Participants mentioned the importance of understanding the system on a general level. This is in line with the study of Kulesza et al. [ 36 ], which suggested that completeness of the explanations is helpful to end users’ mental models. The implication is that having global explanations accessible can help users to increase their understanding of the system.
  • Explanation combined with control: The implication of perceived control is that in future home automation system design, it is essential to retain the availability of user control along with explanations to increase the perceived control of users.
  • Proper visualization combined with text: For both studies, participants mentioned visualization, e.g. icons, could help them understand the explanation better. This finding verified the importance of the mixed modality design with visualization and texts [ 64 ].

6.4 Limitations and future work

A limitation of this study is the collection of data in an online environment. The hypothetical scenario might not have been enough to elicit actual intention and other judgment. Although the use of vignettes can help participants to imagine experiencing a particular situation, several participants mentioned that they tend to make decisions based on the actual usage of the smart home lighting system. It needs to be considered that unrealistic scenarios can be insufficient to capture the real-life dynamics of decision-making processes [ 25 ]. Even if it is possible to have realistic smart home lighting systems, there is still a gap between behavioral intention and actual behavior [ 4 ]. We would like to inform the reader that the current study was conducted in a relatively short amount of time. A couple of minutes may not be sufficient for the cognitive process of adopting a new concept and making judgments. To conclude, with limited time in hypothetical scenarios, the adoption intention and perceived control could be imperfect in eliciting participants’ actual behaviors. A longitudinal study, especially using lighting systems in a real-life environment, is therefore recommended to address these limitations in the future. Another potential limitation is that we have a specific user group comprising the sample. It should be noted that the current sample was predominantly male (413/452, 91%), which are representative of smart lighting system users. Previous studies have shown that there is a gender difference in preferred communication style and the perception of intelligent systems [ 26 , 28 ]. Second, from their frequency and usage of the system they are more likely to be tech-savvy users. Hence, their attitude toward smart home lighting systems might differ from the population mean. In general, this sample is not representative of the general population, but can be representative of the smart lighting system users. Future studies could explore the possible relationship between gender, system experience and explainability with a sample representing the general population.

7 CONCLUSION

Using a mixed-method approach, we examined how users evaluate the explanation design and how explanations influence their perception of the system, including perceived control, self-efficacy, perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, and adoption intention through two studies. We developed two explanation types, and found that users in a smart home context prefer system-centered explanations rather than user-centered explanations due to the effectiveness of the information. Furthermore, we argued that providing explanations has both a positive and negative impact on perceived ease of use, which places a further impact on adoption intention. While we anticipate that those design recommendations can assist future explanation design in smart home context, we acknowledge that, as one of the earliest attempts, it is a starting point. As the systems become more context-aware and intelligent, we hope that the findings of the study could shed light on understanding the complexity and subtleness for future researchers in providing explanations for Human-AI systems.
