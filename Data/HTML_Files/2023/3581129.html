University students are well known for volunteering within non-governmental organisations (NGOs). A significant part of NGO practice is the production of documents that communicate their work to local communities and international stakeholders. However, organisations often struggle to resource translations of these documents, resulting in the exclusion of the very same communities that they want to reach. Although many students are multilingual and are willing to volunteer their time and language skills, there are few structured opportunities configured for such non-professional translation of content in the short-term mode that would fit into the student pattern of availability. We developed Action Translate to specifically support these motivated, non-professional translators within the volunteering constraints of university life. Action Translate leverages machine translation post-editing to support teams of volunteers working on NGO translation projects online. Through analysis of a real-world deployment, we discuss how digital systems can be developed to better support student volunteer translators, specifically in building collegiate interaction and identity as translators for a cause.

1 INTRODUCTION

Many non-governmental organisations (NGOs) operate across national and linguistic borders, however they often struggle to translate their content (such as reports, policy briefs and digital media) into the languages spoken by the local communities they work with [ 20 , 25 ]. English is used as the lingua franca for communication in many international organisations [ 56 ] and it remains the prominent language of NGO reports over local languages [ 22 ]. In spite of the multilingual values (and sometimes multilingual policies) of NGOs, translations often become available "later or not at all" [ 40 ] due to the insufficient resources allocated to translation activities. The lack of translation, especially beyond the major (colonialist) languages, hinders the accessibility of important content that NGOs produce, preventing local communities from being able to meaningfully engage with resources that are directly relevant to them.

Online collaborative translation platforms could provide an answer to this issue by leveraging people's capacity to volunteer their time to translate NGO content for interested communities. In particular, we argue that multilingual university students, which represent a large volume of many university student bodies, are a promising cohort of volunteers that can be harnessed to translate NGO content. They are a well-defined group that has the skills and the motivations to volunteer translations for causes that they support. They are accessible to recruit, and tend to have a good understanding of novel technologies and processes. They also directly benefit from participating in volunteer translation activities, through which they can strengthen their language abilities as well as other core technical and soft skills that are useful in and beyond the academic environment.

Current models of online collaborative translation volunteering are of two types: "solicited" models, where translations are ‘commissioned’ by a not-for-profit organisation or a company, and "non-solicited" models where translation initiatives are put in place directly by volunteers, such as groups of activists, users or fans interested in making a particular content more widely available [ 49 ]. Examples of non-solicited models include fansubbing, Wiki(pedia) translation, and news and literary translations done through crowdsourcing translation platforms such as Yeeyan. "Solicited" models have been implemented by both non-profits such as Translators Without Borders (TWB), whose specific aim is to support humanitarian organisations with their translation needs (for a fee), and also by many for-profit companies wanting to avoid the high budgets linked to professional translation services. Indeed, even large corporations such as Facebook, Google, Twitter and Alibaba have all resorted to crowdsourcing to translate their websites for free.

For a number of key reasons, neither of these models allows to fully harness the potential of multilingual students wanting to contribute translations for global causes that they support. Online collaborative translation initiatives conducted by for-profit organisations raise ethical concerns around the use of free labour to generate profits for private companies, which would be hard to reconcile with the learning outcomes that universities want to provide to students through volunteering opportunities. Non-profits such as TWB only allow professional or formally trained translators to participate in their translation initiatives [ 52 ]. On the other hand, non-solicited models of online collaborative translation are also ill-suited for student volunteering. Often they do not enforce any deadlines for completion, whereas student volunteering needs to be bound to well-defined time frames, with activities that can be completed in short bursts as to not clash with study commitments or go beyond the duration of the study course.

In addition, platforms for non-solicited translations often leave translators to use their own digital tools and follow their own workflow, which is not suitable for beginner translators or for a volunteering activity that needs to be undertaken with minimal training.

There is therefore a need for an online collaborative translation model that suits non-experienced translators and is centred around a digitally-mediated workflow that fits students’ motivations for volunteering, their social and educational goals, as well as their time constraints bound to their course requirements. There is a gap for a workflow designed to allow translation volunteering outside the logics of profit, regardless of the solicited or non-solicited nature of the individual translation initiatives, and that fosters translation volunteering as a purposeful learning or up-skilling experience rather than as the provision of a free service. While this model would be particularly suitable for students (the cohort we discuss in this paper), it could also be transferable to volunteer translation initiatives driven by NGO in-country volunteers or employee volunteering programs.

This paper presents Action Translate: a platform which supports meaningful student volunteering of translation for global causes by leveraging machine translation to present a single point of interaction. Reporting on a three-week deployment period, conducted in July 2022, with 90 students working on the translation into 16 languages of 19 written documents published by the Antarctic Treaty Secretariat, we discuss the design of the platform and how student volunteer translation can be supported at scale, whilst also providing much needed access to NGO translated content to more communities.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Online collaborative translation

Translation has been traditionally conceptualised as the solitary endeavour of a translator armed with a dictionary [ 7 ]. However, this could not be further detached from the reality of modern translation: a highly collaborative activity that has become "a form of human-computer interaction (HCI)" [ 41 ] for which digital technologies are indispensable.

As the Translation Studies literature has acknowledged [ 9 , 32 , 46 , 51 , 59 , 60 ], collaborative and interactive behaviours are more starkly visible in online translation practices, when multiple individuals situated across different locations (and often with no previous connection to each other) translate content assisted by digital technologies. This type of translation activity is often performed by volunteers. Although volunteer translation is not a new phenomenon, it became increasingly prominent with the advent of Web 2.0 and the proliferation of publicly available media content that an increasing number of people wanted to access in their languages. Solicited and non-solicited models of volunteer translation emerged in this context[ 33 , 49 ]. The former based on a company or a not-for-profit organisation outsourcing translation tasks to volunteers; the latter centred on translation initiatives started by collectives of activists or fans acting as volunteer translators to spread a particular content more widely. Professional translator associations and Translation Studies scholars have indicated the problematic nature of engaging volunteers to perform translation tasks, especially when the work is requested by someone else, like in solicited models, rather than engaged with by translators on their own terms. Such activities are said to de-professionalise and demonetise the role of the translator [ 52 ]. There are also ethical concerns around who might profit from this unpaid work. For instance, translation data generated by TWB volunteers was transferred to TWB partner Microsoft to improve the tech giant's machine translation engines for languages such as Haitian Creole and Swahili and essentially "turned into saleable intellectual property" [ 52 ]. When it was launched in 2012, popular language learning app Duolingo also attempted to set up a crowdsourced translation business selling translations done by its users while using the app to learn a language.

"Crowdsourcing" translation has been used as a general term [ 42 ] to refer to online collaborative translation practices “where the work is largely voluntary (i.e. unpaid in financial terms)” [ 53 ]. In other contexts, online collaborative translation models have been known as community translation [ 35 ], wiki-translation [ 21 ], fan translation [ 55 ], user-generated translation [ 48 ], and citizen translation [ 24 ].

The existence of these intrinsically related (although not entirely synonymous) terms show that volunteer translation is often a group endeavour, happening through digitally-mediated exchanges among individuals who commit to the same goal. Furthermore, this terminology offers a glimpse into the range of motivations that lead people to devote their time to translate content without remuneration: such as facilitating their own consumption of content they are interested in (e.g. fansubbing of TV series, films or video games), sharing knowledge that is not available in their language (e.g. translating Wikipedia articles or TED talks), or helping humanitarian organisations in crisis situations where translation is needed (e.g. TWB).

Online collaborative translation has also been used as a helpful activity for translation and language learning. For example, students have been involved in the translation of Wikipedia articles [ 5 ] or in collaborative translation workshops following the TransCollaborate model, where participants are called to navigate an experimental translation process involving an unfamiliar language by collaborating closely with their peers [ 28 ]. Language learning apps such as Duolingo have also used translation as a language learning tool, although in a rather limited fashion, translation tasks being restricted to individual sentences rather than texts.

While many providers offering crowdsourced translations to for-profit companies have stopped operating in recent years, including Duolingo that abandoned its translation business in 2014 after failing to attract enough customers [ 3 ], not-for-profit models of collaborative translation for educational, NGO or activist initiatives are continuing to grow [ 33 ]. This testifies to the centrality of the learning component and the importance of translating for a cause in online collaborative translation.

Compared to collaborative translation work relying solely on traditional methods of digitally-mediated communication (e.g. email), existing online collaborative translation platforms provide some examples of best practices. Platforms such as the Amara subtitle platform (used by TED Translators [ 38 ]) and Yeeyan (China's largest crowdsourcing translation platform [ 59 ]) provide valuable insights on the importance of sourcing content that is relevant for the volunteers involved, relying on strong participatory mechanisms, and placing emphasis on the different identity roles taken on by the volunteers.

Each of these platforms or models, however, also presents limitations. For instance, crowdsourcing platforms are particularly dependent on the transient availability of translators who might be hard to source for certain language combinations [ 6 ]. Additionally, volunteer translators choose what to translate, making it difficult to achieve a complete, deliverable translation in a timely or even predictable period [ 13 ]. Meanwhile, humanitarian translation networks such as TWB are often not suitable for lay translators, as participation involves lengthy application processes which favour professional translators. Despite being a charitable organisation, TWB has also been criticised for its top-down corporate structure with minimal opportunities for learning [ 52 ]. A platform like Yeeyan offers a useful collaborative model of meaning negotiation and translation decisions, which allows volunteers to play different roles and build a sense of identity within a community of like-minded volunteers [ 59 , 60 ]. However, it presents different shortcomings: the translation aspect of the collaborative work is not integrated into the online platform, as the actual translation is done offline and then uploaded; and the platform does not support multilingual translation. It also fails to leverage the potential of translation technologies such as machine translation (MT) and computer-assisted translation (CAT) tools, which would improve the speed and consistency of the translation work and would be particularly useful when working with non-professionals in a time-bound manner.

2.2 Machine Translation (MT)

Attempts to automate translation have been made for many decades with the purpose of finding an alternative to expensive and time-consuming human translations. The complexity of the translation process (both from a linguistic and a cultural point of view) means that the goal of providing fully automated, high-quality translation has not yet been achieved, despite tremendous recent progress in MT technology. Neural Machine Translation (NMT) is currently regarded as the state of the art in MT technology [ 57 ]. However, older computational architectures such as rule-based machine translation (RBMT) and statistical machine translation (SMT) are still relevant as, for the vast majority of the over 4,000 languages existing in the world, there is insufficient data to train a viable NMT system [ 43 ]. To this day, MT output requires a step of human verification and correction (referred to as post-editing ) to reach the desired quality standards in most contexts [ 57 ]. The first rigorous, controlled analysis of MT post-editing in HCI [ 27 ] found that post-editing leads to reduced delivery time and improved quality, both compared to not using MT and to not performing any post-editing. More recently, Herbig et al [ 31 ] confirmed these findings, and pointed out the increasing need for translators to master post-editing skills now that technological advances are making MT ubiquitous in the field.

The benefits of collaborative workflows are widely reported on, both when working with MT and without MT, particularly for minority languages where there are not enough resources to build efficient MT systems [ 6 ]. Ambati et al [ 6 ] promote a collaborative workflow in which translators are working in phases, and output from earlier phases is enhanced in subsequent ones: a model which can also be applied to MT post-editing. Reporting on the design process of a web-based collaborative system for the production of multilingual health communication materials, Laurenzi et al [ 39 ] propose a workflow combining MT with human post-editing. They find human revision to be "indispensable for ensuring correct and fluent output" [ 39 ] but, recognising the progress made in the MT field since the early 2000s, deem this technology to offer acceptable quality for highly sensitive content in the health domain when used in combination with human post-editing. Reflecting on designing a model for bilingual domain experts who are not trained translators, Laurenzi et al suggest working with chunks of texts (rather than the whole document displayed at once, as is usual translation practice), which most of their participants found comfortable.

2.3 Student volunteerism

The question of why people volunteer has been the focus of a large body of research. Student volunteerism, in particular, has been examined since the late 1980s, especially in the United States context [ 44 , 58 ], and continues to attract the interest of scholars [ 30 , 45 ]. Research found a strong propensity for volunteering among tertiary students [ 16 , 26 ] and recognised the educational value of volunteering in terms of building leadership abilities, social self-confidence, and skills in critical thinking and conflict resolution [ 8 , 54 ].

Studies focusing on students’ motivations found that they volunteer because of a set of interrelated external and internal motivations. This is in line with research on volunteering in general, which found that altruistic and utilitarian motives often coexist [ 18 , 19 ]. Studies with different methodologies (for instance, Handy et al 2010 [ 29 ], which analyses survey data with a Heckman Selection Model and Ordered Logistic Regression Model, and Gage and Thapo 2012 [ 26 ], which applies the Volunteer Functions Inventory developed by Clary et al [ 18 ]) come to similar conclusions: the strongest motives for volunteering relate to one's personal values, the desire to help others, as well as to expand one's knowledge. Altruistic motives also lead to higher participation and retention rates compared to resume-building motives.

Comparable findings emerged from studies looking at what motivates people to translate for free [ 42 , 47 , 50 ]. The predominant motivations were to make information available to speakers of other languages, to support a cause or an organisation, to translate content that volunteers found intellectually stimulating and also to gain more translation experience. Material incentives were found not to be among the main motivators for joining an online translation community [ 47 ].

These findings around volunteers’ motivations are not restricted to student volunteers or to the translation context. Other studies, for instance on environmental volunteerism [ 15 ], found that contributing to a cause (e.g. "helping the environment"), expressing one's personal values and learning were first-tier motivations for volunteering.

Knowing the factors that motivate an individual to volunteer is crucial to recruit and retain volunteers [ 15 , 26 ], which is why these are important considerations to incorporate in the design of a platform that seeks to leverage the contribution of volunteers.

Previous work in HCI around supporting volunteering activities through digital technologies (e.g. [ 12 , 34 ]) is especially useful to understand the design criteria that are likely to increase volunteer engagement, participation, and retention. Kane and Klasnja [ 34 ] underline multiple important factors: matching volunteers with tasks that feel relevant to them; giving the volunteers positive feedback about the impact of their work; providing meaningful social connections; recognising and rewarding work; avoiding information overload; minimising logistical difficulties, such as time management and transportation; and recognising that many people volunteer in their downtime, like "during school breaks". Recognising the constraint of time availability, Bernstein et al [ 12 ] focus on the power of micro-volunteering or crowdsourced volunteering in the space of NGO volunteering. Much like crowdsourcing translation, this leverages the power of large groups of people by dividing a task into small fragments that can be accomplished collectively. In crowdsourced volunteering, "individuals are only required to work on small chunks of tasks during their bits of short free times during the day" [ 12 ]. As discussed for crowdsourcing translation, this model is not free from limitations. However, its core principle is one that can be adapted and enhanced to one's specific requirements by taking into account the lessons learnt from previous studies.

Tertiary institutions with high numbers of students from international or multicultural backgrounds have large bodies of multilingual students who, because of their motivations and skills, represent a promising group of volunteers to help communities access previously untranslated NGO documents. If we were to develop models that leverage these sets of motivations and skills to support students who are not specifically trained in translation, then we would gain access to much larger cohorts of student volunteers to engage in online collaborative translation activities. This would increase our capacity to translate more content for NGOs and their target communities over short periods of time, and to expand the pool of languages in which we are able to offer translations.

3 PLATFORM DESIGN

Drawing on best practice in crowdsourcing translation and acknowledging the values, availability, and expertise of university-based volunteers, we developed the following design requirements for a system that would support this type of translation scenario:

  • Human-in-the loop: Combine the expertise of volunteers with state-of-the-art technology in digital translation (MT and CAT tools) to allow for more meaningful progress and terminological consistency [ 27 , 31 ];
  • Single touch-point: All interactions with the process should be in a single platform, rather than multiple specialist tools, to reduce the learning curve;
  • Time-bound: Afford interaction in short, manageable bursts to fit volunteers’ availability. Post-editing and review Tasks for a full document need to be completed by the end of the burst [ 34 ];
  • Open to a range of expertise and experience: Should offer opportunities for breadth of experience, where volunteers can contribute to different roles at different skill levels;
  • Collaborative environment: Allow for teamwork and interdisciplinary collaboration through collaborative features such as chat, shared glossaries and comments, while fostering a sense of community and individual identity as a translator [ 59 , 60 ];
  • Safe environment: Support the collaborative translation of content that is not confidential or high-risk, suitable for volunteers with no previous translation experience. Provide training, and ensure at least two levels of quality checks.
  • Retain volunteers: Maintain consistent volunteer engagement through incentivisation strategies, such as gamification [ 10 ].

3.1 Infrastructure

Action Translate is implemented as a VueJS Single Page Web Application, backed by a ‘server-less’ Firebase architecture of functions and database. Users login via their institutional Google account, and are whitelisted after they have attended an on-boarding session. Volunteers interact with Tasks, the central construct within the platform. These are short pieces of source text that are to be translated, and together form a larger document. Tasks pass through a number of stages within Action Translate, starting with machine translation, then moving through human post-editing and human review. Tasks are created, edited, adjusted and maintained throughout a translation pipeline closely aligned with codified industry practice (ISO 17100 Translation workflow) and follow 4 key workflow steps: import , post-editing , review and export (Figure 1 ). Users are encouraged to perform two separate levels of quality checks: first as translation post-editors, and then as reviewers checking their peers’ translations.

The language from which one translates is commonly referred to as ‘source language’ in the translation industry, while the language into which one translates is known as the ‘target language’. Within Action Translate, users have a personal profile where they indicate which language pairs (source → target) they are able to contribute to. This profile page presents a breakdown of the numbers of Tasks completed in the various stages, the current score received for completing Tasks, and includes ‘badges’ for a variety of activities such as reaching milestones in numbers of Tasks completed in a particular language. Users can download a PDF certificate of participation which includes this information.

Each user also has access to a team page for each of their language pairs. Users are presented with a text-based chat channel with other members of the language group, and a live-updated shared ‘document’ labelled as a glossary for that language pair.

3.2 Workflow

3.2.1 Importing. The source content preparation process involves dividing the text in the original PDF or doc files into segments, and converting the files to XLIFF 1 (industry standard format for representing translation data) using an open source CAT (Computer Assisted Translation) tool 2 . This tool produces a chunked version of the document as XLIFF in the form of identifiable elements (such as headings, titles, footnotes, paragraphs) that can be reconstructed in a document similar in layout to the original after translation has occurred. This XLIFF file is then uploaded to the Action Translate platform by an administrator, who selects the source and multiple target languages for the content, as well as providing additional metadata such as a link to the original document for context. This produces a Task in Action Translate for each chunk and language pair (e.g. English to Spanish), with each Task given a score related to its complexity (word length). The platform then uses either the Amazon Translate or Google Translate cloud service to create an initial machine translation of each individual Task. Once Tasks are machine-translated, they are available in the platform for users to interact with. The administrator can view the progress of both machine translation and human interaction with Tasks through the dashboard, enabling them to provide additional motivation and support to volunteers throughout a deployment.

3.2.2 Locking Tasks. Users can view the source and target text of any available Task, however they can only interact with a Task by ‘locking’ it. This prevents other users from simultaneously interacting with the Task, thus reducing conflict and duplicated work. Once locked, the team's glossary ‘document’ for the Task's language appears next to the Task on screen for reference whilst translating. If a user has not interacted with the Task within an hour, it automatically unlocks and another user can then continue the Task. Locked Tasks are visible to other users, but cannot be interacted with. Once locked, the user is presented with a text-area pre-populated with the machine translation or latest post-edited version, and edits to this text are continuously saved. Regardless of if the Task is locked, a history of actions on the Task can be viewed, and comments can be posted on the Task at any time. This history includes the names of who performed each action and when they occurred.

3.2.3 Post-Editing and Review. At any time, users can select to focus on post-editing (initial editing of a Task after it is machine-translated) or on review (checking of an already post-edited Task). Tasks for each are displayed in a continuous list, filtered by the current user's declared languages. Tasks ‘move’ between the post-editing list and the review list depending on their current state. In post-editing , the user has the option of submitting their edits for review, or to mark the Task as incomplete, which unlocks it and provides them with the option of leaving a message to indicate why they did not complete it. In review , the user has the option of rejecting the post-edited version, which allows them to leave feedback or notes for change and returns the Task back to the post-editing state, augmented with the note. They can also approve the post-edited version, making any minor adjustments they deem necessary. These multiple stages of post-editing and review are designed to create a safe environment where beginner translators feel supported in their work and reassured that their work is going to be verified by more experienced volunteers before submission. At the same time, they also provide more experienced volunteers with agency, giving them the possibility of directly approving a post-edited Task (in the review step) without sending it back to the original post-editor. To encourage the completion of the entire translation process, users only receive a score when a Task they have post-edited is successfully reviewed, or for performing a successful review of a Task. Once approved, the Task is complete and is removed from all lists.

3.2.4 Exporting. Once a translation activity has been completed, and enough Tasks have been translated and reviewed to the administrator's satisfaction, they can export each source-target language pair as a new XLIFF file. This file is then loaded back into the CAT tool and is used to reconstruct the layout of the original document, but in each target language. As is the case with professional workflows, additional work is required within the CAT tool to correct inconsistencies in layout, spacing and other minor visual issues before the new translated document can be published.

4 DEPLOYMENT CONTEXT

In July 2022, we deployed the Action Translate platform as a student volunteer translation program at our tertiary institution in Australia. The program ran over three weeks during a semester break, so that students had more free time to devote to volunteering. Students were tasked with translating 19 publicly available written documents (all in English) published by the Antarctic Treaty Secretariat (ATS). The documents were selected with permission from the organisation with which our institution had previously collaborated. The ATS is an international organisation supporting the 54 signatory countries of the Antarctic Treaty, which regulates international relations in Antarctica, banning military activity and preserving the continent for scientific research. The source content included three key ATS documents: the original text of the Antarctic Treaty, the Protocol on environmental protection to the Antarctic Treaty, and the general guidelines for visitors to the Antarctic. We also selected 14 research reports presented at the XLIV Antarctic Treaty Consultative Meeting, held in Berlin in June 2022. These research reports were short documents of approximately 5 pages each on topics such as species conservation, marine pollution, and Antarctic research expeditions. In the content to be translated there were also two longer documents: a 92-page report on climate change, and a 26-page report on environmental contamination.

4.1 Content Preparation

Based on previous experience with the capability of student volunteer translators, we prepared approximately 5 pages of content per registered volunteer (assuming each volunteer would only engage with a single language). For example, since there were 16 registered volunteers for Bahasa Indonesia, we prepared 5 × 16 pages of text using the CAT tool and imported it in batches of 5 pages (each with a slightly later deadline) into Action Translate for translation from English to Bahasa Indonesia.

Documents that were over 5 pages were split into shorter sub-documents of approximately 5 pages each before being fed into the CAT tool. This ensured that deadlines for 5-page batches could be managed so that sequential sections of a document were completed before moving onto further sections.

Due to the nature of the source documents, each Task varied in length from a single word to a few lines (as produced by the CAT tool) and was given a score based on its length. This equated to approximately 100 Tasks being imported for each volunteer.

4.2 Recruitment

We specifically advertised the program university-wide using separate promotional material for translation students and non-translation students. For the former group, advertising focused more on the translation experience ("Ready to take on real-world translation projects?"), whereas advertising for the latter group put the emphasis on the cause ("This winter break, help protect Antarctica"). We received 158 applications, of which 117 were postgraduates (PG) and 41 undergraduates (UG).

Students who submitted an application were contacted via email and invited to attend a 1-hour on-boarding session via Zoom, where they had the opportunity to ask questions, meet the other volunteers and the team running the program, and familiarise themselves with the program and the platform. Since the experience was open to students with no translation training, we provided training around basic translation concepts, theories and methods in the form of videos created for this purpose by PhD students in Translation Studies and made available on our platform.

After on-boarding, 90 students (70 PG, 20 UG) remained active participants throughout the three-week deployment. As seen in Figure 3 , volunteers came from a range of faculties. Overall, they indicated 16 languages in which they were able to translate: Arabic (ar, 4 active participants), Chinese Simplified (zh, 45), Chinese Traditional - Taiwan (zh-TW, 11), French (fr, 4), German (de, 4), Greek (el, 1), Hindi (hi, 2), Indonesian (id, 4), Japanese (ja, 4), Korean (ko, 1), Malay (ms, 1), Russian (ru, 2), Sinhala (si, 3), Spanish (es, 2), Thai (th, 3), and Vietnamese (vi, 3). Students were given the possibility to volunteer in more than one language by adding multiple language combinations to their platform profiles, however most volunteers only worked with a single target language.

The selected languages were not only languages studied at school or university, but also family languages or heritage languages that are rarely studied in educational contexts, proving the translation potential of a highly multilingual student body.

4.3 Data Collection

During the on-boarding session at the start of the deployment, students were sent a link to an explanatory statement and consent form (on Google Forms) informing them of the study being conducted to evaluate the program. Students were asked to consent (or not) for their log data to be collected from the Action Translate platform and to participate in an optional audio-recorded focus group with other student volunteers. Students were informed about the protocols put in place to securely store their data, about the outputs where the anonymised research data might be published and presented, and about the voluntary nature of their participation in the project, from which they could decide to withdraw at any time until their names were removed from the data and the data analysed. Participants who gave their consent were invited to attend one of 5 small online focus groups in the week after the deployment, with attendance incentivised with a $ 10USD online voucher. Twelve participants accepted.

To understand the role of this type of experience in the training landscape, we also conducted semi-structured online interviews with 5 Translation Studies academics from within the university with both teaching and industry experience. Recruitment for these interviews was not incentivised and relied on personal contacts. The data was not included in the data set for this paper but served to understand the Action Translate program in the context of the current translation industry practices.

The focus groups and interviews, which were audio-recorded and transcribed by the researcher, together formed a corpus of data that was analysed through a thematic analysis procedure following the replicable, six-step Braun and Clarke approach [ 14 ]. The lead researcher used deductive coding to create and apply initial codes to the data. Codes were then reviewed by the whole research team, with themes and their codes being amended several times before being finalised. In addition, we collected extensive log data from the platform, including timestamped logs of all interactions (such as ‘reject’, ‘approve’) with Tasks, comments and glossaries. Basic filtering and descriptive statistics were employed to visualise these data, which we used to contextualise the qualitative findings.

5 FINDINGS

5.1 Task Completion Summary

During the three-week deployment, 90 volunteers from 16 different languages groups interacted with Tasks (4 volunteers in more than one language). Volunteers were not equally distributed across these languages. As such, it is difficult to make generalised statements about participant interaction. We can, however, highlight language groups of particular note. Volunteers in the Indonesian (id) group (all 4 registrations were active) interacted with all their Tasks. All registered volunteers for Vietnamese (vi) and Arabic (ar) (3 and 4 respectively) were also active and interacted with 86.67% and 82.67% of Tasks respectively. The Chinese Simplified language group (zh), on the other hand, had a high discrepancy between registrations and active participants (75 registered, 45 active), but the large size of the team meant that they were still able to interact with all assigned Tasks. The Sinhala (si) group represented an exception, with no discrepancy in volunteer numbers (3 registered and active), but only 28.10% of Tasks that were engaged with.

Since Tasks were loaded before the deployment at a volume commensurate to the number of registered participants, rather than active participants, it is unhelpful to directly compare the total Task progress of individual languages over this window. Instead, we can use the rate of translation that occurred (Tasks per active volunteer) and the number of completed documents relative to active volunteers to compare progress, as reported in Figure 4 .

The source text consisted of multiple, smaller, self-contained documents of around 5 pages (each page of source text approximates to 20 Tasks), therefore any complete multiple of 100 Tasks (5 pages) roughly equates to a completed source document.

We can observe some notable features of Figure 4 : only languages to the right of Japanese (ja), that is 10 languages out of 16, completed enough pages to have at least a single complete document (i.e. >5). However, this cut-off does not seem to have a relationship to the number of people, and seems tied to motivation and engagement rather than scale of participants. For some languages, participants were engaging equally in post-editing and review (el, ms, th, ar and id), but in others there is a clear discrepancy with fewer people engaging in review (e.g. hi, vi and zh-TW) or a small number of individuals who undertook huge numbers of Tasks (ms, hi), completing more than 100 and 200 Tasks per person respectively.

5.2 Recruitment and motivations

In line with findings in literature on student volunteerism [ 29 , 45 ], and volunteerism in general [ 18 ], students were motivated by a range of both external and internal factors to volunteer for Action Translate. As discussed in section 4.2, the Action Translate program was open university-wide, and attracted interest from students from a wide variety of discipline backgrounds. While Translation Studies students were specifically targeted with an ad-hoc advertising campaign, thinking that they would be the cohort naturally interested in this experience, they represented only 12.7% of the total registrations. Interest in translation was still one of the most reported motivating factors for joining Action Translate. However, the program seemed to mainly attract volunteers with minimal or no previous experience in translation seeking to explore their interest further or develop a new skill [ 26 ]. When asked to self-evaluate their ability to translate from English into their target language on a scale from 1 to 5 on the registration form, 50% of volunteers indicated a 4, with 25.3% and 22.2% indicating 5 and 3 respectively. These figures suggest a good to excellent ability to translate into the target language, which is a significant finding when contrasted with the fact that the vast majority of volunteers (84.8%) reported having no certifications or qualifications in translation. One participant (UG in Engineering, Korean) claimed that Action Translate acted as a gateway to translating: a " jump start " to getting exposed to the practicalities of real-world translation and learning by doing. As a focus group participant noted,

"It gave me a new insight about how the translator works in real life and this is a really good start." (PG in Education, Chinese Simplified)

However, another participant added that this may not apply to everyone:

"...for someone with more experience, it might not be as helpful in developing their skills." (UG in Science, Sinhala)

Gaining work-integrated learning (WIL) hours by participating in the program, which we presented as a major incentive during our recruitment campaign, proved to be less motivational than anticipated. Indeed, only 32.3% of registered students required evidence of translation hours as part of their course of study.

While having a volunteering experience to add to their CV was a motivating factor particularly for some international students looking to improve their career prospects in the local Australian context, such utilitarian reasons were reported far less often than more altruistic motives for joining the program. The most commonly reported motivations were the will to contribute to what the volunteers perceived as a worthy cause, raise awareness around this cause, and make a difference.

"I liked how we had to focus on the Treaty for Antarctica, because environment is something that triggers me as a person a lot. (...) I think that it motivated me to go on and not think, okay, I'm not gonna do that or not complete it." (PG in Arts, Greek)

In this sense, the motivations of Action Translate participants were similar to those of volunteers in other contexts, such as environmental volunteers for whom making a difference by helping the environment was found to be the main motivator [ 15 ].

Participants reported that a key motivation was to learn a new skill. This is consistent with previous research findings around student volunteerism [ 26 ] as well as around online collaborative translation practices [ 60 ], which recognise learning, particularly learning from interactions with more experienced volunteers, as a main motivating factor to engage in online collaborative translation. Action Translate participants reported learning new words, including specific terminology about the subject matter in their native language as an outcome of the program. They also shared that this experience helped them to considerably expand their previous knowledge around Antarctica, for instance learning about the tourism guidelines to visit the continent.

5.3 Maintaining participant engagement

The volume of completed post-editing Tasks and completed review Tasks demonstrate that participant engagement was maintained throughout the full three weeks of the deployment period (Figure 5 ). As expected, initial engagement was with post-editing Tasks, with this switching to the review process as time progressed and post-editing Tasks were completed. The data also suggests that increases in Task completion rates correlate directly with encouragement and reminder emails sent by the research team. In particular, review Task completions spiked in the third week of the program, when participants were invited to review the segments already post-edited by their fellow volunteers in order to finalise the translations by the deadline. Several volunteers even enquired about whether they could keep working on the remaining Tasks after the end date of the program, and wanted to know how they could join future iterations of the program.

The design in short bursts seems to support student engagement and a sustainable program life cycle. It provides a manageable time frame within which to complete the Tasks, giving a good sense of progress to volunteers and avoiding volunteer dispersion, which is a common experience in online volunteer translation communities [ 60 ]. The gamified motivational tools (such as badges for the first and twentieth translation or review completed, and the numerical score assigned to each Task), which we specifically included in the platform design to maximise volunteer retention, were appreciated by the volunteers:

"I really liked the gamified aspect with the points, I thought that was really cool (...) It felt like a game. It felt good, I didn't want to stop sometimes." (UG in Science, Sinhala)

Student volunteers liked the gamified aspect of the platform not out of a sense of competition with other volunteers, but more out of a sense of self-accomplishment that they got from receiving a score or a badge for their achievements:

"I like the ‘stamp’ for the first translation. I think you guys can add more. It's a kind of encouragement (...) I also like the scores. Maybe I'm not really interested in others, but I'm interested in myself." (PG in Arts, Chinese Simplified)

In line with the volunteering spirit of the deployment, there was no mandated minimum requirement for participation. Each volunteer interacted on average with 122 Tasks over the three week deployment, but there was a clear disparity in interaction. While most participants completed 1-50 Tasks each, some volunteers completed considerably more, the highest being 2516. Post-editing rates were higher than review rates. This could be a direct response to the gamified approach, with a higher score (1/10th of the post-editing Tasks) placed on them.

Participants reported that stricter requirements may have helped motivate them and helped with time management, while also increasing the sense of authenticity of the program:

"I feel like because there wasn't a hard requirement (...), sometimes it's hard to gauge how much input we should be putting in. I guess, because it's a volunteer work, that's what it is. But in that sense, because of its flexibility, I guess it doesn't feel [authentic]." (PG in Business and Economics, Chinese Simplified)

The lack of hard requirements from the research team led some of the most committed volunteers to set their own goals for self-motivation and self-driven project management:

"I told myself ‘translation one hour per day, five days a week’. That motivated me a lot." (PG in Arts, Chinese Simplified)

5.4 Translation workflow

On average, it took 7 days between a volunteer post-editing a Task and another volunteer reviewing this edit. This is a relatively short timeframe when compared with typical crowdsourcing translation engagements involving unpaid participants, which often present long turnaround times and no guarantee of completion [ 13 ].

Participants reported positive experiences working with machine translation. It was a first-time experience for many of them, even for Translation students, as machine translation is still rarely included in the syllabus of Translation Studies programs [ 23 ]. Students found the machine translations to be of good quality and, while they recognised the importance of the human post-editing, they believed machine translation was a support tool that helped with the speed of the translation process:

"It was really helpful because (...) you had already a work in front of you. So you were just amending the errors (...) you didn't have to waste your time." (PG in Arts, Greek)

Volunteers also commented on the fact that machine translation helped them maintaining terminological and stylistic consistency:

"People got different language habits and it is like [machine translation] offers you a thinking way and you can just change some of it." (PG in Arts, Chinese Simplified)

Interestingly, the high quality of the machine translation and the double human verification process seemed to negate the necessity for the ‘rejection’ of Tasks, which was only used 40 times across all languages in the deployment, and not at all for 11 out of 16 languages. Predictably, the highest number of rejections (24) was for Chinese Simplified, due to the large size of the group. The notable exception was the Japanese language group, where 11.76% of Tasks were rejected (13 rejected Tasks, of which 3 were rejected more than once) and sent back to post-editing before moving on to the final review step. We posit that this could be due to a few reasons: the ambiguity and subjectivity of English -> Japanese translation; the need to find translations that were commonly understood; and it is possible that the cohort of Japanese volunteers strove for higher quality than other language teams as they were all Translation students, instead of being from diverse discipline backgrounds.

Generally, the two-step process of human verification of the machine translation gave students volunteers who were not experienced in translation a sense of confidence, knowing that their post-edited translation was going to be checked by another person before being finalised:

"If you have any question or some review part you are not really sure if it's correct or accurate, just leave it to other people and they will definitely help you to improve your work. And that made me have more confidence about my job." (PG in IT, Chinese Simplified)

This aspect of the design allowed us to support volunteers with different levels of translation experience who could take on different roles (post-editing vs review), depending on what they felt comfortable with.

The segmentation of the source content received mixed reviews. Some volunteers liked working with very small chunks of text as it was "a lot less overwhelming" (UG in Engineering, Korean). Others found the lack of Task context to be inconvenient, particularly regarding the different sentence structure between English and their target language:

"Sometimes the sections were separated automatically and so it makes sense in English, but when you break it off, it is just weird in Chinese." (PG in Business and Economics, Chinese Simplified)

Syntactical differences between languages meant that certain machine translated segments were nonsensical on their own, and made it necessary to retrieve the previous or following segment, which the interface did not afford in a straightforward manner (although an external document with the whole source text was available, see Figure 1 ).

Participants also identified that individual progress was hard to track since Tasks disappeared from the interface once complete, which, when compounded with the ‘infinite scroll’ of the Task list, led to uncertainty about progress and poor time-management.

5.5 Communication and collaboration

The platform was purposefully designed for collaboration, with functionalities such as the chat, the shared glossaries that students could use to establish the translation of certain terms, and the possibility of leaving comments on someone's translation or review. However, we observed that, while students found these collaborative functions useful in principle, they did not use them as much as anticipated. Students attempted communication and collaboration with their peers, but this was rarely successful, especially in groups that had very small numbers of volunteers. The largest language group, Chinese Simplified, was the only one to use the ability to leave comments against a specific Task, although not extensively, with only 26 comments left.

The team chat saw slightly more participation, with 5 groups making some limited use of the feature. Volunteers used this chat channel both to establish interpersonal relations with their peers (introduce themselves to the group, their area of study, their country of origin, etc) and to ask the group for advice about translation Tasks. However, often messages received no answer or the answer was delayed leaving participants with a sense of lack of team (" No team :( ", Spanish volunteer; " I thought I was alone! ", Sinhala volunteer). The lag in communication due to volunteers not being online at the same time caused interruptions to the workflow, made the volunteers feel isolated, and detracted from the authenticity of the experience:

"We're accessing the website at different times. Messages get delayed a lot and so it interrupts the workflow (...) Instead of feeling like we're really working as a team, I feel like a lot of time it's very lonely. And so it didn't really feel like we were really working on something other than a school project." (PG in Arts, Chinese Simplified).

Team glossaries were the most used collaborative feature, with 13 out of 16 language groups utilising the glossary function to some extent. Chinese Simplified (299 words), Japanese (71 words), and Indonesian (58 words) were the groups that used the shared glossary the most.

This data is supported by participants, especially those working in smaller language groups, who reported a lack of communication and cooperation which impacted negatively on their experience.

"I didn't see much cooperation. I suggested some changes and I didn't get any feedback." (PG in Arts, French).

The main issues were not being aware of who the other team members were, and the lack of synchronous interaction:

"I feel like one of the problem is that everyone is working in very different hours (...). We cannot effectively communicate with each other, if we are running into problems and we cannot solve that instantly, then we just kind of get frustrated and just move on." (UG in Arts, Chinese Traditional).

Volunteers in the Chinese Simplified language group reported the most positive experience with collaborative functions, which we posit is due to the size of the group enabling dialogue:

"The group annotation really helped me save a lot of time because a lot of people had already put some meaning of it. This kind of function makes you don't feel alone, and just feel there are a lot of people working with you at the same time." (PG in IT, Chinese Simplified).

However, despite the general positive remarks, even some volunteers in this group pointed out aspects where the platform fell short in delivering the collaborative experience that they were hoping for.

6 DISCUSSION

6.1 Designing for volunteer-centred translation

We argue that moving towards a volunteer-centred design that gives agency and choice to the volunteers over the source content is an effective strategy to maximise volunteer retainment when internal motivations prevail over external ones. In the case of Action Translate, volunteers eschewed other incentives (such as WIL hours), wishing to participate in a program that aligned with their values and provided the opportunity to contribute to a worthy cause whilst learning new skills. Of course, we must acknowledge that the materials used in the recruitment campaigns might have led to some bias, attracting students with particular motivations.

In a volunteer-centred model, students are involved from the beginning of the process rather than just in the post-editing and review components. They take on communication and procedural roles: they suggest NGOs to partner with that align with their values; they liaise with these partner organisations to select source content; they work with CAT tools to prepare the source content for machine translation; they verify the final document, including adjusting the graphic layout that the CAT tool reconstructed before delivering the document to the organisation. Working for a real-world cause means that the participation in Action Translate is not only an intellectual exercise with the goal of gaining quantifiable experience useful for one's studies or career, but also a high-stake activity where having a completed end product to deliver to an external partner by a certain deadline is a key motivator. Since fit-for-purpose translation [ 13 ] is the required standard for most NGOs, leveraging machine translation in a time-bounded process that is double-verified by a human can be an effective and resource conservative method of translating content. By flipping the traditional paradigm of ‘NGOs seeking translators’ into one where groups of volunteers offer ‘translation capacity’, volunteers can have more agency in both the decision making process (what jobs are taken on), and in the practical skills required to deliver a project (the additional client-facing and document sourcing roles).

We suggest then that, to support sustainable and meaningful engagement between volunteers and NGOs in the translation sector, we should be designing for increased volunteer agency in all areas of the translation pipeline, giving them direct ownership and responsibility over job preparation and completion.

6.2 Supporting meaningful engagement through accountability and visibility

Contrary to our initial assumptions, our findings do not support that the program should afford as much freedom in engagement and interaction for volunteers as possible (i.e. few obligations and deadlines). In fact, volunteers said that they felt more engaged and perceived the experience as more authentic when they were held accountable for the work that they were called to perform. This accountability manifests at three levels within Action Translate: the individual, the team, and the external organisation.

In line with gamification literature, which shows the benefits of gamification on engagement and participation in various contexts [ 10 , 17 , 37 ], volunteers were motivated to keep active by the gamified, live indication of their progress and gratification of completion. This type of granular progress reporting is only possible through the micro-Task text segmentation employed by Action Translate, although this model introduces a trade-off in that Task context is harder to ascertain within the source document. The additional implementation of team progress information for each document —for instance, a countdown of the remaining untranslated Tasks for each language combination— would increase the incentive for volunteers who lack this information when completed Tasks ‘disappear’. Such visibility should also manifest at the team level, in which individuals could compare their team's progress against other teams, allowing them to gauge the level of commitment required.

Since in this deployment the external organisation had no connection to the process other than receiving the completed document, no additional milestones were presented to volunteers. However, our findings suggest that implementing additional sub-deployment deadlines would motivate and increase team engagement, support time management, and increase the likelihood of translations being completed.

As discussed above, in a volunteer-centred model, key support roles could be carried out by volunteers, who might take on varying levels of responsibility in multiple roles. The introduction of a project manager would be particularly paramount in stimulating a sense of accountability within the team.

6.3 Designing for heterogeneity through community

As noted through our deployment, a major design challenge is the potentially diverse patterns and make-up of teams, both from an experience and an engagement level perspective. While acknowledging the diverse backgrounds of volunteers, we designed Action Translate to provide a homogeneous experience for all participants. However, the heterogeneous make-up of the language teams (resulting from multi-layered and unpredictable variables in recruitment) meant that the experience of individuals and teams alike varied greatly, and a variety of group dynamics emerged.

Although designed as a collaborative environment (from a homogeneous perspective), Action Translate did not fully achieve its aim to support volunteer collegiality due to the intrinsic diversity of the cohort. We observed interesting patterns of behaviour among different groups (such as a high level of rejections and use of the glossary in the Japanese group, and the wider use of the chat feature by the Thai group), but it is hard to determine the underlying factors that led to these behaviours other than noting that decision-making processes and communication styles can vary greatly depending on participants’ cultural backgrounds. However, we argue that rather than design for a shared set of experiences, we should be designing specifically to support varied configurations and patterns of behaviour across teams. As the composition of cohorts who will join the program cannot be predicted in advance without stringent recruitment criteria, we need to support different (and flexible) modes of operation for different team configurations. The value of building community identity through rich and flexible communication strategies to support flexible group dynamics is well documented in software engineering, through which models such as KANBAN and SCRUM have emerged [ 1 , 2 , 4 ].

As identified by Barker et al., supporting fluid and flexible interaction is an essential component of collaborative activities aimed at acquiring knowledge [ 11 ], however there are trade-offs between synchronous and asynchronous modes of interaction [ 36 ]. Our findings suggest volunteers valued the flexibility afforded by working at any time and from any location, providing them time to reflect and research without external pressure. However, volunteers also noted the value of receiving timely feedback when they were inside the platform, fostering a sense of community and identity within the team. We posit then that a mix of these communication modes is key to supporting flexible team working and foster collegiality and collaborative work in a program where participants volunteer around other commitments.

Learning from these approaches, online collaborative translation platforms should consider explicit increase of intra-team visibility, where volunteers can view the collective composition of their team, the experience level of their team members and what roles they are willing to fulfil. Teams could then negotiate common processes and roles. With this visibility in place both for single instances of a volunteering program and across multiple deployments, teams would maintain continuity and be able to assign mentoring roles to more ‘experienced’ members, whilst junior members would know where and how to seek informal support. Key to building these relationships is the trust that is fostered between members of the team with the knowledge that all translations are checked by a team member during the review process. This creates a ‘safe space’, where volunteers can ask for help without repercussion and feel supported in their volunteering experience.

6.4 Model Transferability

In the current research around Action Translate we have so far focused on a single cohort of volunteers represented by tertiary students from the same institution working on a well-defined task: supporting an international not-for-profit organisation in making its content accessible in multiple languages other than English. The fact that our findings around student participation are consistent with the motivations that prompt volunteers outside the university context is an indication of the model's transferability to different cohorts of volunteers in other settings. As an example, environmental volunteers who speak multiple languages could use such a platform for a translation initiative to support a global environmental cause, or humanitarian organisations could adopt it to translate content in the languages of the countries where they operate, providing basic translation training to in-country volunteers who speak the local idioms. At this stage, our research has not looked at how the work of Action Translate volunteers contributes to the improvement of the machine translation algorithms used by the platform, nor at the ethical implication of this. However, it needs to be acknowledged that working with machine translation engines developed by tech corporations, even if for educational and philanthropic purposes, could potentially mean creating profitable value for these companies through the unpaid work of volunteers. Future iterations should take this into account as a core part of their design and reflect on the ethical questions posed by translation volunteerism. As Piroth and Baker suggest [ 52 ], an ideal model would be conceived as a community-driven initiative, rather than as a corporate-run one, leveraging the strenghts of crowdsourcing to benefit both the communities who will gain access to the translations and the volunteer translators.

7 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we provided suggestions for improving the design of an online collaborative translation platform specifically built to support meaningful student volunteering of translation for global causes. We recommend a volunteer-centred approach, revolving around volunteer agency, accountability and collaboration to create sustainable volunteering platforms that leverage the multilingual skills and the volunteering motivations of university students. We argue this can be done by: affording volunteers agency throughout the translation pipeline; supporting volunteer engagement and interaction through the inclusion of strong accountability markers and collective visibility; and catering for different team configurations and behaviour patterns to strengthen opportunities for collaboration. Deploying online collaborative translation platforms in university contexts presents both pedagogical benefits for the students engaging in the volunteering program and positive social implications, democratising the access to NGO documentation published solely in English or other major languages. Our research opens the door to explore the value of data generated through such platforms to inform the continual development of the underlying machine translation infrastructure. Moreover, the Action Translate pipeline could be used to collect automatic post-editing (APE) training data to enable further study on multilingual APE and domain-specific APE. Moving forwards, we believe that these recommendations are a solid foundation on which to design volunteer translation pipelines, and we hope that they will inform the scaling up of such online collaborative translation platforms not only in more university contexts, but also in other contexts where multilingual volunteers could benefit from this opportunity in terms of professional development and up-skilling in the area of translation.
