Generating Haptic Motion Effects for Multiple Articulated Bodies for Improved 4D Experiences: A Camera Space Approach

CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Motion effects are indispensable for improving 4D experiences in highly interactive applications, such as amusement parks, 4D theaters, and virtual reality games. Their recent emergence calls for effective algorithms generating motion effects synchronized with audiovisual content. This paper presents an automatic algorithm for synthesizing the object-based motion effects that express the movements of multiple articulated bodies inclusively when the objects’ motion trajectories are available in the 3D camera space. By taking the visual velocities and sizes of all object parts, our method computes a motion proxy that represents the objects’ movements by one point and converts the motion proxy to a motion command through a motion cueing algorithm. The motion proxy is determined by linearly combining the velocities, and its best combination was selected from several candidates by user studies. The results of user studies indicate that our algorithm can produce compelling object-based motion effects that enhance the multisensory experience.

1 INTRODUCTION

Viewing multimedia content satisfies our eyes and ears. However, we want more: as a new form of media, mulsemedia (multiple sensorial media) leverages more than the two senses of vision and audition to expand our sensory experiences [ 13 , 48 ]. Mulsemedia has already permeated our daily lives. In arcades, a racing game is equipped with a motion platform simulating the movement of a vehicle [ 77 ]. Also gaining popularity are 4D theaters, where the audience's viewing experience is enhanced with various sensory effects, such as motion, vibration, scent, and wind, in synchrony with audiovisual content [ 75 ]. Vive [ 51 ] and Meta [ 72 ], the major companies for virtual reality (VR) headsets, are conducting intensive research to provide improved haptic feedback through handheld controllers during interaction in VR.

Mulsemedia content is delivered to us through three stages: production, distribution, and rendering [ 13 , 16 ]. At present, a critical bottleneck is in the production of sensory effects because it still relies on manual authoring. This costly process prevents a faster and wider dissemination of mulsemedia content. For facilitation, a few research groups have proposed algorithms to automate sensory effect production based on audiovisual content [ 10 , 22 , 41 , 42 , 45 , 80 ]. Our work described in this paper is in line with such endeavors, with an emphasis on motion effects .

According to the taxonomy of 4D effects established by Lee et al. [ 41 ], motion effects used in 4D films can be classified into four classes: camera-based, object-based, vibration and impact, and context, based on the audiovisual grounds used for the design. Here, an object-based motion effect refers to the movement of a motion chair that applies physical movement to the viewer's body to emphasize the movement of an object of interest, e.g., the fighting Captain America (Fig. 1 ). Among the four classes of motion effects, object-based motion effects are the most frequently used in 4D films. Lee et al. [ 42 ] presented the first algorithm that automatically generates an object-based motion effect by tracking the object's motion projected onto the 2D screen space and converting the 2D motion trajectory to a chair motion. This viewer-oriented method can be used for any video, but it requires a robust object tracking algorithm. Recently, Han et al. [ 22 ] proposed a new technique that emphasizes motion in the 3D camera space. This camera space approach has an advantage over the screen space methods in completeness. For example, a dancer's motion spinning at one position can lead to a rotational motion effect if the motion is expressed in the camera space. However, when projected to screen space, the dancer's 2D position is stationary and its rotation is hardly identified, resulting in no motion effect. Han et al. [ 22 ]’s method centers around a new concept of motion proxy . For an object moving in the 3D camera space, a motion proxy abstracts both the object's translational and rotational motion into one point. A motion effect is computed by expressing the motion proxy's movement with a motion chair. Here, the motion proxy is analogous to the virtual proxy—a standard approach in haptic rendering of rigid objects using a force-feedback device [ 57 , 61 ].

Han et al. [ 22 ]’s algorithm is effective in generating the motion effects of a single rigid body moving in the 3D camera space. In this work, we extend their concept of motion proxy to articulated bodies 1 by proposing a motion synthesis algorithm to emphasize the movements of articulated bodies. Note that depending on applications, camera space motion trajectories are either readily available, e.g., in computer-generated scenes and VR games, or need to be estimated from the image sequences, e.g., in camera-shoot films, using computer vision algorithms. Independent of this issue, we seek effective rendering algorithms of articulated body motion when the motion trajectories are given in the 3D camera space.

While commercial motion platforms in 4D theaters generally support limited DoF motions of roll, pitch, and heave (Fig. 2 ), articulated bodies require a very high degree of freedom (DoF) for motion description. Therefore, we compress such complex information into a single point of motion proxy (Section 3 ). We then present a few methods to compute the motion proxy for a single articulated body (Section 4 ) and multiple articulated bodies (Section 6 ). We generate an appropriate motion command by feeding the motion proxy to the standard motion cueing algorithm (MCA) using model predictive control (MPC) [ 6 ]. In each step of the algorithm developments, we compare the benefits of a few alternatives in terms of viewing experience by a user study (Section 5 and 7 ). Finally, we summarize the lessons learned into design guidelines for the object-based motion effects of articulated bodies (Section 8 ). This is followed by current limitations (Section 9 ) and conclusions (Section 10 ).

The major contributions of our research are with (1) the first proposal of automatic rendering algorithms of object-based motion effects that emphasize the dynamic motions of articulated bodies and (2) the shared design guidelines earned through the research. These outcomes are expected to expedite and benefit the production of 4D multisensory effects.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Authoring Multisensory Effects

We can present immersive experiences by providing various sensory effects together with audiovisual content, such as motion [ 8 , 20 , 31 , 55 ], vibration [ 9 , 28 ], heat [ 58 ], scent [ 5 , 56 ], and wind [ 53 , 60 ]. The first multisensory system was invented in the 1960s. Sensorama simulator [ 76 ], developed by Heilig, created the experience of riding a motorcycle in New York using a stereoscopic color display, a stereo sound system, fans, odor emitters, and a motion chair. Since then, mulsemedia systems have been steadily developed and applied to various domains, such as broadcasting [ 34 , 47 ], theaters [ 23 , 24 ], museums [ 63 , 64 ], virtual reality [ 19 , 33 , 38 , 50 , 59 , 74 ], and interactive environments [ 62 ].

Researchers have designed novel stimulation methods to deliver multisensory experiences, with focused efforts on haptics. For example, Danieau et al. [ 15 ] demonstrated that stimulating the user's head and arms using low-cost force-feedback devices can elicit convincing walking sensations, similarly to riding a motion platform. Danieau and colleagues [ 14 ] also introduced the concept of haptic cinematography and the associated taxonomy while showcasing a few haptic emphasis techniques for such films. Sra et al. [ 70 ] presented a device providing proprioceptive feedback using galvanic vestibular stimulation to replace an expensive motion simulator.

In general, mulsemedia content is streamed to users through three stages: (1) production, (2) distribution, and (3) rendering [ 13 , 16 ]. First, sensory effects are created in synchrony with audiovisual content (production). Second, the sensory effects are formalized into the data to be synchronized, stored, and transmitted with the audiovisual media (distribution). Finally, the sensory effects are rendered through various multisensory devices to users (rendering).

Among the three, the production stage is the biggest bottleneck preventing the proliferation of mulsemedia. In industry, designers use in-house authoring programs to create multisensory effects [ 35 , 36 , 47 , 73 ]. These tools have similar interfaces using multiple timelines, one for each sensory effect. While useful, the designers must annotate all video segments that will accompany multisensory effects. To reduce such efforts, there have been attempts to detect video segments for which sensory effects will be provided and determine the corresponding effect types using the neural networks trained on audiovisual information [ 1 , 10 , 17 , 69 , 83 ]. However, the designers still need to make specific sensory effects manually, which leaves the design process extremely time-consuming. Another useful asset is an effect library, which stores frequently-used sensory effects and enables the designers to load and reuse them whenever necessary [ 29 , 65 , 79 ]. Such effect libraries are useful and can speed up the authoring and production in some cases, e.g., vibration effects conveying the sensations of walking, gunfire, and vibration.

2.2 Automatic Generation of 4D Effects

In the past decade, we have seen increasing research interests in the algorithmic generation of 4D effects from existing audiovisual data, which is essential to accelerate the spread of mulsemedia and related VR applications. This section describes the previous research outcomes mainly pertinent to motion effects. Motion effects have a central role among the various 4D sensory effects [ 41 ].

Shin et al. [ 68 ] were the first to propose a synthesis framework for motion effects that emphasize camera motion for point-of-view (POV) shots, which are named camera-based motion effects in [ 41 ]. The camera trajectory is estimated from sequential images and then used to compute the command to a motion chair using a washout filter. Lee et al. [ 41 ] developed much more efficient video-based generation algorithms of camera-based motion effects with quasi-real time performance and compelling viewer experiences. These algorithms were adapted by Seo et al. [ 66 ] to substitute camera-based motion effects with vibrotactile effects as a much less expensive alternative. Lim et al. [ 46 ] proposed an algorithm for styling camera-based motion effects using a texture image to provide more realistic sensations of vehicle riding, e.g., on a bumpy road.

As for object-based motion effects, the first approach was presented by Lee et al. [ 42 ], where 2-DoF motion effects (roll and pitch in Fig. 2 ) are converted from the 2D trajectory of the object motion projected to the screen. An object of interest is tracked by a computer vision algorithm, and its 2D screen velocity is fed to a washout filter. This strategy of viewer-centered rendering matches the chair's motion to the shift of the audience's visual attention, which results in plausible motion effects for many scenes. Goh et al. [ 18 ] used saliency estimation with the optical flow to generate motion effects for visually salient objects. In Zhao et al. [ 82 ], a force vector is extracted from the velocity of an object of interest tracked in the image. It is rendered using a haptic glove that provides force feedback. These algorithms all operate on the screen space, where the rotation of an object may not be disclosed clearly.

As an alternative, Han et al. [ 22 ] focused on preserving the full 6-DoF motion of an object, both translation and rotation, observed in the 3D camera space into a motion effect. The authors proposed a new concept, motion proxy, to express the 6-DoF object motion by a standard 3-DoF motion chair moving in the roll, pitch, and heave directions (Fig. 2 ). Their algorithm also modulates the amplitude of a motion effect in accordance with the object's visual size. This approach improves viewing experiences compared to the image space-based methods [ 22 ]. All the aforementioned generation algorithms for object-based motion effects are applicable only to a single rigid body.

Additionally, Lee and Choi [ 40 ] proposed a sound-to-vibration translation algorithm for selective audio-tactile feedback (e.g., not responding to background music) by relating perceptual loudness and roughness between sound and touch. Later, this algorithm was adapted to present motion effects that accentuate impact and collision events [ 41 ]. Recently, Li et al. [ 45 ] designed a method to provide localized vibrotactile effects by combining the multisensory information extracted from audio signals and image sequences. For gameplay spectators, Yun et al. [ 80 ] proposed a motion synthesis algorithm that detects gunfire sounds using a deep learning model and generates a motion effect that resembles gun recoil.

Motion effects can also elicit the sensations of walking by leveraging the cyclic nature of human gaits. Amemiya and colleagues conducted a series of studies on walking sensation rendering using a motion platform [ 4 , 26 , 67 ]. For example, they demonstrated that sinusoidal motion effects in the heave direction can deliver more compelling walking sensations than motion effects in the yaw direction for users wearing head-mounted displays (HMDs) [ 4 ]. Recently, Lee et al. [ 39 ] proposed a data-driven framework for automatically generating the motion effects that provide users with walking sensations. Locomotion data in different gaits, e.g., walking, running, and stumping, was measured using motion sensors, and the captured data was converted to motion effects through a few steps of signal processing algorithms.

One last notable approach is synthesizing the motion effects with specific perceptual properties, presented by Han et al. [ 21 ]. By conducting dissimilarity estimation and adjective rating for various motion profiles, they identified smooth-rough and irregular-regular as two primary perceptual dimensions of motion effects. They presented several methods to synthesize a new motion effect with specific properties by interpolating multiple motion effects in the authoring space consisting of the smooth-rough and irregular-regular axes. Their user study showed that the synthesized motion effects express the target characteristics well.

2.3 Articulated Bodies

Objects made of articulated bodies, such as humans, animals, and robots, are one of the crucial components comprising visual scenes. The articulated body can be represented by a tree structure consisting of links and joints. Each link has a length and is connected to another link by a joint. Especially, the translation and rotation of a root link make an external movement with respect to the world coordinates. The angles of the joints determine the internal configuration, i.e., the pose of the articulated body. Thus, the joint rotations make internal movements within the local coordinates frames of the links. In essence, articulated objects move, and their internal configurations change over time. The motion of articulated bodies has been a keen subject of study in several fields, especially computer graphics and robotics, regarding capture [ 52 ], representation [ 2 ], simulation [ 32 , 78 ], rendering [ 3 , 84 ], and control [ 12 , 44 ].

Owing to their dynamic nature, articulated bodies in the scene naturally attract the viewer's attention. Therefore, 4D designers frequently need to craft the motion effects that represent the complex external and internal movements of articulated objects. This is a much more demanding task, both cognitively and physically, than designing other types of motion effects, such as camera-based motion effects or object-based motion effects for a single rigid body. However, we are not aware of the previous research that addressed the topic of generation algorithms of motion effects representing articulated body motion . This urgent deficiency motivated our work reported in this paper.

3 PROBLEM FORMULATION

There are no standard rules for authoring motion effects, and 4D effect designers design motion effects relying on their intuitions and experiences [ 41 ]. Since we needed rules of thumb regarding motion effects for articulated bodies, we watched many regular 4D films at 4DX theaters (CJ 4DPLEX) and analyzed hundreds of motion effects presented with the films. A key pattern we discovered was: a motion effect inclusively expresses the overall movements in the scene, including both translations and rotations of the object parts and multiple objects, within the motion platform's limited DoFs and workspace. Therefore, we set our research goal as designing an algorithm for computing the motion effects that simultaneously express the translations and rotations of multiple articulated bodies while respecting the motion platform's limited performance. In this section, we formulate the rule for the key pattern extending the motion proxy concept in Han et al. [ 22 ].

To express 6-DoF object motion (Fig. 3a) within the motion chair's limited DoFs and workspace, Han et al. [ 22 ] defined the motion proxy for the object as

\begin{equation} \mathbf {q} = \mathbf {p} + cl\mathbf {d}, \end{equation}
(1)
where q = ( q x , q y , q z ) is the motion proxy, p = ( p x , p y , p z ) and d = ( d x , d y , d z ) are the position and direction vectors of a rigid body, respectively, in the 3D camera space, l is the object's frontal length projected onto the screen, and c is a constant (Fig. 3a). The latter term, cl d , is to reflect more rotation as the object size increases. Therefore, the motion proxy accumulates the information on both translation and rotation into one point while modulating the involvement of rotational motion by the object size. Eq. ( 1 ) can also be expressed by its derivatives in the viewer's perspective as
\begin{equation} \dot{\mathbf {q}} = \mathbf {v} + cl\dot{\mathbf {d}}, \end{equation}
(2)
where $\dot{\mathbf {q}}$ is the velocity of the motion proxy, and v and $\dot{\mathbf {d}}$ are the linear velocity and the derivative of the direction vector of the object in the viewer's perspective.

However, it is impossible to accurately reproduce the movement of the motion proxy because of the performance limitations of a motion platform, such as the maximum displacement and velocity. A standard solution is to apply a motion cueing algorithm (MCA), which converts the desired motion to a motion chair command respecting the hardware performance limitations. In Han et al. [ 22 ], the velocity (or its acceleration) of the motion proxy is fed to an MCA to generate a motion command as

\begin{equation} \mathbf {m} = \mathrm{MCA} (\dot{\mathbf {q}}), \end{equation}
(3)
where m = ( roll , heave , pitch ) is the 3-DoF motion command to a motion chair. Motion predictive control (MPC) is used as MCA.

Unlike a rigid body, an articulated body consists of many joints and links, leading to a motion of a very high DoF. To reflect the link movements in the motion proxy, we extend the original formulation of the motion proxy as follows. For an articulated object i in the scene (Fig. 3b), we determine its motion proxy by

\begin{equation} \dot{\mathbf {q}}^{obj}_{i} = \sum _{j=1}^{M_i} w^{link}_{i,j}\mathbf {v}_{i,j} + cl_i\dot{\mathbf {d}}_i, \end{equation}
(4)
where $\dot{\mathbf {q}}^{obj}$ is the velocity of the motion proxy, M i is the number of links, $w^{link}_{i,j}$ is the normalized weight of link j , v i , j is the visual velocity of link j , c is a constant, l i is the line length screen-projected from the 3D line connecting the object's center and outermost point in the direction d i , and $\dot{\mathbf {d}_i}$ is the derivative of the direction vector. Here, the term for the linear link velocities is a weighted generalization of the linear velocity term in Eq. ( 2 ). Similarly, one can use a weighted sum of the direction derivatives of all links for the term related to rotational velocity. Instead, we include only $\dot{\mathbf {d}_i}$ , the direction vector of the root link. In our tests, this simpler form leads to more plausible motion effects for object rotation. As the link weight w link increases, the object motion proxy q obj reflects the motion of the corresponding link more. In this study, we use a human model as an articulated body and divide it into ten parts (a head, a torso, two upper arms, two forearms, two upper legs, and two lower legs) .

Next, we consider the case where multiple articulated objects simultaneously appear in the scene (Fig. 3c). We define a scene motion proxy $\dot{\mathbf {q}}^{scn}$ as the weighted sum of the object motion proxies $\dot{\mathbf {q}}^{obj}$ of all objects:

\begin{equation} \dot{\mathbf {q}}^{scn} = \sum _{i=1}^N w^{obj}_i\dot{\mathbf {q}}^{obj}_i, \end{equation}
(5)
where $\dot{\mathbf {q}}^{scn}$ is the velocity of the scene motion proxy representing the movements of all the objects in the scene, N is the number of the objects, and w obj represents the normalized weight for each object; For example, when the object weights are both 0.5, the motions of the two objects are combined equally.

To determine the link and object weights, w link and w obj , we use size and speed as the main features. This is because, in the 4D films we watched, the larger and faster an object (or a link), the more clearly the motion effect expressed its movement. These features make the motion proxy more reflect the motion of a link or an object if it is larger or faster. Size and speed are also the key variables for estimating salient regions in image sequences [ 30 , 43 ]. We do not consider other visual features, such as color and illumination, related to visual saliency [ 54 , 71 ] to focus on rendering the object motion. Such features will be more relevant when objects are textured under different lighting conditions.

To compute the size of a link/object, two options can be considered: the visual area projected onto the screen and the volume in the 3D camera space. Our tests did not find significant differences in the resulting motion effects. We use the visual area as it has an additional merit of emphasizing the movement of a closer link/object from the camera. Similarly, we choose the magnitude of the visual velocity on the screen as the speed feature of a link/object. Based on these two features, we designed and tested various methods to set the link and object weights. A few best candidates are presented in Section 4 and 6 , respectively.

In this work, we directly extract the values of all variables in Eq. ( 4 ) and ( 5 ) every frame using Unity3D. The visual area of each link is calculated as the area of the region on the screen to which its mesh is projected. The visual velocity of each link, $\mathbf {v}_{i,j} = (v_{i,j,x}^{vis}, v_{i,j,y}^{vis}, v_{i,j,z}^{vis})$ , is computed as in Han et al. [ 22 ]. The vertical and horizontal components of the visual velocity are determined from the derivative of its position projected onto the screen as

\begin{equation} v_{i,j,x}^{vis} = \frac{\partial }{\partial t} \left(f \frac{p_{i,j,x}}{p_{i,j,z}} \right) \quad \text{and } \quad v_{i,j,y}^{vis} = \frac{\partial }{\partial t} \left(f \frac{p_{i,j,y}}{p_{i,j,z}} \right) \end{equation}
(6)
where t is time, p i , j ( t ) = ( p i , j , x ( t ), p i , j , y ( t ), p i , j , z ( t )) is the position vector of each link in the camera space, and f is the focal length of the camera ( f = 781.2 in this study). The depth component is computed by scaling the depth velocity as
\begin{equation} v_{i,j,z}^{vis} = f \frac{v_{i,j,z}}{p_{i,j,z}} \end{equation}
(7)
where v i , j , z = ∂ p i , j , z /∂ t . We used the magnitude of this velocity, ‖ v i , j ‖, as the feature for speed.

4 SINGLE ARTICULATED BODY

In this section, we design several methods that set the link weights, w link , for a single articulated body to obtain the object motion proxy's velocity, $\dot{\mathbf {q}}^{obj}$ , by Eq. ( 4 ). As described earlier, each link weight is determined based on the two features of area and speed. We first define requirements for a valid link weighting method and then present a few weighting policies satisfying the requirements.

4.1 Requirements on Link Weights

Valid link weighting methods need to meet the following two conditions: First, faster or larger links are weighted higher. Second, visually identical links are equally weighted. The first condition can be easily satisfied if link weights monotonically increase with the size or speed.

The second condition requires more care. Suppose a situation where an object includes two links that have the same visual area s and are moving at the same velocity v . Then, we would expect that the two links contribute equally to motion effects, and their weights must be the same. This observation can be formalized as follows. Let link ( s , v ) be a link with the visual area s and the velocity v . Assume that an object has two groups of adjacent links, { link ( s 1 , v 1 ), ⋅⋅⋅, $link(s_{n_1},\mathbf {v}_{n_1})\rbrace$ and $\lbrace link(s^{\prime }_1,\mathbf {v}^{\prime }_1)$ , ⋅⋅⋅, $link(s^{\prime }_{n_2},\mathbf {v}^{\prime }_{n_2})\rbrace$ , as depicted in Fig. 4 . When the areas of the two groups are equivalent, i.e., $\sum _{i=1}^{n_1} s_i = \sum _{i=1}^{n_2} s_i^{\prime }$ and all the links have the same velocity v , the two link groups have identical visual effects. This gives a constraint that the two groups must have the same sums of the link weights, such that

\begin{equation} \sum _{i=1}^{n_1} w^{link}\left(link(s_i,\mathbf {v})\right) = \sum _{i=1}^{n_2} w^{link}\left(link(s_i^{\prime },\mathbf {v})\right). \end{equation}
(8)

This condition is necessary to prevent ambiguity in weighting methods. Many seemingly reasonable methods may assign different weights depending on whether they treat a part as a link or a group of links. For example, one can set uniform weights for all links, i.e., w link ( link ( s i , v )) = 1. Here, regarding a hand as a single link gives a hand a smaller weight than treating it as a group of a palm and fingers. The second condition can prevent this problem because the uniform weighting violates the requirement in Eq. ( 8 ) when n 1 ≠ n 2 ; the sum of weights is n 1 for one link group and n 2 for the other group.

4.2 Weighting Policies

We present three methods to compute the link weights while satisfying the two requirements described in Section 4.1 .

4.2.1 Size. This policy gives each link a weight proportional to its size (visual area) as

\begin{equation} w^{link}_{i,j} = \frac{s_{i,j}}{\sum _{k=1}^{M_i} s_{i,k}}, \end{equation}
(9)
where s i , j is the visual area of the link j of object i . This method allows the motion proxy to reflect the movement of a larger link more.

4.2.2 Momentum. In this policy, the link weight is proportional to both its area and speed, i.e., its momentum, as

\begin{equation} w^{link}_{i,j} = \frac{s_{i,j}\Vert \mathbf {v}_{i,j}\Vert }{\sum _{k=1}^{M_i} s_{i,k}\Vert \mathbf {v}_{i,k}\Vert }. \end{equation}
(10)
It emphasizes the movement of a faster or larger link, so producing a more dynamic motion effect for fast link movements.

4.2.3 Single. Occasionally, expressing the movement of only one link may have a merit in delivering a more focused motion effect. This is achieved by setting the weight of the selected link to 1, while all the other links have zero weights.

4.2.4 Comparisons. Fig. 5 shows an example illustrating the effects of the three weighting policies. The animation shows that a character lowers its arms, and its left arm (right to readers) moves in a longer distance than its right arm. The torso remains to be almost stationary. For this animation, Size creates a weak motion effect because the arm sizes (so their visual areas) are relatively small. Momentum makes the greatest motion effect by responding to the quick movements of the arms (albeit small). Single , set to capture only the torso's movement, makes the least motion effect.

4.3 Post-Processing

Integrating the velocity of the object motion proxy, calculated by Eq. ( 4 ), and mapping it to a motion command may exceed the motion chair's workspace. To prevent this, we scale the object motion proxy q obj by the scale factor α to transform the motion proxy from the viewer's perspective space into the motion chair space. Then, we feed the scaled motion proxy, α q obj , to an MCA to convert it to a motion command m .

MPC is one of the MCAs, and it optimizes the current control input based on a process model and a future trajectory while considering constraints. The optimization is governed by an objective function that quantifies the difference between the reference output and the output produced by the process model. In this study, we adopt an MPC with velocity input, which showed the best performance in Han et al. [ 22 ]. The MPC algorithm solves the following optimization problem: Find m such that

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} & \mathbf {m} = \underset { \mathbf {m} }{ \mathrm{arg}\min } && \Vert \alpha \dot{\mathbf {q}} - \dot{\mathbf {m}}\Vert ^2 + \epsilon \Vert \mathbf {m}\Vert ^2 \\ & \text{subject to} && \vert \mathbf {m} \vert \le \mathbf {m}_{max}, \end{aligned} \end{equation}
(11)
where m and $\dot{\mathbf {m}}$ are the motion command and its derivative, respectively, and m max is the maximum displacement of the motion chair. The scale factor α depends on the specific settings of the camera space and the workspace of the motion chair. We use α = 1.16 × 10 − 4 ; see Section 5.1.2 for the motion chair used in our work. The other parameter ϵ controls how quickly the motion command m converges to zero (the neutral position), and we use ϵ = 0.3.

5 EVALUATION ON A SINGLE ARTICULATED BODY

We performed a user experiment on many scenes showing a single articulated body in order to compare the benefits of the three link weighting methods. All experiments reported in this paper were approved by the Institutional Review Board at POSTECH (PIRB-2019-R018).

5.1 Methods

5.1.1 Participants. Twenty-seven volunteers (10 females and 17 males; 18–38 years old with an average age of 23.1) with normal sensory ability participated in this experiment. The experiment took no more than 1 hour, and the participants were paid approximately USD 13 after the experiment.

5.1.2 Devices. The motion chair (4DX, CJ 4DPLEX; Fig. 2 ) used in the experiment had three DoFs for roll (± 4°), pitch (± 7°), and heave (± 4 cm). The chair is for four people, and the participant sat in the second seat from the left during the experiment (and in all the other experiments). 2D images were projected onto a 94-inch screen using one channel of a polarized projector (EB-W16SK, Epson Corp.). We blocked auditory cues by presenting a white noise sound using a noise-canceling headphone during the entire experiment.

5.1.3 Experimental Conditions. From the CMU mocap database [ 11 ], we selected 12 animations showing an articulated motion of a single person. Then, we prepared 12 videos using Unity3D, three each for the four categories: dance (Video #1–#3), fight (Video #4–#6), sport (Video #7–#9), and daily actions (Video #10–#12), as described in Table 1 . The camera orientation was fixed to the side view facing the center of a person in all videos. Example animation sequences from selected videos are shown in Fig. 6 . The full videos are available in the supplemental materials.

For each of the 12 videos, we generated three sets of motion effects using the three weighting methods, SIZE , MOMENTUM , and SINGLE , as described in Section 4.2 . SINGLE was made by capturing the movement of only the character's torso. In this case, SINGLE is equivalent to the motion effect produced by the algorithm of Han et al. [ 22 ], serving as the baseline. We also tested alternatives, e.g., capturing the movement of only the link with the highest momentum adaptively. However, such selections produced excessively intensive motion effects, so we used the torso for SINGLE .

5.1.4 Procedure. For familiarization, participants had a practice session in which they experienced all three motion effects for the three shortest videos (#7, #9, and #12). The main session comprised 12 blocks of trials, one for each of the 12 videos. Each block consisted of three trials in which three motion effects were presented with the video. In each trial, participants could experience the pair of a video and a motion effect as many times as they wanted. The order of the video was randomized across participants, and the order of the motion effects was counterbalanced across the videos. Participants took a 3-min break after the practice session and a 5-min break after the sixth block in the main session.

After experiencing each motion effect, participants answered a questionnaire that included the following seven questions (¬ indicates a question of negative meaning ):

  • The motion effect matched the movements of the object.
  • I experienced motion effects that I did not understand why they had been provided.
  • I felt tired after experiencing the motion effect.
  • I was distracted from watching the video by the motion effect.
  • The motion effect described detailed movements of the object.
  • I liked the motion effect.
  • Please leave a comment regarding the motion effect.

For each question from Q1 to Q6 2 , participants expressed the extent of agreement between 0 and 100 by selecting a position on a horizontal line. On the line, descriptors were marked at every 25 points: 0–strongly disagree, 25–disagree, 50–neutral, 75–agree, and 100–strongly agree. For Q7, participants entered a response using a keyboard. In the data analysis, we inverted the scores of three questions (Q2, Q3, and Q4) so that a high score indicates a better experience in every measure.

5.2 Results and Discussion

The experimental results are shown in Fig. 7 . We performed a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA on every question from Q1 to Q6 using Motion Effect and Video as the independent variables. We then applied the SNK test for post-hoc multiple comparisons. Motion Effect was statistically significant for Q1 (Harmony; F (2, 52) = 29.52, p < .0001), Q2 (Causality; F (2, 52) = 10.25, p = .0002), Q3 (Fatigue; F (2, 52) = 54.79, p < .0001), Q5 (Detail; F (2, 52) = 33.62, p < .0001), Q6 (Preference; F (2, 52) = 14.32, p < .0001), but not for Q4 (Distraction; F (2, 52) = 1.06, p = .3538). Video was significant for every measure ( p < .0001). We also observed significant interactions between Motion Effect and Video in every measure. More detailed results of the statistical tests are available in the supplemental materials. Also see Fig. 8 for the results of the individual videos.

On average, MOMENTUM showed the best scores in Q1 (Harmony), Q2 (Causality), Q4 (Detail), and Q6 (Preference) with statistical significance (Fig. 7 ). This result indicates that for the source videos, the motion effects generated by MOMENTUM were better in matching the visual events, conveying the sense of result from the visual cause, and capturing detailed and fast object movements. Therefore, the participants favored MOMENTUM more than SIZE and SINGLE . However, MOMENTUM was inferior in Q3 (Fatigue), which implies that its motion effects can be more drastic and tiring. In the results of the individual videos, MOMENTUM also outperformed SIZE and SINGLE in most measures except Q3 (Fatigue) (Fig. 8 ).

These results were supported by the participant's comments about MOMENTUM . For example, one participant said, “It was good to move slowly in smooth parts and to move vividly in abrupt parts.” (Video #7). However, for long videos, some participants mentioned, “It was the most lively. But because the video was long, I was tired, and my satisfaction decreased.” (Video #10). In comparison, since SIZE does not emphasize fast movements, the weights of SIZE were more evenly distributed than those of MOMENTUM . Thus, motion effects made using SIZE could feel unfocused, resulting in relatively low scores. Four participants reported that some object movements were missed or not expressed well with SIZE . SINGLE also received low scores similar to SIZE . It was mainly because SINGLE represented only the torso's movement without considering the arms’ or legs’ movements. It was pointed out by three participants. Nevertheless, SIZE and SINGLE scored comparably to MOMENTUM in Video #3, #4, #6, #7, #8, and #11, in which the movements of all the body parts were highly correlated to each other. This implies that using only the torso's movement may be sufficient to create motion effects in some cases.

Interestingly, we observed that a motion effect can affect the perception of a character's properties in the video. One participant commented, “I felt the more intensive the motion effect is, the stronger the person moved.” (Video #9). Meanwhile, some participants reported that the video context influenced their expectations of motion effects, e.g., “I tended to rate strong motion effects for martial arts more positively.” (Video #7).

In summary, Momentum showed the best performance. However, its abrupt movements sometimes disrupt users’ satisfaction. Keeping this shortcoming in mind, we adopt Momentum to compute the object motion proxy for each of the multiple articulated bodies.

6 MULTIPLE ARTICULATED BODIES

This section presents a few methods to set the object weights, w obj , when multiple articulated bodies appear in the scene. As in Section 4.1 , we define requirements for the object weights and design weighting policies satisfying the requirements. The velocity of the scene motion proxy, $\dot{\mathbf {q}}^{obj}$ , is calculated by substituting the weights into Eq. ( 4 ) and ( 5 ) and then converted to a motion command m through an MCA.

6.1 Requirement on Object Weights

Valid object weighting methods need to meet the following two conditions: First, faster or larger objects are weighted more. Second, visually identical objects are equally weighted. The first condition can be easily satisfied if the object weights monotonically increase with the size or speed.

For the second condition, suppose that there are two objects, obj 1 and obj 2 , consisting of { link ( s 1 , v 1 ), ⋅⋅⋅, $link(s_{M_1},\mathbf {v}_{M_1}) \rbrace$ and $\lbrace link(s^{\prime }_1,\mathbf {v}^{\prime }_1)$ , ⋅⋅⋅, $link(s^{\prime }_{M_2},\mathbf {v}^{\prime }_{M_2})\rbrace$ , respectively, as depicted in Fig. 9 . If their sizes are the same, i.e, $\sum _{i=1}^{M_1} s_i = \sum _{i=1}^{M_2} s_i^{\prime }$ , and they all have the same velocity v , then the two objects have identical visual effects. This gives a constraint that the two objects must have the same object weights, such that:

\begin{equation} w^{obj}(obj_1) = w^{obj}(obj_2). \end{equation}
(12)
This second condition can be violated by many weighting methods. For example, one can set an object weight to be directly proportional to the sum of the velocities of all the links constituting the object, i.e., $w^{obj} = \sum _{j=1}^{M} \mathbf {v}_j$ , but it violates the second condition when M 1 ≠ M 2 ; the object weight is M 1 v for one object and M 2 v for the other.

6.2 Weighting Policies

We design four methods that compute the object weights while satisfying the two requirements described in Section 6.1 .

6.2.1 Additive. This method sets the object weight w obj as the sum of the momentums, i.e., size × speed, of all links comprising the object:

\begin{equation} w^{obj}_i = \frac{\sum _{j=1}^{M_i}s_{i,j}\Vert \mathbf {v}_{i,j}\Vert }{\sum _{k=1}^N \sum _{j=1}^{M_i} s_{k,j}\Vert \mathbf {v}_{k,j}\Vert }, \end{equation}
(13)
where M i is the number of links of object i and N is the number of all the objects in the scene. In this method, the higher the link weights constituting the object are, the more weighted the object is. Therefore, this method can be advantageous for capturing the movements of large and fast objects.

6.2.2 Uniform. This method gives the same weights to all objects, i.e., $w^{obj}_i=1/N$ . For example, if there are two objects, the scene motion proxy q scn reflects the two object motion proxies, $\mathbf {q}^{obj}_1$ and $\mathbf {q}^{obj}_2$ , equally.

6.2.3 Salient. When multiple objects appear, one object may attain the viewer's attention almost exclusively, whereas the movements of other objects are neglected. For example, imagine the Iron Man fighting against many (insignificant) enemies. This method, Salient , is designed for such cases and attempts to express the movement of the most important object at that instant. We use the object weights calculated by the Additive policy to judge the importance of each object. Salient determines the object weights in three steps, as illustrated in Fig. 10 . Here, we denote the object weight of Additive by w add and the object weight of Salient by w sal . First, we make time intervals based on which object has the greatest w add in each interval (Fig. 10a). In each interval, we set w sal of the object with the highest w add to 1, and w sal of the other objects to 0 (Fig. 10b). Very short intervals (less than 1 s) are merged to their preceding intervals to achieve stable motion (Fig. 10c). Finally, for a continuous blending of the object motion proxies, w sal is smoothed using the half period of the cosine profile with a period of 1 s (Fig. 10d).

6.2.4 Single. This method captures only the movement of one object among the many in the scene. The target object gets the full weight of 1, while the weights of all the other objects are zeros.

6.3 Post-Processing

The velocity of the scene motion proxy, $\dot{\mathbf {q}}^{scn}$ , obtained by Eq. ( 4 ) and ( 5 ), is converted into a motion command m . We use Momentum , which showed the best performance in Section 5 , to determine the link weights w link . Additionally, to prevent the abrupt chair movements pointed out in the previous user study (Section 5.2 ), we apply the following attenuation rule, which reduces the variations of a motion effect over time:

\begin{equation} \widehat{\dot{\mathbf {q}}}^{scn} = \beta \Vert \dot{\mathbf {q}}^{scn}\Vert ^\gamma \frac{\dot{\mathbf {q}}^{scn}}{\Vert \dot{\mathbf {q}}^{scn}\Vert }, \end{equation}
(14)
where $\widehat{\dot{\mathbf {q}}}^{scn}$ is the velocity of the attenuated motion proxy, β is a scale factor that makes $\widehat{\dot{\mathbf {q}}}^{scn}$ and $\dot{\mathbf {q}}^{scn}$ have a similar level of signal energy, and γ is a gain for attenuation (0 < γ ≤ 1).

Decreasing γ lowers the fluctuations of the scene motion proxy by following the power function that has γ as the exponent. As a result, when the object movements are strong, using the attenuated scene motion proxy, instead of the original proxy, decreases the motion effect intensity. When the object movements are weak, it rather increases the motion effect intensity. This can be easily understood by comparing what happens to Eq. ( 14 ) if γ = 1 or 0.5. According to our experience, the range of γ for perceptually-best motion effects is 0.5–0.7. We set β = 6.0 and γ = 0.7 in the user study reported in Section 7 .

Finally, the attenuated motion proxy, $\widehat{\dot{\mathbf {q}}}^{scn}$ , is converted into a motion command through scaling and MCA, as described in Section 4.3 .

7 EVALUATION ON MULTIPLE ARTICULATED BODIES

We performed a user experiment to compare the benefits of the four weighting policies that determine the scene motion proxy. The experiment used many scenes where two articulated bodies interacted with each other.

7.1 Methods

7.1.1 Participants. Twenty volunteers (9 females and 11 males; 19–29 years old with an average age of 23.4) with normal sensory ability participated in this experiment. The experiment took approximately 90 min. The participants were paid approximately USD 22 after the experiment.

7.1.2 Experimental Conditions. From the Unity Asset Store, we selected eight animation pairs, as described in Table 2 . Each animation pair showed the articulated motions of two people, the attacker and the victim, fighting each other. Then, we prepared eight videos for them using Unity3D. The camera orientation was fixed to the side view facing the center of two people in all videos. Some animation sequences from selected videos are shown in Fig. 12 . The full videos are available in the supplemental materials.

For each of the eight videos, we used four weighting methods described in Section 6.2 to generate five motion effects: ADDITIVE , UNIFORM , SALIENT , ATTACKER , and VICTIM . ATTACKER and VICTIM were made using Single by capturing the movement of only the attacker and only the victim, respectively.

7.1.3 Procedure. For familiarization, participants had a practice session in which they experienced all five motion effects for the three shortest videos (#2, #3, and #6). The main session comprised eight blocks of trials, one for each video. Each block consisted of five trials in which the five motion effects were presented with the video. In each trial, participants could experience the pair of a video and a motion effect as many times as they wanted. The order of the video was randomized, and the order of the motion effects per video was balanced across participants using the balanced Latin square. Participants had a 3-min break after the practice session and a 5-min break after the fourth block in the main session.

After experiencing each motion effect, participants answered the same questionnaire described in Section 5 . In this questionnaire, we slightly modified Q2 (Causality) to “It was easy to understand the relationship between the video and the motion effect.” This was to test whether the participant could find out which of the two characters the motion effect was focused on. Participants’ response methods to the questions were also the same with the previous experiment. In the data analysis, we inverted the scores of two questions (Q3 and Q4) so that a high score indicates a better experience in every measure.

7.2 Results and Discussion

The experimental results averaged over the eight videos are shown in Fig. 13 . We performed a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA on every question from Q1 to Q6 using Motion Effect and Video as the independent variables. We then used the SNK test for post-hoc multiple comparisons. Motion Effect was statistically significant for every question: Q1 (Harmony; F (4, 76) = 34.1, p < .0001), Q2 (Causality; F (4, 76) = 17.56, p = .0002), Q3 (Fatigue; F (4, 76) = 6.92, p < .0001), Q4 (Distraction; F (4, 76) = 3.34, p = .0141), Q5 (Detail; F (4, 76) = 40.87, p < .0001), and Q6 (Preference; F (4, 76) = 30.55, p < .0001). Video was significant for only Q3 (Fatigue; F (7, 133) = 2.86, p = .0081). We observed significant interactions between Motion Effect and Video in five measures; Q1 (Harmony; F (28, 532) = 6.85, p < .0001), Q2 (Causality; F (28, 532) = 4.74, p < .0001), Q3 (Fatigue; F (28, 532) = 2.89, p < .0001), Q5 (Detail; F (28, 532) = 7.73, p < .0001), and Q6 (Preference; F (28, 532) = 6.43, p < .0001), but not in Q4 (Distraction; F (28, 532) = 0.90, p = .6166). More detailed results of the statistical tests are provided in the supplemental materials. In summary, ADDITIVE , SALIENT , and VICTIM showed the best scores in all measures except Q3 (Fatigue), followed by the UNIFORM (Fig. 13 ). ATTACKER received the lowest scores in all metrics except Q3 (Fatigue).

VICTIM elicited significantly better viewer experiences than ATTACKER even though both were made using the same weighting policy Single . It is presumably because the movement of the victim was more dynamic than the attacker in most videos. The attacker usually showed short and fast movements, such as punching, kicking, and stabbing. On the contrary, the victim exhibited long-lasting and large movements, such as staggering and falling. Eight participants pointed out that ATTACKER missed important movements, while only four said the same about VICTIM . For the same reason, ATTACKER made the participants less tired, leading to high scores in Q3 (Fatigue). However, VICTIM was not always better than ATTACKER . ATTACKER and VICTIM had similar scores in Video #2, #5, #6, and #7, where the attacker's movements were as large and dynamic as the victim's. In Video #5, ATTACKER scored slightly higher than VICTIM in Q1 (Harmony), Q2 (Causality), Q5 (Detail), and Q6 (Preference), although the differences were insignificant.

Both ADDITIVE and SALIENT received the highest scores (similar to VICTIM ). It seems that the object weights changing dynamically and quickly by both methods well captured the important movements of the attacker and the victim. However, participants frequently complained that ADDITIVE and SALIENT presented excessively strong motion effects. Also, SALIENT sometimes missed important movements; one participant said, “No motion was given for pulling out the hand after stabbing.” (Video #5). As illustrated in Fig. 15 , SALIENT did not capture the attacker's quick movement as it was removed by the merging process. The same participant also commented on this: “I could not understand what the motion effect focused on.” (Video #7). This problem can be improved by dynamically adjusting the window size during motion merging or using a better attention model.

UNIFORM resulted in lower scores than ADDITIVE , SALIENT , and VICTIM , due to the weak strength of its motion effects as reported by five participants. This is because the movements of the two objects can be canceled out when they are evenly combined, without emphasizing the movement of a more important object. The weak intensity of UNIFORM also led to the high scores of Q3 (Fatigue).

As in Section 5 , we observed that motion effects could alter the perceived properties of characters and actions. Two participants commented on this: “The movement was excessive, and it felt like a heavy person was standing up.” (Video #1) and “It felt like the attacker was hitting very weakly due to the weak motion effect.” (Video #5).

In conclusion, ADDITIVE , SALIENT , and VICTIM showed better perceptual performance than the others. In our videos, the attacker and the victim were distinguishable. However, the attacker and the victim may not be clearly distinguished in some cases, e.g., when two people beat each other up. In this case, ADDITIVE and SALIENT may have a merit because both vary the object weights dynamically. Although we did not test animations for more than two objects, we expect that ADDITIVE and SALIENT can produce more plausible motion effects in such cases, where the viewer's attention can be dispersed to multiple objects.

8 GUIDELINES FOR OBJECT-BASED MOTION EFFECTS

In this section, we present guidelines for 4D effect designers in authoring object-based motion effects on the basis of our observations and the participants’ qualitative feedback from the user studies.

  • People expect motion effects to be presented for the movements that catch their eyes. This is supported in the user studies by the result that the methods emphasizing the movement of a large and fast part or object were preferred: MOMENTUM for an articulated body and ADDITIVE and SALIENT for multiple articulated objects. Therefore, it is crucial to identify what users would attend to in the scene.
    For complex movements of an articulated body in which many parts show free, uncorrelated, and fast movements as in fast-tempo dance, motion effects that express all or many of them are likely to be distracting and uncomfortable. We tested a method of assigning link weights in proportion to the link velocities relative to the root link (torso) to emphasize their local movements. However, the resulting motion effects were very distracting and unsatisfying. In such cases, a better strategy can be emphasizing the movements of one or a few parts. A good example is SINGLE , which was preferred for a few videos in the user studies.
    When multiple articulated bodies appear in the scene, the main character's movements are usually the most dominant. Nevertheless, it can be better to express the movements of minor characters, e.g., an enemy attacked by a hero, as was VICTIM preferred in the user studies. Additionally, when many main characters show up in the scene, like Avengers, it is necessary to determine which character is the most prominent. For this purpose, computer vision techniques for estimating visual saliency [ 30 , 43 , 81 ] or eye tracking data for the content may be helpful.
  • As mentioned in Section 5 , participants preferred dynamic motion effects, but they became tired when the effects lasted for a long time, which decreased user satisfaction. Thus, the weighting policies that emphasize fast movements ( MOMENTUM for an articulated body, and ADDITIVE and SALIENT for multiple objects) should be carefully used, especially for movies and games with a long duration. To prevent abrupt movements, the motion intensity can be adjusted by scaling and applying the attenuation rule in Eq. ( 14 ). We set the exponent γ to 0.7 in the experiments, but using a lower value can create smoother motion effects with less deviations in motion speed. Of course, a higher value can be advantageous for more dynamic situations.
  • As mentioned earlier, the motion intensity should be carefully tuned according to the context of audiovisual content. Dynamic motion effects can be preferred in fighting scenes, while gentle ones in dance scenes. Moods and circumstances are also important. For example, to emphasize a tense situation, the motion effect can amplify even the fine movements in the scene. Besides, motion effects can be leveraged to augment a character's properties, such as strengths and weights. A motion effect weaker than a visual movement can emphasize such properties as the weakness and lightness of an object, while a stronger motion effect can accentuate the strength or the heaviness.
  • Our algorithm can generate object-based motion effects by taking various animation data as the input. The animation data can be collected using a motion capture system or extracted from animation databases, e.g., the CMU mocap database [ 11 ]. Our algorithm can use such animation data to yield motion effects for specific movements, such as walking and gestures. Putting them together can lead to a motion effect library that provides a huge amount of motion effect segments. These motion effect segments can be transformed and stitched into a motion effect for an articulated body to fit the content. Also, a motion effect for the movement of multiple objects can be created by blending and merging the motion effects of the individual objects. This approach can be particularly effective in generating motion effects for the movements that are not shown visually in the content but can be inferred, e.g., the locomotion in a first-person view. Lee et al. [ 39 ] approached this problem by collecting gait motion data using a motion sensor and generating motion effects for the character's gait that is not visually revealed in first-person shooting games. As an alternative, our algorithm can be applied using the animations of human locomotion. In our test, the synthesized motion effects for running animations showed similar motion profiles (Fig. 16 ).

9 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

Our ultimate goal is to automate the authoring process of object-based motion effects from audiovisual content. Our algorithms require information on the sizes and velocities of many links in articulated bodies. Such information is directly accessible in computer-generated content, such as animation movies and VR games, as in our implementation. For general content, the information can be extracted from the image sequence using computer vision techniques. One option is human pose estimation [ 49 ], which estimates the 3D poses of multiple people even in a monocular video. However, it can still be challenging to estimate the 3D poses in fast-moving scenes or scenes where part of an object appears, which should await advances in pose estimation techniques. Another option is to calculate the motion proxy by aggregating pixel-wise movements, not link-wise movements. In fact, recent techniques for estimating pixel displacements between consecutive images, such as optical flow [ 27 ], depth estimation [ 7 ], and scene flow [ 25 ], show reliable results. We leave an integration of such automatic feature extraction as future work.

Additionally, 4D effect designers need to tune parameters, such as α , β , γ , and ϵ in Eq. ( 11 ) and ( 14 ). These parameters should be adjusted considering various factors, e.g., genre, situation, context, and mood of the audiovisual content. Recently, Li et al. [ 45 ] showcased adjusting the intensity of vibrotactile effects based on the contextual information extracted from the psychoacoustic measures of sound. Such an approach can also be useful for our framework, where strong motion effects are preferred in intense scenes and gentle motion effects in sentimental scenes.

Finally, we tested our algorithm in limited cases for two articulated bodies in the scene. We have not tested more general cases, such as more than two characters or characters using tools, e.g., flourishing a sword and throwing a stone. In such cases, we expect that Additive and Salient would have advantages over the other weighting methods because they lead to a motion effect focused on the main object movement. In addition, the camera was fixed in all the videos used in the user studies, while the camera moves freely in general movies. For camera-moving scenes, Han et al. [ 22 ] compensated for the camera movement using the visual speed of the object relative to the background. This method is also applicable to our algorithm using camera motion estimation techniques [ 37 ].

Our future work will focus on resolving the above limitations.

10 CONCLUSIONS

In this paper, we have addressed a new research problem: enhancing the user experiences elicited by the multisensory 4D content that includes motion effects emphasizing the visual movements of multiple articulated bodies. Our approach is an algorithmic framework that generates object-based motion effects automatically by compressing the complex external and internal movements of articulated bodies into a single point: motion proxy. To this end, we have extended the computational definition of motion proxy from one rigid body [ 22 ] to an articulated body, and then to multiple articulated objects. For each case, a few weighting methods to determine the motion proxy are designed, and their relative merits and drawbacks are evaluated by user studies. Finally, major findings and our research experiences are summarized into design guidelines and current limitations. To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first attempt to automatically generate object-based motion effects for complex articulated bodies moving in the scene.
