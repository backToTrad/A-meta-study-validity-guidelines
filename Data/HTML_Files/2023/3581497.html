Over the last decade, smart home technology (SHT) has become an integral part of modern households. As a result, smart home ecosystems blend with daily social life, appropriated and integrated into personalised domestic environments. The lived experience of inhabiting smart home ecosystems, however, is not yet understood, resulting in a mismatch between ecosystem design and inhabitants’ needs. Drawing on contextual inquiry methods, we conducted an explorative interview study (N=20) with SHT users in their homes. Our thematic analysis reveals how users shape their smart home ecosystems (SHEs), considering social relationships at home, perceived ownership of SHTs, and expected key benefits. Notably, our analysis shows that household members consciously choose ‘their’ level of SHT interconnectedness, reflecting social, spatial and functional affinities between systems. Following our findings, we formulate five implications for designing future SHTs. Our work contributes insights on the dynamics and appropriation of smart home ecosystems by their inhabitants.

1 INTRODUCTION

Recent years brought a rapid growth in the variety and number of available consumer-grade smart home technologies (SHTs). Variously referred to as ’smart’ devices, home–Internet of Things, or home automation gadgets, SHTs expand the range of possible enhancements for domestic environments [ 59 ]. We observe, however, that while individual systems are relatively well understood, we lack insight into how users shape the ecosystems consisting of multiple, functionally different devices. Inaccurate judgment on user requirements may result in abandonment or in unpredictable, user-instigated workarounds, exposing technologies to security and functional threats. We also observe that users expect solutions that would offer sufficient flexibility to adapt the technological companions to evolving scenarios of their lives. A paradigm shift is already noticeable in consumer behaviours, where infrastructurally complex and expensive in-built smart home packages [ 47 ] are superseded by modular ecosystems suitable for gradual adoption and expansion [ 70 ]. This tendency reflects the overall trend for people to lead a more dynamic lifestyle that involves changes in their own social contexts, relocation experiences, and professional status emerging over time [ 45 , 94 ]. Thus, it is vital to fully integrate these computing technologies into the everyday practices of smart home users.

Past research on the lived experience with SHTs looked at users’ motivations and attitudes for adopting new technology [ 46 , 54 ], identified key challenges that SHT users [ 53 ] and developers [ 74 ] face, or studied social context related to reshaping home labour and inter-member dynamics resulting from the increased presence of technology in the households [ 90 , 98 ]. These works show that a holistic view of the socio-technical dimensions of introducing technologies into domestic spaces is necessary to design technology that would both contribute to at-home well being and be accepted by diverse users in the household. More focus is required to understand the artifact ecologies within the home [ 13 , 69 ], concerning both the shared ownership and the complex relationships between inhabitants [ 58 ], as well as the interactions between digital agents themselves [ 14 ]. In our work, we deepen the understanding of smart home ecosystems (SHE) by probing the lived experience with interconnected smart home technologies. Reaching beyond studying particular aspects of home ecology (as done in past work), we provide a holistic, cross-dimensional inquiry on how users, including co-inhabitants, define, shape, equip and interact with the devices and on environmental factors in the smart home ecosystem.

Our work contributes insights on what drives user choices regarding if and how to set up, expand and configure their smart home ecosystems. Our interest in this study is two-fold: first, we explore the diversity of attitudes of different smart home inhabitants and sample their strategies to appropriate the technology to fit the social contexts in the home. Second, we investigate how the interconnectedness of SHTs is shaped by functional and spatial affinities of different technologies, and identify key benefits and drawbacks of transforming the home into an interactive environment. In this work, we provide a detailed, qualitative account that showcases the perspectives of long-term SHT users. We follow the decision process of technology adopters, analyse their motivations and key expected benefits, study social contexts and roles in the household, and reflect on how these factors are shaping bespoke ecosystems.

To this end, we conducted an empirical study of the lived experience in smart home ecosystems . We studied ecosystems already existing in participants’ homes, in contrast to past inquiries, which focused on studying experiences with individual systems introduced as a part of the study, e.g. [ 46 , 53 ]. This allowed us to investigate first-hand experiences of smart homes, build insights about the users’ attitudes, and explore reflections on long-term use. Drawing on contextual inquiry methods, we conducted an explorative interview study (N=20) with SHT users in their homes. From the gathered data, we drew five themes describing central aspects of the lived experience in a smart home ecosystem: Motivations & Benefits , Humans in the Ecosystem , Interconnectedness , Conflicts in the Ecosystem , and Trust & Sense of Control . Using these themes, we discuss the implications of our findings for designing interconnected SHTs.

In summary, our work contributes detailed insights on social, spatial, and functional contexts that shape users’ attitudes toward smart home ecosystems, coupled with deepened understanding of the roles and perspectives of various SHE inhabitants. Our results motivate actionable takeaways, which we articulate as five concrete design implications for designing future domestic ecosystems.

2 RELATED WORK

In this section, we review prior research that inspired and informed our inquiry, covering (1) works defining when a technology is considered “smart” and what constitutes a smart home, (2) works that analyse challenges around formation and appropriation of smart home ecosystems, and (3) past research that probed lived experiences and socio-technical implications of technologies used in domestic environments.

2.1 Understanding the Smart Home

Smart home environments have long been drawing on experiences from ubiquitous computing paradigms, being envisioned as filled with pervasive, interconnected technology [ 4 ] that is self-aware and adapts itself to the needs and desires of its residents [ 51 ]. These concepts were largely inspired by Weiser's vision of calm computing [ 103 ], later extended by Rogers [ 91 ], who emphasized the role of creating engaging experiences that empower users in pursuing their goals and making technology a perennial companion in various everyday activities. A certain dichotomy of focus in describing smart domestic environments has been outlined by Darby [ 30 ], who noted two broad, prevailing approaches, namely “building- and system-focused” and “home- and user-focused”. This differentiation is followed by other works, which drew the line between proactive and passive interaction with interconnected devices [ 74 , 104 ].

Over the last decade, HCI research developed an extensive body of work on smart home technologies (SHT) and services. Multiple works focused on empirically studying particular technologies that were appropriated for domestic use [ 43 , 50 , 88 , 109 ] or studied different interaction techniques and artifacts designed for deployment at home [ 15 , 57 , 62 , 75 , 92 ]. However, beyond important empirical contributions, the system-oriented approach was criticized for insufficient exploration of social context and everyday-life implications of technology use [ 34 ]. An alternative view on SHT looks more closely at user requirements and expected services. Crowley and Coutaz [ 28 ] proposed a taxonomy of services offered by various SHTs, focusing on their benefits as tools, housekeeping aids, sources of advice, or providing media experiences. Various works explored other areas that constitute independent, goal-driven categories of services, such as security and safety [ 4 , 53 ], well-being and assisted living [ 88 , 97 ], sustainability [ 3 , 89 ], energy management [ 8 , 54 , 80 ], and entertainment [ 4 , 46 ]. Following this categorisation, a system would become a part of a smart home environment regardless of its more or less advanced features, but subject to serving a certain role within a pervasive ecology. This view seems to better fit the user-centred approach of the HCI field [ 93 ], while the meaning of particular services may vary across social groups [ 34 ].

In our inquiry, we lean towards a service-oriented viewpoint for the definition of Smart Home Technology. We share the perspective of Taylor et al. [ 100 ], who argued that the “smartness” of the home originates in how users conduct their everyday routines and activities, and the role of technology is to “marshal, display, leverage and work upon” the “intelligence imbued by people”. We decided not to consider stand-alone devices that do not offer computing or interactive elements, unless their role in the wider smart ecology was clearly indicated. Instead, we focused on all systems that provide various services for domestic use, including both the interactive systems (hardware and software, devices and services), and back-end technologies wherever necessary.

2.2 Interconnecting Smart Devices and Systems

While the definitions of a smart ecosystem may vary according to the analytical lenses applied, interconnecting SHTs has been a recurring theme in HCI and related engineering fields. Building upon the ubiquitous computing visions [ 81 , 103 ], mutual awareness and information transfer between systems form the backbone of IoT infrastructures in domestic contexts as well [ 4 ]. However, an interconnected smart home requires answering several technical challenges concerning networking [ 42 ], mutual compatibility of devices [ 2 ], and power management [ 66 ]. The collective effort of researchers and manufacturers has led to establishing and popularising protocols and standards, enabling compatibility across different vendors [ 41 , 106 ], or appropriation of already prevailing networking technologies to better support home IoT [ 88 ]. However, in our inquiry, we do not aim to study the engineering aspects of home technology. Instead, we focus on user perceptions of technologies within the ecosystem and how these perceptions shape the interconnectedness (or lack thereof) between devices.

Recent works indicate the importance of social contexts within a home in shaping the structure of interconnected home systems. Garg and Cui [ 39 ] find that the relationships between inhabitants and respecting inter-member privacy are of critical importance. Along these lines, Yu et al. [ 107 ] find that ecosystems providing services shared by all co-tenants are more likely to employ a high level of interconnectedness and operate using complex, conditional procedures. Other factors include the reservations towards dependency on external services [ 108 ], perceived sense of control [ 19 , 105 ] and the amount of effort invested in configuration and maintenance of the ecosystem [ 46 ]. However, the lived experience of inhabiting smart home ecosystems is not well understood, which limits how the needs of inhabitants can be reflected in ecosystem design.

Our work further investigates the socio-technical aspects of smart home ecosystems from a user-centred perspective. In particular, we aim to build a deeper understanding of users’ strategies and approaches to interconnecting smart home services and related technology appropriation. These investigations will facilitate future engineering efforts in navigating which areas are of significant importance to SH users and help them prioritize focus within the development process.

2.3 Living with Smart Home Technologies

Along with increasing adoption and accessibility of SHTs, researchers have considered the home as an interactive space more holistically. Edwards and Grinter [ 33 ] describe 7 key challenges for developing smart home solutions, indicating different socio-technical aspects of interaction. Later research has widely responded to these directions by studying the lived experience of smart home inhabitants from different perspectives.

User motivations and desires were studied by Jensen et al. [ 54 ], who recognize three user models — the helper, the hedonist, and the optimizer — categorizing frequently expressed user viewpoints. Along those lines, Strengers et al. [ 98 ] conducted an ethnographic study with early adopters to analyse Intel's vision of “Protection, Productivity and Pleasure” as three driving factors for domestic technologies. The factors that make SHT desirable were also studied by Mennicken et al. [ 74 ], who looked at how the technology fits into social values and can become meaningful through supporting lifestyle choices. The technology may also be introduced to domestic spaces out of necessity, rather than deliberate choice. A wide body of work describes various aspects of assisted living [ 29 , 67 , 88 , 97 ] or energy management technologies [ 8 , 54 ]. These works emphasize the importance of the perceived relevancy of systems, coupled with a user's sense of control. Even though prior works provide deep insight into factors driving users’ decisions, reducing the gap between user expectations and the current state-of-art in consumer smart homes still remains a challenge [ 70 ]. Moreover, the extent to which these viewpoints apply to interconnected ecosystems is yet to be studied. Our research aims to provide these insights into the role of the motivations of SHT users on the desired and achieved level of interconnectedness within their SHE.

A technology-focused approach also recurs in studying home technology. Kawsar et al. [ 60 ] observe an "activity first, device later" approach, examining the pervasive role of mobile devices, while Brush et al. [ 16 ] outline the key problems concerning home automation systems. A particular technology that gained significant attention was smart speakers. Luria et al.[ 68 ] identify key barriers for using smart speakers in multi-user spaces, while Voit et al. [ 102 ] explore reasons for their abandonment by users. Other researchers have looked at the impact of AI-speakers on family dynamics [ 79 , 85 ] and their use by children [ 9 ]. Among other closely studied technologies, HCI has examined everyday experiences with cameras [ 43 ], lights [ 55 , 96 ], and media entertainment systems [ 86 ]. While many of these inquiries concern the touchpoints of respective systems with other technologies, they often lack analysis of the role and impact of these touchpoints on shaping interconnected ecosystems. In contrast to previous work, we aim to holistically explore smart home ecosystems, focusing on simultaneous co-habitation with a range of different SHTs. We also look at how the constant presence of technology affects the social dynamics between the inhabitants.

A third approach is to examine how technology affects communication dynamics, redefines housekeeping duties, or shapes different user roles and behaviours. Taylor et al. [ 100 ] studied asynchronous communication between household members and proposed interactive artifacts. This concept was later extended to support relatedness of remote family members [ 57 ]. Adoption of technology into domestic environments was studied by Hargreaves et al. [ 46 ], who emphasize social disruption and learning difficulty as key issues concerning consumer-grade smart home systems. Jakobi et al. also discuss adoption-related themes, e.g. the formation of competence and new tech-oriented household duties [ 53 ]. A different look at emerging household labour was offered by Rode and Poole, who spotlighted the role of gender in digital housekeeping [ 90 ]. Other related inquiries have looked at sustainability [ 3 , 54 , 77 ], privacy awareness [ 71 , 101 ], or culture-specific experiences of technology appropriation [ 61 , 65 ], describing a variety of implications for household ecologies.

Our work borrows its analytical lens from past studies of smart home systems. Instead of studying a particular aspect of interaction with SHTs, we examine the lived experience within smart home ecosystems to understand the factors shaping appropriation by inhabitants.

3 METHOD

We conducted a qualitative inquiry to explore users’ experiences with smart home ecosystems. Interviews offer the opportunity to develop a richer understanding of smart home ecosystems in everyday life [ 7 ]. Moreover, given that we discussed participants’ personal spaces and routines, interviews allowed us to limit collection of sensitive data. Inspired by contextual inquiry [ 11 , 49 ], we conducted the interviews in participants’ homes. This enabled us to contextualise participants’ accounts within witnessed spaces and observe firsthand the solutions users’ applied within their respective households.

3.1 Participants

Given ongoing questions of what constitutes a smart home ecosystem, we derived specific criteria for choosing participants by reviewing the market for smart home technologies following a service-oriented approach [ 28 ]. We subsequently constructed a recruitment survey to navigate the number and diversity of SHT used at prospective participants’ homes. As our study focuses on multi-device ecosystems, we included participants who own more than three functionally unique devices, and those whose ecosystems exhibited interconnection capabilities. To decide which devices to consider as agents of a smart home ecosystem, we followed the approach of Taylor et al. [ 100 ], outlined in section 2.1 . For example, we treated personal informatics devices (smartwatches, etc.) as smart home devices only if the participants explicitly reported using them for interacting with their smart home. We also considered the mobile devices (mostly smartphones and tablets) on a case-by-case basis, differentiating between devices reappropriated for other purposes (e.g. smartphone deployed as permanent music player, tablet used primarily for smart home control) and those used as personal devices. A detailed list of the devices found in participants’ homes is provided in supplementary material. To probe long-run perceptions and limit the novelty effect [ 102 ], we only included participants who had used at least three of the claimed systems for at least six months.

We conducted the study across two locations – two large cities (populations of ca. 200k and ca. 650k) in Germany and Poland in summer of 2022. We recruited our participants using university mailing lists and announcement boards, social media and snowball sampling. In an online survey, respondents identified their devices and the approximate duration of their use. We also asked for a brief summary of household composition describing the type of property, the number of inhabitants, and the relationships between inhabitants. All respondents were asked to explicitly confirm that they would like to participate in the interview. Out of 114 responses, we recruited 20 participants who met our criteria (namely at least three functionally distinct devices, used for at least 6 months) and expressed interest in the study.

3.2 Interview Protocol

To examine holistically the lived experience with smart home ecosystems, we designed a semi-structured interview protocol accompanied by a suggestion for a home tour (participants were free to show what they wanted). We asked the participants to explain their daily encounters and to reflect on their experiences with particular systems. We asked about their routines, motivations, and approaches to using various elements of their ecosystems. We asked about the most frequent maintenance issues and challenges they faced and probed their personal attitudes towards living with SHT. We also discussed their perceptions of the roles and attitudes of other household inhabitants. The open-ended design fostered storytelling and exploring different perspectives of inhabiting a smart home ecosystem.

The interviews were conducted during a visit to the participant's place of residence. A single participant from each household was recruited based on the contact details provided in the recruitment survey. Beyond their own accounts, participants were asked about the viewpoints, stories, and opinions of other household members. Blending direct and by-proxy interviewing has a benefit of providing a more holistic account of experiences and has been successfully used in past work concerning personal technologies [ 63 , 76 ]. We therefore introduced cues in our interview protocol to elicit the views of the participants’ cohabitants. To ensure participants’ safety, researchers took additional protective measures and obtained a negative COVID-19 test result within 24-hours prior to each interview. We began each interview by reiterating the aim of the study and our data protection policies. Further, we informed the participants that they had the right to terminate the interview at any time without providing explanation, while still receiving their remuneration. Given that the interview questions concerned participants’ private life, we emphasized their right to disregard any question without any explanation. To assure anonymity, we asked users to refrain from mentioning names during the interview. Therefore, the transcripts of the interviews do not contain any personal data. We then obtained written consent.

All interviews were conducted with audio-only recording, with the participants’ consent. The researchers took photos of devices and arrangements of particular interest, obtaining ad-hoc vocal consent for each photo, beyond the written one obtained at the beginning of the study. At the end of the session, each participant was given the chance to review all photos and could remove any photos they wanted. Each participant received the local equivalent of $ 30 (USD) as remuneration for the interview. The compensation was determined based on interview duration estimates and calculated at a rate specified by the first author's institution. We applied this protocol for all interviews. The user study was pre-approved by the ethics committee and conducted according to the procedures of the first author's institution, ensuring compliance with local regulations for respective locations.

3.3 Data Analysis

The entire audio material recorded (total time 17:26 h) was transcribed verbatim. The resulting data corpus was supplemented with observational data, e.g., written notes taken by the interviewer. The resulting data corpus was analysed by two researchers, following Blandford et al.’s pragmatic approach to thematic analysis [ 12 ]. In terms of procedure, this approach draws strongly on the 6-phase process described by Braun and Clarke [ 22 , 23 ]. The pragmatic approach is adapted to better fit inductive analysis concerning technological scenarios. The pragmatic approach encourages a more iterative and collaborative process, with more openness towards semantics-oriented analysis that also contains certain quantification. We applied the flexible coding approach advocated by Deterding and Waters [ 31 ], as the scope of our inquiry dictated the dataset to be investigated mostly on semantic and analytical levels. This method also enabled us to categorize selected accounts (e.g. usability issues encountered, levels of interconnectedness) using index codes to navigate their frequency, as suggested by Joffe [ 56 ].

First, we ran a familiarization round where the dataset was assigned to two coders (the 1st and 2nd authors), who open-coded the transcripts. We used an inductive coding strategy to obtain an initial set of codes. We then organised a collaborative code adjustment session, where we derived an initial coding tree based on interview script notes and the open-coded interviews. After the session, the entire data corpus was re-distributed across two researchers, who re-coded the set to align with the codebook and identify open ends. Our analysis process was anchored in iterative sessions of adjusting the coding collaboratively (along the guidelines of Cascio et al. [ 18 ]) and included an interpretive approach, therefore we refrained from establishing inter-rater reliability (as advised by Braun and Clarke [ 22 ] for reflexive TA). Having coded the entire dataset, we held collaborative analysis sessions to merge codes, identify candidate themes and formulate an initial meta-narrative. We initially developed nine candidate themes that reflected the semantic grouping of the codes. We later refined those themes in a collaborative session by merging and dividing the candidate themes with respect to their latent meaning, their role in the process of shaping a bespoke smart home ecosystem, and their presence within the narratives presented by the participants. For example, initial themes of Usability and Issues and Decision Factors and Adoption were decomposed and branched to inform themes concerning user motivations and conflicts within the ecosystem. In our analysis, we closely regarded the contexts where the participants emphasized particular factors or recalled related accounts. Then, both coders individually iterated through the dataset to reflect on and fine-tune the data assigned to respective themes. We concluded our process with another collaborative session, where we constructed the final five themes that describe the shaping and appropriation of smart home ecosystems from the perspective of their inhabitants. We share the resulting codebook, presenting the final structure of codes and their associations to individual themes, as supplementary material.

In our reflexive analysis process, we considered the photographic footage supplementary to the content of the interviews, as the visual materials depicted the devices, arrangements, and interfaces discussed in the interviews. Throughout our process, we illustrated particular participant quotes with related photographs to contextualize quotes and increase coders’ immersion with the dataset and provide reference to particular ecosystems. For data protection reasons (and in line with ethics review obtained), we used only the reproductions of photos taken at participants’ households in reporting our work.

4 RESULTS

In this section, we present the results of our study, organised into five major themes that were developed during the reflexive analysis process: Motivations & Benefits , Humans in the Ecosystem , Interconnectedness , Conflicts in the Ecosystem , and Trust & Sense of Control . We illustrate our results with quotes from the interviews, which are highlighted in italics and identified with participants’ IDs, as listed in table 1 .

We organise the narrative of this section following the chronological links in user's perspective on adopting and using the technology. First, we explore motivations and expectations that drive the decision to introduce SHT into their own household. We then reflect on the social context of living in a smart home, and how the technology needs to be adapted to satisfy different stakeholders. We discuss how all these needs are reflected in implementations of smart home ecosystems, with emphasis on finding the appropriate level of interconnectedness. We explore if and how insights concerning user requirements of particular technologies scale and pertain to interconnected ecosystems. Further, we contextualise these accounts, discussing conflicts, problems, and trust issues that emerged over time. Within all themes, we present the accounts of our participants that showcase common attitudes and contrasting stances to provide a holistic insight into the lived experience of smart home ecosystems.

4.1 Motivations & Benefits

Within this theme, we describe the accounts of adopting smart home technologies and how the ecosystems evolved to their current state. We study what motivated the decision to introduce technology into participants’ domestic spaces. We also reflect on the benefits of living in a tech-augmented household and present users’ visions on future expansion of their ecosystems.

4.1.1 What drives smart home adoption?. Starting with what drives the decision to introduce technology into one's own home, the most frequent reason mentioned was overall interest in new technology and enjoying gadgetry. Participants reported that their curiosity about SHT emerged from noticing it in popular culture and media. However, recommendations from friends and family and an opportunity to witness the systems in action often inspired decisions to acquire particular devices.

In my friend's parents’ home, they have a lot more of these gadgets. When he came back after summer recess, he decided to install these smart switches, shutters […]. We stayed with Supla 1 , as they are happy with them, so why complicate things. (P5)

Respondents emphasized that solving a particular problem in their home (e.g. optimizing heating routines) often accelerated their decision to acquire a piece of technology. Frequently mentioned motivations included increasing inhabitants’ awareness about their own home, an intrinsic desire to save resources or to make their household more eco-friendly. Another recurring motivation considered reducing time and effort spent on household duties. A few participants started building their ecosystem triggered by receiving SHTs as gifts, often provided by acquaintances who already had experience with SHT. We also witnessed decisions resulting from previous, negative experiences. One of the participants shared a story about introducing security measures as a result of a burglary they experienced:

Almost 2 years ago, we had a burglary. That motivated us to invest in an alarm system — we have motion sensors on the ground floor and in the basement, all windows have open/closed sensors. If something happened, the intervention group would come to check on the house. […] We also installed this camera to monitor the terrace window. It starts recording and sends a notification to your phone when it recognizes motion, and it has a really shiny lamp. […] We believe all these would discourage any attempts to break-in, and they help us sleep well. (P9)

We also inquired about user expectations towards different technologies and motivations to adopt more and more devices. The prevalent stance mentioned that the role of technology is to support its users in either frequent or meaningful activities. One of the participants elaborated “[...] when I cook, I check the recipes [...], watch videos, oh and the timers [...] I use voice control here a lot” (P17) . Participants appreciated features that made certain activities more convenient, enabled them to multitask, or reduced their time and effort spent on different household duties.

Our inquiry shows that while users have various imperatives to introduce technology in the home, real-life experiences often help navigate choices or encourage them to take action. This is true both for high-level choices (type of device or service) and for more refined selection of particular solutions.

4.1.2 Direct and consequential benefits. Inhabitants emphasized how technology enabled them to upgrade their favourite rituals or arrange spaces for leisure. One of the participants reported organising a “home cinema” and how it changed the way the family spends their free time together.

Right, so now [...] the screen [unveils] right here. The shutters go down, the night light is switched on, when the ceiling light is on, it goes off automatically and the beamer turns on. [...] You don't even need a remote, it comes at a command "Alexa, Cinema", [...] all you really have to do is press the Apple TV remote control, which is always on the bedside table. [...] Everything was built in so that we can have this as comfortable as possible. Yeah, I love that. (P20)

Considering the expansion of the ecosystem, most participants leaned towards gradual adoption. Having started with a single device to alleviate a specific issue, positive experience with technology inspired them to install further enhancements in their homes. A few participants considered consumer SHT as expensive, which slowed down their acquisition or prevented some purchases. When discussing the benefits of living in a SHE, two participants mentioned that they got used to routines that utilize their technologies. One of them recognised the occasional irritation, as “you need to fix something, and it takes time before you find time to do it all...” (P6) , yet expressed that “[...] I don't think I would want to live in a ’normal’ house any more” (P6) . Participants commented that while they recognise the effort required to maintain a functional smart home, they are willing to invest their resources for the sake of long-term, scalable everyday benefits. Three participants remarked that tinkering with technology is enjoyable, allowing them to increase their own competence and learn new things.

We noted that study participants often emphasized the high-level ecological benefits of introducing technology (e.g. saving time and resources, lifestyle change, supporting desires and enjoyed activities) over direct and particular gains of using various SHTs. While the performance of the ecosystem is often assessed on success in particular chores, it is the cumulative benefit to lifestyle that motivates the inhabitants to further expand their ecosystems.

4.2 Humans in the Ecosystem

As domestic environments, smart home ecosystems are literally inhabited, thus technology plays an ancillary role to the users that shape the ecosystem. Within this theme, we describe roles and attitudes of SHE inhabitants and reflect over their perspectives and sentiments. We study how these dynamics shape the distribution of labour and responsibility across household members. During the analysis, we recognized three dominant user roles — Smart Home Administrator , Active Users , and Passive Users . We uncovered the mutual perceptions of different roles by users of varying competency in operating the ecosystem, and we recognised factors that drive the evolution of their attitudes and consequently their roles in the smart home ecology.

4.2.1 Smart Home Administrator. Through our inquiry, we observed that the role of ’smart home administrator’ 2 emerges within the ecosystem's dynamic, which remains in line with past inquiries [ 99 , 104 ]. That person is the instigator of adopting new systems and takes on the responsibility for setting up, maintaining, and introducing the technology to other household members. Our interviewees emphasized that a key task of an SH-Admin is to provide backup plans and ensure that the operation of technology is accessible to all interested household members. One of the participants regarded technology supervision as “my input to housekeeping, along some other things” (P9) ; and mentioned that troubleshooting tasks became a recognized and respected household duty, treated similarly to cooking or cleaning.

When we asked SH-Admins about the effort it takes to perform their role, they confessed that even though the time investment may be significant, this is usually dictated by their own efforts for upgrading and optimising the ecosystem. Tinkering with SHT was often their hobby, therefore the time spent is perceived as pleasurable and rewarding. Participants reported that if they were to limit themselves solely to upkeep the time and effort invested would not be burdensome. We also observed that maintenance demands decrease over time as SH-admin's proficiency increases and the respective systems become fine-tuned.

“I've got to the stage now where the ecosystem is virtually maintenance-free. At the beginning, there were some minor bugs that annoyed me, like, I don't know, this light can sometimes turn on by itself on the stairs, but now it's not like that any more. [...]The only thing I do now is to keep track of updates to the home assistant system itself, as well as the plugin integration. [...] It takes 2 minutes once a week. It's not that I have to, I can choose not to do it. It would totally work even without it, but I want to stay up to date.” (P8)

The users also remarked that the technology-related responsibilities had made them more engaged in other household duties. The responsibility for troubleshooting different appliances had encouraged involvement and understanding of various activities, beyond the agreed labour distribution. Moreover, the admin duty was not necessarily limited to overseeing the ecosystem in their own household, but had stretched to ecosystems of close relatives. One of the participants showed us notifications about the washer and dryer at their parents’ home in a different city.

“I connected it to my app when we set up the new laundry. [...] Now I know when they wash, I know when they dry, when it's time to clean the filter. Plus then there's a whole statistic of energy consumption, the number of washes [...]. It could be useful when something goes wrong, at least I can deduce from the info in the app, what could be the cause.” (P3)

When reflecting over other members of their households, the SH-admins indicated two frequent reactions to any confusion or malfunction. They praised a proactive attitude, where users attempted basic maintenance procedures (e.g. restarting, connecting to power source, etc.) before seeking help. The contrary view was users being too afraid to act uninstructed, fearful of permanently damaging the system. The administrators appreciated the recognition of their effort and competence by co-inhabitants and felt rewarded when offering support. This finding contrasts with those of past works where the ‘smart home responsibles’ did not assist others [ 73 ], signifying that one of the challenges seen by Edwards and Grinter [ 33 ] was answered somewhat naturally by the social dynamics within the ecosystem.

While the role of SH-Admin is indispensable for introducing, expanding, and maintaining SHEs, we observed dynamics that indicate important social roles connected with their specific chores. Especially within family dynamics, particular members are often authority figures in particular domains (due to life experience or outstanding competency) [ 87 ]. Our observations indicate that technological competency may become another recognised area that will be considered an asset of an individual (like e.g. manual work abilities or cooking skills) that benefits the entire household.

4.2.2 Active Users. We heard multiple anecdotes describing how the SHT is used on a daily basis by a variety of users. What linked those accounts was the focus on benefits and making their lives more convenient. We noticed how users eagerly utilized features of respective systems for particular activities (e.g. managing reminders with a smart speaker or using a multi-room sound system while cleaning the house). Frequently, they had become primary users of respective technologies. In one of the households, we observed that one of the members acted as the main operator but had limited knowledge of the backbone of the ecosystem's operation.

“I can handle the whole house: turn on the heat recovery, watering, blinds, all functions I embrace. But when something happens, I need my husband to do something, because I'm not able to do it myself. I don't have a problem with that, because I have someone who knows [how to fix everything].” (P6)

Participants shared stories describing how they had gradually learned to conduct maintenance procedures and handled frequent problems on their own. Many of them expressed the desire to feel independent in their own home, for which a certain level of competence was deemed necessary. One of the participants mentioned that My husband always sets everything up and installs it, and then I ask him: how it works? (P20) and reflected over her own way to proficiency, stating that: [...] actually now I have a good overview, it took a while [...] until I knew exactly which app I had to use when and how. (P20) Moreover, we noted some users had planned to gradually take over the supervision of their favourite devices from the person considered the SH-Admin for their household. This suggests that the role of an active user would evolve over time towards that of a pro-user, making the current perception of user roles in the ecosystem [ 95 ] obsolete.

However, those who aspired to engage in administration duties had often been discouraged by the high complexity of the ecosystem. Despite having access to all settings, users decided not to explore them, as they had felt overwhelmed by the multitude of options. Their aspirations to take over certain admin duties remained unfulfilled, limiting their ability to deal with potential problems.

The accounts of our participants indicate that non-admin users primarily want the technology to support their independence and the ability to ”take care of oneself”. Lack of basic competency in operating the systems may potentially cause embarrassment for aspiring users. To this end, it is essential for both the SH-admins and the systems themselves to actively support user development.

4.2.3 Passive Users. The third distinctive attitude we encountered is characterised by explicit hesitation towards enhancing the home with various technologies, i.e. passive usage [ 40 ]. Notably, passive users tended not to actively oppose the presence of various SHTs, but their reservations had made them limit or avoid encounters with the technology. Our interview data point towards the basis for timidity being a limited view of the benefits, as passive users often considered the systems expendable. Other discouraging factors mentioned were overwhelming complexity, problems with non-intuitive UIs, and limited digital competencies. Interestingly, the age of participants was deemed to be a marginal concern, usually mentioned in the context of seniors.

On the other hand, we collected accounts where passive users successfully transitioned towards a more proactive approach. Two participants shared that initially sceptical users had significantly changed their habits due to adoption of Smart TVs.

“We got a Xiaomi MiBox to upgrade our old TV set; and it has definitely changed the way we use it. First of all, we gave up the satellite TV that we had been using at home for 20-plus years. We've given that up, we've completely switched to just internet TV, we pay for selected different services that we're actually interested in. [...]” (P1)

These accounts suggest that in the long run exposure to technology that offers a direct benefit can successfully shape users’ attitudes and encourage them to more proactive use. The same participant observed that his parents, who were initially sceptical, started exploring other features of the Smart TV on their own. He was surprised as his “mum started using Spotify on the MiBox. My dad recently installed and subscribed to Eurosport and some other service, [...]” (P1) . The participant noticed that both new users clearly convinced themselves to use the Smart TV more regularly and were more eager to give other SHTs a chance in their home.

Only a few participants mentioned household members who strongly rejected using certain technologies or refused to participate in the smart home ecosystem entirely. According to their accounts, these people presented strong, well-argued viewpoints on why they decided not to participate in the smart home ecology. Major reasons mentioned privacy (both within the household and towards technology vendors), considering SHTs expendable and rejecting participating in their costs (P5, shared flat scenario), or personal views on making technology ubiquitous in all domains of life.

“My cousin, for example, he was completely against all this technology, had his own reasons for it, but there are also people who wouldn't want to have it at all.” (P16)

We observed that passive users understand the consequences of their choices to limit their participation in the ecosystem. However, as the ubiquity of these systems grows, complete avoidance of encountering SHTs will soon become unlikely  [ 70 ]. In our study, we interviewed SH-admins that were forced to moderate their household ecosystems to satisfy the needs of co-inhabitants of various attitudes. In their accounts, we noticed they recognised the forced responsibility to respect and serve the needs of more reserved inhabitants.

4.3 Interconnectedness

When probing user attitudes towards the interconnectedness of their ecosystems, we observed a mixture of approaches that varied in what was considered the ’right’ strategy to link different systems. Within this theme, we discuss the extent to which our participants’ homes formed mutually-aware families of devices, and what benefit they saw in having their devices interconnected. We explore different strategies to grouping devices into subsets, reflecting various affinities, ownership arrangements, and shared values within the home.

4.3.1 Interconnection strategies. Nine of our participants envisioned a fully interconnected home as a goal for their ecosystem. Interviewees expressed the opinion that having all controls integrated into a single, accessible interface is the ultimate objective for shaping their ecosystem. These respondents emphasized the necessity for newly acquired devices to be compatible with the already existing infrastructure.

“I can't imagine having to start up a separate app for each device, whether it'd be on my phone, computer or wherever [...]. I would like to have one Command Centre that you are able to access from any device. So that everyone in the house could find the switches for all the lights in one place, know what the temperature is in a particular room, make it warmer or colder etc.” (P1)

The tendency to unify various systems was increased in households that planned to significantly expand their ecosystems. Participants appreciated the ability to integrate their non-smart devices into the ecosystems through using peripherals like smart switches and transceivers — three participants transformed their old stereo setup and made it compatible with their Echo devices. Another major advantage of an interconnected ecosystem was the ability to establish custom procedures coordinating different systems around the home. Users acknowledged the comfort of automating different functions using time-related and conditional procedures. When discussing ways in which a complex ecosystem is made accessible to all inhabitants, the Smart Home combo-apps (like Apple HomeKit, Google Home or Tuya 3 ) were considered most convenient and effortless. Moreover, the ability to integrate all devices under one software platform was often considered a benefit itself, and a relevant factor influencing future purchase decisions.

“I like first of all that it's practical because you have one interface for everything; you can swipe between the rooms, and you can see what's in that room. You have favourites where you can access them quickly, and generally I'm a big fan of not having to use a hundred apps, yeah that's the advantage of it.” (P20)

However, among those users who sought total interconnection of numerous devices, we observed a tendency to apply semantic grouping within the control software. Organising devices into functional or locational subsets was reported to reduce complexity and make management more efficient. Grouping was also favoured by those users interested in accessing only selected devices within the home, e.g. only within the shared spaces of their house.

While the vision of a unified smart home was tempting for many of our interviewees, not all of them found the benefits of total interconnectedness to be clear. Some users were afraid that such an arrangement may lead to unnecessarily complex and time-consuming maintenance of the ecosystem. Participants told us they missed a functional link between different devices and contexts, which dictated their decision on what (not) to interconnect. One of the participants indicated that “It depends on what it's used for, for example [systems] in the bedroom or the dressing room. [...] I think you would manage it separately. So it depends on the context” (P14). This view was also reflected in different strategies concerning interconnecting or separating elements in the ecosystem. Grouping the devices based on their location in the home was the most frequent strategy. Therefore, such households were equipped with one or more sub-ecosystems, consisting of a few co-located devices (see fig. 2 ). Participants found this strategy to be favourable, as they perceived different rooms in their homes as separate spaces, adapted to best fit various everyday activities. Seven participants emphasized that assigning a particular purpose to respective rooms brings harmony and order in their everyday home life, and that interconnected technology might disrupt this arrangement.

The bedroom is a different space that has its own requirements and I wouldn't see a necessity there, certainly not, even a need for coupling it up so that, for example, from here one could control a TV or something in the living room. [...] We didn't really install much here [the bedroom] as it would bring down our Zen. (P10)

This approach also facilitates different patterns of space ownership present among the inhabitants, enabling the ecosystem to support inter-member privacy. Visiting multi-generational households, we observed that respecting the private space of other cohabitants was regarded as essential to keep their relationships healthy. We explore this issue in more detail within Trust & Sense of Control theme.

Another frequent strategy to managing multiple devices in the ecosystem is to organise them into functionally-related groups. Participants separated devices that belong to different activities and contexts — e.g. entertainment devices were separated from technologies used for household upkeep (see figure 2 ). The motivation for such composition was the perceived benefits of interconnecting devices of similar purpose, but also concerned connecting devices depending on local social contexts, such as household labour distribution.

“When my parents are staying with us, we give them access to the TVs, music system, of course lights, shutters, door lock. But I don't think it's necessary for them to play with my smart scale or fitness gear, or re-program our Roomba. In fact, I doubt they would like to [access these systems].” (P6)

To support these strategies, administration tools for smart homes need to facilitate customisation of grouping and linking devices. As the complexity of smart homes tends to grow [ 70 ], administration tools will need to support subset-level management on both logical and interface levels. In our interviews, we recorded accounts that mentioned workarounds for vendor-provided management interfaces, as some users struggled when forced to navigate tools offering complete admin-level controls (see section 4.2.2 ).

4.3.2 Intentional separation. We also noted accounts of intentional separation of smart devices. This strategy was observed mostly in ecosystems consisting of few devices with diverse functionalities. Their owners’ view was that interconnectedness would bring more trouble and maintenance effort than any expected benefit. Another reason to keep certain technologies separate was their very particular and crucial purpose, coupled with fear that cooperation with other technologies could impair its performance. One of the participants gave an example of a breath monitor used by their infant child.

“One of our daughters experienced nocturnal apnoea, so we got this breath monitor and use it now with our 3rd child. It detects delicate movement on the bed and triggers an alarm [...] This thing must be failsafe. The child forgets to breathe, and it may suffocate, and this thing is to prevent it. [...] There's no way I'm going to complicate or connect anything here, because one mistake and the consequences could be known what...” (P10)

Other reasons for maintaining separation within the ecosystem concerned pragmatic issues, such as using dedicated remote controls (P3, P4, P18) or regarding certain smart functions irrelevant(P10). Four interviewees discussed the differences in practicality of various services between flats and houses. They viewed the extended remote control and security-related features less relevant for people who live in multifamily tenements. However, they speculated about scenarios where they would change their approach to shaping the ecosystem should they move to a larger residence.

Our data corpus shows that refraining from interconnecting particular devices is most often determined by the desire to avoid additional workload or circumvent potential risks, rather than by direct benefit of separation. However, these fears could potentially be alleviated by improving reliability of home networks [ 42 ], and limiting necessary ”digital housekeeping” [ 46 ]. Simultaneously, ecosystem vendors need to better communicate the gains of interconnection of all devices within the home.

4.4 Conflicts in the Ecosystem

In the preceding sections, we showed how different stakeholders shaped the formation of smart home ecosystems. However, implementation often evolves over time. Within this theme, we reflect on participants’ stories about problem implementation, missed decisions, or unexpected consequences of adopting particular technologies to their smart home landscapes. We show how long-term experiences and evolving life situations changed their expectations and decisions concerning SHTs.

Our participants shared stories that exemplified different conflicts that emerged after adopting their first SHTs and during the evolution of their ecosystem. They reported mismatches between technology and interior design of respective homes as a common problem. This issue was most often reported in the context of robot vacuum cleaners under-performing or requiring additional attention.

“I need to take the carpets up and then chase him [the robot vacuum cleaner] through. Every now and then I have to move the shoe rack so that he also vacuums underneath it.” (P18)

However, the participants expressed determination to adapt their spaces so the technology can serve its purpose. Respondents mentioned the aesthetics of many devices being unfit for interiors designed differently from modern, international style. They worried about harmoniously fitting pieces of SHTs into rustic or traditional interiors.

Other users described how technology clashes with their pets. Respondents unanimously considered their pets as household members and ensured that the technology installed does not cause harm or distress to their animals. One of the participants recalled that the presence of pets had determined some trade-offs in configuring the ecosystem.

“It's [corridor light] here so that when the parents get up at night and go to the toilet, [...], you just walk over, and it turns on, but it also has its downsides, because if the cat comes in the night, it also turns on. [...] my room, for example, has no [alarm] detectors at all, there are only detectors on the windows, so the cat can walk freely when we are not at home or sleeping.” (P9)

One other participant reported abandoning their garden lights and watering system, as their dog had repeatedly damaged the deployed units (P4). Further, our participants recognised that the practicality of different devices might vary as their lifestyle changed over time. Many participants limited or altered their use of SHTs when children had joined the family. However, advancing into this new life phase often also motivated purchases of child-dedicated systems, like home cameras or smart nightlights. Changes in occupants’ situations was a frequently recurring issue, as participants reported that e.g. members of the family who had dual residence due to their professional situation struggled with getting used to switching ecosystems (P8).

Inhabitants of heavily automated ecosystems often raised issues concerning conflicts between different agents. Sensor-based solutions were deemed particularly problematic. One of the participants described how an error in the control app of a vacuuming robot had caused significant nuisance in the neighbourhood.

“My wife had an app, somehow the time was changed, well because there was some Chinese software of sort and 4.00am was seen as a 4.00pm. We were sleeping upstairs and the alarm went off. [...] We've seen on a camera that the vacuum robot started to move around. [...] Our neighbours asked if something had happened the next day.” (P2)

Another participant reported losing trust in the measurements of an air purifier, which indicated humidity as extremely high levels of pollution, which led them to abandoning its use in auto-mode.

We asked about other reasons to abandon SHTs. Interviewees indicated that certain technologies (e.g. smart speakers in intercom mode, programmable coffee machine) irritated other members of the household or required too much effort. Malfunctioning was a frequent cause of abandonment, as users did not seek ways to repair faulty gear.

Considering the complexity of in-home dynamics, alternating routines, and overlapping (and sometimes conflicting) goals of household members [ 17 ], conflicts within the ecosystem are likely to occur, as human error is an inherent part of its interaction [ 52 ]. It seems vital to the development of smart home systems that they eliminate errors and blunders that originate solely from technological limitations. Increasing system reliability will contribute to establishing trust towards the artifact ecologies as a whole [ 14 ].

4.5 Trust & Sense of Control

Within this theme, we reflect on experiences that lead to increasing or losing trust towards domestic technologies. We illustrate how various technologies were utilized in individual and shared use
contexts. Participants shared their reflections concerning long-term experience with technology, and how it strengthened or handicapped their trust towards their ecosystem. They also commented on their sense of control over their home, and how it affected their ways of appropriating the technology.

The reliability of the ecosystems was considered a major issue. Inhabitants’ stories revealed that bad experiences or spotting an error in a system's operations quickly led to losing trust in a system. This was especially relevant for devices conducting a form of assessment. One participant discussed how he had abandoned his smart scale, losing confidence in its readings.

We have this smart scale, but I'm no longer using it. [...] I had myself measured with professional gear, and in some parameters like visceral fat, the difference was double. So if someone just needs some hint, it's probably ok, but not for an athlete. (P13)

Participants deliberated about sharing devices with other inhabitants. While they admitted that sharing devices is an integral part of living in an ecosystem, they expressed a sense of ownership over particular (sub-)systems. This ownership was often exercised through locating the devices in their personal spaces in the home, or taking over administration duties from the primary SH-admin. In this context, inter-member privacy was a recurrent theme. Users commented that while they did not mind sharing the device itself, they were hesitant to share detailed usage data with others. One of the participants commented on purposefully locating the smart scale in her bedroom.

“P4: We've had a smart scale for some time now. My parents come to my room and do the measurements, which sometimes irritates me. Everyone has their own account, because it is kind of personal info.

Researcher: If you don't mind me asking, why isn't it located in some shared space, if those visits irritate you?

P4: Because it's mine. It was a gift.”

The hesitation to share and merge personal data was also discussed in the context of multimedia and entertainment. Users commented they preferred not to share e.g. accounts in streaming services, as their recommendations would have been influenced by others, and it could have shared information about their private leisure routines.

Multiple participants indicated discomfort about having to depend solely on smart solutions. They doubted the stability of the SHTs and worried about access to basic functionality in case of an error. This fear was reflected in implementing redundant controls, which enabled control of basic features of key systems individually. This approach was also praised for providing convenient alternatives for passive users.

All these [lighting] work so that you can use them without this smart aspect, if something went wrong. [...] Exactly, it's an add-on, but it's not like it completely replaces the ability to use traditional controls. My parents wouldn't like it. (P8)

Participants discussed their dependence on external services provided by SHT vendors. SH-Admins shared their concerns on handing over control of their system to external vendors (P1, P16, P17). Dependence on servers or services run by third parties was reported to be a turning point for moving towards self-developed ecosystems (P8, P16). Administrators admitted the additional effort necessary to ensure reliable functionality, yet considered it a long-term investment.

On a pragmatic note, users praised the flexibility in choosing the member technologies of their ecosystem, not being limited by compatibility policy of particular vendors. This approach was also often recognized as more cost-efficient, as SH-admins could choose from a wider range of manufacturers. However, they admitted that choosing a rich variety of devices might limit integration of controls. Moreover, SH-admins often digressed that developing their own ecosystem helps them engage in their hobby, as discussed in Humans in the Ecosystem .

In contrast, SH-admins that followed vendor-specific solutions to organise their ecosystem cherished the ease of setup and possible expansion. Limitations concerning the range of choices were not deemed cumbersome, rather users welcomed the co-existence of multiple subfamilies of systems. A few participants complained about insufficient customization, while also noticing that the single-vendor track supports gradual expansion of the ecosystem. One of the participants emphasized that single-vendor systems encouraged interconnecting devices, due to ease of setup.

These Amazon Echos, they are there when you buy them with your own Amazon account, so they are already as good as set up, so then suddenly they welcome you and everything is done, so to speak, just plug them in and you're done. (P11)

Inhabitants of single-standard ecosystems also admitted they lacked understanding of the inner workings, but did not consider it to be a serious problem. While few users expressed some concern about privacy, they had considered the potential risks acceptable, given the benefits of their SHTs. Our interview data indicate that users accept that elements of the ecosystem act as a "black box", given that the responsibility for their proper operation is also covered by the vendor.

5 DISCUSSION

In this section, we identify major findings informed by cross-theme analysis of our results. We discuss user strategies and behaviours that emerged across the presented themes in our data set, indicating high-level phenomena that shape living in the ecosystem. Informed by these observations, we formulate five actionable takeaways that can inform the design of future smart home ecosystems.

5.1 Ecosystems evolve by gradual expansion, instead of being one-stop platforms

Our findings on Motivations & Benefits show how the interest in developing a smart home ecosystem develops over time. The majority of participants started building their ecosystem from scratch, beginning with a single device that alleviated a particular problem, or that was acquired as a gift or gadgetry. Inhabitants who then experienced the benefits of everyday use often became more interested in SHTs over time, leading them to acquire new devices. We observed that subsequent decisions to acquire SHTs were usually more conscious and backed by the inhabitants’ own research into what solution would best fit the already existing infrastructure. These considerations sometimes involved replacement of currently owned devices to ensure the desired level of interconnectedness between all the elements. Interestingly, only one of the interviewees inhabits a pre-planned, one-stop ecosystem (P6).

Problems with unified smart home platforms were pointed out in past work. Significant barriers for users were found to be the necessity of significant one-time investment [ 95 ] and the challenge of planning for unfamiliar technology deployment [ 73 ]. Moreover, inhabitants’ needs, preferences, and lifestyles change over time, reshaping user requirements, as shown by our interview data and past work [ 53 ]. Accounts in section Conflicts in the Ecosystem exemplify how the (dis)appearance of household members or clashes between systems change the demands in ways difficult to foresee. Our inquiry found that the ability to flexibly shape and appropriate one's own technological environment is of particular importance. While the majority of technological barriers for universal interconnectedness are already alleviated via standardisation of communication protocols [ 42 ], ecosystem vendors often discourage this approach. Platforms that share access to their ecosystems more openly (this trend is especially visible for smart speakers [ 38 , 79 ]) often influence user decisions by limiting support of devices from unallied vendors. Future ecosystems should be open-ended, considering users’ preference to actively shape their own home ecology.

5.2 Social, spatial, and functional contexts shape ecosystem interconnectedness

Developers and researchers of smart home technology have long envisioned pervasive, interconnected, self-aware technology [ 4 ] that adapts itself to the needs and desires of its residents [ 51 ]. Not all users, however, share this vision as their ultimate goal. Our study instead showed that the desired level of interconnectedness is actively shaped by inhabitants’ motivations, social contexts, and the overall idea of organising home life. As indicated in Interconnectedness , technology is subservient to the vision of the house imagined by its hosts. Therefore, we observed different approaches to establishing the association between systems, dictated by their social, spatial, or functional affinity. We echo past findings that emphasized that smart home ecosystems reflect social contexts in the home [ 39 ], but we expand these arguments by indicating that the functional links between devices and spatial distribution are equally relevant for developing grouping strategies. Moreover, our
observations in Trust and Sense of Control demonstrate that users’ reservations concerning reliability of technology may encourage separation of systems when infallibility is of particular importance (e.g. the breathing monitor scenario). These findings highlight that smart home technology should support grouping and separation by design. Furthermore, it is necessary that future smart homes better support technological evolution as a response to changing routines, overlapping user goals [ 17 ], and long-term changes in social ecologies [ 14 ]. A promising direction to address these challenges is to encourage agency of individual users within the ecology [ 69 ] and reflection on how their domestic ecosystems affect social context within the home [ 10 ]. The latter aspect is especially relevant for users acting as local SH-admins.

5.3 User roles and attitudes evolve over time

Our inquiry into the roles of Humans in the Ecosystem showed that participants’ attitudes toward SHT were dynamically changing over time as a result of everyday experiences with various systems. Notably, we observed the strong positive impact when the system supports frequent or meaningful activities. We recognized a dynamic wherein exposure to the benefits of technology motivated inhabitants to develop their competence and involvement in interacting with the ecosystem. This shift was observed both for active and passive users. We witnessed active users who had gradually taken over more complex maintenance tasks from their SH-admins, as well as passive users who, spurred by their positive experiences, had started to explore new features. These observations are parallel to past findings indicating how trust in technologies shapes long-term use [ 64 ]. We also expand the argument of Geeng and Roesner [ 40 ], suggesting that evolution of user attitudes may be catalysed not only by changes in occupancy structure, but also by relocation, changing employment status, or aging.

Further, we noted that unexpected limitations and negative experiences are likely to initiate rapid decline in use, which may result in abandonment of certain systems. Past works indicated that abandonment is often caused by technology being a bad fit with the social contexts and routines of users [ 102 ]. Our observations in Conflicts in the Ecosystem and Trust and Sense of Control extend this viewpoint, recognising that, while participants understand that particular technologies have their limitations, their trust in the technology is broken by unkept promises and errors and this may cause a rapid decline in usage. Future smart home systems may benefit from being more understandable and transparent for the user. Trust towards smart home ecologies is likely to be strengthened by limiting the ’black box’ effect in interacting with the ecosystem and enabling the users to understand the logical links between user input, pervasive data, and system responses [ 64 , 89 ]. A large body of work explored this aspect in the context of smart speakers [ 6 , 9 , 78 ], the results of which can also benefit the larger ecologies. Further research is necessary to navigate optimal modalities and points of interaction for delivery of self-explainable interaction in the home.

5.4 Many owners, ‘one admin to rule them all’

While the role of a designated SH-admin was clearly visible across the investigated households, our findings concerning Trust and Sense of Control indicate that the sense of ownership is
distributed among all inhabitants. Our participants expressed a varying sense of responsibility for particular systems according to whom the system “belonged”. Past work indicated that ownership helps organise shared use of personal agents [ 68 ], and we extend this view to other SHTs. Ownership was often exercised through dislocating the devices into personal spaces, becoming the primary user, and seeking competency in configuring the system. These observations correspond with our findings on Interconnectedness , which highlighted the role of different spaces in the home bearing particular social contexts. Respect for co-inhabitants’ private space and property was seen as especially important for non-family arrangements [ 40 ] and multi-generational households [ 32 ]. However, we found that taking responsibility for selected technologies did not imply challenging the authority or competence of the SH-admin, but was considered cordial and supportive. Furthermore, our data highlighted the role of reliability and user feedback in establishing trust towards artifact ecologies. While ubiquity and pervasiveness are desired, an ecosystem's robust performance is assessed based on both the success of atomic actions and its operation as a whole. To this end, designers should consider effective communication of the state of a smart home in ways that would support multi-user operation and that would not lead to an increase in the ”digital housekeeping” workload [ 37 , 84 ].

5.5 “Use my device, but don't look at my data”

Living in the smart home ecosystem brings concerns about how much data one is willing to share with co-inhabitants. Our findings, grouped under Interconnectedness and Trust and Sense of Control indicate that, while household members are used to sharing devices with each other, they would hesitate to exchange and merge their data. The rationale for this view ranges from pragmatic matters to more intrinsic reasons. Users indicated that unified access may prevent them from concurrent use of different devices, or impair performance of recommendation engines, which are part of many entertainment platforms. Inter-member privacy was a recurring theme, as participants preferred not to have others glancing over their shoulders on their leisure routines or health-related data. These findings echo past inquiries concerning user perceptions on interpersonal privacy at home [ 20 , 40 , 44 ], which indicated that users prefer certain activities not to leave a trace [ 21 ]. We find these considerations especially relevant for interconnected ecosystems, as the multitude of agents may potentially increase the odds of inadvertently sharing information meant to remain private. Furthermore, providing users with tools to consciously manage data within the ecosystem is necessary to limit the emergence of separation strategies, which, when used as workarounds, are likely to impair the ecosystem as a whole.

5.6 Design Implications

In this section, we follow our major, cross-theme observations and formulate five actionable design implications that we hope will inform the research and development of future smart home ecosystems. While all these areas require further empirical research to determine the optimal solutions, our findings indicate directions of particular importance to designing smart homes with the ecological perspective in mind.

Smart home technologies should support a “mix and match” model of interconnectedness. This model offers the significant benefit of enabling users to shape their ecosystems over time with modular units, which offers flexibility in functionality and infrastructure. Manufacturers and designers need to recognise that users might interweave multiple devices from various vendors and need to support this practice by design. The trend to acknowledge these customer expectations is already visible in the rising popularity of meta-vendor systems (such as Tuya 4 or Smart Life 5 ), which enable smart home admins to create networks of devices from various manufacturers. In the long run, establishing standards for universal interconnectivity of smart home devices would empower creators of D-I-Y or personalised solutions to address niche requirements. The most recent works led by Connectivity Standards Alliance [ 24 ] show that current interconnectivity standards such as ZigBee [ 35 ] or open-ended vendor protocols for ecosystem integrations (such as Apple HomeKit or Samsung SmartThings) do not sufficiently address the need for accessible and scalable smart home frameworks. To this end, a new royalty-free standard called “Matter” [ 25 ] was developed (v. 1.0 released in October 2022 [ 26 ]) by a consortium of leading smart home vendors. Wide-scale implementation of such protocols will empower users with more agency to shape their ecosystems, enabling them to fulfil their visions of highly interconnected environments [ 4 , 51 ]. As domestic ecologies are defined by users’ lifestyles and personal goals [ 13 ], providing the technological means to orchestrate multi-device systems is vital to enable users to define and appropriate their domestic ecologies.

SHEs should support grouping and separation by design, allowing users to merge and separate individual systems according to individual needs. We believe that SHT designers should consider various approaches to interconnectedness when developing their vendor-specific ecosystems, providing users with agency to shape subfamilies of devices and distribute control over them. To address this issue, more attention needs to be drawn towards dashboard designs, which should efficiently support multi-user arrangements and spatial representations [ 37 ]. Customizable ecosystems need to offer visual depictions of affinities and links between devices. To date, such data presentation is usually limited to one-stop environments (such as offered by Somfy 6 or Grenton 7 ). Furthermore, future intelligent user interfaces (IUIs) may benefit from developing longitudinal awareness of social interactions within the home. Past work showed that proactivity and deepened context-awareness already proved beneficial for individual users [ 83 ]. SHTs that would be truly pervasive and (social) context-aware have the significant potential to aid authentication [ 82 ] and personalisation [ 5 ]. To prove successful within larger ecologies [ 13 , 97 ], however, these advancements need to be developed with the goal of empowering users to shape their own domestic interactive environments.

Smart home vendors should stress the direct benefits that their solutions bring to everyday life. To provide a satisfying and enjoyable experience over long periods of time, smart home ecosystems need to benefit their users in different life situations. The visibility of these benefits is necessary to form long-term engagement and motivate potential expansion of the ecosystem. Simultaneously, it is essential to communicate openly the limitations of respective technologies. Explainability and perception of both advantages and drawbacks of one's own setup are necessary to develop trust over time [ 64 ] and enable users to effectively debug their environments [ 15 ]. Future designs should actively encourage developing one's own proficiency, motivating users to unveil the system's full potential instead of developing potentially harmful workarounds [ 48 , 53 , 70 ]. Past work noted that the organic evolution of user needs and accounting for exceptions and improvisation are an inherent part of smart home interaction [ 17 ], which should be supported by design. To this end, designers may consider data visualisation strategies from personal computing that encourage analytical reflection and consecutive conscious change [ 10 ]. Future research may consider agent-based modelling to explore characteristics of homes as multi-user environments [ 84 ] and look at ecosystems from the perspective of device-, information-, and communication ecologies [ 69 ].

SHEs should recognise hierarchies in the home and reflect them in their configuration. To aid users in exercising the distributed ownership of devices, we encourage developers to recognize different hierarchies of responsibility in the home, and to enable users to reflect these arrangements within the configuration of their ecosystem. To this end, SH-admins need to be provided with usable administration tools that would reach beyond simple access restriction. Atomic moderation of privileges and data access, including forfeiting admin access to certain information, may positively impact user trust in the ecosystem as a whole through respecting their autonomy and decision-making [ 27 , 72 ]. Furthermore, boosting the sense of ownership and control over particular systems may limit users’ imperative to explore potentially harmful workarounds [ 36 , 48 ]. Past research showed such tendencies emerge for personal devices [ 48 ], which may prove especially dangerous concerning networked devices, often related to security of a given household [ 52 ]. Future research is necessary to develop tools and strategies that would couple efficient ecosystem administration with distributed control over multiple users.

SHTs should recognise their users and help them shape how they share their data. To ensure that smart home ecosystems facilitate respecting inter-member privacy, we propose that future SHTs offer mechanisms that would distinguish particular users in the household and enable them to actively shape how they wish to share and restrict their data. We advocate that processing sensitive and personal data within the ecosystem be distributed and require individual user's consent. Further, these mechanisms must not be easily overridden using SH-admin access. While the potential hazards of external privacy infringements were widely studied in the HCI field [ 1 , 108 ], respecting the boundaries within the household garnered limited attention [ 40 ]. Further research is necessary to establish the optimal strategies between usable administration and restricting access to information of individual activity. These requirements may be supported by increasing the role of all individual inhabitants in the socio-technical ecology [ 14 ], empowering them to actively shape what information is shared between particular agents and users. However, given that the relationships between the members are determined by various interpersonal factors [ 87 ], further research is necessary to understand ways in which interactive systems can support (but not force) mutual trust between smart home users.

5.7 Limitations

First, we note that the majority of our interviewees were the SH-admins of their respective households. This implies that we inherently report primarily on their perspectives on the ecosystems and the different stakeholders involved. While understanding the views of those who are most involved and observant of the ecosystems is highly relevant, researching stories of more passive use and abandonment is of equal importance. In our particular case, we considered the views of less involved users only by proxy, as inviting a researcher into their home was naturally more attractive to people who were actively interested in SHT and wanted to talk about it. We also notice that households inhabited by families strongly outnumbered accounts of different household compositions, which impacted our results. Further, we cannot underestimate the cultural and economic factors in the perceptions of systems studied in this paper. The participants were limited to Central Europe and all resided in cities, considering themselves to be households with middle-class income. We expect that the experiences would differ among users from culturally-different geographies, within rural communities, and among users in more challenging economic situations.

6 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we reported on an empirical study of the lived experiences in smart home ecosystems. Drawing on contextual inquiry methods, we conducted an explorative interview study (N=20) with SHT users in their homes. Our interview data analysis revealed five main themes that discuss how different motivations, key benefits, and user roles determine the desired level of interconnectedness in the home, and how these user-defined ecosystems get fine-tuned through everyday encounters and long-term reflections. Our results show that transforming the home into a fully ubiquitous, self-aware computer ecosystem is not the ultimate goal for all users. In contrast, we found that modern smart homes are developed in a gradual way, shaped by social, spatial, and functional contexts emerging in the home. Our results indicate that user roles within the ecosystem are dynamic as they develop a sense of ownership over particular technologies. Moreover, we noted that facilitating the balance between sharing and concealing user data is of particular importance. Following our findings, we formulate five implications that we hope will inform the design of future smart home technologies, so they better serve their role in domestic ecologies. Our work contributes insights on the dynamics and appropriation of smart home ecosystems by their inhabitants.
