‘Specially For You’ – Examining the Barnum Effect's Influence on the Perceived Quality of System Recommendations

Pang Suwanaposee , Computer Science and Software Engineering, University of Canterbury, New Zealand, pang.suwanaposee@pg.canterbury.ac.nz
Carl Gutwin , University of Saskatchewan, Canada, gutwin@cs.usask.ca
Zhe Chen , School of Psychology, Speech and Hearing, University of Canterbury, New Zealand, zhe.chen@canterbury.ac.nz
Andy Cockburn , University of Canterbury, New Zealand, andy@cosc.canterbury.ac.nz

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3580656
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

The ‘Barnum effect’ is a psychological phenomenon under which people assign higher quality ratings to personality descriptions developed ‘specially for you’ than the same descriptions described as ‘generally true of people.’ This effect suggests that recommender interfaces could elevate the perceived quality of recommendations simply by indicating that they are explicitly personalised. We therefore conducted a crowd-sourced experiment (n=492) that examined the perceived quality of personalised versus non-personalised movie recommendations for good and bad movies – importantly, the actual recommendations were identical, and were merely presented as being either personalised or not. Contrary to the Barnum effect, results showed numerically lower mean quality scores for personalised recommendations, but with no significant difference. Our findings suggest that Barnum-like effects of personalisation have at most a small influence on perceived quality, and that designers should not rely on this effect to improve user experience (despite online design guidance suggesting the opposite).

Keywords: AI recommendations , Barnum effect , subjective experience

1 INTRODUCTION

Software-generated content recommendations are an increasingly common part of interaction. Examples are abundant, including recommendations on video and audio streaming services (such as Netflix, Youtube, Amazon Prime, and podcast apps), article recommendations on subscription news services such as New York Times, and shopping recommendations on services such as Amazon.com. Beyond content recommendations, other forms of recommendations are also common in user interfaces, including recommendations for interface settings and tools (e.g., templates and themes in applications such as PowerPoint or Keynote) and tips for improving interface knowledge or performance (e.g., ‘tip of the day’ features). Maximising users’ perceived quality of recommendations is an important design objective as users are unlikely to continue to use recommendations that they perceive to be low quality. More generally, maximising the user's quality assessment of recommendations is likely to positively contribute to the user's subjective experience, and is therefore important for design.

Research from the judgement and decision making literature suggests an opportunity for positively influencing users’ quality assessments of system recommendations. The “Barnum effect” (also known as the “Forer effect”) is a cognitive bias under which people give high quality ratings to generic personality descriptions when they believe that the descriptions have been personally tailored for them. In a 1949 study, Forer [ 12 ] demonstrated that subjects awarded high scores to purportedly ‘personalised’ personality tests that actually consisted of generic statements largely gleaned from an astrology book, and in 1972 Snyder and Larson [ 23 ] demonstrated that subjects awarded higher scores to general personality descriptions when they were labelled ‘specifically for you’ than when labelled ‘generally true of people.’ Related findings have been extensively replicated (see Dickson and Kelly [ 9 ] for a review).

Psychologists’ studies of the Barnum effect have focussed on personality descriptions, with generalisation to domains including astrology and fortune telling. However, it is plausible that Barnum-like effects will further generalise to users’ perceptions of system recommendations, with users perceiving recommendations to be higher quality when they are purportedly tailored especially for the user. Indeed, several websites related to interface design directly refer to the Barnum effect's influence on user experience: “Users prefer content that is curated specially for them” 1 ; “[t]he Barnum Effect is everywhere. The use of a ‘recommended for you,’...or ‘you might also like’ feature on Facebook, Spotify, Twitter, and Netflix gives the illusion of a tailored product” 2 ; “technology companies effectively use the Barnum effect to provide software users with the illusion of a personalized product.” 3

If this online information is correct, then Barnum-like effects could have several important implications for the design and evaluation of recommendation user interfaces. First, the distortion of perceived quality could provide a tool for designers to maximise the user's subjective experience. Second (and conversely), the failure to properly comprehend the Barnum effect could cause designers to impair users’ subjective experience by failing to exploit its potential gains. Third, it could create a confound in interpreting results from evaluations of interfaces that offer recommendations. For example, if Barnum-like effects cause users to perceive personalised recommendations as better than the same recommendations when non-personalised, then any study attempting to assess the effectiveness of a system that factually personalises its recommendations would need to control for the effect of merely presenting its recommendations as personalised.

While there are reasons for suspecting Barnum-like effects will apply in interaction (and design websites stating that they do), there are also reasons why the Barnum effect might not generalise to system recommendations. In particular, studies into Barnum effects have largely examined the perceived quality of personality descriptions. System content recommendations, in contrast, are often less personal, such as providing news, shopping, music, or movie recommendations. Furthermore, it is possible that personalised system recommendations may be perceived to be lower quality than non-personalised ones. For example, a user might be offended when a personalised set of recommendations is particularly poor, with a reaction similar to “it's ridiculous that the system thinks I'd like these recommendations”, whereas they might be more accepting of the same recommendations when not personalised, with a reaction similar to “I can believe that others would like these recommendations.”

We therefore conducted a three-stage study to examine the effect of purported personalisation on the perceived quality of system recommendations – importantly, the actual recommendations were identical across personalised and non-personalised conditions. Each participant was assigned to either a personalised or non-personalised condition in a between-subjects manner. In the first stage, each participant provided their level of agreement with a series of 10 questions that were directed either towards the participant's own preferences (personalised) or towards the average person's preferences (non-personalised, with the ‘average person’ phrasing used for experimental control): for example, “ You feel more drawn to places with busy, bustling atmospheres than quiet, intimate places” versus “ The average person feels more drawn to places with busy, bustling atmospheres than quiet, intimate places.” In the second stage, each participant was shown a set of either good or bad movie recommendations (also between-subjects), prefaced by the text “Based on your answers (about yourself), our system has picked some movies for you” (personalised) or ““Based on your answers (about the average person), our system has picked some movies” (non-personalised).

In the third stage, all participants used a ten-point scale (poor to excellent) to respond to the question “Please rate the quality of the movie recommendations for your taste.”

The main hypothesis was that participants’ perceived quality assessments would differ between the personalised and non-personalised conditions, with participants viewing personalised good recommendations as higher quality than identical non-personalised recommendations.

Results did not support the hypothesis. There was no significant difference between subjects’ quality assessments of personalised and non-personalised recommendations, despite conducting the experiment at a large scale, with 492 participants. Furthermore, contrary to the Barnum-effect, the mean perceived quality ratings were lower for personalised recommendations than non-personalised ones. Therefore, despite several design websites referring to the relevance of the Barnum effect in system recommendations, our results indicate that designers should be cautious about emphasising the personalisation of recommendations.

The contributions of this research are as follows:

  1. We review studies of the Barnum effect and explain its potential relationship to interaction;
  2. We describe an experiment that indicates that personalisation effects have at most a small and potentially negative influence on users’ subjective experience of recommendations, contrary to the Barnum effect;
  3. We discuss implications for the design and evaluation of recommender systems.

2 BACKGROUND

2.1 Foundations in psychology

Forer [ 12 ] used the name “the fallacy of personal validation” to describe his experimental results, which showed a tendency for people to award high quality scores to ‘personalised’ personality descriptions that actually consisted of generic statements. Meehl [ 18 ] subsequently coined the term Barnum effect (after the celebrated hoaxer and showman P.T. Barnum) to “stigmatize those pseudo-successful clinical procedures in which personality descriptions from tests are made to fit the patient largely or wholly by virtue of their triviality.” Many subsequent studies have examined people's tendency to see generic personality descriptions as more accurately describing themselves when the descriptions are purportedly prepared ‘specially for the user’ than when they are ‘generally true of people,’ with extensive replication. The following paragraphs briefly review psychology studies replicating these findings and exposing factors that are relevant to our study. Interested readers are directed to comprehensive reviews provided by Dickson and Kelly [ 9 ] and Furnham and Schofield [ 13 ].

Snyder and Larson [ 23 ] conducted an early experiment that included a factor related to computer-based production of personality descriptions. Their experiment directly compared participants’ scores for personalised versus non-personalised personality descriptions (that were actually identical in both cases), with results confirming higher quality ratings when the descriptions were ‘personalised.’ Their experiment also compared the ratings under two further factors: setting , with the personality descriptions presented to the participant by a clinical psychologist in his office or by a graduate student in the lab; and test scorer , with the participant informed that the personality descriptions were prepared by a human or a computer. Neither setting nor test scorer showed significant effects.

Snyder and Shenkel [ 24 ] examined whether the favourability of Barnum personality descriptions influenced their perceived quality. They found two interesting effects: first, participants considered favourable descriptions to be significantly more true of themselves than of people in general, but there was no difference with unfavourable descriptions; second, participants rated the person producing the personality descriptions to be significantly more skilled with favourable descriptions than unfavourable ones. The tendency for greater acceptance of positive information, also referred to as a ‘Pollyanna principle,’ has been replicated in several studies including those examining the Barnum effect (see [ 13 ] for a review). The results on the favourability of descriptions raise the possibility that any Barnum-like effects that occur during interaction with recommendation systems could vary dependent on the perceived quality of the recommendation.

The cognitive bias of illusory superiority [ 8 , 15 ] could also influence users’ perceptions of poor system recommendations differently when they are personalised versus non-personalised. Illusory superiority is the tendency for people to overestimate their own abilities, as demonstrated by a study of university academics [ 8 ], which showed that 94% of academics rated their teaching ability to be above average, and 68% assessed themselves to be within the top 25% of teachers. During interaction with system recommendations the bias of illusory superiority might suggest that when users interact with particularly poor system recommendations (such as the movie recommendations used in our study), participants might view non-personalised recommendations that are directed to the general public as being more acceptable than the same recommendations when personalised (due to their superior taste).

Studies of the Barnum effect have generally included participants’ sex as a factor for analysis, with most studies indicating no significant difference [ 9 , 13 ]. However, at least three studies have shown significant differences across participants identifying as male and female [ 3 , 11 , 22 ], such as female participants giving slightly lower ratings than males to Barnum personality descriptions [ 22 ]. For consistency with prior methodology, our experiment controlled and balanced for participants’ selected gender.

2.2 Related studies of interaction

Extensive studies have examined algorithms and interfaces that generate factually personalised recommendations, including several recent and substantial reviews of these important techniques and methods [ 1 , 27 , 28 ].

Our focus, however, is not in the factual generation of personalised recommendations, but on the effects (if any) that are derived from merely presenting the recommendations as personalised or not. Within this narrower field, related results are mixed.

In a study of the use of personalised versus non-personalised robots as educational peers for primary school children (aged 7-8), Baxter et al. [ 2 ] found that learning outcomes were improved when the robot peer personalised its behaviours. However, the forms of ‘personalisation’ in this study differ from the system recommendations that are the focus of our work – in particular, the ‘personalised’ robot differed from the non-personalised robot in several ways that influenced its animation, engagement and life-like behaviours, such as ‘exhibiting life-like idling movements’ and ‘respond[ing] to the approach of the child.’ Thus, the results may be explained by the participants having greater engagement with the life-like robot, rather than the forms of personalisation that are our focus.

In another study comparing factually personalised recommendations to non-personalised ones, Peng et al. [ 21 ] reported an experiment in which they gathered a week of self-reported mood data from 26 participants. A personalised animation of that data was created for 14 of the participants, while a generic animation was prepared for the remaining 12 participants. Participants then viewed their assigned animation and rated their emotional engagement with it, with results showing higher levels of emotional engagement with the personalised animations. Although the paper refers to the Barnum effect in its motivation, the experiment does not test the Barnum effect (nor does it claim to) because it compares factually different recommendations, whereas the Barnum effect concerns factually identical descriptions that are falsely presented as personalised or not.

In contrast to these positive findings for personalisation, a study comparing the effectiveness of personalised versus non-personalised invitations to enrol in an experiment indicated no significant difference, with 11.0% enrolment from 254 personalised invitations and 10.2% enrolment from 255 non-personalised invitations [ 26 ]. Colombet et al. [ 7 ] describes a study protocol to compare the effectiveness of personalised versus non-personalised recommendations from a medical decision support system, but results of the study are not available. 4 And a study of the acceptance of personalised versus non-personalised YouTube recommendations among Saudi college students indicated that both personalised and non-personalised recommendations can be successful (no comparison is reported), and also reported that male students were more likely to follow the recommendations than women students [ 14 ].

Two further interaction studies that directly refer to the Barnum/Forer effect are Chan et al. [ 4 ] and Solomon [ 25 ], both of which focus on whether users rate feedback that purportedly stems from AI systems differently to that stemming from other sources, such as a human expert. Chan et al. [ 4 ] examined users’ donor kidney allocation decisions when supported by artificial AI recommendations (i.e., the ‘AI’ recommendations were fake and factually identical across conditions). Their main finding was that users were more likely to accept recommendations when they were purportedly made by an “expert psychologist” than when made by an “AI system.”

The main finding from Solomon [ 25 ] was that users are more likely to accept the recommendations from a decision support system (DSS) when they believe that the system has adapted its recommendations to conform with the user's customisation. Their experiment examined users’ decisions across 12 rounds of a ‘fantasy baseball’ game (predicting the outcome of upcoming games) when assisted by recommendations from a DSS. They used a mixed 2 × 2 design for ‘customisation’ (customised and not customised, between subjects) and ‘recommendation quality’ (good and bad, within subjects). Participants in the ‘customised’ condition were presented with a user interface to customise the underlying DSS algorithm, but importantly their customisations did not factually influence the recommendations, which were the same regardless of customisation. The primary dependent measure was the proportion of decisions that accepted each recommendation. At the end of 12 game rounds, participants completed a survey that was intended to provide insight into three theoretical explanations for any observed effect on recommendation acceptance: the IKEA effect [ 19 ] (“how much effort they put forth to customize”), the Forer effect (“the degree to which they felt they were providing information to the DSS”), and perceived level of control over the recommendations. While the results showed that fake customisation significantly increased users’ recommendation acceptance, none of the survey data yielded a significant explanation for the effect, leading the authors to conclude that “The illusion of control, Forer effect and Ikea effect do not explain this customization bias.” However, absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, and it is possible that any of these three effects (or their combination) may have contributed to the effect, but that the survey data lacked sensitivity to reveal their influence (elsewhere in the paper they state “the survey offers no evidence that the illusion of control, Forer effect, or IKEA effect can explain customization bias,” which is more cautious and correct).

A final area of relevant background concerns current commercial practice in personalisation of user interface recommendations. Film and TV streaming services provide mostly generic recommendations – for example, Netflix includes categories “Popular on Netflix,” “Trending Now,” and “Top 10 Movies in country name ”; and Amazon Prime similarly includes generic categories such as “Recommended movies” and “Top 10 in country name .” However, both of these services also include at least one personalised link: “Top Picks for first name ” on Netflix, and “TV shows we think you'll like” on Amazon Prime. The homepage of online shopping service Amazon.com also uses predominantly generic presentation, with links such as “Shop by Category,” “Health and Personal Care,” and “Most Wished for Video Games.” While homepage inclusion of these categories may be personalised to the user's history, the presentation does not emphasise personalisation for the user. However, Amazon.com does include links that emphasise personalisation, such as “Top Sellers in Toys for you” and a page of “Top picks for you” accessible under link “ First name's Amazon.com.”

In contrast to the mostly generic categories on Netflix, Amazon Prime, and Amazon.com, music streaming service Spotify provides several categories of explicitly personalised content, including “Your top mixes,” “Albums for you based on what you like to listen to,” “Made for you,” and “Shows you might like” (for podcasts).

In summary, prior work in psychology shows that the Barnum effect of personalisation influences the perceived quality of personality descriptions. A ‘folklore’ of interface design (cited in the Introduction) suggests that the Barnum effect improves user satisfaction with online recommendations; and many major online services vary in the degree to which recommendations are explicitly personalised. Yet few prior studies have directly examined the effects in interaction of merely presenting recommendations as personalised or not.

3 EXPERIMENTAL STUDY OF THE BARNUM EFFECT IN INTERACTION

We conducted an experiment on the crowd-sourcing platform ‘Prolific’ 5 to examine whether personalised versus non-personalised presentation of otherwise identical movie recommendations would influence their perceived quality (i.e., the degree to which the user perceives the recommendations to match their individual taste). The experiment also controlled objective movie quality , recommending good and bad movies based on IMDB scores from over 10,000 users; inclusion of this factor was motivated by previous findings on the influence that the favourability of personality descriptions can have on the presence of Barnum effects [e.g., 13 , 24 ]. And consistent with prior studies [ 3 , 11 , 22 ], we also analysed whether gender would influence the results.

The experiment was therefore a 2 × 2 × 2 factorial design for between-subjects factors personalisation ∈ [ personalised , non-personalised ], objective movie quality ∈ [ good , bad ], and gender ∈ [ man , woman ] ( only those responding man and woman were included in the main analysis as there was insufficient data from other genders to warrant statistical analysis ).

3.1 Procedure

The experimental procedure is summarised in Table 1 . It was presented to participants through a series of webpages, with contents as follows: initial questionnaire (each question on a separate page); instructions preparing the participant for the movie recommendations; the ten movie recommendations and question for providing an overall rating of them; ratings for each individual movie; summary questions; and final comments.

The initial questionnaire consisted of ten questions adapted from an online personality test 6 that uses the NERIS Type Explorer®Scale mainly based on the Myers and Briggs theories [ 17 ]. This questionnaire was presented in either a personalised or non-personalised form. For example, the first question to participants in the personalised condition was “You like books and movies that make you come up with your own interpretation of the ending.” For participants in the non-personalised condition, the first question was “An average person likes books and movies that makes them come up with their own interpretation of the ending.” The experimental rationale for using almost identical survey questions across personalised and non-personalised conditions (but directed to ‘an average person’ when non-personalised) was to maximise experimental control and to minimise the confounds that might arise from having non-personalised participants complete a different set of tasks or no task.

Participants responded to each survey question using a five-point Likert item consisting of ‘Strongly disagree’, ‘Disagree’, ‘Neither agree nor disagree’, ‘Agree’ and ‘Strongly agree’. Having selected a response to each question, a ‘Next’ button appeared, and when clicked the webpage proceeded to the next question.

Following the ten initial questions, personalised participants were presented with an instruction page that read “Thanks. Based on your answers (about yourself), our system has picked some movies for you.” Non-personalised participants were presented with an equivalent instruction page that read “Thanks. Based on your answers (about the average person), our system has picked some movies.”

The next page presented ten movie recommendations to the participant (see Figure 1 ) under a title that read “Movie recommendations for you” (personalised) or “Movie recommendations” (non-personalised). The ten movie recommendations were either good (see Figure 1a) or bad (see Figure 1b), with both sets constructed to include one movie from each of 10 genres (Comedy, Sci-Fi, Horror, Romance, Action, Thriller, Drama, Adventure, Crime, Animation). Good movies had an IMDB score in the range 6.5-7.5, and were selected from the top 50 US box-office earning feature films with at least 10,000 votes on IMDB; 7 bad movies were selected with similar criteria, but with a score in the range 2.5-3.5. 8 Each movie recommendation included an icon for the movie, its title, a star-rating (one to five stars based on its IMDB score), and a short text description. The set of good recommendations was identical for all participants assigned to that condition, as was the bad page. At the bottom of the recommendation page participants used an eleven point scale (0 ‘Very Poor’ to 10 ‘Excellent’) to respond to the main rating question (R1) regarding the quality of the recommendations, phrased as “Please rate the quality of the movie recommendations for your taste” for all participants.

The following page presented a list of each of the recommended movies, and asked participants to select whether or not they had seen each movie and assign a rating for it (IR1-10). Participants then advanced to a page of seven summary questions (S1-7 in Table 1 ) which further queried participants’ perceptions of the recommendations (questions adapted from previous studies of recommender systems [ 10 , 16 ]). Participants were then asked to provide free-form text comments on their recommendations or ratings. The experiment finished by collecting data on the participants’ age and gender preference.

3.2 Participants and Apparatus

The experiment was distributed to US and UK participants on Prolific, a crowd-sourcing platform that has been reported to yield participants who are less dishonest than those on Amazon Mechanical Turk [ 20 ]. Participants were randomly assigned to one of four conditions (personalised or non-personalised, with good or bad movies). Participants were paid £0.94 (≈ US $ 1.17) for the seven-minute experiment, based on the recommended ‘Good’ rate on Prolific and an hourly rate of £8.05 (US $ 10.00). A total of 492 participants completed the study, with 239 participants selecting ‘man’ (59 personalised good , 63 personalised bad , 59 non-personalised good , and 58 non-personalised bad ), 241 selecting ‘woman’ (55 personalised good , 60 personalised bad , 62 non-personalised good , and 64 non-personalised bad ), and 12 selecting either ‘prefer not to say’ or ‘not listed’. Participants’ mean age was 36 (s.d. 13.85, min. 18, max. 85).

Software was written in HTML/CSS/JS using the Material UI library 9 . A Firebase database logged all user actions and responses.

3.3 Design and Hypotheses

Responses from the 480 participants who selected ‘man’ or ‘woman’ were analysed using a 2 × 2 × 2 factorial design for between-subjects factors personalisation ∈ [ personalised , non-personalised ], objective movie quality ∈ [ good , bad ], and gender ∈ [ man , woman ].

The primary dependent measure was the overall rating of the perceived quality of the recommendations (R1 in Table 1 ). Secondary dependent measures were the participant's mean rating of the perceived quality of the ten individual movies (i.e., the mean of IR1-10 in Table 1 ), the count of recommended movies that each participant reported that they had watched, and the responses to the summary questions (S1-10 in Table 1 ).

The main hypothesis, H 1 , was that the perceived quality of the recommendations would differ between personalised and non-personalised conditions. In addition, we also examined differences between the perceived quality of good and bad recommendations, between the perceptions across genders, and interaction effects across the three factors.

4 RESULTS

Across all 492 participants, the mean perceived quality (R1 in Table 1 ) of the recommendations in the personalised condition was 4.28 (n=246, s.d.=3.0), compared to 4.55 (n=246, s.d.=2.9) in the non-personalised condition, rated on a scale from 0 ‘Very Poor’ to 10 ‘Excellent’. This yields no significant difference (Welch two-sample T-Test, T 489.4 = 1.02, p = .3), and we therefore reject H 1 .

However, this overall analysis does not account for the potential influence that objective movie quality or gender might have on the results. Analysis of variance of R1 data (perceived quality of the recommendations) from the 480 participants who selected man or woman as their gender is summarised in Figure 2 . There was no significant main effect of personalisation ( F 1, 472 = 1.39, p = .24), with personalised and non-personalised means of 4.57 (sd 2.9) and 4.31 (sd 3.02) respectively. As expected, there was a strong effect of objective movie quality , with recommendations for good movies perceived to be higher quality (mean 6.39, sd 2.22) than bad movies (mean 2.5, sd 2.26) – F 1, 472 = 368.2, p < .0001. There was also a significant effect of gender , with those participants identifying as woman rating the recommendations more highly (mean 4.68, sd 2.95) than those identifying as man (mean 4.2, sd 2.96): F 1, 472 = 7.93, p = .005. None of the two- or three-way interactions showed significant effects (all p > .2).

One potential confound in the experiment concerns the participants’ prior exposure to the recommended movies – for example, if the participants assigned to the personalised condition had coincidentally chosen to watch a higher proportion of the recommended movies than those in the non-personalised condition, then this difference alone might influence the results. We therefore analysed the number of movies that participants reported that they had previously watched, using the same 2 × 2 × 2 ANOVA described above. The overall mean number of movies watched was 3.57 (sd 3.05). As expected, there was a significant effect of objective movie quality , with a mean of 5.8 (sd 2.3) good movies watched, compared to only 1.3 (sd 1.8) bad movies – ( F 1, 472 = 561, p < .0001). As there is no reason for the personalisation of recommendations in our experiment to influence the participants’ past movie watching choices we expected and confirmed no main effect of personalisation – F 1, 472 = 1.18, p = .28. There was no significant main effect of gender , with man participants watching an average of 3.6 movies compared to 3.5 for woman participants – F 1, 472 = 0.05, p = .8. However, there was a significant crossover interaction of objective movie quality × gender , with man participants watching slightly more bad movies (mean 1.5, sd 2.0) than woman (mean 1.1, sd 1.6), while woman watched more good movies (mean 6.0, sd 2.2) than man participants (mean 5.6, sd 2.5) – F 1, 472 = 4.2, p = .041. Analysis of survey question S5 (Table 1 , ‘The recommendations are familiar to me’) showed the same significant objective movie quality × gender interaction ( p = .02). None of these effects suggest a confound in the analysis of personalisation .

Analyses of the survey questions (S1-7) and the mean rating for the perceived quality of individual movie recommendations (IR1-10) showed similar results to the R1 results reported above, with strongly different responses across the objective movie quality of recommendations, less strong but significant differences across gender , but importantly, no significant differences between personalised and non-personalised recommendations for any measure. There is therefore no evidence that personalisation influenced participants’ perceived quality of the recommendations, confirming the rejection of H 1 .

4.1 Participants’ comments

The participants’ comments suggested that their expectations for the recommendations were different between personalised and non-personalised conditions, especially when the recommendation were poor quality. Several participants from the personalised and bad condition provided comments indicating that they almost took offence at the recommendations. For example, p256 stated “Not at all the kind of thing I would choose to watch! I am not sure how they were selected but I don't think the algorithm ‘gets’ me at all!” ; p325 stated “the opposite of what I like” ; p325 “I felt none of the movies represented me AT ALL, so I felt very negative automatically to these movies as a response” ; and p234 stated “I found it worrying and slightly scary that those were the movies chosen from the questions asked.”

In contrast, comments from the non-personalised and bad condition, although critical of the recommendations, showed greater ambivalence towards them, without indicating personal offence. For example, p3 stated “Not really a movie person, but almost all of the movies recommended are the kind that I would scroll past on Netflix if they appeared” ; p30 “I have no idea how the recommendations were generated but it's funny that it showed me a bunch of awful films” ; p38 “It was all a miss when it comes to my taste of movies” , and p112 “most of the recommended movies were bad.”

Four comments from participants in the non-personalised and bad condition provide a potential explanation for why comments from this condition lacked the indications of offence that arose in the personalised condition. P55 stated “the questions answered before the recommendations were about the average person, not myself so any recommendations based on those arent likely to reflect me specifically” ; p64 stated “I felt like because the phrase ‘average person’ was used in the questioning, I may have gave different answers than if you asked ‘do you”’ ; p121 stated “I don't understand how rating what "average people" think is a useful indicator on what I'd prefer” ; and p124 stated “I wonder what the film choices would have been if i entered my own preferences rather than what my assumptions of what the average person wants to watch.” Similar comments, questioning the relevance of an ‘average person’ in personal tastes, were also made by participants in the non-personalised and good condition, such as p173 “I don't know how asking me about an average person is supposed to give accurate recommendations for me” and p198 “My views on the average person has nothing to do with me personally so I think it would be very hard to use that as a basis for recommending movies for me personally.”

There were two main themes in the comments from participants who saw good recommendations, appearing with roughly equal frequency across personalised and non-personalised participants. First, several participants’ appeared to attribute greater sophistication to the underlying recommendation algorithm than existed. For example, among the non-personalised participants, p208 stated “I was very impressed by the selection given to me” ; p189 stated “I was familiar with the movies and pleasantly surprised at the recommendations in terms of how well they suited me” ; and p200 commented “Majority of the films i'd seen or would like to see -really clever!” Similar comments among the personalised participants include p380 “Excellent set of recommendations for me” ; p398 “I was very impressed with the recommendations from the system” ; p449 “Very good recommendations. I have seen all but one of them and really enjoyed every one so this was spot on” ; and p458 “Many of the movies seemed perfect for me. There were a few I wasn't very familiar with but I'd now consider watching them.”

These comments suggest that some of the participants (in both the personalised and non-personalised conditions) were encountering a form of the Barnum effect, viewing the system's recommendations as personalised for them, even in the non-personalised condition where neither the language used to present the
recommendations nor the data gathered from the user had any substantial personal component. We return to this issue in the discussion.

Related to the participants’ perceived sophistication of the underlying recommendation algorithm, at least two comments indicated high expectations for the recommendations when considering the limited data gathered from the participants during the initial questionnaire: p168 (non-personalised) stated “I really love comedies and documentaries! Those are my two favorite genres, so I'm surprised those were not in there” and p164 (non-personalised) “Some art house and less kids or teen movies would have been more to my liking.”

In contrast to the participants who saw sophistication in the recommendation algorithm (where none existed), the second main theme of comments from participants who saw good recommendations criticised them for being too generic and mainstream (which, of course, they were). For example, among non-personalised participants, p131 stated “Representative of many movies that UK adults will have heard about or seen” ; p177 “The movies that was listed looks like mostly mainstream movies that was booming in their own era” ; and p188 “Maybe not selected to my specific preference, just to general popular taste.” And among personalised participants, p393 stated “These were all extremely mainstream movies, and only a couple were movies I would have any interest in” ; p414 “It did seem rather generic overall, but I had seen the majority of them, so I guess that fits what I would watch generally” ; and p440 “The recommendations were very mainstream and not to my taste.”

5 DISCUSSION

The experiment was designed to examine whether explicit statements regarding personalisation would improve the perceived quality of system recommendations, consistent with psychology findings on the Barnum effect. Rather than improving perceived quality, the mean rating of personalised recommendations (4.28) was lower than that for non-personalised ones (4.55), although not significantly so.

The following subsections discuss the results, elaborating on the value of reporting a null finding, generalising on the influence that Barnum effects may still have on some users’ perceptions of interaction with recommender systems, discussing implications for design, and raising areas for future work.

5.1 Importance of the (null) finding

Any null finding has two potential explanations – first, perhaps the finding represents a true-negative in which the measured conditions are the same; second, perhaps the findings represent a false-negative, with the experiment lacking sensitivity to discriminate between conditions.

Regarding the first explanation, as Cohen notes, “the null hypothesis [...] is always false in the real world” [ 6 , p.1308], meaning that for a sufficiently large sample, it is exceptionally unlikely that the true difference between any two means is exactly zero to the n th decimal place. Therefore, given that our experiment compared two conditions that were meaningfully different to one another (gathering data on personal opinions or about ‘the average person’, then explicitly framing the recommendations as being ‘for you’ or not), yet still produced a null outcome, it seems likely that our experiment represents a false-negative finding. What is the value, then, in disseminating the null finding? We see four main reasons for doing so.

First, we conducted the experiment at large scale, with 492 participants. Even with this large scale, we failed to reliably discriminate between conditions. This failure likely stems from a combination of two causes – a small measurable difference between the conditions, and large variation in the data contributing to the measured means. Overall, this suggests that any effect of personalisation versus non-personalisation was at most small.

Second, although our results suggest that explicit personalisation has at most a small effect, several sources refer to the positive role that the Barnum effect has on users when interacting with recommendations. Designers who wish to exploit this purported positive effect could sensibly choose to personalise their recommendations in the manner examined in our study, explicitly framing them as ‘Specially for you’, or similar. Yet our results indicate that designers should be sceptical about the benefits of doing so.

Third, the participants’ mean rating for personalised recommendations was lower than that for non-personalised ones, further suggesting the need for design caution when considering the use of explicitly personalised recommendations.

Fourth, access to well motivated studies that fail to reject the null hypothesis is important in overcoming the “file drawer effect,” under which studies that show no significant difference go unpublished (relegated to the researchers’ ‘file drawer’), while studies that reject the null (possibly incorrectly) are published and become part of the discipline ‘knowledge’ [ 5 ]. The inability for researchers to access the results of well motivated studies means that the same or similar hypotheses are likely be successively tested, especially when a design folklore exists espousing the existence of an effect (such as the Barnum effect). And at the p = .05 significance level, we should expect one study in twenty to incorrectly reject a true null hypothesis. Consequently, unless null findings are published, it is likely that future researchers will expend energy testing the same hypothesis, and more worryingly, it is likely that eventually a study will incorrectly reject the null (by chance), and confirm the false design folklore.

5.2 Barnum effects and recommendations, even without explicit personalisation

Psychology studies of Barnum effects have been largely limited to the perceived quality of generic personality descriptions. Our experiment, in contrast, replaces personality descriptions with movie recommendations.

While it could be the case that the shift from personality descriptions to movie recommendations is sufficient to render unobservable the otherwise robust Barnum phenomenon, an alternative explanation for our null finding is that some of our participants perceived both our personalised and non-personalised conditions to be providing actually personalised recommendations. Under this explanation, the Barnum effect could have applied equally (or similarly) in both conditions, as follows.

Our personalised condition was explicitly designed to induce Barnum effects, by gathering personal data and by presenting the recommendations as being ‘for you.’ However, it is possible that participants believed that the underlying recommendation algorithm in the non-personalised condition was achieving personalised recommendations based on the data that participants provided about the ‘average person.’ For example, if a participant provided a ‘strongly agree’ answer to survey question 7 (Table 1 ), that participant might think ‘the algorithm now knows that I'm the sort of person who thinks that the average person is very intrigued by things labelled as controversial.’ Any participant who believed that the recommendation algorithm was sophisticated could therefore be susceptible to Barnum-like effects, even in the non-personalised conditions.

As mentioned in Section 4.1 , comments from some participants in the non-personalised condition indicated that they attributed such sophistication to the recommendation algorithm. For example p200 referred to the system as “really clever” and p208 commented “very impressed by the selection”.

5.3 Design considerations

The results indicate that the perceived quality of system recommendations is not meaningfully improved simply by presenting the recommendations as specially for the user. This finding is contrary to various sources that suggest that the Barnum effect influences users’ perception of recommendations. Furthermore, although our results showed no significant difference, the mean quality rating for our personalised condition was lower than that for non-personalised , suggesting that if a difference exists, presenting recommendations as personalised may harm rather than improve perceived quality.

Two issues are worth highlighting regarding the user interface ‘folklore’ that refers to the influence that the Barnum effect has on users. First, it is possible that the folklore is factually correct for some users – that their perceptions of recommendations are raised by the user's belief that the recommendations are personalised for them. However, as discussed in Section 5.2 , it seems that some users attribute substantial sophistication to recommendation algorithms, and therefore they may believe that recommendations are personalised even when they are not and even when they are presented without any mention of personalisation. In this case, the influence that the Barnum effect has on these users is a moot point – any interface that presents recommendations to the user could encounter its effects, regardless of how they are presented, and therefore there is probably little that designers might do to mitigate or enhance its effects.

Second, while some of our participants were impressed by the (non-existent) sophistication of our recommendation algorithm (potentially explained by a Barnum-like effect), many more criticised the recommendations as being ‘mainstream’ or ‘generic’ and for failing to account for their personal tastes (the opposite of a Barnum-like effect). The criticisms were particularly forceful from participants in the personalised and bad condition, indicating borderline offence taken at the algorithm's poor recommendations. When these comments are considered in combination with the lower mean quality score for personalised than non-personalised conditions, they suggest that designers should be cautious about choosing to emphasise personalised curation of content for users. Further studies could examine this potential effect, but the dominance of non-personalised recommendations observed on Netflix, Amazon Prime, and Amazon.com (Section 2.2 ) suggests that proprietary studies on major platforms may already have observed this effect. Regardless, the suggestion of negative outcomes from personalisation indicate that designers should dismiss the most obvious implication of claims that the Barnum effect plays a positive role in online recommendations – i.e., designers should be cautious about explicitly emphasising personalisation, particularly if there is a risk that the recommendations may poorly reflect the user's taste.

5.4 Study limitations and opportunities for further work

We see two further areas for future work that arise from limitations in our study.

First, our study focused on the perceived quality of recommendations. We did so because perceived quality (of recommendations) seemed to be a relatively short step from perceived accuracy (of personality descriptions) that has been abundantly studied under the Barnum effect. If claims that the Barnum effect influences interaction with recommendations are true, then they should be directly observable by examining perceived quality. However, it is possible that other interaction measures might reveal Barnum-like effects. For example, it is conceivable that measures of how much users like or enjoy recommendation interfaces might be influenced by the interface's claim to personalise content to the user. Studies have already shown that users feel more positively towards user interfaces simply because the user has engaged in purported customisation that factually has no effect [ 25 ], so perhaps similar positive outcomes might emerge when the user interface claims that it has adapted to the user. Future studies could examine this possibility. However, given our participants’ comments, we suspect that positive effects would not be revealed.

Second, our study used an initial questionnaire (see Table 1 ) as part of the method to engender a personalised versus non-personalised experience, with the questionnaire consisting of a series of questions based on a common personality survey. We used an adapted personality survey because this is the standard method used in psychological studies of the Barnum effect (see Section 2.1 ), and because there is a lack of research on Barnum effects that do not involve the use of personality surveys. However, any real recommender system is unlikely to expose users to a personality survey, instead, it would gather its data regarding the users’ preferences from the history of the users’ interaction (such as selecting many movies of a particular genre, such as horror or comedy). Further work could therefore study the occurrence of Barnum-like effects where users initially express their preferences within the same domain as the recommendations, such as providing a liking assessment for each of several movies before rating the quality of a set of movie recommendations from the system.

6 CONCLUSION

The Barnum effect suggests that users may perceive otherwise identical system recommendations as being higher quality simply because they are presented as being ‘specially for you.’ Conversely, though, users may perceive the quality of bad recommendations that are ‘specially for you’ as being worse than the same recommendations when directed to ‘the general user’, because users may ascribe above average taste to themselves (consistent with illusory superiority). Our study examined whether the perceived quality of otherwise identical movie recommendations was influenced by having them presented as personalised to the user or not personalised, across both good and bad recommendations. The lack of significant differences in the results suggest that purported personalisation has at most a small measurable effect, and participant comments suggest that personalisation of poor recommendations risks invoking a stronger negative reaction than might otherwise occur.
