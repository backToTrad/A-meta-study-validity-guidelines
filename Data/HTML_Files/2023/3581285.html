We present DuoRhythmo, a collaborative accessible digital musical interface (CADMI) that gives people living with Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (PALS) the experience of remotely and collaboratively creating music in real-time. We designed DuoRhythmo specifically to be utilized for eye tracking and optimized it for head- and computer mouse interaction, as well using a user-centered design approach. Together with five PALS, we completed a mixed-methods evaluation to assess the accessibility of DuoRhythmo. Participants described the CADMI using the Microsoft Desirability Toolkit (MDT) as fun, empowering, accessible, easy to use, engaging, and stimulating and gave an average System Usability Scale (SUS) score of 79.5. We suggest further research on remote collaboration within the field of accessible digital musical instruments (ADMIs) using the term CADMI to explore the positive effects of collaborative music-making on the quality of life of PALS.

1 INTRODUCTION

Every year, there are approximately 140,000 people diagnosed worldwide with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [ 45 ]. People living with ALS (PALS) have both their physical and mental well-being impacted [ 2 , 60 ]. As the disease progresses, PALS gradually lose control over all muscles besides their oculomotor ones [ 40 , 56 ]. As a result, their quality of life is significantly affected. Everyday functions like social interactions become difficult; leisure activities also become problematic, and inaccessible [ 42 ]. Previous research has shown that listening to music and musical creation has various health benefits, such as lowering blood pressure, reducing anxiety, and managing pain [ 8 , 58 , 59 ]. Utilizing music in multiple ways as a therapeutic device can further enhance these effects and become a substantial tool in managing mental and physical symptoms [ 13 , 25 ].

Oculomotor muscles for most PALS remain unaffected, although some saccadic dysfunctions might occur in later stages of the disease [ 51 ]. Generally, eye tracking technology is widely used to help patients retain some autonomy [ 11 ]. Eye tracking assisted voice banking applications help PALS to communicate and maintain relationships with others [ 12 ]. Furthermore, eye tracking control systems can assist them with steering their wheelchairs [ 20 ]. Such applications also make it possible for PALS to conduct some recreational activities. For example, the EyeHarp [ 64 ] is an accessible digital musical instrument (ADMI) that allows users to create music with their eye gaze or head movements.

In this paper, we examine the possibilities of musical co-creation to help improve the quality of life of PALS by combining social interactions with the joys of music listening and composition as a form of leisure activity. Our research aims to investigate means of real-time, collaborative music creation inspired by the Dato DUO's [ 14 ] simple yet effective interface for shared musical experiences. Previous research within eye gaze interaction for PALS and eye tracking-based ADMIs are investigated to gain insight into prevalent issues in the area and understand existing design conventions. To emphasize the importance of a collaborative aspect for ADMIs, we suggest using the term collaborative accessible digital musical interface (CADMI).

Using an agile design approach [ 53 ], we developed a CADMI prototype by the name of DuoRhythmo. This paper presents a mixed-methods framework to evaluate the user experience of a CADMI, which is optimized for remote testing together with people who cannot easily communicate verbally as primary research participants. Our results (n=5) indicate that DuoRhythmo is accessible for various input devices for PALS in various stages of their illness and was perceived positively by the test participants.

The main contributions of this research are: 1.) Through a mixed-method user experience evaluation (UXE), it presents qualitative input from PALS about using a CADMI; 2.) An interaction design paradigm for creating an accessible interface rooted in eye tracking and a continuum of accessible inputs for motor-impaired users; 3.) Insights about conducting a remote UXE with PALS during a global pandemic.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Eye Tracking for Interaction

Novel interactions focusing on eye tracking have seen a continuous rise in development over recent years. Eye tracking technology has seen a steady rise in availability due to its increasing cost-effectiveness, such as GazeSpeak, a smartphone-based communication device with eye gaze for people with motor disabilities by Zhang et al. [ 65 ]. Kane et al. [ 32 ] developed an advanced augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) device, combining eye tracking with computer vision. Devices for AAC powered by eye tracking provide people with ALS an accessible way to communicate; however, their latency is still a crucial challenge. Hence, Kane et al. [ 32 ] introduced computer-vision-based object recognition to incorporate contextual information from the user's surroundings. These systems can help AAC users have faster, more efficient conversations by suggesting words and phrases related to the current scene. Eid et al. [ 19 ] present a case study with a person living with ALS about a novel eye gaze controlled wheelchair for navigating unknown environments. Here, the wheelchair is operated by eye gaze, making it accessible for users who cannot control their hand movements, such as PALS.

Using eye gaze as the primary input for controlling systems has several challenges, including but not limited to: Midas touch, eye-movement speed, and accuracy. The Midas touch problem, as addressed by Jacob [ 30 ], is important to consider when an interface relies solely on eye gaze control. Utilizing eye gaze as input, the saccades of the eyes can cause imprecise selection on the interface. [ 30 ]. Thus, shorter eye gaze times should be a passive, non-command input, and additional interaction paradigms should be used for component selection, such as dwell time [ 30 ].

Hornof published pivotal work on the intersection of eye tracking, and musical expression [ 26 ]. He analyzed the problem of eye movement speed and accuracy from the perspective of musical interfaces. Utilizing eye gaze as input, the saccades of the eyes can cause imprecise selection on the interface. To overcome this, interactive components should be appropriately oversized [ 26 ].

The selection speed with eye gaze controlled interfaces can be problematic, particularly with musical interfaces, where the musical tempo is required. The limit for eye tracking is 250-300 ms when “tapping” along the beat with the eyes. According to Hornof et al., 50% accuracy of selection was achieved for inter-beat intervals of 250 ms and higher, while the most optimal performance was accomplished with 300 ms and higher. This is considerably more time than tapping along the beat using one's hands [ 26 ].

2.2 Eye Gaze Controlled Music

Hornof also analyzed the previous work conducted on eye gaze controlled music interfaces [ 26 ]. The first concepts of eye-controlled musical interfaces relied on early experimental eye tracking methods, where several eye positions were tracked to trigger sounds by Polli [ 49 ], and video-based eye-movement recording software implementation by Kim et al. [ 33 ].

In the last decade, the increasing availability and the convenient workflow of commercially available eye tracking devices have made it easier to develop gaze-based musical interfaces. One such example is Margulies and Anderson's project to adapt Ableton Live [ 34 ] to be operated with eye gaze [ 1 ].

Another example is the EyeHarp [ 64 ] project, which follows a radial interface metaphor and aims to create an accessible musical interface. Here the eyes have a resting position in the middle of the interface, and every interactable element is equally distanced from the center of the interface. In accordance with Fitts’ laws [ 21 ], this is beneficial for minimizing the amount of eye travel and yields a less eye-fatiguing experience.

Other interfaces, such as Netytar [ 16 ], utilize an isomorphic interaction to cope with the complexities of eye gaze assisted navigation. The key success of this interface was eliminating the delayed audio feedback, which can often be an undesirable consequence of using dwell time for interaction. The designed isomorphic interface minimizes gaze shifts to play standard musical intervals, thus reducing eye fatigue [ 16 ].

EyeJam, developed by Morimoto et al. [ 39 ], introduces a novel selection interaction technique, the so-called context switching paradigm, where specific notes are selected by bridging the gap between two target locations. In their evaluation, the authors found that the developed solution is less error-prone and helps carry on with more accurate timing control.

Hands-Free Sound Machine is an eye gaze controlled 16-step sequencer [ 23 ]. It enables its users to create complex musical compositions with MIDI samples and custom audio files and supports connection to physical instruments. It is a single-state dwell-based interface where triggering specific notes is achieved via dwell time. The product employs users to compose musical pieces or perform via a semi-live, loop-based technique.

Similarly, Sound Jam [ 5 ] is also a loop-based electronic musical composition tool; however, it resembles an established design found in many digital audio workstations (DAWs). The interface's core concept revolves around the audio-sample clipping metaphor found in popular DAWs, such as Ableton Live [ 34 ]. Fundamental aspects of this concept are alignment and quantization. These enable the users to launch the composing clips scheduled in sync with the next downbeat of the song. The clips can be edited later to allow modification of the musical piece.

Finally, Cyclops is an instrument designed for improvisation and live performance [ 47 ]. It gathers inspiration from the Dato DUO synthesizer and breaks the composition into three main areas: instrument, sequence, and effects. The Dato DUO's collaborative interface influences this division. Like the previously introduced projects, dwell time is used as the primary selection input for eye gaze, with different dwell times allocated to various controls of Cyclops. Although Dato is the main inspiration for Cyclops, one missing feature from Cyclops, according to Payne et al., is musical co-creation and is addressed as one of the main areas that need further exploration and research [ 47 ].

3 DESIGN PHILOSOPHY OF DUORHYTHMO

The design of DuoRhythmo was inspired by the projects described in the previous section and the literature on ADMIs. Frid [ 22 ] presents a systematic review of ADMIs, and claims that there is an increasing number of contributions each year in this research domain. Her findings show that the field mainly focuses on physical, tangible, or touchless controllers to control digital musical instruments. Consequently, the predominant target user groups of ADMIs are people with physical impairments. One of the main conclusions of the review is that it is impossible to determine systematic and absolute design guidelines for ADMIs. As particular users and user groups have different needs, ADMIs must facilitate adaptive and customizable interaction methods for the individual user's specific needs [ 22 ]. Illsar et al. [ 27 ] emphasize the importance of ADMIs to focus on customization and personalization to meet the needs of individual users. This approach can help empower users by providing new opportunities for creative expression. By tailoring the instrument to the user's specific abilities and preferences, ADMIs can help to create a more inclusive and accessible musical experience.

We especially regard interaction customization as a core design principle and embrace it as one of the key elements of DuoRhythmo's design philosophy. We focus primarily on eye tracking as it is one of the conclusive input modalities for PALS since, towards the later stages, they can often only move their ocular muscles to control interfaces. We propose that starting with designing for the most constrained interaction paradigm allows us to gradually expand the design to less extreme inputs for PALS in an earlier stage of their disease. Thus, designing to facilitate a continuum of accessible inputs in juxtaposition with the target group's ALS progression is significant while creating an ADMI for PALS.

The coronavirus pandemic has proven to be an extraordinary situation globally. Due to social distancing measures, people worldwide have become dependent on digital technologies to maintain social and professional connections. Many activities remained unaffected by this shift to online existence. However, due to the lack of physical contact, other activities, such as collaborative music creation or design processes, had to be reimagined [ 52 ]. This distributed digital living caused by COVID-19 also ignited enthusiasm in a wide variety of people toward collaborative and creative endeavors. As a direct effect of this, distributed performance practices were revitalized [ 52 ]. To facilitate distributed creativity in collaborative music-making for PALS, telematic or network music performances enabled by internet technologies must be capable of connecting to an accessible instrument, or an ADMI would need to be extended with a built-in collaborative aspect. Such a collaborative accessible digital musical interface (CADMI) could potentially remove the access barriers to collaborative music-making for PALS.

Due to the global pandemic, design processes and principles needed to be adapted to create a CADMI that a diverse group of people could use with varying levels of musical knowledge and physical abilities. When designing for inclusion and accessibility, the best practice is to involve the target group. This ensures that their decisions steer the direction of the development, ultimately creating a prototype that suits their needs [ 52 ]. The development of DuoRhythmo embraces a user-centered, agile design approach based on the three pillars of inclusive design presented by the Performance Without Barriers (PwB) research group [ 50 ].

The three pillars can be seen in Figure 2 , and we primarily aim to design towards accessibility and usability to achieve an inclusive experience for PALS. Following an iterative process, we gathered input about the target group to make informed decisions about the design process. These inputs take shape in various ways during the process, such as remote user interviews with PALS, remote prototype demos with PALS and their caretakers (CALS), and regular online consultations with expert stakeholders in designing ADMIs. The final stage of this user-centered design process is a remote UXE of DuoRhyhthmo with five PALS.

4 SYSTEM DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION

Figure 3 shows how two users across from each other use Duo-Rhythmo. The user at the bottom of the figure adds beats to the radial music sequencer, while the user at the top modulates the musical sequence with effects. The panels rotate every 60 seconds, swapping the roles of the users so that the one who was previously modulating the sound now adds beats to the sequencer, and vice versa. The spatial carousel features five drum sounds and their corresponding effect interface, allowing each drum sound to be modulated individually. After the spatial carousel has rotated 180°, the first drum and modulation are presented again, and the cycle repeats.

We used Unity [ 63 ] as a development platform to create Duo-Rhythmo, as well as the Tobii eye tracking SDK [ 62 ] for eye tracking interactions. Wwise [ 3 ] was utilized for real-time audio modulation, and Normcore [ 43 ] for networking architecture. The prototype's user interface (UI) is primarily designed for eye tracking interaction; moreover, it supports interaction with a computer mouse, touchpad, and head mouse. DuoRhythmo is designed and optimized for a Tobii Eye Tracker 5 [ 61 ]. However, it is usable with other Tobii eye trackers, especially those that support Windows Eye Control [ 38 ].

For eye tracking interaction, we developed a dwell time function that provides immediate feedback to the user on the progress of confirming a selection. This feedback is shown directly on the UI element the user is looking at, with the element filling up from the center. The amount of time the user has to dwell on the element to trigger it can be adjusted using the accessible settings menu, which offers five different dwell time speeds to choose from. This allows users to customize the system to their individual needs and preferences. These are shown in Figure 4 . We believe that allowing users to choose their preferred dwell time speed enhances the system's personalization, as discussed by Illsar et al. [ 27 ].

One primary consideration of the UI design is to reduce eye travel time as it provides a resting position in the middle. The radial UI also allows for the quantization of the created beats. It does this by restricting users to add a beat every 16 th node instead of at any point in time. We hypothesize this way of quantization leads to less error-prone beat generation, especially for PALS, as it requires less attention for precise ocular or mouse movements to achieve a quantized beat. We also used the radial sequencer to represent the concept of Euclidean rhythms [ 17 ]. Using an implementation of the euclidean algorithm [ 24 ], we aim to provide a way for users to create fundamental rhythms easily and intuitively. We based the implementation of the basic networking functionality on the Dato DUO's [ 14 ] collaboration metaphor between beat generation and effects modulation. Moreover, to avoid networking latency, the data synchronization is limited to triggering actions on local devices; instead of sending audio data across the network, local audio files play on event changes. The synchronized data across connected devices include beats per minute, the individual beats and effect values corresponding to each drum, and timing for navigation within the app.

Our system aims to enhance the users’ ownership over their music creation and self-expression. We created rooms with different genres and incorporated sound modulation effects to achieve this. Users can change the tempo by modulating beats per minute (BPM) and also the pitch of their created beats. They can add reverb, use a low-pass filter, and apply a flanger effect. The different themed genre rooms each have a selection of five different sounds and are based on a classic drum set-up consisting of a snare, kick, hi-hat, crash, and tom-tom. We sourced the sound samples from Splice's [ 57 ] sample library and categorized them into five themes: traditional rock, ambient, electronic, djembe, and handpan. The teaser Figure 1 shows DuoRhythmo's designed interface, the 16-note radial Euclidean sequencer, and the sound modulation effects.

5 REMOTE USER EXPERIENCE EVALUATION (UXE) OF DUORHYTHMO

To evaluate the user experience of DuoRhythmo from an accessibility standpoint, we created a mixed-methods framework that combined quantitative and qualitative data collection techniques. We aimed to gain insights from PALS about their initial experience using DuoRhythmo and to assess how well the prototype aligns with the principles of inclusive design in the field of accessible music technology. The use of a mixed-methods approach is recommended by Lucas et al. [ 35 ] to improve the evaluation of inclusive music projects, as it captures both objective and subjective data about the user experience. Our approach aims to identify any challenges PALS face while using DuoRhythmo and determine its potential value for their personal use.

As ALS is a progressive and rare disease, there are evident difficulties in establishing an end-user UXE of ADMIs for PALS [ 37 ]. Evaluating inclusive music projects is also difficult, as the unique needs and challenges faced by the target users of ADMIs make it difficult to establish a standardized evaluation methodology suitable for all users [ 22 ]. Furthermore, the global COVID-19 pandemic added additional challenges, as PALS were at a higher risk of severe illness and required extra precautions to protect themselves.

To address the challenges of evaluating inclusive music technology for individuals with motor impairments, such as ALS, we carefully tested and refined our prototype through usability tests with non-disabled users. This allowed us to identify and address potential issues with the prototype before exposing our test participants to unnecessary risks. However, Majaranta et al. [ 36 ] found that user models for individuals with motor impairments cannot be adapted based solely on data from non-disabled test participants. As Davanzo and Avanzini [ 15 ] have pointed out, the importance of conducting end-user studies with individuals with motor impairments cannot be understated. By conducting end-user studies with our target population, we aim to ensure that our results are relevant and meaningful for individuals with ALS and that our prototype is designed to meet their unique needs and challenges.

5.1 Mixed-Method UXE Design

As speech impairment is a common complication of ALS, with approximately 75% of individuals with the disease unable to speak as the disease progresses [ 18 ], we placed a strong emphasis on designing an accessible UXE procedure for test participants with communication impairments. During this process, we learned that our five recruited test participants would all be able to speak verbally, but we still found great value in the ideas proposed by Beneteau [ 7 ] for conducting qualitative research with individuals who have difficulty communicating. Therefore, we followed Beneteau's suggestions to focus on short, well-prepared UXE sessions and share detailed instructions with our participants beforehand to allow them to prepare their initial responses. To do this, we took the following steps:

  • We kept the UXE session short to avoid causing fatigue for our test participants.
  • We gave test participants extra time to formulate their answers to questions.
  • We sent a detailed description of the UXE procedure to test participants beforehand so they could prepare their initial responses.
  • We used reaction cards as a starting point for the semi-structured interview and asked test participants to elaborate on their chosen words.

By following these steps, we aimed to ensure that our UXE was accessible and inclusive for all participants, regardless of their communication abilities. In our study, we wanted to gather quantitative data on the usability of DuoRhythmo, to understand how well our end-users could learn and use the product to achieve their goals. To do this, we used the System Usability Scale (SUS) [ 10 ], a well-known and reliable method for measuring usability. We interpreted the results of the SUS survey using the grading scale proposed by Bangor et al. [ 4 ], which categorizes the overall usability of a product based on the mean score obtained from the SUS survey. Petrie & Bevan [ 48 ] suggest using the SUS scale to effectively evaluate user satisfaction and experience, as it is a more reliable measure than ad hoc questionnaires.

As our test participants completed the SUS scoring sheet and reflected on their user experience with DuoRhythmo, we asked them to choose at least five words from a list of reaction words provided by the Microsoft Desirability Toolkit (MDT) [ 6 ]. The list of words, shown in Table 1 , was designed to capture the participants’ subjective reactions to the product. We then conducted a semi-structured interview to allow the participants to elaborate on their selected words and discuss their overall experience with the prototype.

5.2 Participants

The recruitment of test participants took place during the development of DuoRhythmo with the help of the PALS and CALS Advisory Council at the International Alliance of ALS/MND Associations [ 44 ]. The five PALS test participants consisted of two females and three males based in Turkey, Holland, the UK, the USA, and Iceland, as shown in Table 2 .

Some participants had difficulties with verbal communication due to their reliance on a ventilator. However, none of the participants were dependent on AAC devices. Despite this, we found that our study's design, which initially included considerations for AAC users, was beneficial for optimizing time usage during the UXE for all participants.

P1 is an expert user, having been musically adept before his ALS diagnosis and having experience using both a head mouse and an eye tracker to compose musical scores using EyeHarp [ 64 ] and his breath for playing the breath-powered instrument Jamboxx [ 31 ]. P2, P3, P4, and P5 did not have previous experience playing music or operating ADMIs. Overall, the test participants generally preferred to interact with traditional computer input methods such as a mouse and keyboard if their condition allowed them to do so.

Despite their initial preference for traditional forms of computer interaction, several of our test participants noted that they expected to transition to more accessible input methods, such as an eye tracker or a head mouse, as their motor impairment progressed. For example, P4 used an eye tracker daily as an alternative to a computer mouse when her arms became tired due to her condition. Participants P1, P2, P3, and P5 had experience with eye tracking for interaction, but they preferred using other methods because they found them more effective than the eye tracker.

5.3 Procedure

We individually scheduled a conference call with each of our 5 test participants. For each session, there were three researchers present; one to conduct the session, one in charge of the technicalities, and one that would improvise together remotely with the test participants through the DuoRhythmo interface. Once a time slot was agreed upon, we made sure to prepare each participant as much as possible by sharing an agenda for the meeting and a tutorial video on how to operate DuoRhythmo.

The UXE was facilitated using Microsoft Teams and recorded for thematic analysis. Participants were free to use any input modality they preferred and were given a task to navigate the interface and create music. They were first asked to interact individually with the interface to familiarize themselves with its features and learn how to use it with guidance from a researcher. The task involved making a complete revolution around a spatial carousel and interacting with each panel so that the participants would be comfortable collaborating with a researcher later.

After completing the collaboration task, participants were sent a link to the online survey where they could rate their experience using the SUS. They were also asked to select five or more words from a list of MDT reaction cards that described their user experience. This was followed by a semi-structured interview, where they were asked to 1.) Elaborate on their top five reaction words; 2.) Provide suggestions for future development of CADMIs; 3.) Reflect on the potential impact of having a CADMI such as DuoRhythmo available for people with ALS.

5.4 Data Analysis

The responses for the SUS were recorded via an online survey tool, then we calculated the mean and interquartile range of the individual items of the SUS questionnaire based on the five responses and visualized them through boxplots. Even though our small sample size cannot suggest deep underlying trends, through analyzing the distribution of the data we aimed to identify areas of interest regarding DuoRhythmo's specific aspects of usability. Due to the small sample size of the quantitative data gathering, most individual items showed unanimous trends. Thus, we only highlighted two usability areas with diverse responses, which might indicate underlying usability issues.

We transcribed the semi-structured interviews using Otter.ai [ 46 ], then we organized the transcribed data for a thematic analysis using NVivo [ 28 ] and MAXQDA2022 [ 55 ]. The researchers facilitating the semi-structured interviews and transcribing, then performing the thematic analysis, are the same research group members. We interpreted the results via an inductive thematic analysis [ 9 ], where one member of the research group coded the transcripts and discussed the coding rationale with another member of the research group afterward. The main goal of the qualitative data analysis process is to identify connections between participants based on their experience of interacting with DuoRhythmo. Thus, this process aims to extend the insights gathered from participants’ elaboration of their MDT reaction words and uncover deeper underlying themes of the prototype's user experience.

We identified specific transcript passages linked by a common theme as initial codes. This is followed by determining a framework of thematic ideas, where we created themed categories based on the thematically coded passages of text. A total of 43 primary codes were extracted initially, then we further iterated to form a final number of 28 codes. The codes are clustered into six themed categories.

6 RESULTS

6.1 System Usability Scale (SUS)

The individual SUS scores are presented in Table 3 . The results of the SUS survey suggest that DuoRhythmo's usability is satisfactory but may have room for improvement. The overall mean score was 79.5, which falls within the "good" range according to Bangor et al. [ 4 ]. A score of 80 or above is considered "excellent."

The participants who used accessible input devices such as eye trackers and head mice recorded the highest scores on average, with a mean score of 82.5. The design of DuoRhythmo was primarily catered for eye tracking devices, which might reflect why P1 and P4 gave the highest overall usability scores. P2, P3, and P5, who used other, non-accessible interaction methods, rated the usability lower than the other two participants.

Based on the boxplot representation of the scores in Figure 5 , we selected two items from the SUS survey for further analysis as they showed potential usability issues based on the varied responses from participants. The selected items were Q9 "I felt very confident using the system" and Q10 "I think that I would need the support of a technical person to be able to use this system." Both of these items had a score range of three, the largest deviation among the SUS scores. This suggests that participants had varying levels of confidence and perceived need for technical support when using the system.

Q9 asked participants about their confidence levels while using DuoRhythmo. The range of responses suggests that there may be two factors at play. First, participants’ prior musical expertise possibly affected their confidence while using the system. P1 was a professionally trained musician and an avid guitar player before being diagnosed with ALS. He also regularly uses other ADMIs to create music. Hence, P1 giving the highest rating to this question is not unexpected. On the other hand, P5 had no musical experience and faced technical complications while installing DuoRhythmo on her computer, which could only be resolved with the help of the research team. These factors may have contributed to P5’s low scores on Q9, suggesting a possible relationship between musical expertise and the ease of installing the prototype.

Q10 depicts users’ reflections on their technical abilities and whether they feel proficient in using the system independently. The responses to this item correspond with participants’ years of experience living with ALS. P4, diagnosed earlier than a year ago, had the lowest score, while P1, who has been living with ALS for 30 years, scored the highest. There is a possible correlation between the number of years diagnosed with ALS and the participant's score on Q10.

6.2 Thematic Analysis

This section describes the responses of the MDT [ 6 ] survey, followed by further elaboration from the test participants, presented in six themes outlined by the inductive thematic analysis. On average, the participants selected 7.2 words, and 33 out of 36 selected words were positive. All participants selected fun; four chose empowering and easy to use; three selected accessible, engaging and stimulating. The three selected negative words were busy, complex, and stressful. Figure 6 shows the distribution of words chosen in a histogram.

6.2.1 Effects of musical co-creation on quality of life. Several participants found the concept of musical co-creation to be a meaningful feature of DuoRhythmo. P5, for example, described it as a fun experience: “I especially liked doing it together. It made me curious, and that was fun.” (P5) She also emphasized the playfulness of musical collaboration: “Well, when you play together, it's like a game. And playing with each other, I liked it. But also on your own. But I think, together, it's much more fun.” (P5)

While playing together with one of the researchers, P1 reflected on the phenomenon of collaborative discovery that can occur through musical co-creation: “I noticed that the friend and I can make music together. I can see, I can hear my partition. I can hear all the parties and we can discover together.” (P1)

Participants also discussed the importance of collaborative experiences like DuoRhythmo in mitigating loneliness and depression among PALS. P5 found value in DuoRhythmo as an outlet to ease minds: “People don't have to think they are ill or something, you know? They can forget their minds. And I don't have a problem with forgetting my mind, but I know a lot of people have trouble with it. So I think it can help them.” (P5)

It was also suggested to use DuoRhythmo as a tool to reach out to other PALS and connect with them while playing together: “If I could use this to; hey, can you play with me this thing? And we could start some relation. And, I am just thinking of this as a starting point to drag this person out in life.” (P3)

P1 mentioned the challenges that the ongoing pandemic has posed for people with ALS, particularly in terms of socializing during a lockdown: ” Nowadays, we need fun more than ever. Because of the pandemic and lockdown, and you know, depression, loneliness. They need someone to make something together, and this is the fun part of the software.” (P1)

6.2.2 Musical exploration as a form of mental stimulation. Most participants described how challenging it is for PALS to find activities that can stimulate their intellectual needs while being accessible. Several participants highlighted the enjoyment of simple musical exploration in DuoRhythmo: “Just being able to play with the different sounds and different beats. It wasn't a burden to me, I thought it was quite enjoyable.” (P2) The availability of various rooms for different genres further encouraged participants to explore new sounds: “I like how you have different rooms in there. So also, you know, helped me to explore the instruments that I had no idea that is out there.” (P4)

The ability to easily change the effects and hear their corresponding sonic feedback in the composed beats encouraged participants to explore further. P2 found it engaging to experiment with modulating different effects and see how changes to the UI elements affected changes in the sound domain: “It engaged me because it is making me think about using both sight and sound, particularly with the measures or sliders. It made me actually think about the process. In that sense, I found it engaging.” (P2) P4 also had fun while exploring the direct sonic feedback based on changing UI elements: “(...)fun to see, you know, it's like cause and effect, choosing different buttons, and there's a different sound.” (P4) P4 also liked the Euclidean algorithm feature, and it helped her to explore more complicated beats further: “Wow (…) I love this functionality! (…) That is really cool.”

As someone with prior musical knowledge and a lifelong history of playing instruments, P1 appreciated the features in DuoRhythmo that encouraged musical exploration: “This is very important. Making music again, for me, and people just like me.” (P1) . He continued elaborating on playing rhythms as a way of mental stimulation: “Stimulating because the rhythm is the basis of life, I think. And, when I play the rhythm and the rhythms around, I feel some positive feelings that stimulate me.” (P1) Similarly, P2 found the process of creating pleasing beats mentally stimulating: “I found the idea of making sound, that was in some sense pleasing to listen to. And watching how you could do it, and then actually doing it - and changing it yourself - I found it mentally stimulating.” (P2)

6.2.3 Accessible interaction design paradigms. Most of the participants commented on the accessibility of interacting with Duo-Rhythmo. P1 compared it to other non-accessible DAWs, which he found too complex to control: "But it was very difficult for me to work on the digital audio workstation, it's very complex to me, because I have only one mouse click, and I cannot handle all the things together with just one click." (P1) He reflected on the accessibility and ease of use of DuoRhythmo: "Accessible means for me that I can play music with just one click." (P1)

Other participants also highlighted the overall accessibility of DuoRhythmo. P2, who had a small degree of motor impairment due to his ALS progression, did not face any difficulties interacting with the prototype using a computer mouse: "Well, I mean, it's easy to navigate. Certainly as I was using a mouse with my hand. It is easy to operate in that sense." (P2)

P4 mainly used her eye tracking device to create music via DuoRhythmo. She appreciated the flexibility and range of input options available, as she typically prefers using a mouse but switches to an eye tracker when her arms become tired: "(...) from an accessibility perspective, being able to use eye gaze, I think that's huge. And my arms get tired easily. So that's why I use both mouse and eye gaze." (P4) She found that the target sizes were optimal for eye gaze-based interactions, and P2 also agreed with this assessment, even though he did not personally use an eye tracker: "I would imagine people with a head-mouse or eye gaze, because of the spacing of the various buttons, would also find it accessible." (P2)

6.2.4 Musical expertise affecting the overall experience. When designing DuoRhythmo, we aimed to develop a creative outlet in the form of musical co-creation, which is also suitable for the musically untrained. Our participants reflected on the complexity of the prototype, and we identified various patterns. Before trying the prototype, P2 felt concerned about the ease of use without any musical background: "Having watched the tutorial, I was unsure if I would be able to properly apply what I learned." (P2) After using DuoRhythmo, P4 also expressed similar concerns about the complexity of the app: "I'm not a musician. At first glance the screen, the interface can feel like a lot." (P4) However, she also suggested that the complexity of the app might pose a challenge for people without prior musical expertise in the long run: "So I think it's a relative thing, it really depends on the player. (…) And then as people play along, learn and get up to speed about the app, it may not be complex anymore.” (P4)

In contrast, P1 had a quick learning experience with the app, which may have been due to his extensive musical knowledge: "Easy to use, because, in a minute, I learned how to play some instruments together. And I learned how to, turn some effects on and off." (P1) Additionally, DuoRhythmo's quick learnability reminded him of physical drums: "And in a short time, I learn what can I do with this software, just like a real drum." (P1)

After the initial learning phase, P2 found that using DuoRhythmo to play music was easy: "But once it was clear how to be used, I thought it was easy to use from a perspective who could not play an instrument to save his life.” (P2) Similarly, P3, who had no prior musical knowledge, was pleasantly surprised by his own performance after playing around with DuoRhythmo: "Well it was surprising to me that I could maybe make something out of this. Something good, because I have no experience, and only bad experience, in my ability to sing or play instruments." (P3) Furthermore, P5 felt empowered to create music for the first time ever using DuoRhythmo: "It was empowering because, for me with zero music experience, I can make some kind of music." (P5)

6.2.5 Usability issues of the prototype. Several participants pointed out usability issues with the navigation and UI of DuoRhythmo. A particularly problematic area was the effects UI, which is controlled by sliders. P2, for example, did not have a comprehensive understanding of the effects beyond the BPM slider: "Beat per minute, I understood that from the tutorial. I don't understand the others." (P2) P4 also noted difficulties in using her eye gaze to modify effects with the sliders: "I think it's hard to use eye gaze to do the sliders." (P4) She suggested that more practice would be necessary for her to become proficient at controlling the sliders.

Another area of the prototype addressed by the participants was the sub-nodes on the sequencer interface. The passive representation of the sub-nodes did not effectively communicate their intended purpose of representing other instruments in the sequencer and instead led users to believe that they were interactive: "What are the little circles under the kick drum?" (P1) "So the smaller circles near to the center, do they serve any functions?" (P4) After receiving a direct explanation from one of the researchers, P4 was able to successfully use the sub-nodes in her composition.

6.2.6 Future considerations. In addition to the usability issues described above, participants suggested new features and potential future directions for DuoRhythmo. P2 proposed making the tutorial more extensive and providing more information about the prototype in various formats: "Still, a tutorial or a written instruction to explain it more in-depth than the shared introduction video would be nice to have." (P2) P3 proposed some functional features that he believed would help him feel more ownership over the beats he created, such as the ability to add his own sounds: "And of course, I can't wait for the ability to maybe add sounds to this. Like a lake flowing or some volcano erupting or, you know, these sound effects that could be in the background, and you could add on to it." (P3) Furthermore, he suggested the feature to save or record his compositions after completing a session in DuoRhythmo: "Is there a possibility to record it, keep it, or store it and come back to it, to work more on it?" (P3)

7 DISCUSSION

The process of research, design, development, and UXE of Duo-Rhythmo provided insights on various levels. Through our mixed-methods study design, we uncovered practical implications regarding the interaction design of CADMIs for PALS. We also gained insights into the possible effects of CADMIs on the quality of life of PALS and how they might be extended to other target groups. Finally, we formulate our learnings about conducting a remote UXE.

DuoRhythmo enabled PALS with varying musical and physical abilities to experience musical co-creation. We demonstrated this through the UXE, where the accessible UI and the networking capabilities of the prototype allowed participants to collaborate and create music remotely with one of the researchers. Our mixed-methods study involved five participants. Despite the small sample size, we believe that our use of mixed methods allowed us to discover valuable insights. Especially considering the reasoning provided by Nielsen in their work on small sample sizes [ 41 ], as well as the simulations conducted by Smith et al. [ 54 ], which show that small-N designs can be effective in uncovering relationships between participants. Nevertheless, such a small-scale quantitative evaluation should not lead to firm conclusions. Instead, we used the quantitative SUS data to identify problematic usability areas and uncover their roots through the users’ qualitative feedback, following an explanatory approach [ 29 ].

During the semi-structured interviews, PALS pointed out tangible usability improvements described in the thematic analysis. One of these was the design of the inner circles, which represent the activated nodes on the other drum panels. P1 and P4 found this confusing. Moreover, P4 also pointed out her difficulties when interacting with the sliders using an eye tracker. This was a design problem we had spent considerable time trying to solve. Once we explained the design rationale behind the sliders, she still saw difficulties in how well they would perform for eye tracking interaction. We suggest future testing sessions with additional users to properly assess the effectiveness of the developed eye tracking interactions for the sliders.

The SUS item related to users’ confidence (Q9: "I felt very confident using the system") suggested that prior musical knowledge might affect usability. This was further investigated in the theme "Musical expertise affecting the overall experience." Our analysis showed that participants with prior musical knowledge tended to have a more positive attitude toward the system and reported higher levels of confidence in using it. This suggests that the design of CADMIs should be personalized according to the musical abilities of the intended user group to ensure the best possible user experience. We believe that our initial goal of creating a CADMI for novice users was achieved to some extent, but it is clear that PALS, without prior musical knowledge, needed more time to learn how to use DuoRhythmo.

Following the suggestions by Ilsar et al. [ 27 ], we designed the prototype to offer functionalities for musical self-expression through customization and personalization. Although our system did not offer optimal learnability for novice users, all participants pointed out how they found musical exploration mentally stimulating, as identified in the theme Musical expertise affecting the overall experience . Regardless of their musical background, PALS enjoyed playing around with various effects, exploring their direct sonic feedback, and exploring new instruments within various genres.

Designing for accessibility has been one of our key drives while developing DuoRhythmo. We embraced the guidelines provided by Ilsar et al. [ 27 ], and Frid [ 22 ] regarding accessible and adaptive interaction methods when designing ADMIs. The participants welcomed this positively, as outlined in the theme Accessible interaction design paradigms . P4 interacted with the prototype with an eye tracker and a computer mouse simultaneously, showing the interaction design's flexibility. The consistent overall SUS scores among test participants with a range of input modalities further demonstrate the value of the adaptive and accessible inputs in DuoRhythmo. Notably, the highest scores were achieved by participants who used accessible input devices, emphasizing that DuoRhythmo was designed with eye tracking as the primary method of interaction from the outset.

By extending ADMIs to the collaborative domain of CADMIs, we were particularly interested in how PALS assessed musical collaboration and its possible implications based on interacting with DuoRhythmo. Described in detail within the theme Effects of musical co-creation on quality of life , several participants found value in a CADMI as individuals or as a group lacking social interaction. PALS proposed the potential of connecting through musical co-creation to be helpful to combat isolation and loneliness, which often become increasingly apparent as the disease progresses. Depression is prevalent at the later stages of ALS, as pointed out by Taylor et al. [ 60 ]. Based on the feedback provided by the participants, we believe that CADMIs has a promising potential to help mitigate such mental issues.

Conducting user tests on a prototype developed with input from the target group can be difficult, especially during a global pandemic, as noted by Schroeder et al. [ 52 ]. This is particularly true when designing for an accessible target group with specific input devices for interaction, such as PALS. To facilitate the remote testing sessions with our end-users, we took additional steps to prepare. Before the UXE session, we shared documentation containing a tutorial video, testing agenda, and project description with our participants. This allowed them to prepare independently and ask questions in advance if needed. During the session, we assisted with installing the prototype on their computers. This step proved challenging for some participants due to limited technical expertise as we encountered unforeseen issues with their firewall. To address this, we prepared an alternative solution that was effective for remote user experience testing. We launched DuoRhythmo on our computer and shared the screen with the application running through a Microsoft Teams meeting. Then, we guided PALS to request control of our computer through Teams’ native functionality. While this solution provided a viable alternative for remote testing sessions, it also introduced a less controlled testing scenario that relied on a stable streaming connection. Obtaining direct data from our target group during a global pandemic is a novel endeavor emphasized by Schroeder et al. [ 52 ], and as such, utilizing creative but unproven methods can be beneficial for achieving distributed user-centered design.

7.1 Limitations

While we believe that our small-scale study is a useful starting point for further research, we acknowledge that it has limitations in terms of generalizing our results to a larger population. For instance, our study only involved a small number of participants. Further research using a more extensive and diverse sample would be necessary to understand the potential of CADMIs for PALS fully. One limitation of our research is the lack of data on the potential for CADMIs to improve the quality of life for PALS through continuous use. We propose running a longitudinal study to investigate the relationship between the quality of life of PALS and the use of a CADMI. This will be more feasible given the results of the UXE, and such a study could provide valuable insights into the possible long-term benefits of using CADMIs for PALS.

As we conducted the UXE sessions remotely, we could not control the testing environment, which may have introduced inconsistencies and bias. However, remote testing also offered ecologically valid settings. Previous researchers have noted the challenges of coordinating testing with PALS, as they often have limited opportunities to travel long distances [ 37 ]. Our remote testing approach allowed us to overcome these challenges and gather more representative data. One disadvantage of our remote testing approach was the inconvenience of the installation process. As some participants pointed out, installing DuoRhythmo at the beginning of the testing session was cumbersome. This may have impacted their ability to engage with the system during the testing sessions fully. In future studies, we plan to circumvent this by releasing the software on the Windows Store to lessen the installation process, ensuring a more seamless testing experience.

7.2 Future Work

Given the amount of feedback, there is a long list of possible improvements to be made for DuoRhythmo and its evaluation. For instance, we identified that the sub-nodes were problematic and causing confusion. To address the issue, we plan to reduce their sizes and revise their placement, alongside introducing them one at a time as they are activated rather than all at once. This will free up a lot of UI space for DuoRhythmo, possibly improve the visual organization, and heighten the usability.

We propose the need for further testing to gather feedback on the collaborative nature of a CADMI. Future research analyzing and understanding the characteristics of musical co-creation should help improve our understanding of the importance of musical collaboration in the context of accessible technologies. There are several possible directions regarding the use case of CADMIs. Is DuoRhythmo best suited in a personal home setting for PALS and CALS to enjoy a moment of co-creation together? Or will it have a more significant impact in a clinical setting for more severe cases to be used as a mediation tool to reach out to people that have locked themselves in? Overall, it will be rudimental to know what the long-term benefits of CADMIs might pose to both PALS, but also a broader spectrum of accessible target groups. And to measure this, we imagine a longitudinal enjoyability study on the possible impact of CADMIs.

We hope that our work in extending ADMIs to a collaborative domain inspires other researchers to create new projects within this field. Our approach to designing and developing a CADMI is only one way of accomplishing it. We believe that creating new collaborative musical experiences for different accessible target groups will be of significant value to the accessible technological research community. We hypothesize that DuoRhythmo and the concept of a CADMI could be extended to other accessible user groups, particularly where physical or intellectual disabilities induce isolation, loneliness, and depression. We suggest broader target groups with motor impairments and user groups with developmental impairments.

8 CONCLUSION

This research presents the design and evaluation of DuoRhythmo, a collaborative accessible digital musical interface for PALS during the COVID-19 pandemic. With the proposal of the new term CADMI and the prototype of DuoRhythmo, we hope to emphasize the positive aspects of a remote collaborative musical experience for PALS. We followed a user-centered and iterative process with stakeholders from the PALS and CALS communities and presented a remote UXE of DuoRhythmo with five PALS. Participants described the prototype as accessible, empowering, fun, engaging, and stimulating and gave an average SUS score of 79.5.

This work offers three main research contributions: 1.) Through a mixed-method UXE evaluation, it presents rich qualitative input from PALS about using a CADMI; 2.) Interaction design paradigm on creating an inclusive interface rooted in eye tracking but facilitating a continuum of accessible inputs for motor-impaired users; 3.) Insights about conducting a remote UXE with PALS during a global pandemic.

With the implementation of our CADMI, we hope to inspire future researchers to develop collaborative musical interfaces through inclusive and accessible methods. We present a CADMI designed specifically for PALS; however, we believe that other users can benefit from a collaborative, remote, and inclusive way to play music together. Thus, we propose further exploration in this field and other methods for designing and evaluating CADMIs.

Bringing the experience of musical co-creation through accessible digital musical interfaces to the target group of PALS seems to be encouraged by the experts within the field and is also welcomed by both musically adept and inept PALS. Based on the results and feedback received during the project, we believe that future iterations of DuoRhythmo and possible new collaborative accessible digital musical interfaces can contribute to improving the quality of life for PALS.
