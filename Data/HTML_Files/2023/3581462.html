Potential and Challenges of DIY Smart Homes with an ML-intensive Camera Sensor

CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Sensors and actuators are crucial components of a do-it-yourself (DIY) smart home system that enables users to construct smart home features successfully. In addition, machine learning (ML) (e.g., ML-intensive camera sensors) can be applied to sensor technology to increase its accuracy. Although camera sensors are often utilized in homes, research on user experiences with DIY smart home systems employing camera sensors is still in its infancy. This research investigates novel user experiences while constructing DIY smart home features using an ML-intensive camera sensor in contrast to commonly used IoT sensors. Thus, we conducted a seven-day field diary study with 12 families who were given a DIY smart home kit. Here, we assess the five characteristics of the camera sensor as well as the potential and challenges of utilizing the camera sensor in the DIY smart home and discuss the opportunities to address existing DIY smart home issues.

1 INTRODUCTION

Smart homes are becoming more pervasive as we encounter more types of sensors that can help automate our daily lives [ 6 ]. At the same time, researchers have raised issues regarding an automation-prevalent society [ 6 , 17 , 18 , 42 , 48 ] and the importance of user intervention and control to the use of automated systems [ 6 , 65 ]. In this regard, various studies have been carried out on do-it-yourself (DIY) smart homes to support user empowerment and meaningful advancements in people's home lives.

A DIY smart home system consists of multiple sensors and actuators, and users can connect these to customize the features [ 26 , 27 , 64 , 66 ]. In particular, home sensors can collect data on residents’ activities and home conditions to perform appropriate smart home features [ 28 , 51 ]. Therefore, effective use of sensors is an important requisite in building user-centered DIY smart homes.

IoT sensors, which have been commonly used in smart homes, can only provide information at an indirect level, which means only limited activities can be inferred [ 25 ]. Based on the limitations of these existing IoT sensors, new types of sensors, such as multimodal sensors [ 7 , 8 , 34 , 47 , 59 ], camera sensors [ 37 , 41 , 45 , 70 ], and audio sensors [ 20 , 32 , 36 , 53 ], have emerged to recognize more specific and concrete activities. Such advances in sensors have changed the way people and sensors interact as well as the way sensors are utilized in smart homes. While these new types of sensors have great potential, their increased complexity gives rise to new challenges for users to effectively harness this potential and master their use.

In this study, in response to these sensor changes, we explored the potential of DIY smart homes with a machine learning (ML)-intensive camera sensor as well as the additional challenges that have arisen compared to commonly used IoT sensors. We chose a camera sensor from among the advent sensors since it is used in a wide array of domains as a result of its broad applications, and related studies using camera sensors in home environments are actively being undertaken [ 4 , 23 , 24 , 41 , 43 , 45 , 60 ]. To this end, we investigated users’ DIY smart home building process by providing them a DIY smart home kit with a camera sensor and commonly used IoT sensors. We conducted a seven-day field diary study with 12 families. On the basis of this study, we derived the potential and challenges of camera sensor utilization, which differ from those of IoT sensors in DIY smart homes, and synthesized discussion points to revisit the issues addressed in current DIY smart homes from the perspective of camera sensor utilization. By doing so, we investigate the potential of reducing current DIY smart home user experience barriers based on the characteristics of the camera sensor. In addition, we shed light on how cameras should be utilized to unleash their new potential without jeopardizing the advantages of commonly used IoT sensors by highlighting challenges that may arise as a consequence of their distinctive characteristics.

2 RELATED WORKS

2.1 User Experience with DIY Smart Homes

In DIY smart homes, users can actively connect various sensors and actuators to customize features that solve their complex problems to meet their individual needs [ 26 , 27 , 64 , 66 ]. However, for non-technical users, building DIY smart homes is a challenge in itself; hence, one of the main objectives regarding DIY smart homes is to reduce and/or remove existing technological barriers that must be overcome before users can adequately use, control, and monitor these smart features [ 38 ]. Users should be able to collect appropriate data from sensors and control the physical environment through actuators to achieve this goal. However, there remain several challenges and limitations in the current state of DIY smart home setups. First and foremost, considerable time and effort are required to learn to use and control these sensors and actuators for smart home features [ 53 ].

Moreover, IoT sensors commonly used in existing smart homes are numerically operated, detecting the state of an object or person based on numerical values [ 14 ]. Commonly used IoT sensors are low cost, are easy to install, and have few privacy issues, but these sensors provide information at an indirect level, allowing inference of only limited situations [ 25 ]. For example, when using a door opened/closed sensor, one cannot definitively answer the question, “Did the children come home from school?” [ 33 ]. This limitation creates a gap between the actual question posed by the user and the response from the sensor [ 14 ]. Such gaps in using sensors can be a limitation in allowing users to build DIY smart homes to suit their needs.

New types of sensors have recently emerged to overcome these limitations, and the ML-intensive camera sensor is a representative example. Its development has enabled sensor systems to answer the exact questions of their users [ 32 , 33 ]. These sensors can fill the gap between a user question and the corresponding answer by a sensor because they can specifically recognize and respond to desired situations. The advent of these novel sensors could alter the user experience of smart homes built with commonly used IoT sensors as well as provide new potential and challenges. Thus, the following section describes the detailed advance of a new type of sensor: the ML-intensive camera sensor.

2.2 Emergence of ML-Intensive Camera Sensor for Use at Home

A camera is one of the representative examples of emerging sensors [ 23 , 43 ]. At home, it is often used to protect people from strangers [ 45 ]. However, its uses and roles extend well beyond crime prevention (e.g., accidentally capturing fun moments [ 4 ] or monitoring children and pets [ 24 ]).

Moreover, applying ML technology to a camera sensor enables users to ask the desired questions, especially those based on visual information. A related study proposed Zensors [ 22 , 33 ] (i.e., a camera sensor platform that allows users to arbitrarily generate sensing situations for all visually observable attributes). Camera sensors have also been used to predict human activities through object recognition [ 37 ]. Additionally, a study attempted to integrate a mobile phone camera into home automation, which focused on the potential of the user to intuitively interact with the object through the camera [ 41 ]. For example, an appropriate internet of things (IoT) control button can be presented when a certain device is recognized (e.g., displaying an on-off button when the camera senses an IoT light bulb). In addition, several studies on home services using home cameras have been conducted (e.g., detecting objects and human faces to find lost items [ 69 ] and taking pictures of meal recipients after meal delivery [ 60 ]).

Advances in ML-intensive camera sensors enable explicit interaction, which may offset users’ lack of technical experience that hinders the construction of smart homes [ 53 ]. However, the unfamiliarity and increasing complexity of new types of sensors make them still difficult to use. Research on camera sensors at home has been primarily conducted along with studies on security and privacy [ 23 , 43 , 45 ]. Additionally, research on assigning meaning to and using camera sensors at home was conducted [ 23 ]. However, the usage of camera sensors is becoming more diversified, and although there are currently considerable discussions about camera sensors in the field of human-computer interaction (HCI), the study of actively using ML-intensive camera sensors in a real DIY smart home environment is still in its early stages. Therefore, this study focused on investigating the process of building a DIY smart home using a camera sensor to explore new user experiences. The following sections explain how we devised our study to explore the various novel user experiences of DIY smart homes that can be associated with the advancements in sensors by focusing on using an ML-intensive camera sensor.

3 METHODS

This study was designed to discover the new potential and challenges arising along with the advent of ML-intensive camera sensors. To achieve this goal, we conducted a comparative study between commonly used IoT sensors and a camera sensor. We expected to effectively shed light on these new potential and challenges by comparing users’ experience with the most widely used in-house IoT sensors in DIY smart homes to that with ML-intensive camera sensors, thereby discovering not just new potential for camera sensors that may benefit the current DIY smart home building process but also challenges specific to situations utilizing camera sensors as opposed to commonly used IoT sensors. For this purpose, participants individually derived ideas and implemented DIY smart home features for seven days, and a diary study [ 46 ] was undertaken to document the process in detail. We offered participants a DIY smart home kit and a diary to record all their activities and experiences in this process in their own homes in real time.

3.1 DIY Smart Home Kit

First, a combination of sensors and actuators had to be selected for inclusion in the DIY smart home kit. For IoT sensors, we selected the most commonly used types of sensors that could also be sufficiently replaced by a camera sensor. We referred to three studies on sensors in smart homes [ 31 , 34 , 66 ] and listed the types of the commonly used IoT smart home sensors they included, namely, illuminance, temperature, humidity, air pressure, acceleration, gyro, timer, door opening, button, socket, vibration, sound, color, proximity, and magnet sensors. Further, we excluded the types of sensors that are better suited for audio sensors than camera sensors or suited for neither type of sensor. In addition, we also selected more generalized sensors if there are sensors that can perform similar functionality. For example, since the proximity sensor can perform the functionality of the door opening sensor adequately, it was included as a more generalized sensor among the two sensors. Consequently, the representative commonly used IoT sensors were classified into illuminance, proximity, touch, movement, and tilt.

For our study, we needed a software tool that could connect to sensors and support participants in implementing features with minimal technical constraints, and we selected the Cubroid coding block set (Figure 1). The intention of Cubroid coding blocks [ 9 ] is to teach children how to code. The sensors and actuators are constructed in the shape of Lego bricks, allowing children to easily construct desired shapes. Furthermore, the Cubroid coding blocks can be linked with the block coding-based Scratch program [ 52 ], allowing users with minimal programming experience to quickly develop desired functionality. In addition to the proximity, illuminance, and touch sensors included in the Cubroid coding block set, we had to include movement and tilt sensors. In that sense, since the micro:bit sensor [ 16 ], which can also be connected to the Scratch program, was so close to the sensor we were looking for, we chose to use it in conjunction with the Cubroid coding block set (Figure 1).

For the camera sensor, we used a camera sensor [ 10 ] sold by Cubroid. This sensor provides a simple wireless camera that can also be linked to Scratch and facilitates the implementation of the function to create an ML class and classify images using Google's Teachable Machine [ 21 ]. Teachable Machine is a web-based GUI tool that uses a camera to help users create customized ML image classification models without professional technical knowledge. Because Teachable Machine could easily enable participants—including those with little ML knowledge—to build their own ML models, we considered it ideal to explore the use of camera sensors without any technological hindrance.

For the actuators, we aimed to provide the experience of deriving and implementing the necessary smart home ideas rather than focusing on constructing a real, working smart home. Each Cubroid coding block provided to participants included an LED, a light, a motor, and a sound actuator. Further, when we delivered the kit, we verbally explained the types of included DIY smart home actuators and their functions to the participants to facilitate their understanding. We also encouraged participants to build the simulation prototypes to express their intended functions even if they were unable to build fully functional smart home features that control actual home appliances in a realistic way using the given kit.

We had three participants with knowledge of ML, three with experience in building DIY smart home features, and six without either. However, as block coding had to be actively used in this study, all 12 representative participants had to have experience with block coding. All participants agreed to participate in the study, which was approved by our institutional review board (IRB No.KH2022-071).

3.3 Study Method

After the participants received the kit provided by the researcher, each of them conducted a diary study using the kit. In the DIY smart home building process proposed by [ 66 ], our study was intended to be organized around the motivation phase for deriving smart home feature ideas and the implementation phase for putting those ideas into action. As the primary focus of our study was the participants’ sensor utilization experience of the participants, we opted to concentrate on these two phases that entail the most active sensor use. Consequently, our study consisted of three sessions: icebreaking, deriving smart home ideas, and implementing derived ideas (Figure 2).

After performing icebreaking activities (Session 1) on the first day, the activities of deriving smart home ideas at least two on one day (Session 2) and implementing them on the next day (Session 3) were repeated thrice, as shown in Figure 2. We expected that the participants would develop their own criteria for selecting sensors by repeating the process of derivation and implementation of smart home ideas three times. We did not give any direction in this process about the selection of commonly used IoT sensors and the camera sensor. Instead, we encouraged participants to self-determine and utilize the sensors they considered most suited for implementing the feature. We did, however, require participants to utilize a camera sensor at least once, which they had less experience with before, when implementing a total of more than six ideas, enabling participants to compare their experience in building a feature using commonly used IoT sensors and the camera sensor. Table 2 in the Appendix shows the features of participants implemented with the two types of sensors. Subsequently, we conducted the study with the participants who agreed to take part.

3.3.1 Session 1: Icebreaking. The purpose of icebreaking was to teach the participants to use the sensors and actuators. Therefore, we explained how to connect sensors and actuators and conducted a Teachable Machine tutorial [ 63 ] to teach beginners to use a camera sensor. Because the connection and building functions of all blocks had to be developed using Scratch, we also briefly described the core coding blocks of Scratch programming.

3.3.2 Session 2: Deriving Smart Home Ideas. Session 2 involved selecting the activities needing improvement at home and deriving smart home ideas to solve them. To help participants effectively identify the required improvements and derive smart home ideas, we organized this session by referring to existing studies. Some studies helped users to derive smart home ideas by asking them to reflect on familiar elements of their homes, such as recalling the routines of their family members [ 67 ], defining what home meant to them [ 58 ], and exploring the objects in their home [ 40 , 56 , 64 ]. Woo and Lim [ 67 ] proposed Routinoscope, a new concept for a smart home system using routines as a resource for DIY smart homes designed to reflect one's own routines and develop a family's collaborative smart home ideas. In another study, Park and Lim [ 40 ] asked participants to draw their own house maps to understand their perceptions of their own homes. In a similar study, Sull and Lim [ 56 ] asked the participants to reflect on their health-related habits by looking around their homes and exploring the objects associated with them to design health-promoting technologies. In addition, Williams et al. [ 64 ] helped participants derive ideas about object modification using defamiliarization methods that challenged familiarity as they walked from room to room to find objects to be modified. With reference to these studies, we developed an activity in which participants looked back at their homes and routines to effectively discover desirable smart home features. To achieve this goal, Session 2 comprised five steps (Figure 3).

  1. Drawing a house map and writing core activities: Participants drew a map of their own house, including key objects/appliances, and wrote down their usual activities in each area. The activities of each family member were marked in different colors for distinction.
  2. Writing down the routines of all family members living together: By referring to the house map and activities described in the first step, they created a table listing the routines of family members.
  3. Selecting activities to be improved with smart home features: By referring to the house map and routines, the participants selected at least two activities per day to be improved using smart home features.
  4. Deriving smart home ideas: Participants derived smart home ideas based on the activities they chose to improve. They planned to put their thoughts into reality by choosing sensors and actuators, considering the data that would be acquired with the sensor and the functionality that would be carried out with the actuator. They also wrote a detailed explanation of why each sensor and actuator was selected.
  5. Writing down difficulties: Participants wrote about the difficulties they faced during Session 2, especially related to using sensors, on the “Writing Difficulties” page. We did not provide a specific format to record these difficulties, as this was mainly intended to serve as a reference for participants during the interview session.

3.3.3 Session 3: Implementing the Smart Home Ideas. Session 3 comprised four steps (Figure 4). In Session 3, participants implemented the ideas they had derived in Session 2. After connecting the sensors and actuators to the Scratch program, they programmed the required functions using coding blocks and placed the sensors and actuators in the appropriate locations in their homes. During this implementation process, they recorded their screens to quickly reflect on their trial and error in Step 4.

  1. Recalling smart home ideas: The participants were reminded of their smart home ideas derived in Session 2.
  2. Implementing ideas using Scratch: The participants implemented the features of their smart home ideas using the Cubroid coding blocks. After implementing each feature, they captured the corresponding Scratch screenshots and attached them to their diaries.
  3. Positioning the sensors and actuators in appropriate locations: The participants placed the sensors and actuators in appropriate locations, verified that they were working properly, and uploaded working videos on Google Drive, which we provided for archiving purposes.
  4. Recording trial and error: The participants watched their recorded videos, and reflected on them, and wrote down all their trial-and-error process. They freely wrote about the difficulties they faced, the methods to solve them, and the problems they encountered that they were yet to solve.

After completing the three sessions, we interviewed participants with the aim of comparing the experience of using a camera sensor with that of using commonly used IoT sensors. The interview was divided into three segments: experiences in deriving the smart home feature ideas, experiences in implementing those ideas, and perceptions of using particular sensors to implement those ideas. In the first segment related to idea derivation, we asked the participants about their motivation for deriving each smart home idea and the criteria for selecting a particular sensor. In addition, to compare the experience of using a commonly used IoT sensor to that of using a camera sensor, we asked about the difficulties and differences while deriving ideas and the help required to actively use a camera sensor in this process. In the second segment related to implementation, we asked participants to explain the implementation process of their ideas. In particular, we asked about the difference in difficulties they encountered when using commonly used IoT sensors and a camera sensor. In the third segment related to perceptions, we explored the new potential and challenges posed by the camera sensor by asking the participants to share ideas that would benefit from the active use of a camera sensor. The participants were also asked to share the advantages and disadvantages of a camera sensor compared with those of commonly used IoT sensors.

3.4 Data Analysis

The audio interviews with the participants were recorded, and approximately 1080 minutes of data were collected through interviews with the 12 participants. All data were digitally transcribed. After extracting key quotes from the interviews, we qualitatively analyzed the interview data through thematic analysis [ 2 ]. To qualitatively analyze the results based on the experiences of the participants in using sensors, two HCI researchers with experience in performing qualitative data analysis conducted open coding [ 55 ]. Because our study tried to compare the difference in experience between commonly used IoT sensors and a camera sensor in the process of building DIY smart home features, we determined that identifying the DIY smart home sensor utilization process was necessary before comparing that experience. First, we generated an initial code list based on Woo and Lim's [ 66 ] comprehensive DIY smart home building process. However, since the previous study covered the entire lifecycle of DIY smart home use instead of particularly focusing on the sensor usage process in ideation and implementation, we enlarged the code list by coding data that were relevant to the detailed sensor utilization process. Because each participant used a different level of words and specifics to describe the sensor utilization process, coders engaged in a discussion to identify terms applicable to all participants. The two coders were able to achieve an agreement on the codes and eventually identified the phases of the DIY smart home sensor utilization process (Figure 5) by continually discussing viewpoints on the codes during the analysis process. Subsequently, we analyzed the results by comparing the experience of using commonly used IoT sensors with that of using a camera sensor within each phase of the process. After comparing the experiences, we synthesized the results, focusing on the potential and challenges posed by the new DIY smart home with an ML-intensive camera sensor.

4 FINDINGS

The study findings were organized based on the differences in user experience between the commonly used IoT sensors and the camera sensor for DIY smart home building. The experience of using the IoT sensors and that of the camera sensor were compared by relying on the sensor utilization process, as presented in Figure 5. The DIY smart home sensor utilization process was organized into five stages.

First, participants had to select a sensing situation and a sensor . Since there are numerous ways to sense a situation, the onus was on the participants to choose the sensor and determine how to utilize it. For example, to decide how to determine if the air conditioner is turned on or off, participants could sense if the wings are open or closed with a proximity sensor (P7) or whether the power lights are on with an illuminance sensor (P9). After selecting a sensor, it was necessary to determine the specific role of the sensor to recognize a specific situation through the sensor. After that, participants needed to place the sensor appropriately because the location of a sensor can greatly affect its role and capability. For example, P7, who wanted to use a proximity sensor to detect the activated air conditioner via wing movement, had to select a sensor location where the wings could be sensed only when the air conditioner had been turned on. After selecting the appropriate sensor location, participants made rules as intended based on block coding. After that, through the process of testing and modification of the feature , participants had to verify whether that feature worked as intended. Errors were to be eliminated via a repetitive process of trial and error. These five stages were conducted for both the commonly used IoT sensors and the camera sensor, although the way in which each stage was accomplished differed (Figure 5).

Next, from 4.1 to 4.5, we describe the findings regarding the new user experiences that the characteristics of the camera sensor brought to the DIY smart home, as well as the potential and challenges caused by the new user experiences of the camera sensor.

4.1 Undefined Nature of Sensing Functionality: Matching a Sensor to the Sensing Situation

When participants used the commonly used IoT sensors and the camera sensor, they showed differences in the order between selecting a sensor and determining the sensing situation. As the functionalities of commonly used IoT sensors are already defined, participants had to first consider the functionalities of the sensor and then reconstruct the possible situations to be sensed. For instance, an illuminance sensor is a sensor that solely detects the quantity of light, and a proximity sensor measures whether there is an object nearby. In contrast, when using a camera sensor, which has no predefined sensing functionality, participants were able to match the functionality of the sensor to the situations they really wanted to sense without any restrictions. This is because the camera sensor can detect all visually detectable properties, allowing a single sensor to perform several functions.

The camera sensor was able to recognize all the various situations that I had considered, so I thought it could be used to recognize any situation that I wanted. (P3)

It also allowed participants to freely select any situation that they wanted to sense without worrying about how to configure the sensor to detect the desired situation.

I think that the only thing that can directly recognize the movement of push-ups is the camera sensor. (P3)

In contrast, when using a commonly used IoT sensor, participants had to reconstruct a sensing situation according to the sensor's functionalities. Some situations were difficult to recognize directly using commonly used IoT sensors. This is because commonly used IoT sensors have limitations in the functionalities of sensors provided, with each sensor already having a clear predefined function and purpose. Therefore, participants sometimes integrated another sensor to implement features with their own novel ideas to detect certain situations, despite the complexity of this process.

It was difficult to determine whether a person was entering or leaving based just on the detection of the door opening (with a movement sensor). Consequently, I had to consider what other factors might differentiate the two situations, and I determined that it would be appropriate to place a proximity sensor in the hallway. If I grabbed my inner door handle and then walked down the hallway, I went to work; if the reverse occurred, I returned home. (P3)

Therefore, when using commonly used IoT sensors, participants should identify the sensor's functionality before selecting the sensor, and then reconstruct a suitable sensing situation that corresponds to the sensor's functionality. However, when using a camera sensor, it is not necessary to reconstruct the situation to be sensed.

Potential: Participants did not have to think about the regularity of implementing smart home features when using the camera sensor. As the camera sensor could directly learn participants’ most naturally occurring behavior, there was no need to reconstruct sensing situations that were suitable for the sensor's functionality.

I think I'll use this camera sensor a lot for sensing situations because it recognizes my behavior, even if I act naturally (without worrying whether the sensor can detect it or not). (P1)

For example, a camera sensor can be used to detect the action of a person who approached a closet door and raised their hand to open it rather than using a proximity sensor to detect whether there is a person in front of the closet.

I think that the only way to predict the natural action of approaching and opening a closet door would be via a camera, even if I didn't get really close, touch it, or perform any direct actions. (P11)

Another good example of such convenience is shown well in one of P5’s examples (Figure 6). P5 was to check if their two children arrived safely, and in order to do so, it was essential to confirm whether the kid who returned home was the first or second child. The P5 built each class with a camera sensor so that the camera could instantly distinguish the first and second children by recognizing their faces. If this functionality had to be implemented using commonly used IoT sensors, it would have been necessary to establish if the child was the first or second child based on their routines, such as when each child typically returned home.

The advantage of sensing a direct situation is that participants were able to recognize the desired situation in the camera sensor without having to consider their routines. Participants did not always follow their normal routines, and in certain instances, there were no routines at all. With the camera sensor, participants did not have to find common rules in target situations to implement in smart homes.

Challenge: Even though it was possible to teach the camera sensor their most natural behaviors, participants sometimes struggled with it because they did not have a clear idea of the camera sensor's ability. Instead of performing behaviors participants wanted to be sensed, they sometimes performed other unnatural triggers that they thought the camera sensor could better recognize. They thought that such triggers would be better recognized due to clear visual differences. This happened mostly due to their unclear knowledge of how to effectively teach their desired behaviors to the camera sensor. Consequently, although the camera can learn their more natural and desired behaviors, the participants gave up teaching it and rather applied what they thought were more recognizable triggers even if they were unnatural gestures.

Initially, I intended to divide my "Attempting to open the door" gesture into three stages based on the angle of my arm, but I decided that raising my hand would be simpler and more distinguishable, so I implemented the feature with that gesture despite the fact that it is not my natural gesture. (P11)

As previously mentioned, sometimes the participants worried about the sensor's accuracy, which hindered their ability to select the most needed sensing situation. This notion led participants to employ only specified, tested triggers for the camera sensor as opposed to thinking up their own ideas for utilizing the sensor.

I found in the tutorial how to clearly differentiate between the left and right hands, therefore I tried to use these two motions as the cooking timer's triggers. (P2)

As a result, participants needed support in picking a trigger that allowed them to effectively sense the natural situation they desired to detect as well as accurately teaching the picked trigger.

4.2 Teaching Sensing Role Based on Demonstration: Reifying Camera Sensor's Roles through Bottom-up Teaching

When using commonly used IoT sensors and the camera sensor, participants differed in their approach to narrowing down the sensor's specific role. When using commonly used IoT sensors, participants adopted a gradual top-down approach by setting constraints, whereas for the camera sensor, they utilized a gradual bottom-up approach in which the sensor's role was determined through teaching based on demonstration.

When using the camera sensor, participants had to create ML model classes and provide training examples for each class. This procedure facilitated highly direct communication, as it allowed participants to demonstrate their desired sensing situation to the camera sensor. Using training examples supplied by the participants to reify the role of the sensor was an example of a bottom-up approach.

I created a class called "When I Want to Open the Closet Door" and put image data into that class regarding gestures involved in opening the closet. (P11)

To sense my wake-up time, I tried to recognize the stretching gestures, and I actually took images of them as if I were waking up in the morning. (P1)

The bottom-up teaching was more useful for the participants with less technical knowledge since they intuitively presented the appropriate scene to the camera sensor. This allowed participants to easily specify the role of the camera sensor according to their desires.

In contrast, participants determined the commonly used IoT sensor's role via a gradual top-down approach by setting constraints so that the sensor could clearly perform specific sensing. When the sensor's sensing range was too wide, they had to narrow it down by progressively adding conditions. Participants narrowed down the role of sensors in various ways, including by adding more sensors and setting temporal or spatial constraints on them. Participants added "time" as a constraint factor, which limited the execution of certain features to a designated time period.

I attempted to implement a feature that would notify me if the gas valve remained open after cooking, but with the proximity sensor installed in the kitchen, it was hard to determine whether I was cooking or just present in the kitchen. However, since I usually cook between 6 and 8 p.m., I disabled the alert function at that time. (P12)

Participants included "space" as another one of the constraints. For example, when movement was detected within a specific space through the proximity sensor, it was designated as the occurrence of a representative activity mainly performed in that space. This limited the usefulness of the sensor role in situations in which only one activity was performed in a specific space.

My bed is a sleeping space only. When I'm in bed, I just need to turn the light on low. [...] I placed a proximity sensor next to the bed, and if the sensor detects something, the "Turn the light on low" feature will be activated. (P9)

As such, participants who used commonly used IoT sensors employed a top-down approach to narrow down the sensor's role, in which multiple conditions were set to sense the desired specific situation. In contrast, participants who used the camera sensor employed a bottom-up approach, which allowed the camera sensor to include all desired situations via direct teaching based on representative image data.

Potential: Participants were free to modify and expand the features they had already built. This is because when implementing a feature using the camera sensor, they could input the situation they wanted to observe as an individual class. Therefore, participants reported that it was easy to modify and add sensing situations, even after the initial setup.

At first, I thought it would be nice if the camera only recognized me, but there are visitors who come to my house often, so I made additional classes and taught the camera with their images. (P12)

I first thought it would be sufficient for the camera to recognize just the situation in which I was studying, so I created a class named "studying." But after seeing how well the functionality worked, I decided to create another class named "knitting," since I wanted to be able to change the lighting's color depending on what I was doing at my desk. (P11, Figure 7)

In contrast, when a feature was implemented through a commonly used IoT sensor, it was difficult to modify and add other situations that participants wanted to detect since they generally had to replace an existing one or install new sensors and rebuild the rule to account for the changing situations.

I wanted automated exercise tracking. First, I installed a touch sensor on my room's doorknob and built a feature to record my physical touch after working out. [...] However, to automatically record this, I had to affix a new proximity sensor to the location where I placed my fitness bag. [...] This process seemed more like starting from the beginning and implementing new features than modifying existing ones. (P2, Figure 7)

As a result, smart home features will require continuous updating, as participants’ needs are constantly changing. Thus, the bottom-up approach adopted by the camera sensor may be helpful in terms of maintaining and improving the features.

Challenge: Participants said that they needed further support in teaching the appropriate roles of the camera sensor. To use the camera sensor for its intended purpose, the ML model must be properly trained, but participants noted the difficulty and unfamiliarity of training ML-intensive camera sensors. The main challenge was determining how to separate classes so that the situation could be adequately grouped and recognized. Following the division of the classes, it became essential to teach using their own image data. Furthermore, participants reported that the inability to clarify which elements of the image they wished to teach made teaching less effective.

I attempted to teach the camera sensor various hand gestures, but the image I captured was too tiny to see my hands. I wanted the camera sensor to just learn the part of the image containing my hands. (P2)

Moreover, participants had difficulty determining if the ML model had learned enough to utilize the camera sensor as intended after teaching. They said that they needed visible indicators demonstrating the model's learning outcomes during training to successfully teach the ML model.

It was challenging since the sensor couldn't show the outcomes of my teaching. It would be great if the camera provided a visual cue indicating the training results. (P6)

4.3 Room-Wide Sensing Approach: Deploying Camera Sensor to Incorporate Multiple Smart Home Features

After specifying the sensor's precise role, participants had to choose an appropriate location for the sensor, as the sensor's location has a significant impact on its capability and role. The criterion for selecting the appropriate location differed when participants utilized commonly used IoT sensors and the camera sensor. Participants had to come up with sensor location ideas to improve accuracy while using commonly used IoT sensors; when utilizing camera sensors, they had to place sensors such that multiple features could be implemented by one camera sensor.

Participants mostly sought to utilize the camera sensor when they needed to detect many situations at once since it captures the whole room.

If the whole house is being monitored by a camera, there is no need to employ many commonly used IoT sensors; instead, all smart home features may be integrated into a single camera sensor. (P6)

At the same time, participants who were worried about privacy wanted the camera to run just for the purpose of implementing features, and they wanted to choose a position that included the areas they desired to observe while excluding private areas that did not need observation.

I wanted the camera sensor to just monitor the execution of these functions to avoid being unnecessarily observed in my daily life. (P1)

I intended to limit the camera's activation range to a portion of the room rather than the whole area. (P9)

Therefore, participants had to choose a location that could catch all of the situations in which they wanted to be recognized while also avoiding observing events that they did not want to record using a camera sensor.

In contrast, when using commonly used IoT sensors, participants considered sensor placement to improve accuracy. Participants had to come up with ideas for sensor locations to identify sensing situations relatively accurately when deploying the commonly used IoT sensors to implement features. For example, if participants wanted to detect sunlight without interference from their inside lighting, they spent a significant amount of time determining the sensor's location.

I experimented with moving the illuminance sensor to distinguish between when there is a lot of sunlight and when the inside light is just turned on. (P8)

Similarly, when it was necessary to minimize interference from the robot vacuum cleaner when deploying a proximity sensor, participants put the sensor at a height to prevent the sensor from recognizing the robot vacuum cleaner.

The robot vacuum cleaner usually goes on the floor, so I had to place the sensor high in front of the sink. (P6)

As such, commonly used IoT sensors have limited capabilities and roles, but the position of the sensor decides whether the situation can be observed accurately and in detail.

Therefore, participants had to consider the location of the sensor placement to improve accuracy when using commonly used IoT sensors and to effectively incorporate various features when using the camera sensor.

Potential: The camera sensor naturally led participants to consider the association between features. For commonly used IoT sensors, individual features were each implemented using a single sensor, making the participants think of each feature's role separately. In contrast, for the camera sensor, a wide range of participants’ behaviors could be recognized, and various features could be implemented with just one sensor. Participants thought about the relationship between the features and considered whether they affected each other. This is meaningful because the participants’ features were not arranged independently, but there were features that were connected to each other, such as in a hierarchical relationship. With the camera sensor, features that appeared complicated or sophisticated could be quickly implemented by easily defining the relationship between numerous features, which was challenging with commonly used IoT sensors.

First, I made a feature to turn on the kitchen light if there is a person in the kitchen; then, I made another feature to play music when a person is cooking. [...] As a result, since the second feature must always be executed after the first, I built the rule by ensuring that the prerequisites for the execution of the second feature are met after the first. [...] It was straightforward to create such a connection since it was possible to capture an entire situation using a single camera sensor and build both features together. (P4, Figure 8)

However, if each feature were built with individual commonly used IoT sensors, it would be difficult to consider their association and construct features that are related to one another. For example, P10 built a feature to turn off the light when a person leaves the room and a feature to close the window when the light turns off, hoping to connect the two results linearly (Figure 8). However, P10 could not figure out how to do this since the two features could not communicate with each other. On the other hand, if many features could be incorporated into a single camera sensor, an organic connection between various features could be defined. This consideration can make a real “smart” home feasible rather than create an ineffective combination of individual features.

I think it would be smarter to install a camera sensor and create situations where it can recognize my overall behavior and execute the features organically. (P3)

Challenge: Intuitive recognition of what features are enabled in one camera sensor was very difficult. Unlike the commonly used IoT sensors, whose physical presence directly reminded users of established smart home features, camera sensors’ fixed and remote location made it difficult for participants to readily recognize and map established features to their respective areas and actuators. Therefore, these new experiences made it difficult for participants to remember all their smart home features at once.

Sometimes I study at my desk, sometimes I just rest, and sometimes I just do my hobbies. Whenever I'm at my desk, I think I can use the sensor as a feature to optimize the environment by sensing the things that are on my desk. Then, more than three features may be implemented at the desk alone, and I doubt I'll recall if additional features are built here. (P11)

Since many features can be implemented in a single space, I think I'm going to forget what all the features are. (P2)

This became even more noticeable when they implemented personalized features for multiple individuals in one camera sensor, as the camera sensor could readily identify each member. If the members’ routines differed, participants wanted to implement a personalized smart home feature. For example, P9 had a routine in which he/she used a hair dryer after taking a shower, so he/she tried to sense whether the dryer was used to detect if he/she had taken a shower. However, since P9’s other family members did not use a hair dryer after taking a shower, P9 had to set another routine that could be applied to those particular members.

My dad doesn't use a hair dryer as part of his routine, so I'll need to implement another routine for him. [...] Countless features will need to be implemented to apply individual routines for each family member to each smart home feature. (P9)

This had the advantage of freeing participants from thinking of the smart home features as being family-oriented and enabled individual feature implementation to meet the individual needs of family members. However, since the features that each family member wanted to build differed even for the same situation, it was very difficult to effectively remember whose features were entangled in one sensor.

4.4 Visual Element-based Sensing: Building Intuitive Image Data-based Rules

Participants had to implement rules after choosing suitable sensor locations. They established rules based on numerical data while using commonly used IoT sensors, while they established rules based on intuitive image data when using the camera sensor.

The camera was able to acquire intuitive data since it is a sensor that directly identifies differences in visual elements. Even if the image data collected by the camera sensor is not converted via the data filtering process, it is still possible to determine what the data means. For example, it was possible to directly recognize exactly how many empty hangers there were (P11) and the push-up gesture (P3). To determine whether an object is present, participants can directly ask the camera sensor whether the object is actually in the desired position rather than sensing the movement of the object with a movement sensor and converting a numerical value into a situation result.

We continually need toilet paper and cosmetics. Thus, instead of depending on proximity sensors to tell me that the object is no longer there, I'd want a camera sensor to examine and inform us of how many are left. (P9)

As a result, the participants could receive image data from the camera sensor and implement image data-based rules.

In contrast, while utilizing commonly used IoT sensors, participants needed a data filtering process to get meaning from the data since commonly used IoT sensors record all situations in the form of numerical values. For example, they found data meaning by judging whether the number recorded was even or odd.

If the proximity sensor records an odd number, it means a person is entering the bathroom, and if it records an even number, it means a person is leaving the bathroom. (P8, Figure 9)

However, only participants familiar with programming did so, while other participants with little programming knowledge gave up implementation.

Therefore, making intuitive image data-based rules with the camera sensor was easy for participants because they did not need to go through data filtering to derive meaning from the data.

Potential: The implementation process and results when using the camera sensor were much more readable than those when using commonly used IoT sensors. Since the camera sensor collects intuitive image data, it could help participants make rules algorithmically simple. In contrast to commonly used IoT sensors with a data filtering process to lend meaning to the data, the detection of a direct situation could lead participants to implement readable rules.

When looking at the implemented coding blocks with commonly used IoT sensors, it was very complicated and difficult for others to distinguish the meaning. In contrast, when using a camera sensor, participants implemented intuitive algorithms that were easy to understand, even for others. Referring to the example in Figure 9, both ideas implemented the features of turning lights on and off based on people going in and out, but the code used for the camera sensor (P6) was much easier to understand intuitively compared to that used for the proximity sensor (P8). The clarity of these features may have advantages when participants modify features later or when other members need to modify them.

Challenge: The property of visual element-based sensing raises privacy concerns. As previously mentioned, the image data captured by the camera sensor might include a great deal of direct information that does not undergo a data filtering process; therefore, participants were concerned about privacy issues. Specifically, participants’ sensitivity to privacy even varied between each room of the home and each specific area of a room. Participants said that the living room, a social area for families, is a location where privacy is less essential, but their bedroom is a place where privacy should be respected more.

I used a camera sensor in my living room but not my bedroom. My bedroom is more private than my living room, so I just want to use a proximity sensor that only detects the presence of people in my bedroom. (P7)

Some participants said that the camera should not capture the bed or the area in front of the closet in their rooms but that it is okay if it captures desks and dressing tables.

I don't expect camera sensors will be used to implement sensitive privacy-related features, such as those related to taking off clothes or sleeping. (P9)

Therefore, prior to implementing a feature with the camera, the participants asked themselves whether it was okay to be continuously monitored by a camera at a certain location, and if not, they opted for commonly used IoT sensors that could not capture visual information to feel safer.

4.5 Intuitiveness of Sensing Results’ Rationale: Modifying Features Using Cause-driven Approach

After building a rule through block coding, participants had to check whether the feature worked well in the desired direction. If errors occurred, the feature was modified through trial and error. When using commonly used IoT sensors, participants modified the rule by changing environmental factors and observing sensor values, which was an effect-driven approach. If an error occurred when using the camera sensor, the participants modified it by trying to review the previous image data used for teaching, which was a cause-driven approach.

If participants wanted to discover why the feature with the camera sensor worked incorrectly, they looked back at the data they had already entered. This is because the camera sensor analyzes the situation based on visual clues, allowing participants to immediately comprehend the sensing result's rationale. After reviewing the data, they tried to filter out the unfit data and added a variety of new image samples.

For images where the door was slightly opened, it is difficult even for humans to determine whether a person is leaving or entering. Thus, I filtered out this kind of data and left just clear image data that represented the class. (P6)

In contrast, if an error occurred while using a commonly used IoT sensor, the participants modified the feature using an effect-driven approach. It was important for IoT sensors to properly select numerical values corresponding to the desired situation. As a result, participants recreated various situations they wanted to sense, observed how the numerical values changed, and then selected the appropriate numerical values again.

I monitored the illuminance sensor data while pushing up and going down to detect the push-up action and picked an intermediate value to distinguish the two motions. (P3)

The trial-and-error process using commonly used IoT sensors was not performed to find the correlation between the sensor value and environmental factors. Participants concentrated on watching diverse situations and attempting to choose meaningful values rather than understanding which variables did or did not affect the IoT sensor's values. In contrast, to detect and remove visible causes of error in the camera sensor, a cause-driven approach was necessary.

Potential: Rather than self-experimentation, participants likened the debugging process using camera sensors to a spot-the-difference puzzle. When utilizing the camera sensor, participants first reviewed the previously submitted data and then filtered out the unsuitable data by determining the source of the low accuracy. Participants were able to identify unsuitable data intuitively by comparing images based on visual differences. This process was clearer and simpler than debugging code, similar to a spot-the-difference puzzle.

When I looked over the image used for training, I saw that the placement of the laptop had moved and the end of the laptop had been shot together. I assumed that was why the sensor couldn't learn precisely. (P4)

In contrast, with commonly used IoT sensors, participants had to conduct self-experiments by reproducing various situations to determine the cause of malfunctioning. However, it was difficult to replicate every possible situation, and even if participants could, it was a challenge to determine what factors caused feature inaccuracies.

I moved back and forth to experiment, but the sensor suddenly reacted. I think it was due to the sudden strong wind at the time, but I'm not sure. (P2)

The cause-driven approach of trial and error used with camera sensors accurately identifies and eliminates the cause of malfunctions, which will reduce operational errors when using the features in the future, unlike the effect-driven approach utilized with commonly used IoT sensors, through which users cannot intuitively spot the cause of malfunctions.

Challenge: Participants stated that it was important to improve the efficiency of the data screening process. A participant who took over 400 images for one class stated that it was tough to filter all of the images.

Even though I only had two classes, I shot 400 images of each one. If the accuracy was low, I had to double-check each one. It was unthinkable. (P1)

Furthermore, even if they did not examine all the data, participants wanted to know the variables influencing accuracy.

Nobody has informed me if clothing or other variables may be a problem. That is something I am curious about. (P1)

As a result, the process by which participants reviewed numerous images and spotted incorrect images made employing the camera sensor difficult.

5 DISCUSSION

In this section, we address the DIY smart home issues as a result of the new user experience and the characteristics of the camera sensor. Section 5.1 explores new opportunities for camera sensors, concentrating on concerns in the present DIY smart home, and Section 5.2 addresses the privacy issues connected with camera sensors based on our findings of the five characteristics of camera sensors.

5.1 Opportunities Relating to DIY Smart Home Issues with Camera Sensor

5.1.1 Implementation of Individual-oriented Smart Home Features. Prior studies have considered the notion that smart home features installed in the house are often seen as belonging to the family rather than the individual [ 27 ]. One of the primary factors driving this notion is the inability of smart home features to detect specific family members, thereby pushing the development of smart home features in a family-wide fashion. Since multiple requirements may exist within a single feature, it is vital to determine the family's shared needs to avoid need conflicts [ 29 , 50 ]. Therefore, current smart home studies have been done to aid in the creation of family-oriented features; however, this approach has led to the development of restricted features that meet the demands of all members, mainly childcare, distribution of public space usage, and housework distribution [ 12 ].

As stated in Sections 4.1 and 4.3, the use of the camera sensor in smart homes has permitted the recognition of individual members of multi-person households. Due to the capabilities of these camera sensors, users were able to build features based on their personal needs without having to consider the requirements and routines of the family group as a whole. Building individual-oriented features will avoid family-oriented constraints and might aid multi-person households in building personalized smart home features for each individual. For example, rather than focusing on common family routines, users could add smart home features that are tailored to their own routines. In addition, even if the same feature is performed, the result of the feature execution may be personalized to meet the needs of specific members. One thing we need to be aware of is that effective support for the personalization of DIY smart homes might result in unintended consequences that undermine the value of family togetherness. Family-centered features, such as managing family members’ schedules to create family time, revisiting family memories, and dividing chores efficiently, may help the formation of a strong family connection [ 40 ]. Therefore, shared features and devices have a positive effect on family socialization, but individual features and device use might lead to family members’ isolation [ 39 ]. We did not investigate this approach in our study; nevertheless, future research should consider the preservation of individuals’ value as family members even if they build individual-oriented smart home features among themselves.

5.1.2 Intuitive Implementation Enabling Family Involvement. To develop a successful DIY smart home, it is essential to build new features and debug their errors. However, those with little technical knowledge often struggle to build and debug features [ 29 ]. One reason for the difficulty of this process is that the features work like a black box, making it difficult to determine which factors are causing the smart home feature to malfunction [ 11 , 27 ]. Therefore, most smart home features in homes were developed by technologically skilled individuals. Noting these challenges, HCI researchers focused on the experience gap between pilot users who actively participate in smart home building and passenger users who utilize the features produced by pilot users [ 29 ]. Due to these differences in member-to-member experience, the features produced by pilot users did not satisfy the demands of passenger users, and they led to an imbalance in the use of smart home features among members [ 30 , 50 ].

As stated in Section 4.4, implementing features using camera sensors has the advantage of being intuitive and readable to users due to their image-based implementation. Therefore, users with little technical knowledge may readily build features, and even if they do not build certain features themselves, they can intuitively understand how they function. Even if a feature were to be built by a pilot user, a passenger user may be able to modify it to fit his/her needs. Active participation by all family members in the building of smart home features will reduce the discrepancy in experience caused by members’ varying levels of technical knowledge and enable the long-term maintenance of smart home features. On the other hand, individuals with little technical knowledge want support in the form of suggestions for the use of camera sensors and for guiding the teaching process for the camera sensors in the desired manner. The inability of users to comprehend the sensing capabilities of the camera sensor may complicate the process of building new features and the modification of existing ones. Participants sought to select well-recognized trigger actions or objects while performing them as naturally as possible (Section 4.1). Therefore, proposing a trigger for which the camera sensor has a high recognition rate may aid users who are unfamiliar with the camera sensor to generate ideas for camera usage easily. For instance, after allowing users to simulate the situation they want to detect, camera sensors may suggest the least similar of two well-recognized situations. In addition, support for the data screening process (Section 4.5) and a channel to monitor the learning results (Section 4.2) are necessary to allow the user to effectively teach the camera the desired direction. Referring to previous work [ 68 ], visualization of sound data into clusters allowed users to visually identify datasets previously unincorporated into the learning system. It is possible to design a channel that monitors the learning outcome by referring to studies [ 62 , 71 ] that demonstrate the relationship between the learning outcome and the factors impacting the ML model's learning outcomes.

5.1.3 Easily Adjustable Features to Evolving Needs. The process of building a DIY smart home never stops; rather, the smart home building process is continually repeated since the home's features must adapt to the changing needs of the family [ 1 ]. Users’ routines, privacy preferences, and relationship types were deemed essential for the implementation of smart homes [ 6 , 49 , 61 ], and any change in these elements might affect users’ smart home needs. In the case of families raising children, for instance, home IoT support must be modified since parents’ safety requirements change as the children grow [ 57 ], children's awareness of technology rises, and the interaction between parents and children evolves [ 19 ]. Consequently, the process of users adding and modifying features to satisfy changing demands is crucial, since they are activities that must be undertaken continually as homes evolve [ 27 ]. To modify features, however, users must repeatedly perform the DIY building process (resetting the sensor to be used, changing the sensor's placement, modifying the actuator's results, etc.).

As stated in Section 4.2, camera sensors can effectively reflect the evolving needs of users since it is simpler to modify their already-implemented features than with commonly used IoT sensors. With commonly used IoT sensors, participants had to attach and connect more sensors to enhance the features. However, with camera sensors, users just needed to create a new class and teach a new situation to be sensed. Moreover, the user was able to teach the sensor's capabilities using a bottom-up approach, making it easy to adjust the established rules. Consequently, it is possible to easily enable the modification of features that will aid users in the long-term use of smart homes and continuously reflect their changing needs. To meet the evolving needs of users, it is necessary to stimulate continuing user reflection on already-implemented features. According to [ 13 ], the advantage of living in a prototype is that users may become physically immersed in the process of refining already-built features because users might be naturally presented with clues and ideas for enhancing, modifying, redesigning, and constructing prototypes. On the other hand, since the camera-sensor-based smart home feature lacks a tangible form, it may restrict the potential for continuing reflection. As a result, it may be required to visually highlight unseen smart home features. Prior research has been conducted to physically represent non-visible data for the purpose of user reflection [ 35 , 54 ], with some cases proposing an approach that displays tracked data on everyday objects connected to monitoring to help users reflect spontaneously during their daily activities [ 3 ]. Even though smart home systems with camera sensors do not support physical visualization, camera sensors are visual element-based sensors; therefore, the camera and augmented reality (AR) technology described in [ 70 ] can provide an opportunity to help visualize invisible interactions between objects and humans, allowing users to reflect on the smart home's features.

5.2 Privacy Issues with Camera Sensors

While it is true that the widespread usage of home cameras for CCTV purposes has made the use of cameras in the home more common and accessible, privacy issues connected to home cameras remain an alarming problem. Although we did not conduct studies concentrating on privacy concerns here, it is possible to reflect on how the five characteristics of camera sensors that we derived from our findings are linked to privacy issues. In this section, we will discuss the five characteristics of camera sensors that need attention or may be helpful in addressing privacy concerns.

5.2.1 Room-wide Sensing Approach (Section 4.3). Due to its room-wide sensing approach, a single camera sensor can incorporate several features, but this also causes it to capture non-required areas [ 23 , 43 ]. Moreover, the intangible and unclear aspect of camera sensors’ currently captured areas may increase privacy concerns [ 45 ]. Therefore, the participants endeavored to choose a position from which the camera could detect just the necessary areas (Section 4.3). When observation is not wanted, it may be necessary to build hardware and software that restricts the camera's field of view and avoids accidental data gathering. Using a tablet linked to the camera to block areas that do not need to be seen might be useful, and if the camera is monitoring, visualizing the area with ambient feedback could further increase privacy awareness.

5.2.2 Visual Element-based Sensing (Section 4.4) and Intuitiveness of Sensing Results’ Rationale (Section 4.5). The fact that the camera is based on visual elements and produces sensing results with an intuitive rationale has aided the intuitive implementation and modification of features. However, as noted in Section 4.4, the visual element-based sensing of camera sensors heightened participants’ privacy concerns, and as a result, they occasionally favored commonly used IoT sensors that could only recognize situations in an abstract manner. Consequently, it may not always be required to detect the most detailed situation that the camera sensor is capable of detecting, and it may be necessary to purposefully restrict the capabilities of the camera sensor. When privacy is an issue, it may be possible to reduce image quality intentionally or enable the camera to capture the scene with a limited color palette. Existing research has been conducted to obscure data acquired by camera sensors [ 15 , 43 , 44 ], and referencing these studies to create a tool that allows users to customize the sensing capability level of the camera to suit their privacy concerns may help to solve user privacy issues. Future research may investigate the factors that determine the difference between the privacy sensitivity of the camera sensor and the commonly used IoT sensor, as well as ways to design the camera sensor so that it feels as secure as the commonly used IoT sensor, based on the characteristics of the IoT sensor.

5.2.3 Undefined Nature of Sensing Functionality (Section 4.1) and Teaching Sensing Role Based on Demonstration (Section 4.2). Although the aforementioned characteristics of camera sensors may cause privacy concerns (Sections 5.2.1 and 5.2.2), the fact that the camera sensor does not have a predefined sensing functionality and users must teach the sensor its sensing role based on their demonstration may mitigate these concerns. The Peekaboo cam study [ 5 ] on the controllability of home camera automation highlights the need to design controls that allow users to choose when and what data is taken, as well as what data is shared. Using a camera in a DIY smart home should thus emphasize the process of helping oneself in determining what the sensor observes, how it interprets the situation, and what outputs should be given. The DIY control of the camera's capabilities will enable users to address privacy concerns regarding the management and control of data; therefore, it will be necessary to emphasize the DIY aspects of the camera sensor for users’ privacy concerns and examine the compatibility between user control and automation to prevent users from losing control.

6 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORKS

This study was conducted with one representative from each household to decrease complexity. Therefore, additional considerations may be necessary when using a camera sensor in a real-world household setting, since the corresponding representatives may not have considered the diverse demands of other family members. Also, in future works, privacy issues between individual family members must be considered. Specifically, sensors with different privacy standards, such as camera sensors, may necessitate additional study, particularly to assist families in obtaining privacy-related agreements. Additionally, each household's representative was supposed to have had at least one experience with block coding because, in our study, participants had to develop the features using a Scratch program based on block coding. However, if DIY smart homes utilizing camera sensors are created based on the potential shown in our study, non-technical users will be able to effectively build smart home features. Future research thus needs to recruit participants with varying levels of technical knowledge.

Moreover, although this study focused on the motivation and implementation processes to investigate the participants’ experiences with a camera sensor, the overall process of constructing DIY smart home systems has a substantial influence on user experience. Because this is an early study on the use of a camera sensor in DIY smart homes, we focused on the building process and the potential and challenges of employing camera sensors rather than observing practical usage. Therefore, future research may encompass the usage and modification of features over an extended period, which was not addressed in this study. We anticipate that our results might inspire future research that examines the practical user experiences of ML-intensive camera sensors in DIY smart homes that are meant to meet these potential and challenges.

7 CONCLUSION

In this research, participants freely built smart home features using a kit including a camera sensor, commonly used IoT sensors, and actuators and conducted a seven-day diary study. After this study, we performed an interview based on the diary to compare user experiences with the commonly used IoT sensors and the camera sensor. According to the DIY smart home sensor utilization process, we investigated the new user experiences with the advent of ML-intensive camera sensors and derived the findings with a focus on the new potential and challenges. By summarizing the findings of this study, we were able to generate discussions on the opportunities associated with camera sensors for existing DIY smart home issues. We believe that these discussions may lead to the design of more successful and user-centric DIY smart homes based on ML-intensive camera sensors.
