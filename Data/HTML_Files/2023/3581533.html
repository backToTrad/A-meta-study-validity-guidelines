Understanding Discussions Around Culture Within Courses Covering Topics on Accessibility and Disability at U.S. Universities

Laleh Nourian , Department of Computing and Information Sciences, Rochester Institute of Technology, United States, ln2293@rit.edu
Kristen Shinohara , School of Information, Rochester Institute of Technology, United States, kristen.shinohara@rit.edu
Garreth W. Tigwell , School of Information, Rochester Institute of Technology, United States, garreth.w.tigwell@rit.edu

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581533
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Teaching accessibility is essential in training technologists and designers. However, the topics of accessibility and disability are vast and intersect with culture (social constructions). Since cultural background is an influential factor in design decisions, which could have implications for accessible design, we wanted to understand whether and how courses at U.S. institutions address the importance of cultural influences when teaching accessibility and disability topics. We surveyed 72 students from U.S. institutions and ran 14 follow-up interviews with students who took technical and non-technical courses. Using reflexive thematic analysis, we found similarities and differences in how technical and non-technical courses approach accessibility teaching. We found a lack of cultural focus in accessibility teaching in the technical courses, which can be improved by adopting teaching approaches from non-technical courses. We also make recommendations to improve course design, such as including people from different cultures and disabilities to help develop courses.

1 INTRODUCTION

Cultural background is an important part of people's identity and can influence daily experiences, including the experiences of disabled people 1 [ 51 , 80 ], how products are designed (e.g., web interface design) [ 13 , 68 ], and how people interact with technology [ 55 ].

Research in the area of accessibility and Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) has generally explored teaching accessibility within computing and technical courses [ 3 , 17 , 49 , 62 , 63 ]. However, it is unclear whether discussions on the influences of cultural differences are being integrated into accessibility education. We focus on education because education is the essential component for training future designers and developers to have sufficient knowledge of accessibility [ 37 , 42 , 83 ], and to apply their future design practices in a way that is inclusive to all users [ 65 ]. But little work compares what is taught in technical and non-technical courses covering accessibility and disability topics.

Having sufficient knowledge about the experiences of disabled people plays an essential role in designing accessible content [ 23 , 39 ], which could be achieved by integrating disability studies into accessibility research and education [ 39 ]. Even though there has been an increase in the salience of disability studies in accessibility research in the last decade [ 23 ], there is still a gap in creating an agenda for what “disability studies-informed accessibility research” should be [ 23 ]. Since culture is found to be an essential component of disability studies [ 80 ], we believe that topics of culture should be integrated into accessible design courses too.

We would like to define some of the terms used in our paper to ensure readers have a sufficient understanding of the key points discussed in our work:

Definition 1: Culture . In general, culture is a difficult term to define because its definition depends on the context of its usage [ 67 ]. In our study, we adopt the definition of culture from the work of Reinecke et al. [ 52 ]: Culture is the social norms, habits, and preferences in a society that can be different in another society at the country level [ 52 ] such as the language that the majority of people in a society use [ 35 ]. Thus, when we speak of culture, we mean the social constructs (e.g., habits, preferences) users have adopted into their everyday life that impact many things, such as the way they use or interact with digital devices.

Definition 2: Technical Courses . Courses that primarily focus on the design and implementation of digital services (e.g., HCI courses, Software Design courses).

Definition 3: Non-technical Courses . Courses that primarily focus on theoretical knowledge about disability and do not mainly include technology design (e.g., Disability Studies, Disability, rights, and culture).

Our work focuses on understanding how accessibility education incorporates discussions around topics on culture in both technical and non-technical courses, aiming to uncover useful teaching approaches we can transfer from one area into another. Focusing on accessibility education is important because we want designers to understand and create accessible content [ 37 , 42 , 83 ]. Prior work has investigated teaching accessibility within computing and technical courses and its challenges in general [ 3 , 17 , 49 , 62 , 63 , 73 , 74 ], but they did not focus on culture in accessibility education.

Accessibility is fundamentally determined by design, and prior work emphasizes the influence culture has on design decisions. For instance, culture and language are inseparable [ 35 ], and designing for bidirectional languages (language that consists of both directions: Right-to-left and left-to-right such as Persian and Hebrew) is different from designing in only one direction (e.g., English) [ 18 , 19 ]. Furthermore, culture can influence people's preferences for digital interface design aesthetics, layout, and even interaction behaviors [ 28 , 29 , 47 , 54 , 58 ].

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) [ 30 ] is a primary resource for accessible design, but may not support cultural design preferences well. Although WCAG was developed by experts around the world, the only mention of cultural differences in the guidelines is brief comments about idiom translation, human language differences, and names [ 30 ] with no more discussions on cultural differences in design. Moreover, there are indications that assistive technology must be implemented differently in some countries, which requires developers to be aware of such issues. For example, the correct pronunciation of Japanese by screen readers is not a trivial task due to a single Kanji character having multiple pronunciations [ 79 ], and ruby elements in Japanese websites can conflict with screen readers [ 20 ].

However, the majority of research findings at the intersection of disability and technology in HCI venues are from the countries in the Global North [ 5 ]. One issue resulting from this knowledge gap is that current accessible design resources may not fully support international designers because the cultural background of both designers and users can impact design preferences [ 9 , 12 , 29 , 44 , 54 , 58 , 77 ], and accessibility resources should be developed to acknowledge these differences. Accessible design practice that is not sensitive to cultural differences will likely result in design marginalization. Digital Design Marginalization (DDM) draws attention to the exacerbation of a digital divide where certain users are negatively affected in wider social settings beyond the direct use of a digital system [ 64 ].

We ran an online survey completed by 72 students in U.S. institutions along with 14 follow-up interviews to explore what topics on culture are discussed when teaching accessibility in courses. Our survey findings highlighted that both technical and non-technical courses integrate topics on culture mostly via lectures when teaching accessibility or disability studies. However, this integration is happening more often in non-technical courses, mostly focusing on theoretical topics on socio-cultural practices or the representation of disability in different societies.

Our interview findings, however, revealed more details on the similarities and differences between technical and non-technical courses. Both technical and non-technical courses discuss culture-related topics, but non-technical courses mainly emphasize deeper discussions around disability perception and socio-cultural aspects of disability or accessibility in different geographical regions. The results of our interviews suggested that technical courses can benefit from having more cross-cultural discussions around disability at a deeper level when teaching accessibility. These discussions will help toward training more globally conscious designers.

In summary, we make the following contributions:

  • We present the first survey and interview data on the inclusion of culture-related topics into teaching accessibility in university courses in the U.S.
  • We provide a comparison between technical and non-technical courses that cover topics on accessibility and disability, and highlight the variations in how culture is discussed within the courses and what approaches can be adopted from the non-technical courses into technical courses.
  • We provide a set of recommendations to benefit accessibility education in terms of the inclusion of culture into it.

2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK

2.1 Accessibility and Disability Studies Research

The area of accessibility research mainly focuses on creating technology that is usable by people with different impairments, and disability studies centers around understanding and improving the lived experiences of disabled people from a socially-constructed lens [ 23 , 39 ].

Even though accessibility research has grown in the past 24 years [ 38 ], studies have shown that there are gaps in accessibility research that are related to prejudice against disabled people, oversimplification of disability, and the ignorance of connections between disabled people and their allies [ 23 ]. These existing gaps come from the misunderstandings towards disability among accessibility researchers and designers which show the importance of incorporating disability studies into accessibility research [ 23 ]. Thus, increasing designers’ and developers’ knowledge about disability.

Moreover, culture is an influential aspect in the conception of disability and the experiences of disabled people [ 51 ] because it shapes the way people think about themselves and others. Disability is viewed differently from culture to culture, and this fact can impact various aspects of disabled people's experiences such as education [ 14 ] Thus, disability is best understood in cultural and social contexts [ 2 ] and we believe that incorporating disability studies into accessibility research means including topics on cultural differences too.

2.2 Accessibility and Disability Studies Education

Sin et al. [ 64 ] conceptualized Digital Design Marginalization (DDM), which focuses on the social consequences of digital design such as excluding or pushing away certain groups of users (e.g., disabled people, older adults) [ 64 ]. Studies in the Digital Design Marginalization (DDM) area have argued that there are pedagogical gaps in teaching future designers how to consider the needs of many user groups such as older adults and disabled people [ 65 ].

Accessibility education and training are crucial if we wish designers and future technology professionals to have a proper understanding of accessible content [ 3 , 37 , 42 , 83 ] and avoid building marginalizing designs [ 65 ]. However, there is a lack of proper accessibility and disability studies training in HCI and computing areas, which is considered a problem for industry [ 3 , 49 ].

Some prior studies have identified multiple gaps in accessibility education. The work of Shinohara et al. [ 63 ] explored accessibility education among 352 institutions in the U.S. indicating that the biggest gap in accessibility education was related to unclear teaching objectives and lack of knowledge [ 63 ]. Baker et al. [ 3 ] argued that accessibility is disproportionately covered only in a small number of elective or HCI courses and highlighted the lack of clear research on what aspects of accessibility are currently being taught [ 3 ].

One of the integral elements for creating good accessible content is to have a proper understanding of disability, which can be achieved by integrating disability studies into accessibility and assistive technology research [ 23 , 39 ]. Yet, there is a lack of research on the integration of disability studies in HCI and accessibility education among the CHI proceedings. Therefore, there are opportunities for us (the CHI community) to explore and expand research on accessibility from an educational lens to identify effective ways to integrate disability studies into accessibility teaching, thus, improving accessibility education in technical and computing courses in the most effective way.

We argue that the integration of disability studies in accessibility education can and should lead to incorporating topics on culture into accessibility education as well. Disability and culture are closely linked [ 2 ], and when teaching topics on disability, the socio-cultural aspects of disability usually is included in the course curriculum [ 46 ], because disability is perceived and learned differently in different cultures and societies [ 50 ]. Therefore, culture, as a crucial component, needs to be embedded into accessibility education too.

Non-technical courses (e.g., disability studies courses) that focus on theoretical disability topics often incorporate culture in their curriculum [ 26 , 46 ]. So we tackle the idea of how approaches that are applied in those courses could be useful in technical courses that mainly focus on the technological aspect of accessibility (i.e., digital accessibility). Our work aims to understand how well accessibility and disability knowledge is taught in technical and non-technical courses. We compare whether technical courses discuss culture as a part of the course curriculum, and, if yes, how different topics on cultural differences within accessibility education compared to the non-technical courses are taught.

2.3 Digital Accessibility, Design, and Culture

When taking a broader look at accessibility, we find that inaccessible design still exists and can be rooted in different factors [ 57 ], which are related to poor education settings, lack of proper design tools that support accessible design, and inappropriately scoped and budgeted projects [ 11 , 36 , 49 , 69 , 71 , 72 , 73 ]. One of the most widely used accessibility resources is the W3C's Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.1 [ 30 ], however, the guidelines are not yet translated into all official languages such as the Persian and Turkish languages. The lack of all translations for WCAG means international designers need to use it in a non-native language, and research has already found designers are critical of WCAG's current written presentation as being difficult to understand [ 69 , 73 ]. This problem raises the question of whether some designers find it challenging culture-wise to use the guidelines. WCAG 2.1 [ 30 ] only mentions cultural differences in the context of idiom translation, human language differences, and names. Yet, design preferences (e.g., color, layout, and text) [ 9 , 12 , 16 , 28 ], information seeking and organization [ 9 ], decision-making and navigating interfaces [ 15 , 31 , 32 , 55 ], and different languages in design [ 18 , 19 ] are influenced by cultural differences.

Multiple studies at the intersection of digital design and cultural differences (e.g.,  [ 29 , 31 , 32 , 44 , 54 , 55 , 58 ]) have made some recommendations for inclusive design such as interfaces that automatically adapt to the user's cultural background [ 53 ]. This prior work demonstrates the influence of cultural differences on digital design and more specifically, the usability of visual design. However, we raised the question of how different cultural preferences can impact the accessibility of digital design, and if courses include discussions about these issues.

This perspective on cultural adaptation is inspired by increasing discussions around postcolonial computing [ 24 ] which focuses on design, research tactics, evaluation, and development of technology that non-Western cultures are forced to adopt. One of the important works relative to postcolonial studies is the recent work from Barbareschi et al. [ 5 ], which argued that most of the findings at the intersection of disability and technology in HCI venues are from the Global North. Barbareschi et al. do not discuss the influence of culture explicitly but highlight that there is a lack of literature on accessibility and disability studies in the Global South, even though 80% of the disabled people in the world live in this region [ 5 ].

Since user experiences and design preferences are different from culture to culture, we raise the question of whether the dominance of the Global North on the established accessibility practices and standards results in ignoring the cultural aspects of the societies in the Global South and under-represented countries, and what implications it might have for accessible design. In their recent work, Vollenwyder et al. [ 78 ] discussed the controversial arguments on whether the established accessibility standards are helpful for improving usability and user experience, for all user groups with or without disabilities [ 78 ]. For example, there are arguments on the disconnect between design preferences (i.e., what users and designers desire) and WCAG [ 78 ] such as color selection and contrast preferences [ 40 ]. WCAG imposes restrictions on contrast and color selection [ 40 ]. Meanwhile, design preferences can be impacted by the cultural background of people [ 12 ], meaning that contrast and color preferences can be impacted by cultural differences as well. Therefore design students, practitioners, and educators need to learn more about different cultural values and preferences to avoid incorrect assumptions that may be obstructive for certain groups of people [ 76 ].

2.4 Summary and Research Questions

Studies have shown that there is a need to incorporate topics on disability studies into accessibility practices to help designers and developers fully understand disability and create accessible content. We argued that it is important to include discussions on cultural differences in technical courses that teach accessibility since culture is an essential component of disability studies and because it can impact design preferences too. However, we do not know if discussions on cultural differences in technical courses are actually happening when accessibility is taught and how topics on culture are discussed, if at all. Thus, we seek to answer: RQ1 : What cultural topics are being discussed within technical and non-technical courses, if any? RQ2 : How are cultural topics being taught in technical and non-technical courses? (what teaching methods are being used?)

Moreover, to come up with ideas for improving accessibility education, we asked our participants to share their views to help us identify potential needs or issues when including topics on culture in courses teaching accessibility and disability studies: RQ3 : What are students’ views and feedback on including cultural topics in accessibility teaching?

3 ONLINE SURVEY METHOD

We ran an online survey with students from U.S. institutions to identify if there were discussions happening around cultural differences in courses that cover accessibility or disability topics. In the U.S., the number of graduated computing and information students has grown, leading to more people working in the industry who build technology for the global market 2 , and many of these students come from different cultural backgrounds 3 . Considering the cultural diversity in U.S. institutions, such courses in computing should be devised in a way that can benefit all students. Thus, we shared our survey among U.S. institutions, taking advantage of the cultural diversity in U.S. society and the presence of international students.

3.1 Survey Materials and Procedure

We ran an online questionnaire among current students 18 years or older at U.S. institutions, to understand if topics on cultural differences are included in courses that teach accessibility and disability studies. We used an online survey to collect initial data to familiarize ourselves with the current pedagogical settings. We shared our survey among students and departments that were both technical and non-technical. Examples include (but are not limited to) courses related to Computer Science, Design, Disability Studies, HCI, Information Studies, Social Studies, and Software Engineering. We consider courses to be technical if they primarily focus on the design and implementation of digital services (e.g., HCI courses, software design courses) and non-technical courses, primarily focus on theoretical knowledge about disability, and do not mainly include technology design. (e.g., Disability Studies). We used multiple ways to recruit participants for our online survey. We spread the flyer of our questionnaire on different online platforms such as LinkedIn, Twitter, Reddit, and Facebook as well as using our academic network to advertise our survey. We also identified relevant groups and mailing lists of institutions in the U.S. and reached out to the primary contact person (e.g., dept chair or instructors) to share our study. To identify institutions, we searched for disability studies, Computing, or Design programs in the U.S.

Our survey consisted of a total of 53 questions covering three parts: demographic questions, questions related to courses teaching accessibility, and questions related to courses teaching disability studies. Each of the last two parts consisted of a sub-part related to the inclusion of culture in their courses.

The demographic part included questions about students’ gender, age, cultural background, level of education, and major. In the second part, we focused on accessibility teaching. First, we asked a multiple-choice question about whether students have ever had courses that taught accessibility. For the students who had a course that covered accessibility, we asked these follow-up questions: the name of those courses, how much accessibility topics were covered in those courses, and how it was taught. But if students indicated that they never had accessibility taught in a course, they were directed to the next part (Disability Studies courses). We then focused on culture-related questions and asked students whether any of those courses that taught accessibility covered topics on culture. We provided a combination of open-ended and multiple-choice follow-up questions for the students who said yes to these questions to find out how culture was discussed, what topics on culture were taught, and whether they were taught in the context of design and digital accessibility.

The last part of the survey was the same as the second part but instead of asking about accessibility, we focused on courses teaching disability studies. At the end of the survey, we asked for participants’ emails and if they were interested in taking part in a follow-up interview.

3.2 Survey Participants

We received 141 responses and removed duplicates, invalid, and incomplete responses. Our questionnaire was completed by 72 participants (Man=23, Woman=42, Non-binary=7) who were aged between 18-64 years old (42 of the participants were 18-24, 23 were 25-34, 2 were 35-44, 4 were 45-54, and one participant was 55-64). 34 participants indicated they were undergraduate students, 37 graduate students, and one participant self-described as an adult student-on-the-side. Our distribution method resulted in a good variety of U.S. institutions. Our participants studied at various types of institutions, including public and private institutions, R1 and R2 institutions, colleges and universities without a Carnegie research classification, Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs), and Primarily Undergraduate Institutions (PUI). The geographical spread of institutions also covered different regions of the U.S. (Midwest, Northeast, Northwest, West, Mid-Atlantic, South, Southeast, and Southwest).

3.3 Survey Analysis

We read through the responses to familiarize ourselves with the data and used an open coding analysis approach [ 75 ]. We split the data set into two groups based on the following: 1) Students who had at least one technical course covering accessibility or topics on disability studies and 2) Students who had at least one non-technical course covering Accessibility or topics on disability studies.

We assigned initial codes to the responses to the open-ended questions and created themes by collapsing the codes to provide a concise understanding of the data. Moreover, survey participants were given the ID letter P e.g., P1, P2, and so on.

4 SURVEY FINDINGS

In the following subsections, we focus on the survey responses from students who indicated that they had at least one course that taught accessibility or disability studies and wrote the name of their courses in the survey. To begin the data analysis, we split the survey data into two groups: students who had technical courses (e.g., HCI, computing, design courses) and students who had non-technical courses (e.g., Disability Studies, Pathology courses). Among the 72 survey participants, 38 of them had at least one technical course and 71 students had at least one non-technical course that covered accessibility or topics on disability studies. We note that some students had both technical and non-technical courses so we put them in both groups.

4.1 Technical and Non-technical Courses Covering Accessibility or Topics on Disability Studies

To get a good understanding of the courses and their structure, first, we asked our participants how the lecturers taught the topics on accessibility and disability in the courses. Then, we narrowed our focus down to discussions on culture. Our survey aimed to collect data on what culture-related topics were taught and how they were taught (i.e., teaching approaches such as lectures, projects, etc.). Finally, we wanted to know whether those topics on culture were discussed in the context of design and digital accessibility or not.

How much of the courses covered topics on accessibility or disability studies? We asked a multiple-choice question about how much accessibility was covered in students’ courses (e.g., as the main topic of the whole course, in one or two lectures) [ 3 ]. More than half of the students (18 out of 38 students) in technical courses mentioned that accessibility was covered in their course “as a theme” (i.e., the primary learning objectives of the course was not accessibility, but it included some activities or topics focused on users with disabilities) and only six students mentioned that their courses covered accessibility as the main topic of the course. On the contrary, for the non-technical courses, the majority of students mentioned that accessibility and disability topics were covered as the main topic of their courses (42 out of 71 students).

How were the topics on accessibility or disability studies taught? In a multiple-choice question, we asked about how accessibility and disability studies were taught to learn about the adopted approaches or activities. We also provided the students with an open box to write any other activities that were used in their course. The most mentioned approach in the technical courses was “Lectures” as indicated previously [ 3 , 63 ]. Similarly, lectures was the most mentioned technique for the non-technical courses too, and in the provided open box, few students indicated they had class discussions and guest speakers.

4.1.1 Topics on Culture in Accessibility or Disability Studies Teaching. In this part of our survey, we asked questions about how topics on culture were taught, what culture-related topics were taught, and whether those topics were discussed in the context of digital design and accessibility.

How were topics on culture discussed in the course? Among 10 students who had technical courses that covered topics on culture, the most repeated response to the question about how topics were covered was “Lectures” (eight students). Similarly, in non-technical courses, the majority of students mentioned they had class lectures that discussed topics on culture (51 students). The second most repeated response for both technical and non-technical courses was “Reading academic papers” (34 students in non-technical courses and six students in technical courses)

What topics on culture were discussed? In an open-ended question, we asked students to write the culture-related topics that were taught in their courses. The total responses to this question were in three themes: topics related to the 1) “Intersection of disability/accessibility and culture” such as multicultural issues within disability studies, different views of disability outside of the West, disability rights in the U.S., and across different world cultures, and history of disabilities around the world. 2) “Cultural/societal topics” such as topics related to specific cultural or societal practices such as collectivism vs. individualism culture, intersectionality, and impact of multi-generational living on disability in different cultures. 3) “Miscellaneous” which includes responses that did not directly or clearly indicate culture. Almost all responses were related to the intersection of disability and culture except for one that mentioned a topic related to accessibility and culture (P37: “ Various cultural considerations regarding accessibility, and how other countries handle accessibility ”). In addition, a total of eight students in non-technical courses provided unclear responses (e.g., some students only wrote “race” or “gender” with no more clarification) or they were not directly related to “disability and culture, accessibility and culture” (e.g., LGBTQ disability). The survey data were mostly general responses with little details provided, for more information, we conducted follow-up interviews.

4.1.2 Were the topics on culture discussed in the context of digital design and accessibility?. Out of 38 students from technical courses, only one of them indicated that the topics on culture were taught in the context of accessible design, and among the 71 students from non-technical courses, 17 students (23.94%) mentioned that culture was taught in the context of design and accessibility. These responses suggest that in both types of courses, culture is not taken into account in terms of accessible design to a good extent. However, to get a better understanding of why this is happening and what participants exactly meant when they responded yes to the question, we conducted follow-up interviews with the participants that volunteered to take part in the interviews. We also recruited participants for our interviews from outside the survey responses.

4.2 Summary on Survey Findings

Prior studies emphasized the importance of including non-technical topics in technical courses (e.g., cultural and social topics) [ 6 , 56 ]. However, we needed to have a more granular understanding of the inclusion of topics on culture into technical courses that teach accessibility while also learning from non-technical courses. In summary, as we anticipated according to the prior findings, our survey results had shown that technical courses that teach accessibility or disability topics discuss topics on culture less often than non-technical courses. Moreover, the primary finding of our online survey is that topics on culture that were discussed in both types of courses were theoretical topics usually in the context of socio-cultural practices, the representation of disability in different societies, and rarely in the context of digital accessibility and design. In addition, our survey findings showed that the most common teaching approach that is used to cover topics on culture in both types of courses was class lectures.

5 INTERVIEW METHOD

Our survey responses provided us with a broad understanding of the courses, but we also planned follow-up interviews to further explore the initial insights we had gained. We drew on prior research and our survey findings to facilitate a detailed interview discussion to investigate our research questions in more depth.

5.1 Interview Materials and Procedure

5.1.1 Recruitment. Our interview participants were both some of our survey volunteers and new participants who did not fill out our questionnaire (28.5% of the interview participants were additional participants). We screened to ensure the new participants had taken accessibility or disability studies courses that covered discussions on cultural differences. Our screener questionnaire collected data on student demographics such as age, gender, and other questions about their major, the courses they have had, and the topics of culture that were discussed within their courses if any. To advertise our interview, we spread our short screener questionnaire using our academic network as well as sharing it on different online platforms such as LinkedIn and Twitter. We also searched for Disability Studies, Computing, or Design programs in the U.S. and reached out to the primary contact person (e.g., dept chair or instructors) to share our study.

5.1.2 Interview Guide. We conducted online interviews via Zoom. Our interviews were semi-structured and consisted of three parts: Part 1 : The interview had a brief introduction for the participants about the study and the interview procedure. Part 2 : We asked detailed questions about their responses (e.g., asking them to elaborate on a response or provide examples for clarification). Part 3 : We asked participants to provide their insights about the inclusion of topics on culture in accessibility teaching and their suggestions for improving accessibility education. Part 3 helped us identify how students think and feel about the inclusion of culture in accessibility regardless of their own cultural background, as well as their perspectives on the improvement of accessibility education in terms of cultural inclusion.

5.2 Interview Participants

We recruited a total of 15 students across both technical (six) and non-technical (nine) areas. However, we had to eliminate one of the students from a non-technical course due to the lack of sufficient data that was caused by poor internet connection during the interview resulting in 14 final participants (Female=8, Male=4, Non-binary=2). We used data from the remaining 14 students (technical courses=6 and non-technical courses=8) with the age range of 18-64 (8 participants were 18-24, 5 were 25-34, and one participant was 55-64) who were from different cultural backgrounds such as North American, African, and South Asian. Four of the students were additional (new) participants that we identified via our screener questionnaire, and 10 students were from the survey volunteers. Our interview participants were located in different regions of the U.S.: four students studied in the Midwest, one student in the Southwest, two students in the Southeast, two students in the West, two students in the South region, and three students in the Northeast. Our participants studied at various types of institutions, which covered both public and private, including R1, and R2. (undergraduate=8, graduate=6). 5.3 Interview Analysis

For the interview data analysis, we used Braun and Clarke's reflexive thematic analysis approach [ 7 , 8 ]. We analyzed our data according to each research question. Using reflexive thematic analysis, we identified patterns for the topics that were taught in courses (RQ1). Along the way, we also considered interesting responses from the participants that did not quite fit within the research question but counted as useful, such as students’ personal opinions on the topics that were taught. Then, we identified teaching approaches that were used in those courses (RQ2) to see what were the most common or interesting approaches that were adopted in courses for discussing topics on culture. Finally, we analyzed our participants’ responses and suggestions about the inclusion of culture into accessibility teaching and aimed to find patterns for them. We also extracted insightful responses. Moreover, interview participants were given the ID letter I e.g., I1, I2, and so on.

6 INTERVIEW FINDINGS

We identified four final themes that encompass the topics and concepts that were covered in all the courses (RQ1). These themes (i.e., topics) were mostly delivered in the form of lectures and class activities (e.g., class discussions, projects, and reading sessions) in both technical and non-technical courses (RQ2). In both types of courses, some students mentioned that they had invited speakers and some design projects. However, the main common pedagogy to discuss culture within accessibility or disability topics was through class activities or lectures by the instructors.

We reflected on both types of courses that we considered in our study: technical courses and non-technical courses. Our data analysis for the last research question (RQ3) revealed five themes that encompass students’ recommendations for improving accessibility education by embedding culture into it. The student's suggestions were: 1) Culture and disability inclusion in course syllabus design, 2) Culture and disability inclusion when teaching courses (e.g., having a class full of culturally diverse students), 3) Being specific on the target culture and covering anthropological views too, 4) Covering accessibility fundamentals in all design courses, and 5) Including interactive class activities on culture.

Next, we discuss our participants’ suggestions and describe the final themes that we generated based on RQ1.

6.1 Learning about disability representation and consideration in different countries

Our participants mentioned that there were some discussions in the courses around topics of disability in different countries, races, or cultures. Disability and culture are two inseparable components because disability is best understood in cultural and social context [ 2 ].

The class discussions were usually centered around the social model of disability (disability from a social lens which focuses on the experiences of disabled people in societies) and how disability is being represented in some other countries (e.g., Nigeria, Eastern Asia), different cultures (e.g., indigenous culture, Hispanic culture), and different races (e.g., Black people). Discussing the social model of disability is usually in conjunction with the socio-cultural aspects of disability [ 46 ] and our findings, so far, are in-line with that. However, even though courses talked about countries other than the U.S., most discussions were U.S.-centered or Western-centered with a focus on the sub-cultures of the U.S.:

  • “ So it was [like] Eurocentric in the sense of [like] it was very white focused. It wasn't very international or it wasn't even very [like like] on the global experience and none of these classes tend to go global ever because the US is always American-centric or Eurocentric. ” (I5, non-technical course)

However, besides the social model of disability and different views towards disability in other locations or races, some students mentioned there were few discussions about how disability is being addressed and how disability rights work in other countries. Having those discussions appeared to be useful. For example, I9 believed that having discussions about disability rights in other countries was really eye-opening:

  • “ I remember, they had us like watch a few videos just about how disability rights movements that were happening in other places [...] I remember, there was one [like] documentary in particular that we've watched where they have [like] really bad disability rights, and it was [like...] Oh definitely eye-opening. ” (I9, non-technical course)

Some of the other students had discussions about how resources or research are being dedicated to disability support in some countries and what the factors are that affect disability support. Students’ responses revealed that the culture, language, and beliefs of people in a society can influence disability support in that country. For example, I14 talked about the invited speaker they had in class, who was from Argentina, and who discussed the lack of resources for disability research in Argentina because of the negative views towards motor disability:

  • “ A) [...] from her experience, people don't want to study motor issues as heavily where she lives and then B) the idea that someone with motor issues is going to be out there, like you know, giving like, giving talks and doing you know kind of these more social oriented things, that was kind of frowned upon. ” (I14, technical course)

Likewise, I7 pointed out class discussions on disability in African countries:

  • “ In Africa, most of the people with disability are always, You know, are looked down upon and they may end up receiving poor services in terms of health and education and also normal relationship with people. ” (I7, non-technical course)

In addition to talking about the class discussion, I7, who was originally from an African country, used the opportunity in class to highlight their own clarification, knowledge, and perspective since they had lived experiences to share. Moreover, I5 mentioned culture and language in disability support:

  • “ I did another project for my language and identity class where I talked about how the language and culture that we associate with disability affects the way that we are able to give resources, help, and also be inclusive as a society. ” (I5, non-technical course)

After comparing technical and non-technical courses, we found that the discussions around disability representation and addressing different cultures, races, or countries are mostly happening in non-technical courses, while only two students from technical courses talked about disability representation and resources in other countries. Our participants’ responses and feedback showed that learning about disabilities beyond cultures or races can be eye-opening and useful for their future careers. For example, I2, a student who studies speech pathology (non-technical course), mentioned the lack of cultural considerations in their courses:

  • “ I really wish we were given a few more [like] concrete resources and tools to use [...] We should have practiced it hands-on [...] Like, see, I'm a speech pathologist and I get a family in from some country that I've never even heard of before. And I'm like you know! I need to learn about what to expect, how I should talk to them. ” (I2, non-technical course)

I2 expressed concerns about the challenges they might face in the future when working as a pathologist because they are not receiving proper education in regard to culture and its importance. Thus, even though discussions around cultural topics are happening more often in non-technical courses than in technical courses, there is still a need to embed more of these discussions when teaching about disability topics or accessibility. Drawing on our findings and prior work, we believe that having the knowledge about disability representation in different geographic locations and cultures can be helpful when learning about disability or accessibility (i.e., because disability is best understood in socio-cultural context [ 2 ] and it is closely linked to accessibility [ 39 ]).

6.2 Discussions around interconnections between tech and society in different communities

When discussing the topics on cultural differences some students (mostly from technical courses) mentioned that their courses covered topics around the interconnections between culture and technology design or technology usage in different cultures. This theme encompasses discussed topics on social aspects of technology such as how culture influences technology usage in some other countries (e.g., East Asian countries), how technology impacts traditional and cultural beliefs, the history of technology usage, the history of how technology impacts disability, how religion can impact technology design/usage, or how some technologies (e.g., AI) discriminate some communities (e.g., lower strata).

Students mentioned that some topics were brought up in class that was not directly about accessibility but made a good point about how technology and society can be linked in different ways. For example, I3 talked about some class discussions about topics on different technology usage in different countries, and the discussions were led by the students:

  • “ So we have people from different countries in the class and then we discussed certain technologies, we would say how things are different in our country and how people might use technology, like a certain device can do one thing in the US, we might use it for something else in the different in another country [...] We came to the conclusion that not all technology can be transplanted as it is in different places, they have to be adjusted and tweaked to suit the requirements of the people of that particular region. ” (I3, technical course)

Even though these topics are not directly about digital accessibility, they might be good background knowledge to impart in technical and design courses [ 34 ]; for students preparing for careers in design, learning the non-technical side of technology, the human side, can be useful to cover important aspects of design that do not come across in courses that emphasize technology implementation only [ 6 ]. Including the non-technical topics (e.g., cultural factors in design) in technical courses help students understand the link between technology and society, thus, increasing their responsiveness and empathy [ 6 , 56 ].

Another student mentioned discussing the diversity of technology usage in other countries through a disability lens:

  • “ There were a lot of different products that we just did discuss. Diversity was mentioned in terms of folks with disabilities but in terms of culture, There was some stark differences that we mentioned comes with geographies [...] We definitely compared some Southeast Asian countries vs the Western countries in terms of how products are used and utilized. ” (I4, technical course)

These topics suggest that there are discussions around the impact of socio-cultural components on technology usage and design which are happening more often in the technical courses. Besides delivering these topics through class lectures and class activities, some of the topics were emphasized through class discussions in which all students provided introductions about themselves and talked about their cultural background, how technology is used in their country, or maybe how disability is viewed in their cultures, etc. We found this approach interesting because it gave the instructor and students an initial idea of other countries and the cultural diversity of the class. Some students pointed out having more culturally diverse classes as a beneficial way to embed cultural discussions when accessibility is being taught. This suggestion, besides being interesting, however, comes with its challenges; having a class full of international students from diverse cultures can be a source of challenges to academic staff, lecturers, and instructors since they are expected to meet the learning needs of these students [ 59 ].

6.3 Class discussions around socio-cultural differences and their influence on design

Based on our participants’ responses, both types of courses (technical and non-technical courses) had discussions on cultural differences in different regions and how they can influence technology and product design. Discussions around culture in design were mainly in the context of language in design (I14), one student mentioned color and different cultural perceptions in design (I11), choice of food in food app design (I8), religion and design (I7, I15), and race in design (I2, I13). For example, I2 mentioned what they discussed when they were considering challenges related to race and skin color in augmentative and alternative communication devices:

  • “ We've talked about, broadly speaking, skin color and how there's their skin color options, but sometimes even that is a little bit simplistic like what if you're a mixed-race family. And what color do you pick? What if your mom is white and your dad is black, do you pick the black icons or do you pick the white icon? Can you make a mom icon white and the dad icon black or do you have to pick one of them. ” (I2, non-technical course)

Another interesting thing was the importance of language alignment in design which was mentioned by I14:

  • “ We want everything to be standardly aligned, so for English, you know that means left aligned, but if you are using alignment as kind of a design pillar, it means that if you're writing something in Hebrew you actually have to flip a lot of your [like] design thinking because you want to control the reading speed of people and direct their kind of like hierarchical view. ” (I14, technical course.)

I14 discusses how some other languages that have different reading/writing alignment than English need different design considerations. This matter was also argued and explored by the recent work of Goldenberg and Tractinsky [ 18 , 19 ] which suggests that languages with more than one alignment (bidirectional languages) need specific design elements and variances.

However, the responses from our participants indicated that they did not have deeper discussions around the impact of culture on technology design except for some passing class discussions. Some students suggested that topics on culture need to be embedded into education and they argued different ways that this approach can be applied. For example, I14 emphasized the benefits of including anthropological perspectives in accessibility teaching. I14 also added that to talk about cultural topics, the courses need to have a narrow focus on particular cultures and go deep into details such as people's opinions towards a particular topic or the history of that culture. For example, I14 shared how they discussed Brazilian culture in particular:

  • “ So basically to learn about different cultures, we pick kind of a particular narrow topic so, for example, in Brazil, we looked at transgender communities, and this, it was interesting because the way that we looked at it is was very anecdotal so we read a bunch of stories from a bunch of people who are transgender in Brazil and then people who have opinions about people who are transgender in Brazil. ” (I14, technical course)

Moreover, other students suggested that their courses should dedicate more time and resources to the topics on cultural differences and have more interactive activities and class projects that embed culture as a component into it. For example, I11 argued that projects and hands-on activities can help students to learn class topics better and avoid forgetting:

  • “ Just more time on it like I feel like if we had like two solid lectures, or I don't know, maybe a project on it. I think a project would be good because for me that's how I like remember stuff more like I don't remember that much [...] I remember something like sure! if I take notes on it, but I remember more on projects. So if we have anything simulated... like oh you're designing for this culture like you design for these people now I think that would be good. ” (I11, technical course)

We believe that these suggestions could lead to some improvements in accessible design education because design and culture are linked in many ways [ 18 , 29 , 52 ].

6.4 Discussions on accessibility in different geographic locations

This theme is made of the topics on culture that were mentioned by students from both technical and non-technical courses. Students in both types of courses highlighted that they had lectures, discussions, or activities around accessibility either in physical spaces or digital spaces. We wanted to inquire about any discussions on culture, other countries, other races, etc. when accessibility was taught. In addition to the topics around disability in other countries, or communities (subsection 6.1 ), there were some similar topics for accessibility such as a political aspect of accessibility (e.g., governments’ support for accessibility) or different accessibility considerations in different countries due to language barriers. Two students mentioned that their technical courses had a few discussions on how accessibility is different in other countries:

  • “ I think, the accessibility functionality is more prominent in the West than in South Asia, like in Asia in general [...] so that is what some of the studies signified, [showed] that there was a lot of you know [like] infrastructure built for them, for example, simple things like ramps were very prominent. ” (I4, technical course)

I4 also mentioned the language barrier and how it can impact accessibility:

  • “ I think it wasn't way deep into it (culture and accessibility), but as far as I think some of these elements that [are] definitely introspective more in terms of how do you build products where the language is not a barrier [...] Then, there were discussions around how we can break that barrier by using other forms of mediums like Google translate [...] If you have captioning in one service, how can they be translated on other languages. ” (I4, technical course)

In addition, I11 mentioned that accessibility was discussed in a global aspect very briefly (i.e., availability of accessible software in different countries), but the class did not go any deeper into the topic of how each country deals with accessibility, or how designs can be exactly different for each culture.

Among the students from non-technical courses, there were few mentions of discussions on accessibility in other countries as well. Those discussions were in the context of accessibility support from the government and the impact that culture can have on accessibility. I7 mentioned that they discussed how disability is treated differently in different cultures, which might lead to different accessibility needs for people in that culture:

  • “ I also did some of social studies and under [under] social studies we covered much on to do with culture. How different cultures, you know, [it] may affect people with disability. And this may hence, affect you know, various needs, for you know, accessibility. ” (I7, non-technical course)

Knowing the importance of culture in accessibility education is only one part of the problem. There is a need for a better-designed accessibility course curriculum that has fundamental disability studies embedded into it before jumping into accessibility topics [ 82 ]. We need to know how culture can be embedded into accessibility education, what approaches need to be followed, and what things need to be put first as the highest priorities.

7 DISCUSSION

Our main contribution is insights into the inclusion of culture within accessibility education. We ran an online survey and follow-up interviews with students in U.S. institutions who were from both technical and non-technical backgrounds to see how those areas were teaching accessibility or disability studies in their courses. Moreover, the other contributions of our work are recommendations on how to build accessibility courses that can embed topics on cultural differences into their curriculum.

The findings from our survey data indicated that in contrast to non-technical courses discussions around topics on culture are not happening in technical courses very often when accessibility is taught. This finding was aligned with prior works [ 6 , 56 ], which showed that technical courses rarely discuss non-technical topics. However, our survey findings also extend prior work to reveal that cultural discussions in each of the technical and non-technical courses mostly happened in the context of socio-cultural practices and representation of disability in different societies, and rarely in the context of digital accessibility and design. In addition, class lectures were the most common activities that were used to cover topics on culture in both types of courses.

The findings from our interviews extended our initial understanding developed from our survey responses. The interviews with our participants revealed a slight difference from the survey data, highlighting that topics on culture were discussed in both course types, in contrast to our survey findings, but still those discussions happen more often in the non-technical courses at a deeper level. Thus, the interviews were great opportunities to have deeper discussions with participants who could elaborate more on their responses. Our interview findings suggest that technical courses could benefit from more cross-cultural discussions on disability when teaching accessibility, thus, helping educate more culturally informed designers and developers.

7.1 Reflecting on how to build accessibility courses that embed culture into the curriculum

(RQ1) What cultural topics are being discussed within technical and non-technical courses if any? (RQ2) How are those topics being taught?

Our findings from the interviews showed that technical and non-technical courses are similar in some cases (e.g., both discuss accessibility and disability in other countries, both include topics on culture in the course) but there were some stark differences too. Non-technical courses that teach topics on disability, use some approaches that we believe could be useful in the technical courses. According to our interview findings, most non-technical courses provided a platform (opportunity) for students to understand disability correctly at the beginning of education and build up on that. Comparing the courses, non-technical courses (e.g., feminist disability studies, disability, and world cultures, disability in U.S. history) dedicate more of the course materials and resources to the discussions around disability understanding from a global and personal perspective, by defining and clarifying the cultural perceptions towards disability. Some technical courses have also focused on the history of disability with a view of its interconnections with technology (e.g., how technology has impacted disability over the years). Some of these discussions happened through informal class orientations in which students talked about their background such as how disability is represented in their culture, how technology is being used and viewed in their country, etc. Forming these discussions as a planned activity in the course curriculum could be beneficial for increasing students’ fundamental disability knowledge before exploring technical and complex topics. We found these discussions interesting since they can reduce inaccurate assumptions and familiarize students with the important role of culture in different aspects [ 21 , 76 ] including disability and, consequently, accessibility. This process could provide an improved reframing of disability perceptions as also one of the students mentioned. Applying the same approach in technical courses teaching accessibility would enhance disability understanding and awareness towards cultural influences in accessible design.

So far, different approaches have been devised and recommended for teaching technical courses [ 33 , 34 ]. Walker argued the importance of theoretical foundations in web design courses [ 81 ]. Two theories were argued in Walker's work: genre theory and activity theory. Genre theory focuses on demystification to provide better communications by understanding audience expectations, social context, etc [ 81 ]. Activity theory argues that activities are informed by the specific systems and motives of people involved in them who share a common object, as well as the wide range of tools they use [ 25 ]. Moreover, based on the results of a workshop led by Krupczak et al. [ 34 ], every technical course that centers around technological literacy (i.e., the broad understanding of technology and all types of technological devices and process [ 34 ]), should be delivered in some determined standard modes [ 34 ]. One of these course modes, “ Technological Impacts, Assessment, and History Courses ”, aims to focus on the relations between technology and culture, society, and history [ 34 ].

Thus, having discussions around culture in technical courses is beneficial way in computing and technical areas and more specifically, in the case of our study, accessible design as well. Even though there have been diverse theoretical approaches and discussions around cultural differences in design, disability understanding, or social interactions, none specifically focused on the influence of cultural differences on accessible design. It is also important to note that one of the key challenges in incorporating inclusive design practices and cultural topics in a course, is the time constraint throughout a semester [ 65 ]. In their study, Sin et al. [ 65 ] argue that course duration and limited time are systemic institutional issues that can lead to non-inclusive teaching. Therefore, to achieve practical ways for building better inclusive materials in accessibility courses, time constraints, and limited time resources must be taken into account.

7.2 Recommendations on Embedding Culture into Accessibility Teaching

(RQ3) What are students’ views and feedback on including cultural topics in accessibility teaching?

Our participants’ insights and positive feedback towards the inclusion of cultural topics in accessibility education, showed the importance of having more culture-related discussions when accessibility is taught in the courses, mostly by incorporating cultural topics in the course syllabus, having a narrow focus on a specific culture, and teaching accessibility fundamentals in the design courses. Drawing on the suggestions from our student participants, along with prior work and our findings, helped us come up with recommendations on how to incorporate culture into accessibility education in a way that will not lead to more complexity and eventually, ignorance. Moreover, since this work is a new area that has never been explored before, we need to come up with modifications and recommendations in a way that can be helpful instead of confusing. Thus, it is best to prioritize the solutions and start from a place that is the most crucial or the most fundamental, similar to the approach Goldenberg and Tractinsky [ 19 ] applied to their new research study when they explored bidirectional design for the first time. We suggest the following hierarchical approaches for embedding topics on culture in accessibility teaching:

7.2.1 Institutions can seek help from or hire cultural studies, disability studies, and accessibility researchers. To embed culture into accessibility teaching the first thing to do is to have sufficient resources and knowledgeable instructors. The same approach has been recommended by Shinohara et al. [ 63 ] but for incorporating accessibility into computing courses. Just as culture can influence design, we believe that accessibility, as an important component of design, can also be impacted by different cultural preferences. Since we could not find any research or adequate resources on cultural differences and accessible design, there are opportunities to investigate different teaching approaches. We suggest that as a beginning, HCI and accessible researchers dedicate more studies to this area with the help of researchers from cultural and disability studies areas, thus, benefiting from different perspectives to increase awareness. Such research studies can focus on identifying what cultural topics need to be taught and start with topics that have the highest priorities in accessible design. Doing so can help to avoid confusion for both instructors and students.

7.2.2 Dedicate part of design and development courses to the topics on culture and foundations of accessibility. Discussions or activities on cultural differences are not inclusively considered in courses that have design focuses [ 48 , 70 ] which was also shown by our findings. In general, there is still a lack of cross-cultural design projects in education [ 70 ] and we believe that it can be helpful for design and development education to embed culture in courses that are primarily focusing on digital technology creation (e.g., interface design) in order to familiarize the students with the area of cross-cultural design and postcolonial computing. Course instructors can introduce prior work as a starting point for discussing the importance of considering the relationship between culture and design (e.g., color, layout, and text preferences [ 9 , 12 , 16 , 28 ]).

Moreover, design courses should include foundations of accessible design and the key concepts in the curriculum so that more students become familiar with accessibility [ 63 ]. It has been pointed out by some students from technical courses that accessibility was mentioned as passing and not sufficient resources or time was dedicated to it. This issue was argued in many prior works [ 27 , 63 ] and also some of our interview participants expressed their concern towards a lack of proper courses on digital accessibility.

7.2.3 Ask disabled people from different cultures to take part in devising courses that mainly focus on advanced accessible design with disability and cultural topics integrated into it. Besides including foundations of accessible design in the design courses, in separate courses, accessibility can be taught as the main focus of the course in which disability studies are discussed as background knowledge and accessibility topics are taught and implemented in more detail along with the integration of topics on culture.

As mentioned in the previous sections, incorporating disability studies into accessibility teaching is essential [ 39 ] and as we found in our analysis, one of the benefits could be to re-frame students’ perceptions towards disability and lead the focus slightly to the factors that can potentially impact disability. These factors can be extracted and clarified by going through the history of disability [ 43 ], maybe from different social or cultural perspectives in different countries, similar to the approach from non-technical courses. We suggest that while building up on these discussions, culture as a component of the target society can be incorporated into the course.

Therefore, we suggest that when focusing particularly on accessible design, culture should be embedded as an important aspect of design and accessibility. To develop a good curriculum for such courses we recommend involving disabled people from other cultures to devise the milestones of the course curriculum. User/research subject involvement has been shown to be beneficial [ 10 ] because to explore a target group and find a solution for it, researchers need to be familiar with that topic and one proper way is to involve people who are connected to that particular topic into the study [ 22 ]. The importance of having disabled people in the process of curriculum devising has been pointed out but previous studies too [ 43 ]. Following such a procedure can help create a course curriculum that can utilize the insights and feedback of disabled people who are from different cultural backgrounds.

7.2.4 Have people with different disabilities and cultural backgrounds when teaching accessibility in courses. To embed culture into accessibility teaching, certain considerations and procedures need to be taken into account. According to the discussions we had with students and the recommendations they provided, we argue that it will be helpful to ask disabled people from another culture to take part in the course, share their insights, and have interactive discussions with students about different cultures and disability representations in other geographic locations. It is generally expected in accessibility research that participants are themselves representative users (i.e., people who have a disability or impairment) [ 60 ], and disabled people find it important to take part in research or development processes since it is an opportunity to help others [ 41 ]. Including representative people within classes will provide a more valid experience. However, a concern to address would be to ensure people invited to contribute to those courses are properly reimbursed for their time, effort, and expertise.

Some prior studies have also emphasized the importance of students’ connection to disability communities during the educational experience [ 66 ]. It has been pointed out by some students that having guest speakers from a different culture or a type of disability was definitely eye-opening and insightful. Some students also discussed the benefit of having a diverse class with guest speakers or instructors from different geographic locations and disabilities. Thus, bringing different people with various perspectives into the class can help the students benefit from new cultural perspectives. However, it might be best to focus on one or a couple of cultures to avoid complexity.

7.3 Limitation and Future Work

Due to the COVID-19 global pandemic, we conducted our interviews online which was beneficial in some ways such as being able to talk to our participants in different parts of the country without requiring them to show up in person. However, we faced some issues due to poor internet connections where some of the participant's voices or videos cut out. We had to remove one participant because of an extremely unreliable internet connection.

Our work only focuses on courses within U.S. institutions. We benefited from the perspectives of international students in U.S. education settings since U.S. institutions are culturally diverse 4 . For example, six of our interview participants were from other countries (e.g., South Asian countries, and African countries), and courses are training students to be future designers or researchers in a global market. However, there is still an opportunity in future work to investigate course structures in other countries, mainly in the Global South (i.e., the lead author is from an under-represented country in the Global South), and expand on prior works (e.g., [ 1 , 4 , 5 , 45 ])

In addition, while valuable, our work only benefits from the perspectives of the students while there is a chance to get more detailed information by conducting research with course instructors and lecturers, who can be better sources. However, the data that we collected provided the perspectives of students which resulted in useful findings that can help improve teaching accessibility topics. We discuss how accessibility teaching can benefit from different approaches but we do not know what the best way of devising a course would be unless we have professional opinions of the course creators as well. Thus, for future work, to take any actions toward our recommendations, we need to talk to the course creators, instructors, or lecturers to learn from their side too.

8 CONCLUSION

We conducted a survey among 72 students in U.S. institutions with 14 follow-up interviews to understand the inclusion of topics on culture into technical and non-technical courses that teach accessibility or disability topics. We sought to find what cultural topics are being discussed within technical and non-technical courses if any (RQ1), how cultural topics are being taught in technical and non-technical courses (RQ2), and what are students’ suggestions for including cultural topics in accessibility teaching (RQ3). Our findings revealed that even though both technical and non-technical courses discuss culture, there is still a lack of nuanced cultural focus in accessibility teaching in technical areas. To increase the discussions around culture in accessibility education in the technical courses, we can benefit from some useful approaches from the non-technical courses. In contrast to the non-technical courses, technical courses do not usually discuss topics on culture, disability history, or disability perception from a global perspective, which can lead to a lack of sufficient disability understanding. Consequently, leading to a lack of accessibility knowledge and cultural influences on accessibility as well. We make several recommendations such as including disabled people from different cultures in accessibility course development and teaching to support more cultural sensitivity in accessibility course delivery.
