Games that feature multiple players, limited communication, and partial information are particularly challenging for AI agents. In the cooperative card game Hanabi, which possesses all of these attributes, AI agents fail to achieve scores comparable to even first-time human players. Through an observational study of three mixed-skill Hanabi play groups, we identify the techniques used by humans that help to explain their superior performance compared to AI. These concern physical artefact manipulation, coordination play, role establishment, and continual rule negotiation. Our findings extend previous accounts of human performance in Hanabi, which are purely in terms of theory-of-mind reasoning, by revealing more precisely how this form of collective decision-making is enacted in skilled human play. Our interpretation points to a gap in the current capabilities of AI agents to perform cooperative tasks.

1 INTRODUCTION

Artificially intelligent agents have become embedded in many systems we use today. Often these systems require the AI agent to work closely with a human cooperator, such as self-driving vehicles, co-creation tasks, and command and control scenarios  [ 10 , 40 , 73 ]. As capabilities of AI systems increase, humans’ role as operators of a system are shifting to a peer-like interaction between humans and AI agents, specifically in the form of human-AI teams  [ 60 ]. Yet, teams carry with them unique and complex social challenges, such as shared common goals, developing shared mental models, knowing and fulfilling one's assigned role, and a need for trust  [ 33 ]. Therefore there are more requirements that AIs need to meet before they can be said to be part of a human-AI team, as opposed to some other cooperative group structure.

Achieving effective human-AI cooperation and, in turn, human-AI teaming, is a priority in AI research literature. Dafoe et al. present a research agenda that includes understanding agents’ beliefs, incentives, and capabilities as well as communication between agents and building a shared language. Note that the ability to understand another's beliefs and desires is known in humans as theory of mind reasoning, which is a persons ability to reason about or with consideration of another person's mental state [ 28 , 77 ]. Theory of mind reasoning is key to human's ability to operate in social environments as it assists us in developing mental models of how others will behave  [ 25 ]. In the context of high performing teams the importance of developing a strong shared mental model is emphasized in the literature  [ 14 , 63 ]. Therefore, having AI agents perform some level of theory of mind reasoning is crucial for achieving affective Human-Ai team performance  [ 3 ]. Traditionally researchers have used games to both study AI behaviour and develop AI capability, as games have come to represent benchmarks for human intelligence. Chess and Go for their strategic complexity and large state spaces, Poker for elements of deception and bluffing are a few of the most famous example  [ 12 , 42 , 66 ]. A common theme among these benchmark games is that they have traditionally been purely competitive. As AI research aims towards developing cooperative AI agents that can operate within teams, cooperative games have begun to gain interest as new challenge spaces for AI development. Cooperative games are also an ideal setting for understanding human-AI cooperation as they provide both a systems and narrative context  [ 34 ]. One such example is the cooperative card game Hanabi, which features partial information and restricted communication. Theory of Mind reasoning is core to playing the game, and as such Hanabi has been proposed as a suitable challenge space for developing AI agents with this reasoning capability  [ 5 ]. Humans excel at the game compared to artificial agents, with first time players scoring higher than many of the current best Hanabi models when playing with unknown teammates  [ 57 , 64 ]. Researchers typically attribute this to humans’ ability to interpret implicit information, a general ability to perform theory of mind reasoning, and also our ability to use eye tracking and timing as side channels of communication  [ 5 , 32 , 47 , 48 , 65 ]. Yet, there are no studies to our knowledge that start by analyzing human gameplay to understand what reasoning and communication techniques humans use to achieve such high scores. When proposing Hanabi as a test bed for studying theory of mind reasoning in artificial agents, Bard states “while hard to quantify, it would seem theory of mind is a central feature in how the game is first learned” and that empirically evaluating human performance is difficult due to a variety of confounding factors  [ 5 ]. This suggests that a qualitative approach, as opposed to a quantitative one, is better suited for understanding how theory of mind is used in human Hanabi games.

In this study, we observed three live Hanabi sessions consisting of 14 participants. We sought to understand the behaviours and techniques used by groups of humans playing Hanabi that enable them to achieve such high scores without ever having played the game before. We find that essential to the cooperative process of play is:

  1. the physicality of the game pieces which allows players to distribute cognition;
  2. players’ ability to establish group norms by negotiating grey rules throughout play and;
  3. the social context and previous experience of players which gives players distinct roles within the team.

These behaviours reveal that humans do not merely act cooperatively in these scenarios but form teams; furthermore, a purely internal conception of theory of mind reasoning does not fully explain the cooperative process, nor humans’ ability to achieve high scores. The main contribution of this paper is through providing a rich understanding of human play in Hanabi as well as emphasizing the importance and explanatory power of distributed cognition to this setting.

The implications of our findings are that AI agent developers should consider how physical artefacts contribute to individual and team reasoning and attempt to design agents that engage with these behaviours by either physically or virtually embodying said agent. Agent designers should also aim to incorporate a level of rule flexibility to acknowledge and adapt to the rule negotiation behaviours that occur within human Hanabi teams. Finally, the importance of social roles in coordinating human teams indicates the need for AI agents to detect and adhere to the social structure and roles established during the game. While the importance of flexibility in the context of ad-hoc Hanabi teams is well known, our findings emphasize the importance of embodiment and social roles.

2 RELATED RESEARCH

Our work aimed to understand the cognitive processes and cooperative behaviours that occur throughout human Hanabi games. As such we reviewed the current human-AI cooperation literature, as well as the state of artificial Hanabi agents, and important cognitive theories around human cooperation and games.

2.1 Human-AI cooperation and teaming

Human–AI cooperation is a field of research which aims to help humans and machines find ways to improve joint welfare  [ 22 ]. Human-AI cooperation is often used interchangeably in the literature with both human-AI collaboration and human-AI teaming, but there are important distinctions between these concepts. Human-AI cooperation is an umbrella term that encompasses collaboration and teaming. Human-AI collaboration involves AI taking on the roles of assistants, advisors, or facilitators. The human ultimately has agency over the final decision-making and the AI is there only to assist the human in achieving their goal. This has been studied heavily in the HCI and AI literature, in the form of decision assistants in medical diagnosis, university planning, scheduling and diabetes self-management  [ 4 , 6 , 46 ]. The requirements of designing AI collaborators such as artificial co-creators and conversational facilitators has also been explored recently  [ 35 , 40 , 58 ]. Yet the distinction between a collaborator and teammate makes it difficult to generalise these requirements to AI teammates.

In human-AI teaming scenarios the AI agent takes on the role of a teammate, which emphasises very close coordination where both the AI and the human are individually trying to achieve a shared goal  [ 33 ]. For AI agents to be classified as teammates they need to fill distinct roles and uniquely contribute to the team's performance  [ 43 ]. Teams also come with more complex social and structural requirements compared to merely cooperative groups. Successful teams are interdependent, with each member assigned specific roles and responsibilities. They share common goals, mental models, and each team member knows and fulfills their role  [ 9 , 14 , 19 , 20 , 36 ]. Studies on human-AI teaming have shown that perceiving AI agents as team members can have positive effects on team dynamics and productivity  [ 52 , 76 ]. Yet, it is still questioned whether AI agents are capable of being classed as teammates  [ 33 ]. Importantly the requirement for teammates to be “responsible” for the tasks they are given seems particularly hard to attribute to artificial agents  [ 20 ]. While some argue that because of increased levels of automation, full responsibility for tasks can be placed on AI agents this carries with it a whole slew of hard to address ethical and legal issues such as who is to blame if an AI agent were to fail at an assigned task that it was given responsibility for [ 18 , 31 , 41 ].

Shared mental models are also a crucially important aspect of teams, and their importance is well documented  [ 53 , 63 , 68 ]. A shared mental model is a shared understanding of how a system works, which one can use to describe, predict and explain behaviour  [ 8 ]. Currently there is a lot of research into how humans form mental models of AI systems  [ 2 , 3 , 30 , 75 ]. Humans find this difficult as often there isn't even a known conceptual model of how these systems will behave, especially in the case of neural networks which are often a black box  [ 1 ]. Even if one does exist often it is not good enough to predict behaviour, or the cognitive load associated with understanding the behaviour of an AI is too high to be useful  [ 2 , 30 ]. Games have been suggested as the ideal environment for understanding how humans create mental models of AI  [ 75 ].

2.2 Artificial Hanabi agents

Games provide sophisticated yet well-defined challenges for progressing AI and have traditionally been used in AI research as benchmarks for aspects of human intelligence in order to develop agents that can match or exceed humans in those domains. Significant progress has been achieved in traditional competitive strategy games such as Chess, Go, Starcraft, and DOTA with artificial agents defeating professional players in each of these domains  [ 42 , 54 , 66 , 78 ]. Recently, the card game Hanabi was proposed as a new challenge domain for AI research due to it presenting novel problems that have traditionally been difficult for AI to overcome. Unlike the previously mentioned games, Hanabi is a fully cooperative, partial information game with restricted communication  [ 5 , 64 ]. In Hanabi, two to five players play suits of cards in the correct order, all while only being able to see the cards in their teammate's hands and aiming for a maximum score of 25. Players may communicate using clues, which restricts the amount of information given. This seemingly prevents “cheap talk” and the use of side channels  [ 27 ]. Hanabi is classed as a non-zero sum game, meaning one player's optimal strategy depends heavily on what other players do. From an AI perspective, this makes Hanabi a multi-agent problem which has proven to be especially challenging  [ 13 , 72 ].

The performance of Artificial Hanabi agents is measured across two paradigms, self-play and ad-hoc. Self-play involves groups of AI agents which are aware of each others general strategies beforehand. These models have achieved near perfect scores (24.6/25) using techniques from the hat-guessing game, but this strategy is impractical for human use  [ 21 , 45 ]. In ad-hoc scenarios, agents are unaware of the strategies used by other players. Using algorithms that exploit known symmetries of the environment Hu et al. produce agents that achieve 15/25 when playing with humans yet is still below the average score of humans at 18/25  [ 37 , 57 ]. Ad-hoc scenarios are of particular interest to the HCI community as they provide opportunities for developing AI agents that perform well at human-AI cooperation as it is often the case that the AI is unaware of the strategies and behaviours of its human cooperators. This has typically been done by developing models that incorporate aspects of human communication identified from the literature. Liang et al. [2019] developed a Hanabi agent that uses implicature to improve performance with human players. This did not affect overall game score but increased the likelihood of players believing the AI to be human  [ 47 ]. Egers and Gottwald, used agents that gave clues intentionally and interpreted timing, which lead to improved scores when paired with human players  [ 32 , 49 ]. Agents developed by Osawa and Sato, estimated other player's hands and exploited the time taken to give a clue to adjust confidence levels  [ 56 , 65 ]. Although positive effects have been shown from incorporating these aspects of human communication into Hanabi agents, human-like scores are still currently unattainable. The general approach has been to refer to literature on the behaviours humans use in scenarios similar to Hanabi and create AI agents that utilise some of these techniques. To our knowledge there is currently very little research into how groups of humans play the game, what communicative behaviours they use, and how these contribute to humans achieving such high scores.

2.3 Cognition in Hanabi

A major reason why Hanabi poses such an interesting yet difficult challenge to AI agents is that it requires some level of theory of mind reasoning. This is reasoning about mental states, which includes desires, intentions, knowledge and beliefs. This ability typically develops in humans around four to six years of age  [ 7 , 28 ]. Theory of mind reasoning contributes to humans ability to adapt socially, communicate with others and maintain social relationships  [ 25 ]. It also contributes to our ability to operate in multiagent settings, such as driving on the road  [ 16 ]. This reasoning can be seen during games of Hanabi when players “signal” playable cards. This is when a card is played that was only partially identified as playable because it was inferred that the intention of a clue related to that card was that it was fully playable  [ 5 ]. Yet signalling alone cannot account for the high scores achieved by novice players. Bard et al. describe further use of theory of mind in high level plays done by experienced players. One example, known as a finesse, involves players performing second-order theory of mind reasoning, which is reasoning about someone's model of your own mental state. Cognitive psychology indicates this type of reasoning is difficult for people to perform, especially untrained users  [ 51 , 74 ]. Therefore, it is unlikely that the high scores achieved by first time Hanabi player can be attributed to this higher order theory of mind reasoning. Other cognitive, reasoning, and communication techniques might be at play here that explain these high first time scores.

To our knowledge the only other research done on human Hanabi gameplay was by Rogerson et al. In their study, the authors looked at groups of game players, including ad-hoc Hanabi teams in order to understand the interplay between competition and cooperation during boardgame play. They show how boardgames are a form of cooperative work which is situated and distributed  [ 62 ]. This concept of distributed cognition originates from Hutchins  [ 38 ]. He describes distributed cognition as a cognitive system with structures and processes distributed between internal and external representations, across groups of individuals, and across space and time  [ 38 ]. This means that the cognitive work that is required for a certain process to occur is not located solely within each individual but is shared between individuals and cognitive artefacts that physically make up that environment. Previously, HCI has had a focus on solitary decontextualised activities but has seen an increased focus on these activities in context  [ 15 , 44 ]. By expanding our understanding of the cognition of a system beyond just the internal mind of actors within the system, we can explain how these larger cooperative processes occur. This framework has been used to understand how software teams, airline cockpits and navy vessels operate  [ 26 , 38 , 39 ]. This contrasts with the traditional approach to cognition which posits that cognition takes place entirely inside the mind, and is therefore inaccessible to anyone else. This inaccessible mind approach seems to be currently followed by Hanabi agent developers, where they model cognition occurring during the game as isolated minds in the form of hidden matrices of information  [ 49 , 56 ]. The framework of distributed cognition indicates that the advanced reasoning occurring during human Hanabi games might not just be located in the internal minds of players, but rather embodied in the environment and supported by their cultural and social structures during the game.

3 METHOD

In this study we wanted to understand what behaviours humans used that might explain their superior performance when compared with AI agents. We aimed to identify specific behaviour patterns and to develop a richer account of them, including their significance within the game, that went beyond general notions of theory of mind reasoning and implicit communication. As such, the methods we used were qualitative, with a focus on categories of actions performed across the observed games. We wanted an unconstrained environment so as not to restrict any potential cooperation or communication tactics that may naturally take place. Our participants consisted of players with a range of skills and experience in Hanabi. We believe that this better reflects the diversity of cooperators an agent may encounter in a team.

3.1 Hanabi game rules

Hanabi is a 2-5 player cooperative card game, in which players must share information to create stacks of ascending cards. They do this while only being able to see the cards in their teammate's hands; their own cards are concealed from them by facing outwards. On each player's turn, there are three possible actions to make. A player can play a card then draw a card, discard a card and draw a card, or give a clue. A clue is given to a single teammate about the cards in their hand. A clue provides information about either the colour or suit of a card or cards. When giving a clue the current player indicates which cards in the teammate's hand are of one colour or suit. When they do this, they must indicate all cards described by that clue, meaning if their teammate holds two blue cards, the current player must indicate that both of those cards are blue. The number of clues that can be given over the course of the game is limited; these are tracked by “clue tokens”. The team starts with eight clue tokens and each clue consumes one token. Discarding a card will gain back a token. If a player correctly plays a card, it goes on top of a pile or starts a new one. If the card is incorrect, it is discarded and players lose a "storm token". Players start with three storm tokens and the game ends when the last storm token is lost. The game also ends if players complete all piles, in which case they score the maximum 25 points. If there are no more cards left to draw, there is one more round and the players achieve a score equal to the number of cards correctly played.

3.2 Experimental set up

We analysed recordings of three Hanabi sessions with a total of 14 participants, aged between 18 and 61. We considered this a sufficient number of participants because our aim was to concentrate on identifying common recurring patterns of play behaviour, rather than aiming for a comprehensive account of all the possible plays in Hanabi. Within the extensive video sequences that we analysed, recurring patterns were clearly identifiable across the 14 players and these were sufficient to furnish an account of what is distinctive about human play. Since we were interested in the behaviour of ad-hoc groups, players were recruited with varying levels of experience in Hanabi with six participants having never played before. Players generally did not know each other before hand, except for some friendship pairs. Participants were asked about their level of familiarity with Hanabi. We categorised players as either experienced or new depending on if they had previously played Hanabi. Players were not remunerated for their participation but were provided with refreshments during the sessions. Two sessions had five players while the other had four. Session one (G1) had two new players and three experienced. Session two (G2) had one new player and four experienced. Session three (G3) had three new players and one experienced. Sessions were run at the ANONYMISED lab. Video cameras were used to record the session from different angles and a wearable eye-tracker was worn by one participant each session. Eye tracking was used to gain qualitative insight into where that participant's attention was drawn to at certain points throughout the game. We decided to allow players to choose who would wear the eye glasses. This was clearly limitation in that our eye movement data is likely to be biased towards certain types of player. However, we reasoned that mandating eye glassed for players would detract from the naturalistic game experience that was more important for this study; and also we note that eye movement data was not the main focus of our analysis, but rather was used for supplementary clues. Eye tracking recordings were used throughout analysis to better understand the decisions made by that player given the information that was available to them and also to further immerse the researchers in the video data to gain a stronger contextual understanding of the data. Two groups used the card version of Hanabi, and one group used a "deluxe" version of the game which replaces cards with tiles. Each game typically lasted between 35 and 40 minutes.

3.3 Analysis

We used observational data from video recordings to construct themes using a version of reflexive thematic analysis (RTA)  [ 11 , 70 ]. We take a relativist position to RTA as we acknowledge that in a game setting, knowledge and meaning are created as people engage with the world. As has been shown, game components are situated within a “historically and culturally constituted” context  [ 69 , 71 ]. Therefore, because we are interested in the behaviours that allow players to reason and communicate in order to achieve high scores, it is necessary to use a data collection method that acknowledges that the knowledge and communication is “historically and culturally bound”  [ 70 ]. As the raw data was in the form of live game recordings and their transcripts, the subject of analysis exist within and in relation to the specific context of the game, which can be considered the cultural context.

A latent coding approach was taken, in which codes were based on the meaning behind the specific utterance or action  [ 11 ]. This was done because, as previously pointed out by Bard  [ 5 ], much of the reasoning within Hanabi occurs through interpretation of actions to extract implicit information. Given that our aim is to identify and understand how humans communicate and transfer knowledge, a semantic approach would be too shallow to identify how information travels between players in the game as much of this information is implicit. A semantic approach would also miss any of the contextually dependent practices that contribute to superior performance.

Our approach to coding and theme generation followed Braun and Clark's six step approach to Thematic Analysis  [ 11 ]. In the first phase we familiarised ourselves with the data by watching and re-watching video recordings from the player perspective, using the global view to reveal any hidden information to understand the private knowledge of each player. In this phase the recordings were transcribed, which included player utterances as well as documenting physical actions that were in response to, or seemingly informed by, player actions within the game. This involved watching the recordings with a focus on each individual player as well as a holistic view of the team. In the second phase, initial codes were generated based on these transcripts by the lead researcher. These codes were then discussed and further refined in consultation with the other two researchers. In the third phase, codes were grouped into initial themes. In the fourth phase, initial thematic structure was reviewed and developed. The way we ensured our revised themes were a good fit to the observed gameplay was whether or not they could be used to explain how a complex move occurred, which we detail in the final section of the results (Phase 5). In the final phase, the literature was revisited to further refine and name the identified themes.

4 FINDINGS

Games lasted on average 37 minutes with an average score of 19/25. Quotes are labelled by game (G1 to G3) and player (P1 to P5) where labelling is clockwise starting from the player with the gaze tracking glasses (P1). We transcribed the utterances as well as physical actions performed by players. These actions helped players to reason about the cards in their own and other players’ hands, as well as helped in knowing what to do on their turn, where to focus their attention and what other players knew. Using thematic analysis, we identified four themes that describe behaviours exhibited by players during the game, which informed and influenced how they reasoned throughout the game. These are:

  1. manipulation of physical game artefacts;
  2. establishing roles;
  3. coordinating play;
  4. negotiating grey rules .

The following sections will outline each of these. We then included an example of a complex sequence of play, and used our themes to explain how this was able to occur.

4.1 Physical artefact manipulation

Hanabi contains many game pieces that are manipulated by players. These include the cards or tiles in a player's hands and the shared game pieces on the table such as the clue tokens, played cards, the deck and the discard piles. These elements are utilised to store or communicate information between players.

The most common artefact manipulation action is players moving their own cards or tiles. The game starts with players holding four or five cards or tiles with the face of each pointing away from the player that holds it. As players get more information about the cards or tiles in their hand, usually through clues given to them, these are moved around in their hand or on the table. This is done through rotating , ordering , or repositioning . As we will show, rotation and ordering are used similarly for both cards and tiles. Yet, tiles afforded more opportunities for repositioning due to them being placeable on the table, meaning that players are not limited by the dexterity of their hand.

Manipulation of a player's cards or tiles typically serves two purposes. Firstly, it serves as a memory aid to the player holding those cards or tiles, reminding them that something was known about the card that was manipulated, whether that be colour, rank, if it's playable or discardable. Secondly, since teammates are also able to see how a player has physically manipulated the cards in their hand, this also reminds teammates that the player knows something about certain cards. In this way the artefacts acted as a separate channel of communication between players.

4.1.1 Rotation. Rotation of cards and tiles was used in every game we observed. Both cards and tiles are rectangular in shape. When players first received their cards, all players would hold them vertically. The same is not true for tiles, where different players would initially orient their tiles vertically or horizontally. In all games, as players gained information about their cards/tiles, they would be rotated. As an example, we follow P1 in G2.

At the start of Game 2, P1 initially rotates all their cards to be the same orientation. P1 continues this trend of holding unknown cards vertically, placing new cards vertically on the right side of their hand.

When told that certain cards in their hand are fives, P1 rotates those cards horizontally. Throughout the game, P1 stores the information that certain cards in their hand are fives by physically rotating those cards. This serves as a memory aid to P1, reducing the cognitive load of having to remember previous clues. In this case, since the other players could see P1 do this, they can now be more confident that P1 will remember that clue given to them. This means that players 2-4 can make plays that rely on P1 knowing that two of their cards are 5s without fear of P1 forgetting the previous clue. In game 3, a new player (P1) asks an experienced player (P2) why they rotate their cards.

G3 – P1 – 10:32 “Can I ask you a question about the way you are holding your cards? Do you know what that is? [points to the vertical card in P2’s hand]”

G3 - P2 “I don't know what that is, I'm holding it that way because I don't know what that is.”

Here, we see P2 explicitly explaining their rotation conventions. This card rotation technique appears in every game observed, where vertical represents unknown and horizontal represents known.

In the tiled version of the game, rotation is also used to show which tiles have had clues given about them. Yet, unlike cards there is no typical convention for how tiles should be first held (vertically or horizontally). In game 1, players begin with different starting rotations, P1 with unknown tiles horizontal and P2, P3, P4, P5 with them rotated vertically. Soon after observing how the other players rotate their tiles, P1 swaps their rotation style to match the rest of the team.

4.1.2 Ordering. How players order the cards in their hand is also utilised to store and communicate information. The typical conventions seen in the games are that the order of cards/tiles represent the time spent in hand, with either left or right being the oldest card/tile. Some players also use left or right to depict known or unknown, similar to rotated or unrotated cards. In game 3, P2 advises P1 to "try and keep it [a new card] away from your other cards" . This preserves the order in which cards are picked up, meaning that the player does not have to remember which card has been in their hand the longest. This is important as the assumption is that cards that are in a player's hand a long time, and have had no clues given about them, can be assumed to not be very useful at the time of play. One can then deduce that such a card is either safe to discard, or useful for a later time. This ordering behaviour is quickly adopted by the other players in the game. The same behaviour was observed in the tiled version of the game, where ordering represented how recently a tile was drawn and if something was known about that tile.

4.1.3 Repositioning. Repositioning is where players move cards or tiles outside the typical line they keep them in. For cards, this line is how they are held between thumb and the first few forefingers. As the game progresses and many different pieces of information are learnt about players’ cards, the need for more dimensions of distinction between those cards arises. This leads players to holding cards in different ways in order to represent different pieces of information. In game three we can see all players repositioning specific cards in the same way. For example, during the game P2 is told that a card in their hand is a five. They then move this card from where the rest of the cards have been held all game, between thumb and forefingers, to between ring and pinky finger - what P5 calls “The five holding technique” .

Immediately after this, P3 was given a clue that they were also holding a five. They then copied the repositioning behaviour from P2. After this exchange P4 also copies the other two by placing a five between their ring and pinky fingers. This exact same technique is seen in game 2, the other game that was played with cards:

G2 – P1 – 36:20 “I will spend a clue to say that is your (P3) five [P3 places the clued five between pinky and ring finger]”.

In the games we analysed, only clued fives were ever held in this way. This may be because holding cards in alternate grips begins to become difficult when you are trying to represent multiple different pieces of information. As there is only one five in each colour, this gripping technique is limited to a smaller number of cards. Since fives are so limited it is almost always preferable not to discard them. Therefore, accidentally discarding a five is something most players will want to avoid. By putting these cards in a special place, they run less risk of accidentally discarding that card.

While repositioning of cards was limited to this five holding technique, the affordances allowed by tiles saw players repositioning them in more distinct ways. Tiles are typically placed on the table; there is not a need to grip them. This removes the dexterity restrictions of holding cards in awkward positions and also extends the physical space that players can use to place tiles. Players utilised height, depth and distance to store and communicate information.

Unlike when holding cards in hand, tiles can be moved in front or behind one another. This new axis of movement was utilised by players in game 3 to store information. P5, for example, used depth to indicate playability of tiles, placing known ones in front of their other tiles. It is also pertinent to note that by moving tiles forward, P5 is physically moving them closer to the already played cards which acts as a physical reminder in the environment that closer to played cards means playable.

Stacking tiles on top of one another was also used to store information. When P1 is told that two tiles are fours, they move them to the right of the line and stack them on top of one another. Seeing this, P3 also stacks their two known four tiles on top of one another.

To represent that two known tiles have the same piece of known information, both players stack the tiles on top of each other. It is important to note that P3 was not told to do this but intuitively mimics P1’s behaviour. This in turn adds an extra reminder to P3 about what their tiles are in case they forget. If P3 were to look around the table and see what other tiles were stacked in the same way theirs are, they would see P1’s fours similarly stacked. This not only reminds P3 that these stacked tiles are the same number, but also that they are both fours, exactly like P1. P3 then builds upon this stacking convention further:

G1 – P3 – 14:46 [P3 is clued that the 4 on the bottom of the stack is green as well as another tile in their “hand”. P3 places the green of unknown rank next to the known green 4, which is on the bottom of the four stack]

G1 – P3 – 35:07 “These two are fours and these two are green…If I ordered them correctly this one should be a fitting match.”

G1 – P1 “Absolutely. Venn diagram.”

Here, P3 uses the vertical column to represent a tile is a 4. They also use the horizontal row to represent the tile is green. Hence, they can store and remember later that the intersection is a green 4. P3 is using a combination of height and ordering to represent number and colour respectively. This type of information storing is only possible due to the three-dimensional nature of the tiles, as cards could not be held in the same way.

A unique use of repositioning only observed in the tiled game was the use of dynamic movement to communicate to the rest of the group. Typically, the way tiles and cards were physically manipulated was used to represent information stored in players’ memories. These are static representations in that it is not the movement into these positions that is communicative but the positions themselves that represent something. Yet, dynamic movement was used by a player in game 1 to communicate.

P4 was previously given a clue that revealed a fully playable tile in their hand. On their turn, instead of playing that tile, they move the tile to the side, a distance away from their line of tiles, and proceed to give a clue to P5. Here, P4 is acknowledging that other players probably expect them to play the known tile. The physical movement of the tile indicates that they are aware of this, but consider another move more important. To not move that tile would risk other players mistakenly thinking that P4 does not know or has somehow forgotten that the tile is playable. Here, P4 is aware of the others’ desire for them to play the clued tile, and communicates their intention to play it later by physically moving the piece.

4.2 Coordinating play

Throughout the games observed, players were able to coordinate moves and strategies between team members. The official rules of Hanabi don't allow players to speak to their teammates or communicate intentions or plans. Yet, it is useful to know what teammates intend to do and if teammates are wanting you to perform certain actions. Therefore, human players find ways to coordinate the moves of their teammates and the group through a variety of other means. We categorise these behaviours into two distinct forms: nudging and goal setting.

4.2.1 Nudging. Nudging behaviours are when someone other than the current player tries to influence that player's action. Players determine whether nudging is appropriate through a combination of factors. The current player could be new to the game and therefore a more experienced player might assume they are unaware of what appears to be the optimal play at the time. They could be missing key information, such as important knowledge of their hand or forgetting a previously mentioned clue. These reasons cause a player to give some sort of signal to the current player.

These signals can come in many forms, and generally what is allowable or not is determined during play as grey rules are negotiated. Throughout the games we observed, nudging actions took the form of players asking reasoning questions or stating common knowledge at specific times. Technically no new or hidden knowledge was revealed through these utterances, yet the precise timing of them indicated something to the active player, which may help them perform a more favourable action during their turn.

A behaviour we observed that was intended to nudge players to perform an action was getting teammates to question their reasoning. When the current player is indicating they are going to perform an action, or when they are unsure of what action to do, other players will sometimes get the current player to sound out their reasoning. The outside players, typically the more experienced ones, will then question the logic in order to guide the current player to a certain action.

In game three, P1 was deciding what move to make (clue, play or discard):

G3 – P1 – 29:54 “What can I tell you [P2]”?

G3 – P2 – 29:58 [points to the singular clue token] “Do you need to tell me anything? You reckon you can play something or discard something?”

P1 indicates they are thinking about giving P2 a clue. Rather than explicitly telling P1 not to give a clue, P2, who can see the cards in P1’s hand and that there is only one clue token left, uses the clue token piece to indicate there is a better alternative play. After this exchange P1 looks down at their cards, stares at a rotated card in their hand for a few seconds, then decides to play it. It turns out to be a playable red three. A similar behaviour occurs in game 2:

G2 - P2 - 32:52 “I'm very tempted to throw down this green”

G2 - P5 “To do what”

G2 -P2 “To drop it down and play it”

G2- P5 “What are the pros and cons?”

In this case, P2 wanted to play a playable green card but was showing reluctance. P5, the more experienced player, asked P2 to sound out their logic. This eventually led to P2 playing the card, which successfully scored the team an extra point.

Another way players nudge teammates is through stating common knowledge - information that all players have access to. Therefore, in theory, nothing new should be revealed to players when this type of information is stated. This could be the rules of the game, how many clues are left, which cards have been played or discarded so far. For example:

G3 - P1 - 31:17 “We've got no clues, so I have to discard”

G3 - P2 “Discard or play”

Here, P1 is considering what to do on their turn, indicating that they should discard because “we've got no clues” . Typically, this is a good move, as it regains a clue token. Yet, P2 staring at P1’s hand gives a rules reminder to P1; they can “discard or play”. By this point in the game, all players are aware of the rule that one can play or discard if there are no clue tokens. This rules reminder conveys no extra information, as technically all players have access to all the rules all the time. In this instance, unbeknownst to P1, they had a playable card in their hand that they had previously been told about but were currently forgetting or ignoring. By reminding P2 that they are allowed to play a card, P2 nudges P1 to perform a play action. Rules reminders intended to nudge are given even though all players have demonstrated they know the rule being stated. The extra information communicated by this reminder comes from the precise timing of when this reminder was given.

A related behavior is stating board/game knowledge that is available to all players to nudge an action. Like rules reminders, specific timing of reminders of common knowledge can prompt certain actions to be performed. An example is seen in game 3:

G3 - P3 - 31:23 “We haven't made any mistakes [points to the lack of available clue tokens]”

G3 - P2 “Yes we haven't made any mistakes so we can probably carry a mistake or two”

In the above example, it is P1’s turn and they are deciding what to do. P3 and P2 remind P1 of a specific element of the current board state, that there are all four storm tokens left and that there are no clue tokens left. P2 and P3 are encouraging P1 to take a gamble and play a card. This leads P1 to play a card they had incomplete knowledge about. While this can be considered general gameplay advice, it's inherently laden with information because those encouraging an action can see that P1 has a playable card in hand at the time. Another example of this behaviour is seen in game 2:

G2 – P3 - 5:25 “So you [P2] don't know anything and we still have plenty of clues”

It's currently P2’s turn and they are taking their time deciding what to do. P3 nudges P2 to give a clue by stating two commonly known things, that P2 has been given no information about their hand and that there are lots of clue tokens left to give clues.

4.2.2 Goal setting. Nudging actions aim to influence a single teammate to do something immediately. Complementary to this is goal setting. Goal setting aims to coordinate the overall short-term focus for all players, given the current board state. This could be getting more clue tokens back, cycling through cards, or completing all of a certain colour or rank. Behaviors that aim to set goals are generally not directed toward a specific player and instead address the group as a whole. Goal setting behaviors will typically take the form of a general statement about the board state. For example in Game 3:

G3 - P1 - 13:35 “We've got one more colour, we've got a red one.”

In this instance all colours of one have been played except for a red. P2 makes a general statement here about information available to all players. In doing this P2 is bringing focus to the missing red one. This sets a goal of finding and playing this last red one.

Goal setting statements could also be more general than this. In Game 2, P3 is the active player and is taking their time looking at the hands of the other players:

G2 - P3 - 3:25 “Clues so far are not good”

Here P3 is assessing the possible clues they could give to other players. By stating that the clues are “not good” they imply that there are no easily playable cards in anyone's hand. This statement does not directly communicate specific information about cards in other players’ hands, but does inform players that there is no obviously playable card. This indicates to other players that they should focus on discarding some of their cards. This will likely reveal playable cards and coordinates the team's focus on discarding actions. In game 1, a goal setting statement was used to focus the group on regaining more clue tokens.

G1 – P5 - 6:54 “Based on what I can see around the table and because we're running out of clues, I'm going to discard this one.”

Here P5 is discarding a one to regain clue tokens. Their general statement about information available to all players brings the group's focus to the lack of clue tokens. This statement implies that one of the group's goals should be to regain some of the used clue tokens.

By setting goals and nudging, players can direct either the group as a whole or individual players. Players found ways to do this without revealing hidden information, using publicly available information such as rules and board state. The precise timing of remarks based on this public information is what conveys the hidden meaning during nudging actions. By focusing the group's attention on a specific element of the board, players better organise the team's collective intention around a single goal, which can now be achieved more efficiently because all members are working towards it.

4.3 Role establishment

A striking observation we found across all three games was that participants actively developed and maintained social roles in relation to their status in game play knowledge. This basic finding of role establishment is central to the account we develop here about Hanabi game play, and provides an important context for the rest of our findings. A player's role determines the meaning behind their actions. Different players’ performance of the same action was interpreted differently based on the player's individual role. The two roles we identified were teachers and followers. Teachers were experienced players whose actions were typically to teach accepted rules and conventions to followers. Followers were newer players with less experience in the game, who mimic the actions of teachers. It is nothing new that during a board game newer players follow and are taught by more experienced players. Yet, in the context of Hanabi, these roles have real implications on how utterances are interpreted and reacted to which affects group performance.

In each Hanabi session we identified at least one player who took up the role of teacher. Teachers were appointed based on previous play experience. The goal of teachers was to get every player to have a consistent mental model of how the game worked. This meant teaching the rules from the game book, the conventions used by experienced players, and how a player should typically reason throughout the game. This makes it easier for players to reason about how their teammates might act.

In game where there was only one experienced player, that player immediately took on the role of teacher. In games with multiple experienced players the teacher role was either shared, or explicitly given to a certain player.

G1 – P1 – 00:35 “Any questions don't hesitate to ask, the only reason I jumped in to teach is because P5 said they're hopelessly bad at teaching”.

In G1, P1 publicly announced that they would teach the game. Yet this does not stop P5 from occasionally interjecting with teaching statements:

G1 – P5 – 3:25 “At the start of the game when we've got clues, it's probably best to give a clue to someone else.”

This also demonstrates one way teachers taught convention throughout play, through explicit statements. In the above quote, the active player is P2, who having never played the game before, is taking their time deciding what to do on their first move. P2 then goes on to tell P3 about a 4 tile in their hand but violates the rule stating you need to clue all of a certain number in a player's hand. P2 is then corrected by P5:

G1 – P5 – 3:55 “Remember we clarified in the rules, if there was more than one of something in someone's hand you have to tell them about all of them”

In this exchange, the teachers use explicit statements to teach conventions of the game, during play. This method of teaching was seen in all gameplay sessions. Teachers also teach players how to reason by sounding out loud their own reasoning process:

G1 – P1 – 5:32 “Based on what you've told me earlier P5 and the fact that P4 gave a similar clue, they would have only done that if both were so safe so I'm playing on as well.”

At this stage of the game, no other player has previously explicated their reasoning process out loud. Since P1 was designated as the teacher, they are using their turn to teach P2 and P3 their reasoning process. This encourages those players to perform similar, hence predictable, reasoning on their turn. P1 repeats this behaviour later in the game, after which P2 mimics their behaviour, and later P3 as well.

G1 – P1 – 13:24 “Now that you haven't told me anything and that's my oldest card in my hand [points to rightmost tile] I'm actually going to discard it.”

G1 – P2 – 14:00 “Since I know that this is a one and all the ones have been played I'm going to discard it.”

G1 – P3 – 17:27 “I have no idea what they are [points to tiles in their hand] I know that these two are 4s.”

While P1, P2, and P3 are all performing the same behaviour, because P1 has taken up the teacher role, these utterances carry different implicit meanings. Beyond the literal meaning conveyed in each utterance, when P1 sounds out their reasoning it is implicit that followers should copy this type of reasoning because P1 is a teacher. When P2 and P3 sound out their reasoning, this signals to the group that the followers are now adhering to the agreed-upon reasoning process. Therefore, the group can now be more confident about each players intention given what they know.

Teachers also used questions to standardise reasoning. In game 2, P2 is the only follower, being the only inexperienced Hanabi player. Midway through the game, P5 had established themself as one of the teachers by explicitly stating gameplay conventions.

G2 - P5 - 25:37 "One of the things I do is I flip it so on the back I know that I know something about it."

In an exchange between P2 and P5, P2 initially is hesitant to play a card in their hand but eventually decides to play it. Their reasoning for this is questioned by P5.

G2 – P5 – 34:30 “What would have stopped you from playing that?”

G2 – P2 “If we were closer to the bomb point [P2 points to the storm tokens on the table]”

G2 – P1 “Oh yeah…Nah”

After P2 explains their reasoning, P1, who had established themself as a teacher by initially teaching many of the rules, jumps in to show they disagree that such reasoning is appropriate.

In game 3, P2 performs a similar questioning behaviour, but this time to nudge P1.

G3 – P1 – 29:54 “What can I tell you [P2]”?

G3 – P2 – [points to the singular clue token] “Do you need to tell me anything? You reckon you can play something or discard something?”

In each gameplay session, whenever a question like this was asked by a player in the teacher role, it was to direct or teach followers. Followers picked up on this, each time adjusting their reasoning or responding correctly to the nudging action. When followers ask questions, these are always interpreted as information seeking questions and not attempts at nudging or teaching reasoning.

G3 – P1 – 10:32 “Can I ask you a question about the way you are holding your cards? Do you know what that is? [points to the vertical card in P2’s hand]”

G3 - P2 “I don't know what that is, I'm holding it that way because I don't know what that is.”

Here the question asked by P1 was to clarify why P2 holds their cards that way, which is then copied by P1. P1 repeats this again later in the game.

G3 – P1 – 15:50 “I'm not allowed to give 2 pieces of information?”

The main distinction between questions asked by teachers and followers was that teachers’ questions had the subtext of reinforcing a certain line of reasoning or nudging a player. This subtext was usually executed by followers in the form of adjusting reasoning or executing the nudge. Followers’ questions had no subtext and were not treated as nudging actions. It was through the establishment of these roles that players knew which questions had subtext.

Overall, roles are used by players to interpret what the subtext behind statements were and also which players to follow in order to homogenise group convention. A player's role was established and maintained throughout play by the actions they performed and their known previous game experience.

4.4 Rules negotiation

While Hanabi has a seemingly clear definition of what types of communication are or are not allowed, there are many communicative behaviours that players engage with that are not addressed by the rules. The rule book outlines certain actions that are and are not allowed. For example, a player cannot look at their own cards or tell people what specific cards are in their hand. Yet, the rule book doesn't address many other possible actions such as casual table talk, the order players point to cards when giving clues, whether players can tell others about what they know, or if players can ask others what they know. We refer to these as the set of “grey rules”. Players come into a game with different opinions on which grey rules to follow. Typically, experienced players, or followers, will adopt the grey rules demonstrated by teachers.

In Hanabi, the physical action of both playing a card and discarding a card is the same, a player puts a card/tile face up on the table. Therefore, it can be ambiguous what players are doing when they put a card/tile from their hand on the table. The rule book doesn't address this issue so it is up to players to determine how to deal with this ambiguity. In game 1 we can see two experienced players enforcing the grey rule that one should say what they intend to do before they place a card/tile.

G1 – P1 – 2:28 [P1 places a tile from their hand, face up on the table]

G1 – P5 “I think it's best to always say whether you are playing it or discarding it.”

G1 – P1 “Yes you're absolutely correct.”

Later in the game, P5 reinforces this rule when P3 moves a tile from their hand face up onto the table.

G1 – P3 - 4:30 [Moves a 4 from their hand into the middle of the table]

G1 – P5 “Were you playing or were you discarding?”

This is immediately adopted by P2 who proceeds to always announce whether they are discarding or playing. All players except P3 follow this rule, but P3 now plays by placing the tile silently next to the other played tiles and discards by placing it near the discarded tiles. Here we can see P3 is using the placement of physical pieces of the game to indicate their intention. Instead of announcing their intention, as recommended by P5 - who has taken on the role of the teacher - P3 adheres to the principle of the rule by using the physical pieces to disambiguate their intention. Once P3 adopts this behaviour of disambiguation P1 ceases to ask P3 if they are playing or discarding, and a common understanding of how to clarify players’ intention has been reached across the group. In this game, this grey rule is established relatively quickly, with all players following it.

In game two, two experienced players spend most of the game negotiating a certain grey rule that they have come into the game with different opinions about. The rule is whether a player is allowed to ask another player what they know about their cards. Initially, P1 establishes that asking others what they know is not allowed.

G2 – P1 – 10:05 “You're also not allowed to ask people what they remember about the clues that we've given them and that sort of stuff.”

As a result, no players engage in this behaviour, with players silently reminding themselves about previous clues.

G2 – P3 – 13:50 [P3 mouths something silently to themselves while pointing at their clued cards]

18 minutes into the game, P5 joins the table. P5 immediately establishes themself as an experienced player by asking about which conventions the group is using.

G2 - P5 – 17:23 “Are we using any particular conventions?”

G2 - P1 “We're just using the core rules hopefully.”

P5 then goes on to violate the previously established grey rule of asking others what they know.

G2 - P5 - 18:53 “P1 what do you know about your cards?”

G2 - P1 “Um are we actually allowed to answer that question?”

G2 - P5 “Yes”

G2 - P1 “Really?”

G2 - P5 “You can clarify what people know”

G2 - P1 “Fair enough”

Both players had established themselves earlier in the game as teachers, by giving strategic advice to the new player P2. This negotiation continued throughout the game, with P5 asking others what they know, and P1 stating players are not allowed to do so. It is only after a successful clue given by P5 that P1 concedes the point and allows others to ask what players know, hence reaching a group consensus.

G2 - P1 – 20:35 “That was a very interesting clue, although it only worked because of the recap mechanic.”

G2 – P1 – 32:24 “What do you remember about your cards?”

The interesting clue was given by P5 after using the “recap mechanic” which was P5 asking what other players knew. By the end of the game, P1 is using this same mechanic that earlier they claimed was a rules violation.

Over the course of the game, through play and discussion, teams come to collective agreements on grey rules. This occurs even when experienced players come into the game with different opinions on which grey rules are allowable. Players then use these grey rules to perform other behaviours such as manipulating artefacts and coordinating play.

4.5 Interpreting a complex sequence of play

To demonstrate in practice how these four behaviours that humans exhibit during play influence their ability to get such high scores, we now outline a scenario in which every behaviour is exploited to perform a complex and fruitful sequence of plays. 19 minutes into game two, the players have scored only 5 points so far. There is currently a green 1 on the board. P1 holds the green 2, but only knows its colour. They have used ordering to isolate that card. The green 3 is in P2’s hand; again, they only know the colour. It is positioned next to their other known green card (see Figure 1).

G2 – P5 - 19:45 “I'm going to spend one of our very valuable clue tokens to tell you that … so you [P1] know something about that one [points to the isolated card in P1 hand]”

G2 - P1 - “I do know something about this one”

G2 - P5 - “And you [P2] know something about that one [points to the grouped greens in P2’s hand]”

G2 – P2 – “I know these are green”

G2 – P5 – “[points to P2’s hand] This card is a 3.”

G2 – P1 – [Plays their green card, which is revealed to be the playable green 2]

Since P1 can see that P2’s green 3 is not currently playable, they deduce that their green card is the green 2 required to make that card playable, and successfully play it. P5 uses the negotiated grey rule of asking players what they know to bring attention to specific cards in P1 and P2’s hand. Since P5 has established themself as a teacher, this question is interpreted as a nudging action rather than a clarification question, indicating to P1 and P2 that their next moves should involve these cards. This grey rule also reveals P5’s intention to the other two players, allowing P1 and P2 to engage in more informed theory of mind reasoning. P5 can see which cards P1 and P2’s have partial information about because P1 and P2 have rotated those cards to represent their partial knowledge. As a result of this, P5 was able to explicitly communicate the information that P2 is holding a green 3, while also implicitly telling P1 that they hold the green 2 and that they should play it, using only one clue token to reveal two playable cards distributed across two players hands.

G2 - P1 – 20:30 “That was a very interesting clue, although it only worked because of the recap mechanic”

After this exchange, P1 ceases to comment on the use of this grey rule, and even goes on to use it themself:

G2 – P1 – 32:24 “What do you remember about your cards?”

Although the group started with different interpretations of this grey rule, negotiation during play led them to the consensus that employing such a tactic is allowable.

This same type of clue was seen in game 3, where P2 first clarifies that P3 and P4 know something about their cards, then gives a clue to P4 about a currently unplayable card. P3 then deduces that the card clarified by P2 is playable, therefore making P4’s card now playable. Just as in game 2, the teacher uses an established grey rule to identify certain known cards in P3 and P4’s hands. These cards are identified as known because of the way P3 and P4 repositioned them in their hands. This clarifying statement nudges the two players to perform certain actions.

These complex sequences of play were only possible because players exploit artefact manipulation, coordinating play, roles and negotiated rules. What is even more striking is that these plays were performed by players who had never met, some of whom had never played the game before, within their first game.

5 DISCUSSION

Through the study reported here, we have identified some of the key behaviours used by human teams to communicate and reason with each other through the game of Hanabi. IWe will no consider the significance of the human gameplay, and review how each of these behaviours contributes to the overall performance, which in turn gives insight into the previously established superior performance of humans compared to AI agents in Hanabi. Firstly, we discuss how humans use a set of recurring artefact manipulation behaviours to offload and distribute cognition to the physical game pieces. We then explore how grey rules are continually negotiated and exploited to allow for additional modes of implicit communication, enabling players to nudge one another and set overall goals, which in turn enables more skillful and effective play. We argue that the social roles maintained by players throughout the game help to establish the group as a team. Giving responsibilities and tasks to certain players, facilitates the encoding of implicit information into statements. Finally, we comment on how some of the identified behaviours might be deployed in AI agents.

5.1 Physical artefacts can become the tools for theory of mind reasoning

Humans are able to conduct theory of mind reasoning because they have better access to representations of other players’ minds through the physical artefacts of the game. Rogerson et al. [2018] point out that knowledge of a game's state is distributed amongst the players and physical pieces of the game. They illustrate that the components of the game are used for offloading cognitive tasks such as remembering which card was clued by rotating it. Our study pointed to distinct categories of artefact manipulation that are common across different play groups: rotation, ordering and repositioning. By physically performing these behaviours, players not only offload cognitive work but also signalled to other players their own private information. In this way, these behaviours act as side channels of communication, a way for players to transfer knowledge between each other outside of explicit clue giving. This concept of communicating through artefact manipulation has been identified elsewhere as feedthrough [ 24 ]. McEwan et al. [2016] explore how feedthrough is used throughout Chess games as a form of implicit communication between players that allows them to have a conversation on physical, syntactic, and strategic levels  [ 50 ]. Similarly, we can view the manipulation of artefacts in Hanabi as a form of feedthrough, in which players communicate mental states such as intention and memory to one another. Bard et al. [2020], acknowledge that unlike signalling games, Hanabi's communicative actions are mixed in with the environment  [ 5 ]. Extending this, our work demonstrates that it is not just communicative actions that are physically situated but also private and public knowledge. In this way, what was previously conceived as private information becomes publicly accessible because it is situated in the observable environment.

The depth and complexity of artefact communication in the games observed was tied to the affordances of the tiles or cards. We observed a higher diversity of physical communication techniques particularly in the game where players used tiles. This was related to their 3-dimensional nature which meant that, unlike the 2-dimensional cards, they could be stacked and placed on the table. Because of the stacking and placing affordances offered by tiles, players are no longer restricted by the dexterity of their hands and hence we observe tiles being balanced on top of one another, and more regularly manipulated when compared to the cards. If we compare this to a digital playing environment, none of these affordances exist and hence this extra channel of communication is inaccessible to players. This reliance on the physical artefacts for representing and communicating private information requires that AI agents wanting to operate cooperatively with humans in this context must interact in some meaningful way with these artefacts. If humans’ theory of mind reasoning in this setting heavily relies upon artefacts, then to display similar theory of mind reasoning in AI agents seems to also require the use of artefacts. To avoid the challenge of making a robotic hand to hold cards, we believe a promising way of pursuing artefact manipulation would be through embodying an agent in virtual reality (VR).

5.2 Grey rules enable skillful play and side channels of communication

Throughout every game players continually renegotiated certain grey rules . This allowed players with different initial interpretations of these grey rules to successfully play together. The rules negotiated were typically actions not addressed in the rules book, such as whether a player can ask another player what they know, or whether a player must announce whether they are playing or discarding a card. This formed the set of grey rules which players attempted to follow throughout the game. This reflects the idea that a game's rules cannot be fully described as each player has a personal view of what it means to play  [ 67 ]. Rules negotiation also served to clarify the meaning of actions that could be seen as vague. For example, players in G1 agreed that one should "always say whether you are playing or discarding" as the action of placing a card on the table was ambiguous. While this was followed by all players, P3 instead uses the physical pieces rather than utterances to adhere to this rule. Once P3 starts doing this, all other players cease to ask for clarification of P3’s actions. Here we can see that a grey rule is accepted by the group, even if not every member adheres to it in the exact same way. The essence of this grey rule - that one must clarify whether they are playing or discarding a tile - is being adhered to, irrespective of what form it takes.

Yet, we can also see the opposite happening in G2, where players spend most of the game negotiating whether one is allowed to ask players what they know about their cards. In a way this clarifying question serves the same purpose as rotation and reordering of cards; both indicate what a player knows about their cards. Yet, in this game P1 takes issue with P5 continually asking others what they know. This is until this clarification directly results in "a very interesting clue" , after which P1 stops protesting to clarifications and even uses it themselves. This same behaviour is pointed out by Rogerson et al., who state that players will prioritise more skilled play over adherence to the rules  [ 62 ].

An important point to address is whether developers of Hanabi agents should just dismiss these behaviours as cheating. We would argue that doing so is a mistake. If the ultimate goal in developing Hanabi agents is to have them play well with humans, then to ignore the behaviour of rules negotiation that many players engage with, reduces the problem to an inaccurate - albeit simpler - imitation of reality. This rules negotiation behaviour has not previously been relevant to game AI due to the focus on competitive environments. In such environments it is not to any player's benefit to allow their opponent to reinterpret or renegotiate the rules, even though humans also do this in competitive environments  [ 50 , 62 ]. Yet, when it comes to cooperative settings it is to every player's benefit. It therefore seems that the main reason to not engage with the observed rules negotiation that occurs during human games of Hanabi would be to simplify the problem. If Hanabi agents want to fully engage with all aspects of human-AI teams in Hanabi, having the ability to adapt to players renegotiated rules is essential.

5.3 Humans form sophisticated team structures in Hanabi

Our results show that participants perceived the game as a teaming scenario, rather than a merely cooperative group. The four behaviours identified in the results each correspond with Groom and Nass's requirements for successful team behaviour. Teams require a shared common goal. All players not only have a shared goal of scoring the maximum points but they also develop sub-goals through coordinating play, such as getting more clue tokens or cycling through cards. Players “know and fulfill their roles” through establishing themselves and acting as either a teacher or a follower. This also aligns with Converse et al.’s distinction between teams and groups, where groups consist of members with homogeneous roles and responsibilities  [ 14 ]. Cooke et al. [2000] further this idea by stating that teams might have overlapping knowledge, but role specific knowledge is required  [ 20 ]. We can see this reflected in the results where teachers used their previous experience in the game to take on the responsibility of teaching followers game conventions and how to reason. We can also see players taking on different roles on a more micro scale throughout the game. As the game state changes, the information each player has access to does as well. This accessible information determines whether a player is more likely to give clues to signal playable cards, discard cards in order to regain clues, or play cards. In this way a player adopts an overall role of either teacher or follower, but also more temporary roles depending on their access to information in the board state.

Players also continually developed a "shared mental model"  [ 33 ] of the team. This standardised the meaning of certain ambiguous actions which allowed for implicit communication to occur through artefact manipulation, rules reminders, and gestures. It was usually the responsibility of the teachers to ensure that a consistent shared mental model was achieved. This is evidenced by the fact that it was often teachers who clarified the meaning behind these ambiguous actions. This has important implications for human-AI teaming in Hanabi as it shows that humans give the responsibility for establishing and maintaining a shared mental model to distinct members of the team. This means that AI agents need to adhere to these social roles in order to understand whether it should follow another player's lead or try to enforce a mental model itself. A shared mental model in human-AI teams requires not only humans to form mental models of AI, which alone is proving to be challenging, but for AI agents to form mental models of humans and the team as a whole  [ 30 ]. The philosophical question of whether AI is capable of having a mental model is beyond the scope of this paper. Yet, reducing mental model to simply mean "an AI that can accurately describe, predict and explain behaviour" it seems necessary that an AI be able to do this if we are to say an AI playing Hanabi with humans is truly a human-AI teaming scenario.

Other than shared mental models the number of members in a team also influences humans’ perception of AI teaming scenarios. Human-AI cooperation in Hanabi has traditionally been studied in 2-player dyadic situations due to the technical limitations of the agents not being able to play with more than one player  [ 32 , 48 , 65 ]. Yet, it has been shown that humans are more likely to view cooperative groups with AI as teams if there are greater than two members  [ 60 ]. This could explain why the behaviours identified in this study, which closely align to those expected in a teaming scenario, do not appear in other studies with humans playing with artificial Hanabi agents. We therefore suggest that to study human-AI teaming in Hanabi, it is crucial to include more than two players.

5.4 How AI agents might engage with physicality and social roles in Hanabi

Effective teams form shared mental models - a shared understanding of the problem, with plans and strategies for solving it, understanding of cues and information, and roles and responsibilities of team members  [ 55 ]. We observed our participants forming such shared mental models as they adopted and mimicked each other's implicit communication behaviours throughout the game. For example, players in game 3 all adopted the common “5 holding technique” and developed a shared understanding. This implies that for a human-AI Hanabi team to be effective, its members should aim to develop a shared mental model. Specifically, an AI agent would need the capabilities to incorporate and understand the implicit communication and reasoning techniques used by their human teammates, some of which we have presented in this paper. We recommend that Hanabi AI agents would be more effective as a teammate if they engage with and utilise these behaviours.

Although our study has highlighted each of the four themes as important patterns of behaviour for successful human Hanabi play, it does not provide guidance on how to implement each of them in a human-AI team. In particular, we believe further work is required to understand what it would mean for an AI to engage with rules negotiation and coordinated human-AI play, and therefore we don't discuss those themes further in this section. We describe this under Future Work, below. On the other hand artefact manipulation seems to be the clearest and most promising avenue for Hanabi agents to pursue. In order to achieve this, a promising approach is to embody the agent and the game in some way, either in real space or virtual reality (VR). By embodying the game, we could enable not only humans to engage with the affordances allowed by the physical pieces of the game, but potentially AI agents as well. If the AI agent can detect how these affordances are utilised, and in response to what, then it might be possible to create semantic associations between a piece of information (a card is clued as a 5) and how an object is physically manipulated (placed between ring and pinky fingers). Naively, an agent could start with a hard coded convention on what each movement or position represents (e.g. horizontal cards are known, vertical cards are unknown) and adjust the confidence of its guesses based on those card positions. This is similar to how Sato & Osawa [2020] use timing to adjust guess confidence  [ 65 ].

Another important aspect of the games physicality is the fact that players are aware that teammates can see them manipulating their cards. This extends artefact manipulation beyond a simple cognitive offloading technique  [ 59 ] which an individual human might use to a communication technique that other teammates can understand. Therefore, for AI agents to engage with this channel of communication, the capability to rotate one's own cards or tiles would be beneficial for human teammates in understanding the AI's intentions, beliefs and desires. This could help inform human players that the AI is aware of a particular convention, allowing them to utilise this technique with more confidence that an AI will understand what they are doing. This brings the AI closer to human play, which in turn assists the team in developing a shared mental model. To avoid the challenges associated with embodying an AI in a physical robotic body, a promising approach for future research is to investigate human-AI Hanabi teams in VR or through flexible digital interfaces such as Tabletop Simulator or Card board  [ 17 , 29 ]. These programs try to simulate the manipulation of physical game pieces in 2D and 3D digital space.

Engaging with the way human players establish roles in Hanabi presents a different challenge. While we see humans naturally take up different roles during games of Hanabi, interesting questions arise about what roles an AI agent might adopt. Some inexperienced players may enjoy having the AI take on the role of teacher where it attempts to help the newer player through prompts about rules or general assistive statements. Other players may feel this makes the game too easy. This presents a promising avenue for future work. For example, Guzdial et al [2019] investigate what roles machine learning algorithms should take on to best collaborate with people to support creative expression. They find participants vary in their desired interactions and role of the AI assistant  [ 35 ]. Following a similar study design in the context of Human-AI Hanabi teams seems a promising way to explore the expectations of Hanabi players when it comes to the role an AI teammate should play.

Importantly, these implications of our findings for future AI are all concerned with the improvement of Human-AI teams, rather than focusing on individual AI agent's performance. We believe approach is important when designing AI agents that perform well in cooperative team settings. As Dafoe et al. highlight, there is a need for improved understanding of the development of shared social norms and institutions to support cooperative dynamics.  [ 23 ].

5.5 Limitations and future work

One of our main aims in designing our study was to create a naturalistic playing environment and experience, so that we could observe the richness of genuine human game play. This was necessary but carries with it an important limitation that the players and play had idiosyncrasies that were not reflective of Hanabi play generally. To mitigate the problem of representativeness, we focused our analysis of the recurring behaviour patterns in our data. While the sample size of 14 participants is too small to make definitive conclusions about how all humans play, we considered that the observed extended sequences of game play contained some likely to common and recurring aspects of human play. The sample size was also appropriate as our study was always intended to be exploratory, and it is telling that we nevertheless generated four themes that can inspire future research. The use of thematic analysis of game play footage provides a richness to our results, which gives a holistic understanding of the gameplay techniques used in this space. At the same time this richness comes from a highly interpretive analysis which is influenced by the judgment of the researchers. We attempted to mitigate this limitation through highly iterative and collective analysis process across the three authors. Consistent with this approach, we have identified some key behaviours, but have not attempted to estimate their typicality or prevalence in Hanabi game play more generally. However, we believe that type of detailed, human-centred analysis is valuable to illustrate the subtle behaviours that make up human Hanabi game play, which are currently not reflected in the Hanabi AI literature.

Further work in this space, which could take a more structured and experimental approach, should investigate the potential for an AI to engage in rules negotiation as well as coordinated human-AI play. Moreover, there is scope to extend the use of eye tracking from a purely qualitative recording device  [ 61 ] to examine how both novice and experienced players interact with the game.

We do believe that it would be valuable if future research in this space were to take a more structured experimental approach. We see potential in a more detailed analysis of player's gaze throughout play. This could be used to identify the most important or useful artefacts throughout the game or how often players observe each other's hand manipulation behaviours. Our study used eye tracking as a purely qualitative tool but as stated by Rogerson, Gibbs and Smith [2017] eye tracking has the potential to provide a more detailed understanding of player's attention during play  [ 61 ].

Furthermore, useful future work could also productively investigate whether and how an existing human team inculcate a new AI team member that has been introduced. It may be that human players utilise artefacts, social roles, and negotiated rules amongst themselves, while excluding the AI agent, or they may attempt to involve the agent in these complex behaviours. In order to investigate the effect of physical artefact manipulation in the context of human-AI teams, flexible Hanabi interfaces which allow manipulation of game pieces could be used. Good candidates would be platforms such as Tabletop Simulator or Card board  [ 17 , 29 ].

6 CONCLUSION

Throughout this paper we have identified specific behaviours used by human players of Hanabi that explain how even novices can achieve high scores in ad-hoc groups through the utilisation of the physical artefacts, renegotiation of rules and the establishment of social roles. Through these behavours, human teams readily establish side channels of communication, embed private knowledge and intentions in the physical arrangement of game pieces, and coordinate their play. If AI agents aim to perform well in teams with humans, designers must consider how best to accommodate these cognitive processes. To contribute to similar levels of performance in ad-hoc human-AI teams, AI agents must go beyond traditional notions of internal theory of mind reasoning and find ways to participate in physically situated and distributed forms of cognition as practiced by human teams.
