BubbleTex: Designing Heterogenous Wettable Areas for Carbonation Bubble Patterns on Surfaces

CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Materials are a key part of our daily experiences. Recently, researchers have been devising new ways to utilize materials directly from our physical world for the design of objects and interactions. We present a new fabrication technique that enables control of CO 2 bubble positions and their size within carbonated liquids. Instead of soap bubbles, boiling water, or droplets, we show creation of patterns, images and text through sessile bubbles that exhibit a lifetime of several days. Surfaces with mixed wettability regions are created on glass and plastic using ceramic coatings or plasma projection leading to patterns that are relatively invisible to the human eye. Different regions react to liquids differently. Nucleation is activated after carbonated liquid is poured onto the surface with bubbles nucleating in hydrophobic regions with a strong adherence to the surface and can be controlled in size ranging from 0.5mm – 6.5mm. Bubbles go from initially popping or becoming buoyant during CO 2 supersaturation to stabilizing at their positions within minutes. Technical evaluation shows stabilization under various conditions. Our design software allows users to import images and convert them into parametric pixelation forms conducive to fabrication that will result in nucleation of bubbles at required positions. Various applications are presented to demonstrate aspects that may be harnessed for a wide range of use in daily life. Through this work, we enable the use of carbonation bubbles as a new design material for designers and researchers.

1 INTRODUCTION

Physical materials for displaying information on everyday surfaces [ 68 , 69 , 70 ] have gained importance in recent years. Several fabrication techniques have been proposed to create materials [ 71 , 72 , 73 ] or change properties [ 65 , 66 , 67 ] in a controlled manner for new outputs. Radical Atoms [ 33 ] and adjacent visions [ 31 , 39 ] have demonstrated that we are undergoing a “material turn in interaction design” [ 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 ] where physical materials themselves are changing with properties embedded in them to make our daily activities more dynamic.

While we interact with several materials in the context of our daily life, one such material is bubble media in water. Human interaction with bubbles in water is implicit and embedded in our daily life. Bubbles made through soap, carbonation bubbles in drinks, natural air bubbles in water or vapor formed during boiling processes are only some of the places where we naturally encounter bubbles of various kinds. Because of our playful associations with bubbles [ 34 ], their satisfactory/reflective essence [ 44 ], and their ubiquitous nature, they are an excellent material [ 22 ] for designing rich artifacts or interactions.

Several researchers have developed projects showcasing soap bubbles in air [ 16 , 20 ], bubbles in liquids using complex mechanisms [ 3 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 20 , 64 ] or droplets [ 15 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 ] on hard surfaces. These devices often have associated electronics that require constant energy consumption. Prior bubble fabrication is also hard to scale because of linked electronic costs, may not be possible on vertical orientations or on large surface areas, and bubbles are often shorter lived than sometimes desired. We believe, in the landscape of Human Computer Interaction (HCI) projects with bubbles, bubbles inside water media remain untapped. In this paper, we specifically focus on bubbles in carbonated water to show various applications in different scales enabled through our fabrication technique. To our knowledge, none of the prior projects have been focused on such control of carbonation bubbles, that turns their random nature into a controlled stable media.

We propose a new fabrication technique to selectively modify the hydrophobicity properties of surfaces (glass/plastic) such that bubbles in carbonated liquids nucleate at specific positions on surfaces. This turns stochastic nucleation into controlled nucleation of bubbles for images, patterns, and text on various surfaces. Such surface modifications are relatively invisible to the human eye and may only be seen under strong or direct light. On pouring carbonated water in containers of various shapes (horizontal/vertical) and sizes, bubbles nucleate, coalesce, and grow to accurate sizes (1.0mm – 5.5mm) thus creating patterns, images, or text on surfaces. Such bubbles are highly stable and can keep sticking to the surfaces for longer than a week without significant disturbance. We equate this work with other digital fabrication techniques [ 65 , 66 , 71 , 72 , 73 ] in the domain of human computer interaction that enable new forms of material use and expression.

To this end, we developed a design tool that converts images into pixelated forms for corresponding physical bubble positions on surfaces. Inspired by Sylvester et al. who mentioned bubbles as a material [ 22 ] between “neither real nor fully virtual”, we extend the temporality of bubbles in our work. The process from initial nucleation to stabilization takes an average of twenty minutes, depending on the saturation of carbon dioxide (CO 2 ) in the liquids. We demonstrate two different techniques of such surface modifications, one that is manual and non-specialized, and another using plasma for quicker outputs. Both methods produce food-safe surfaces. Outputs as large as 13” x 13” images with thousands of bubbles and several applications at the interplay of surface and bubble media are demonstrated as well. Materiality has been shown to be important for humans [ 45 ] and we argue our fabrication technique enables a new material avenue for designers.

Our contributions in this paper are:

  • Patterning methods for selective hydrophobicity on surfaces to manifest images, texts, and patterns with CO 2 bubbles of carbonated liquids. Such nucleated bubbles have a lifetime of one week or more.
  • Demonstration of applications using this fabrication technique, including bubbles in vertically oriented surfaces and large-scale patterned outputs.
  • Design tool to convert images into pixelated forms for corresponding physical CO 2 bubbles on surfaces.
  • Technical evaluation encompassing bubble properties (stabilization time, degradation time), surface properties, coating, and liquid properties (concentration, color, surface tension)

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Surface Wettability

Wetting is the ability of a liquid to maintain contact with a surface. Several commercial hydrophobic coatings or lab-based methods (carbon nanotube coating, soot-templating, graphene, nanomesh) can change the wettability [ 2 , 3 ] of a surface. This modification changes the way liquid droplet(s) interact [ 6 , 11 ] with the surface, also known as the contact angle of a droplet. Surfaces with superhydrophobic coating [ 1 ] have a high contact angle for droplets while super hydrophilic surfaces might have a low contact angle. For practical purposes, many containers/tumblers for daily use are used as filled as opposed to surfacial amounts of water only. In this paper, we focus on patterns possible inside liquid media as they are more applicable to daily life. Our method shows heterogeneous wettability regions and their interplay with carbonated water can distinctly control bubble size and their placement. We also enable such design through our software tool that converts images/text/patterns into pixelation that corresponds to physical bubble nucleation.

2.2 Gas Bubble Nucleation

Nucleation is the beginning of a new thermodynamic phase via self-organization. Several researchers have studied nucleation [ 4 , 5 , 55 , 56 , 57 ] phenomena in various media. By studying the growth kinetics [ 6 , 57 ] of nucleates and changing surface conditions on which liquids boil, researchers can tweak heat transfer [ 7 , 58 , 59 , 60 , 61 ] in various conditions. In the context of carbonated liquids, such nucleation may occur in supersaturated fluids after depressurization because of surface abrasion, roughness [ 58 ] or hydrophobicity [ 1 , 7 , 13 ]. Bubble nucleation may occur anywhere in a liquid but surfaces with micro/nanostructures leading to cavities [ 2 ] make this more likely. Surfaces with higher hydrophobicity leading to less wettability are energetically favorable for nucleation [ 7 ].

Instead of fully suppressing wettability, we selectively change the surface hydrophobicity/hydrophilicity in various regions to control bubble size within carbonated liquids. Initial bubbles nucleate, coalesce to corresponding bubble sizes, are not buoyant, and stick to surfaces for longer than a week. Such controlled bubbles that do not become buoyant for many days on surfaces within carbonated liquids have not been shown previously. We show how such phenomena may be easily harnessed without complex mechanisms and a software-assisted fabrication technique to develop several applications designed in real life.

2.3 Electrowetting

In terms of controlling fluid shape and flow, methods of electrowetting [ 46 , 52 ] and digital microfluidics [ 47 ] have been developed by researchers. These involve dispensing a liquid droplet and possibilities of droplet manipulation [ 48 , 49 , 50 ] on a planar electrode array. Apart from lab-on-a-chip mechanisms for biochemistry, [ 15 , 51 ] have shown interaction possibilities using EWOD (Electrowetting-on-Dielectric. Tokuda et al. showed basic shapes formed by liquid metal (GaIn) under a 7x7 electrode [ 25 ] control. Such methods use high voltage, highly specialized materials and have been shown to manipulate a limited number of droplets in horizontal orientation under strictly controlled conditions, making them hard to scale for daily use.

2.4 Ephemeral User Interfaces

In the concept of ephemeral interfaces [ 12 , 36 ], researchers advocate for including materials from all dimensions of life including elements that may last for a limited time. Bannon et al. analyze ephemerality in the context of human needs [ 29 ] versus permanence. Such elements may include ice [ 77 ], fog [ 76 ], condensation [ 26 ], solar light [ 74 ], atmospheric humidity [ 78 ] or fire [ 75 ]. Specifically, in the context of bubbles [ 16 , 20 , 24 ], the lifetime may be too short as opposed to what we physically observe inside the liquid within tumblers/vessels. Through this work, we introduce a new form of material i.e., carbonation bubbles that are inside the liquid, and extend the temporality of bubbles as an ephemeral media [ 36 ]. Instead of bubbles on the surface of the liquid [ 54 ], surfacial adhesion affords visibility from outside transparent glass/plastic containers.

2.5 Liquids and Interaction Design

Information Percolator [ 17 ] is a collection of tubes with rising air bubbles in water that serve as ambient media in the background [ 30 , 32 ]. To represent information through bubbles, researchers have utilized falling water [ 41 , 42 , 28 , 83 , 88 ] controlled rapidly controlled magnetic valves or colored liquid actuated through clear liquid [ 43 , 84 , 85 ], anti-bubbles [ 87 ], and algae photosynthesis [ 86 ]. Venous materials [ 54 ] use fluids within channels displaying flow and color change. Several of these works are installation based for ambient experiences. We ground our work as a fabrication technique that affords applications space for carbonation bubbles within liquids. Instead of relying on tethered installations or one-time setups, we focus on the applicability to general vessel/container surfaces that we use in our daily life.

2.6 Bubble Media in HCI

Projects utilizing bubbles have been proposed by HCI researchers for olfactory integration [ 16 , 40 ], displays [ 18 , 19 , 20 , 23 , 24 , 27 , 28 , 63 , 64 ], and haptic feedback [ 21 ]. We are inspired by the whimsical nature of these works and build upon them while striving for simplicity of fabrication for accessibility to designers.

In particular, BubBowl [ 8 ] and Utakata [ 9 , 10 ] utilize electrolysis [ 14 ] to show alphabets and images using powered electronics affixed on a single vessel that disappear after power off. However, our daily interactions with fluids normally do not involve electricity. Such a function also needs to scale to multiple containers and vessels easily and hence, a surface modification technique is more approachable.

Moji-biru [ 53 ] shows paraffin coating to a ceramic surface. When beer is poured gently into a cold cup, a froth pattern appears on the surface. However, such patterns only exist for a short period (5mins), and work with small cold containers because of pattern disruption with the liquid flow and only binary regions. With BubbleTex, we extend this through carbonation bubbles at room temperature surfaces that can last more than a week, grayscale bubble compositions (by controlling bubble sizes), large scale patterns (on surfaces as large as 13” x 13”), bubbles on vertical wall and design of such patterns through a software tool. BubbleTex is a software-aided fabrication technique to pattern surfaces for controlled bubble nucleation. We demonstrate such low energy surfaces activated by carbonated water may be used in a variety of daily applications.

3 IMPLEMENTATION

While nucleation of gas bubbles in liquids is a well understood process [ 13 ], we use this mechanism to create non-wettable sites (Fig 2b ) for carbonation bubble patterns without active electronics hardware. In fizzy water and beverages, carbon dioxide is dissolved into water at elevated pressures to make it supersaturated with gas. When a bottle is opened to release pressure, bubbles nucleate in tiny, entrapped gas cavities (EGCs) on the surface (Fig 2c ) of bottle/containers. Bubbles can be seen to be nucleating repeatedly at these sites (miniscule crevices/strands), as dissolved CO 2 diffuses into existing bubbles to escape the liquid.

The following steps encompass our fabrication workflow to create sites with heterogeneous wettability on a substrate.

i. Create patterns using illustration software or using our custom software for automatic pixelization (Fig 3a )

ii. Cut a positive or negative mask (depending on the substrate and coating method below) on a vinyl cutter (Fig 3b )

iii. Choose a plastic or glass substrate and apply the mask (For glass: Negative mask that shows bubble sites, For plastic: Positive mask that hides bubble sites).

  1. If the substrate to be coated is glass: Use a ceramic hydrophobic coating (Section 3.1), where glass is initially hydrophilic and coated sites become hydrophobic (Fig 3c , Option 1: Coating drying time: 24 hours)
  2. If the substrate to be coated is plastic: Plasma exposure of plastic substrates (Section 3.2), where plastic is initially hydrophobic and all areas except bubble sites become hydrophilic. (Fig 3c , Option 2: Instant, No drying time)

We provide the detail of each fabrication method and its respective considerations below.

3.1 Fabrication Workflow with Hydrophobic Surface Coating on Glass

In this method, we use Regular ( HomeDepot, 599047 ), Tempered (HomeDepot) or Soda-lime ( Kinto 8434 ) glass as a substrate which is initially hydrophilic (wettable) in nature. An illustration software (Adobe Illustrator) or our custom design tool (Section 3.3) is used to create desired patterns (Fig 3a ) and print out an image mask using a vinyl cutter (Cricut Maker 3) (Fig 3b , Fig 6a ). The sticker mask is pasted on the surface and a SiO 2 -based ceramic hydrophobic coating (Fig 3c , Option 1) is applied on a cotton pad. The cotton pad with absorbed coating is rubbed onto the glass in a lateral motion followed by a vertical motion (Fig 6c ) and left to dry for 24 hours. This creates a surface with distinct wettability regions that are relatively invisible to the human eye and may only be seen under strong or direct light. Coated surfaces produced using SiO2-based polymers ( Source: Nanocoat 1000 TM ) are food-safe matching with coating specifications. Spray coatings ( 303 Graphene Nanospray ) were found to be ineffective whereas alternative ceramic coatings ( Drexler GlassCoat ) produced robust results. Borosilicate, Pyrex, or similar resistant glass varieties (i.e., with a low percentage of Silica) did not produce any controlled nucleation pattern results due to their material composition.

When carbonated water is poured into the containers, bubbles might get trapped in EGCs. However, hydrophobicity enhances this process (Fig 2c ) by earlier activation of nucleation by increasing the contact angle of bubbles in the EGCs. Small bubbles nucleate first (Fig 2d ), coalescing into each other and becoming large corresponding with the size of the coating on the surface. These large bubbles may become buoyant (Chaos Stage, Fig 4 ) at first due to the saturation level of CO 2 in the liquid. As levels of gas normalize (Stable Stage, Fig 4 ), bubbles stabilize at nucleation sites adhering to the hydrophobic regions with an attractive force [ 11 ]. Such stabilized configurations of bubbles can last for a week or more without energy consumption, with a repeatable process of pouring to bubble image formation. Complex patterns may be fabricated such as patterns with blank areas with no bubbles surrounded by densely ordered bubbles.

In this method, we observed that glass surfaces with minute dirt stains or micro-scratches on them may lead to a noisy output (Fig 6b , bottom) with unwanted bubbles sticking at various points on the surface. Noise usually affects the surrounding parts of the desired pattern without affecting the pattern itself. This is because liquids move from hydrophilic to hydrophobic areas. Cleaning or polishing the dry surface with 70% alcohol using a microfiber cloth led to the removal of these unwanted noise bubbles (Fig 6b , top).

3.1.1 Area Coating vs. Pixelation Bubble Sites. We found that area coating (Fig. 5b )yielded less stabilization (i.e., random-sized bubbles and bubbles bursting or becoming buoyant due to size or collision dynamics between neighbors) in the final bubble patterns versus coating specific points (Fig. 5c ). Our experiments revealed that converting the image into pixelated circles and coating corresponding pixels yielded very high stability and less noise (i.e., bubbles disappearing randomly). Bubbles stabilized at specific points (Fig 5c ) were found to stick to the surface without disturbance for a week, with some size volumetric reduction (Section 4.3) during this time due to gas escape.

3.1.2 Size control of bubbles. Our methods also allow size control of bubbles from 0.5 mm to 6.5 mm (Fig 7 ). This is executed by varying the diameter of the pixelated circles on the mask for corresponding hydrophobic sites. We observed bubbles nucleating, coalescing, and stabilizing to the size of the coating underneath. Sizes above and below our control range showed lesser stability or accuracy in dimension. Such size control of bubbles allows the creation of contrasting images (Fig 7 ). For example, images converted into grayscale have sharp contrast which can be represented by the size of bubbles. Darker areas have high bubble density, whereas lighter areas have low bubble density.

3.2 Heterogenous Wettability Regions on Plastic Substrates using Desktop Plasma

In this alternative method of fabrication, we use a plastic (polystyrene) substrate ( Petridish PS-90, Montaro ) that is normally hydrophobic in nature. We followed a similar workflow as in Section 3.1 for fabrication of the mask, a positive mask that hides the bubbles sites (Fig 3c , Option 2). An illustration software (Adobe Illustrator) or our custom design tool is used to create desired patterns. Copper tape was cut via a plotter ( Cricut Maker 3 ) and a transfer tape is used to transfer the mask on the surface (Fig 8a ). Desktop Vacuum Plasma ( Model: YHS-R, Sakigake ) is turned on for 180 seconds (Fig 8b ). All areas, except the ones masked with the stickers, are exposed during this time. The exposed areas become superhydrophilic in nature (Fig 8c , d) while the areas protected under the mask remain hydrophobic.

This results in mixed wettability regions on the surface that are like the ones produced using ceramic coating (Section 3.1). On pouring carbonated water, bubbles nucleate in the relatively hydrophobic areas leading to controlled patterns (Fig 8d ). It was observed that the hydrophilic nature of exposed material (plastic) gradually wears out in 48 hours, which suggests that a new pattern may be coated on the same surface again. Ceramic coating method, with a longer preparation time of several hours, provides a robust and repeatable use of dishes for several days (tested currently for a week without observable effects on output). Plasma exposure method has a quick turnaround (180 seconds) but also has limits in the reuse of the dishes after 48 hours. While such natural wearing out of a pattern provides an opportunity to reuse dishes for projecting new pattern(s), longer plasma exposure longer may also improve the reusability of a single pattern on the dish.

3.3 Software

Creating images manually by drawing circles in an illustration software, especially for large images with several contrast features may be a cumbersome process. We designed a custom tool based on p5.JS that allows researchers and designers to create such wettability patterns by consolidating image import (color or grayscale), grayscale conversion, and corresponding bubbles pixelation in a single pipeline (Fig 9 ). User-imported images are analyzed against the background.

Our algorithm analyzes the input image to create a comparative brightness map (Fig 9a , 9b), following which darker areas of images are tagged as proportionately dense in bubble density and lighter areas are sparse in bubble density. For easy accessibility, the tool may be used online and outputs a .svg directly compatible with the fabrication machine for cutting the masks. The software allows scaling of the image based on the expected physical output depending on the surface area available for coating.

4 DESIGN SPACE & TECHNICAL EVALUATION

In this section, we clarify the density of nucleated bubbles, their stability, and behavior under various conditions as affected by the liquid, surface tension, temperature, or surrounding environment.

4.1 Bubble Density and Size Accuracy

We define the pitch as the minimum possible distance between bubbles for each bubble to be discrete and not pop randomly. In densely packed areas, we found the pitch through repeated observations to be 200 microns (0.2mm). This is currently limited by the fabrication method of the mask, where attempts to reduce pitch often led to the cutting of physical boundary between circles by the blade of the vinyl cutter. Alternatively, when the mask cutting on pitch < 0.2mm succeeded, this vinyl mask boundary between dense circles would break during the application of the coating, leading to leaks from one bubble boundary into the other. However, such tolerances may be improved using more precise cutting machines.

To evaluate the control over bubble size, we ran tests (n=10) to check bubble sizes against predefined sizes each time after the chaos stage had passed. Ten rows of various bubble sizes were created. Each row started from 0.5mm to 6.5mm with increments of 0.5mm. Bounds were found through prior experiments where attempts to create bubbles smaller than 0.5mm or bigger than 6.5mm yielded significantly low success in terms of bubble stability (bursting or becoming buoyant) or size. Gradations below 0.1mm were not measured for practical purposes. For observations, we used a light panel and placed a diffused paper with gradations over it (Fig 10 ). A transparent glass petri dish was placed over this paper to observe if the bubble had grown to the defined size. Such readings for bubbles that stabilized were taken at t=30 mins. Range of 1.0mm to 5.5mm was found to be most stable with sizes at either extreme i.e. r=0.5mm or r=6.0mm (or higher) to be less accurate in size. In the range of 1 mm - 5.5 mm, the accuracy of bubbles nucleating or growing to the predefined size was found to be between 84% - 99%, with middle sizes such as 3.5mm or 4.0mm bubbles being the most accurate.

4.2 Stabilization Time

Stabilization time of the overall bubble pattern is affected by the following factors: a) Levels of carbonation in a liquid, b) Size of container and c) Amount of carbonated liquid in the same container. Chaos stage is defined as the time after the initial pour when saturation levels of CO 2 are high and are nearing an equalization point. At this stage, bubbles nucleate fast, coalesce, and often pop or become buoyant after which they start to nucleate again at the same position. Bubbles physically removed during the chaos stage reappear in a matter of seconds. We define stabilization time as the time from the initial pour that starts the coalescing of small bubbles to their projected size to ultimately the moment (Fig 11 ) when bubbles on the overall pattern are now sessile in nature. (i.e., not bursting or becoming buoyant). While several factors may affect such stabilization time, we demonstrate variance in stabilization time by increasing the amount of liquid poured into the container. It is observed that the stabilization time increases with the amount of liquid in the container. Low bubble stability is when bubbles do not form (due to their size dynamics as evaluated below), burst or become buoyant during the chaos stage or when bubbles depart from their positions after the stabilization time. High bubble stability is when bubbles stick to corresponding positions for long durations (as expected for several days when undisturbed).

4.3 Bubble Specifications and Liquid Parameters

4.3.1 Carbonation concentration and liquids. Our initial experimental observations revealed that different sparkling beverages had different effects on stabilization time, where some beverages would produce stable bubble patterns faster than others. This is due to the variance of concentration of CO 2 in different liquids. We used a carbonation tester (Fig. 12b , standard instrument protocol available online) to test dissolved CO 2 in several off-the-shelf sparkling beverages (Fig. 12a ) from a nearby market. Pressurized bottles were brought to atmospheric pressure before testing dissolved CO 2 in the liquid.

Subsequently, to find the desired concentration of CO 2 that led to the formation of stable bubbles, we used a carbonation machine ( Terra SodaStream ) to add CO 2 to water. For consistent stream pressure, carbonation was added in discrete amounts by turning on the stream each time for 2 seconds. It was found that 5.81g/L of CO2 (total four seconds of stream) was optimum for the formation of stable patterns. Bubbles were stable for expected long durations as opposed to not forming at all. Carbonation levels above this number extend the stabilization time of bubbles in the pattern, whereas levels below this number showed no formation at all or lesser than expected stability. Such desired (minimum) CO2 concentration may change in pattern conditions with a huge number of bubbles.

4.3.2 Effect on Sugar on Stability. High sugar leads to low surface tension in liquids [ 79 ]. We found experimentally that liquids with high sugar (such as Coke instead of Diet Coke) affected the stability of bubbles, where bubbles would form but easily pop after a short duration (∼10 minutes in a 100 mm petri dish with 50 mL of liquid). To observe the change of bubble formation/stability behavior with sugar, we prepared four batches (50mL each) of water with 0, 20g, 40g, 60g of sugar added respectively. We carbonated this water ( Terra SodaStream ) to the same level and poured it into a 10 mm petri dish to observe bubble formation and stability. It was found that carbonated liquids with high sugar were not able to form stable bubbles (Fig 13 ), with stable bubbles popping faster in high sugar liquids. Zero sugar carbonated formed stable bubbles that continue to be sessile with an observed time of many days.

4.3.3 Effect of Liquid Temperature and Room Temperature. Liquid temperature is important for the breadth of our design space and applications. While all our applications are demonstrated at room temperature (17 o C–26 o C), liquid temperature (between 5°C -20°C) itself was found to be an insignificant factor (Fig 14a , b) in the stabilization of bubbles. An electric cooler ( Azfun, Amazon ) was used to cool the bottled liquid ( Perrier Sparkling ) at four different discrete values measured through a temperature probe ( Kizen, Amazon ). Bubbles produced on the pattern (Sierpinski Triangle in Fig 4a ) after stabilization time were similar (Fig 14a , ∼3% deviation) at all temperature values. As carbonated drinks are often delivered under different temperatures and change when kept at room temperature, such consistency under different temperatures could be helpful in daily applications where patterns don't disappear easily or in a short time.

4.3.4 Stability during movement. As opposed to the temporary nature of bubbles that we are generally familiar with, carbonation bubbles stabilized through our technique are quite steady. In fact, normal manual movements of the containers during their pickup/transfer/use did not lead to bubbles being separated from the surface. To experiment with the surface adhesion of the bubbles, we placed a stabilized bubble pattern on a glass petri dish on top of an orbital shaker ( Thermofisher MaxQ ) at the lowest shake setting (Fig 15 ). We found that after five minutes of shaking on the lowest level (Fig 15a , 15b), some noise bubbles around the pictures disappeared. However, carbonation bubbles that were on the surface due to wettability contrast kept sticking (Fig 15c ) to the surface. Such stability may be a strength in applications noted in Section 5. Increase of the shake setting to a vigorous (Level 4) mid-range (Fig 15d ) led to ∼50% bubbles departure (Fig 15e ).

4.4 Degradation Time

In contrast to the limited lifespan of soap bubbles, vortex air bubbles or vapor during boiling, carbonation bubbles provide longer stability and thus more potential for dynamics on surfaces. During the initial pour on surfaces, CO 2 in the liquid is supersaturated. Nucleation of bubbles is observed on hydrophobic regions with small bubbles coalescing into large bubbles eventually matching the size of the coating. When CO 2 in the liquid reaches an equalization point, bubbles become stable and adhere to the surface. Due to the nature of gases, a very tiny amount of gas escape begins after bubbles have reached stability. Any visible difference in the size of bubbles is only evident in 24 hours. In 72 hours, bubbles still adhere to the surface but shrink in size. We observed that bubbles would continue to be on the surface (Fig 16 a-f), albeit with size reduction, even after one week.

In the experiments above, we reused the dishes with a coated pattern several times. A newly coated dish was used at least 25 times without any effect on the coating and subsequent bubble stabilization. We also washed with mild soap ( Myers Soap ) without any effect on the bubble stabilization on the surface post-wash.

5 APPLICATIONS

Several applications were designed to demonstrate a range of possibilities and relevance to various daily contexts using our fabrication technique. Some examples have a mix of these distinct possibilities:

  1. Control of carbonation bubbles and their specific size at various positions on surfaces (plastic/glass)
  2. Large surface area with thousands of bubbles, with an intermix of dense and sparse areas
  3. Addition of colored dyes to liquids
  4. Bubbles on chemically etched glass to reveal information
  5. Bubbles sticking on vertically oriented or tapered surfaces
  6. Freezing liquid with bubbles maintained in them
  7. Using straws/stirrers to remove bubbles from a dense bubble canvas

5.1 Data Visualization

Base of a glass container 20cm (W) x 20cm (L) x 8cm (H) was etched (Fig. 17a ) using an etching cream ( ArmourEtch, Amazon ) applied over a laser cut mask. After the glass was etched, we created a vinyl mask with bubbles and their positions/sizes representing data on a map. Such visualization is relatively hidden at first and is revealed after the pour (Fig 17a ). Such scenarios and data physicalizing strategies [ 82 ] may be applicable in playful educational contexts to reveal true information after students have decided on an answer. In other scenarios, it is also possible to add layers to such visualization through glass plates that are dipped one after the other.

5.2 QR Code

Micro QR code was generated using a generator ( Qrafter ), encoding alphabets (ACM) in them. This QR code was coated on a glass petri dish (10cm diameter). Carbonated water in the dish is just enough to cover the nucleated large bubbles. A small amount of food dye (Fig. 14b , Fig. 17d ) was added for visual effect and contrast. Since the bubbles stick to the surface of the dish and are not on the surface of the liquid, the contrast between colored liquid and bubbles is visible (Fig. 17b , 18d). Scanning was found to be quite effective and worked 8/10 times on an iPhoneX with an app from the App Store ( Micro QR ). Such QR code or similar signifiers [ 81 ] may initially be hidden in a normally pressurized bottle. However, when a bottle is depressurized, nucleation starts to occur, and a code may then instantly appear on the surface of a bottle. In addition, such QR codes may be used in restaurants where they appear directly on the surface of a glass or a dish after carbonated water is poured instead of having them on a paper menu.

5.3 Patterned Drinking Glass/Tumblers

Prior techniques have a heavy emphasis on electronics while still being limited in terms of possible orientation. Since our fabrication technique is built upon the phenomena of natural nucleation within a liquid, vertical or tapered vertical orientations are possible as well. To this end, heat-resistant 220mL transparent soda-lime glasses ( Kinto 8434, Kinto 8429 ) were coated on the side walls with initials, short words and separately on the base (Fig 17c , e, f). Due to small amounts of liquid and hydrophobic spots, results appeared as soon as the liquid was poured and stabilized in less than five minutes. Several glasses with names of initials may be used in playful scenarios or in group gatherings for each person to identify their drinks during parties and avoid mixing them.

5.4 Large Scale Coatings

Glass containers of 35cm (L) x 35cm (W) x 5.5cm (H) were fabricated using 0.25” thick window glass (HomeDepot, # 92436). Inspired by pointillism and dot painting art, we found portrait images of animals (Fig 17h , Fig 18a , 18b) with high visual contrast in their facial features. We imported these images into our design tool, changed thresholds to capture features and scaled them for physical fabrication. The average amount of bubbles in these large-scale bubble photos was ∼4200 (70*60). The output is exported as .svg and fed into the vinyl cutter (Cricut Maker 3). Cutting time for such large photos takes an average of 60 minutes. Manual coating was performed on glass containers because of their size and material. After pouring sparkling water ( Perrier 750mL ), pictures emerged in a matter of seconds and bubbles stabilized in ∼45 minutes. In ephemeral media and concept art enabled at this scale, the disappearance of bubbles over a long time may not only be helpful to execute without electricity but could also be useful for poetic representations.

5.5 Bubbles in Popsicles

Considering the stability of bubbles in Section 4.4 , we prepared patterns on a food-safe petri dish. Diet Coke was poured into this dish that was used as a mold and later flash-frozen. The alphabets that appeared as bubbles after the initial pour were still in place in the frozen popsicle form (Fig 17g , 18c). Layers of bubbles within frozen forms may contribute to different textures in popsicles and such avenues present more possibilities for food design [ 80 ].

5.6 Multi-layered Side View

Multi-layered slots were created inside a clear acrylic box to hold large microscope (3”x 2”) slides. Carbonation bubbles attached to the surface are an added advantage allowing the creation of layered displays or visualizations. Glass slides ( Benzmicroscope, CA6101 ) were coated with patterns and held on slots inside the box (Fig 17d ). When carbonated water is poured, bubbles nucleate and stabilize on the positions to create shapes. Seeing from the lateral view, an overall shape emerges from the composition of different layers (Fig 17d ). Such a layered approach may allow designers to create 3D bubble compositions as well.

5.7 Bubble Canvas

It is noted that the large containers with thousands of stabilized bubbles may also serve as a bubble canvas. Using a thin brush or directly using fingers, bubbles can be removed to create patterns (Fig 18e ) and may serve as a new form of expression. Similarly, the bottom of the cup is shown as a bubble canvas, where a straw is being used to draw an emoji by removing bubbles (Fig 18f ). Such interaction with bubbles is pleasing and satisfactory while enjoying a drink.

6 FUTURE WORK AND LIMITATIONS

6.1 Streamlining fabrication

Our software currently allows users to import or edit images, draw illustrations, and/or write the text. These are subsequently converted into appropriate pixelation patterns that are used for fabrication on a vinyl cutter/plotter etc. However, instead of using a separate software for fabrication machines, our software could potentially be extended to directly produce toolpath/cutting commands to help streamline the process further.

The density of bubbles on the glass substrate is currently limited by the fabrication of the vinyl mask. Such a mask however could be fabricated with more precision using lasers to increase the density of bubbles.

Fabrication using Desktop Vacuum plasma was found to be an effective technique. We found that hydrophilic patterning projected through plasma lasted for two days before the plastic surface showed its normal hydrophobic properties. Overlaying a new pattern over a previously projected pattern may help in increasing material reusability for the future.

6.2 Material testing and nucleation effectiveness

We believe creating intricate patterns that are activated by bubble nucleation and no electronics is a strength of this fabrication technique. In some cases where refresh capabilities may be desired, hydrophobic polymers may be modified. Such hydrophobic materials may be actuated using electronics not using electronics on the artifacts themselves but perhaps embedding them in table mats etc. on which the objects rest. Electric fields from within such mats could potentially toggle hydrophobicity to then have corresponding nucleation of carbonation bubbles.

This work may have commercial implications for products used in daily life. Accordingly, several new hydrophobic polymers may be tested in the future to improve the nucleation effectiveness further along with more varieties of surfaces. For example, other coatings may reduce the stabilization time of bubble patterns further, surfaces may even be executed using edible hydrophobic coatings [ 62 ] with short cure time and materials such as metals, paper might be possible as well. In addition, retriggering of nucleation on expected sites through liquid refills and the effect of wider temperature settings on nucleation may need to be further studied.

6.3 Experiments on coating degradability

Our current experiments show repetition of bubble patterns (tens of times) appearing on a glass coated with hydrophobic coating. However, long-term experiments with hundreds of repetitions or even further may need to be performed to study coating degradation before commercial use. In addition, the effects of hard detergents, strong direct heat or washing methods may need to be understood further.

To this end, our second method of pattern projection using desktop plasma was promising to reuse a plastic dish to project a different pattern after 48 hours. However, longer plasma exposure (>180 seconds) and its effects on prolonging single-pattern reuse need to be studied further.

6.4 Workshops and user studies for scenarios

While bubbles in popsicles are currently in the form of aesthetics to add name initials of people or branding to popsicles, user studies will be needed to test if experiential effects such as texture difference can be perceived by users. Separately, bubble canvas may add interesting possibilities in kids and adults’ educational workshops and games and multi-layered bubbles may be developed further for 3D displays through their overall composition or through the lens effect of bubbles.

7 CONCLUSION

We presented a fabrication technique that enables the creation of discrete wettability regions on glass and plastic substrates. Surfaces are made heterogeneous in terms of their behaviors with liquids by coating them with a ceramic hydrophobic coating or projecting select regions with plasma. Pouring carbonated liquids on such heterogeneous regions leads to nucleation of CO 2 bubbles at specific points with control of bubble size within the range of 0.5mm-6.5mm. Bubbles adhere to surfaces instead of floating on liquids, are sessile in nature and can be manifested on small/large surface areas including vertical orientations. Our design workflow comprises a custom tool that allows easy conversion of images to pixelation forms suitable for fabrication corresponding to nucleation. Bubble sizes, stabilization after an initial chaos stage and effects of CO 2 concentration, temperature and sugar are evaluated in this paper. Based on these factors, we demonstrate a wide range of applications showcasing strengths of the technique and its applicability to daily life. Bubbles are a whimsical design material with playful associations. Through BubbleTex, we enable such control of bubble media for the design community and enable a rich interplay within liquid media for our daily objects and interactions.
