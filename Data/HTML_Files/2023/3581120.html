Can we crowdsource Tacton similarity perception and metaphor ratings?

High-fidelity vibration actuators in recent mobile phones allow designers to crowdsource user evaluation of vibrotactile (VT) Tactons. Yet, little work has examined whether online crowdsourcing platforms can provide comparable results to lab studies. To address this question, we conducted two studies with iOS devices in the lab and crowdsourced settings. In Study I, 40 users provided pairwise similarity ratings for 12 VT Tactons that varied in their parameters (e.g., duration). In Study II, 40 new users rated pairwise similarities for 14 Tactons representing different metaphors (e.g., heartbeat). They also rated the Tactons’ match to the metaphors. In both studies, the resulting similarities and perceptual spaces strongly correlated in the lab and crowdsourced settings. Furthermore, 60% of the metaphor ratings were statistically equivalent in the two settings. We discuss the results and outline directions for future work on haptic crowdsourcing.

1 INTRODUCTION

Vibrotactile icons (i.e., Tactons) can improve task performance and user experience in a variety of application domains. Previous work has shown the utility of Tactons for immersive interactions in virtual reality (VR) [ 18 , 27 , 38 ], direction notifications in autonomous vehicles [ 7 , 9 ], rehabilitation in medical care [ 2 ], and spatiotemporal information transfer in mobile devices [ 11 , 37 ]. VT Tactons can vary on several parameters such as amplitude, duration, envelop frequency, carrier frequency, and rhythm. The combination of these parameters provides a large design space for various application use cases. With the widespread inclusion of vibrotactile (VT) actuators in mobile phones, designers can create a range of Tactons for notifications [ 21 , 42 ] and game effects [ 24 ] in mobile applications.

Designing Tactons often involves identifying VT patterns that are easy to distinguish and mapping them to the target application information via user studies. Haptic designers usually compile an initial set of VT pattern candidates by controlling vibration parameters [ 10 , 16 , 17 , 39 , 44 ] or using pre-existing VT sources (e.g., libraries). Then, they run user studies to identify a subset of the patterns that are easy to distinguish [ 23 , 26 ] and evaluate how well each VT pattern can represent the target information [ 35 ]. The most common method for identifying distinguishable patterns is to ask users to rate the pairwise similarity of the vibrations, visualize the perceptual structure of the pattern similarities through dimensionality reduction, and select the most distant vibrations in the perceptual space [ 23 , 44 , 45 ]. To link the selected Tactons to the target application information, the designers may ask users to rate the Tactons on relevant words or adjectives. The designers often run these studies in a lab environment where they can control the haptic hardware as well as the user's attention, posture, and other environmental factors during the experiment.

A few VT studies have attempted to crowdsource user evaluation through mobile devices [ 1 , 8 , 35 ]. These studies showed that one can collect a lot of data in a short time frame by using online platforms such as Amazon Mechanical Turk [ 8 ]. Furthermore, they provided evidence that crowdsourced data can obtain similar results to lab studies [ 1 , 35 ]. However, their VT patterns were limited to rhythmic and single-frequency vibrations in Android phones and APIs. In addition, the Android results showed relative inconsistencies for a subset of the VT patterns in comparison to iPhone devices. Haptic designers often need to evaluate Tactons that vary on several parameters or have a complex waveform pattern. Yet, little is known about the viability of crowdsourcing for a diverse set of vibration patterns that vary across all known VT parameters.

We assessed the feasibility of crowdsourcing user evaluation for a diverse set of VT Tactons in two user studies. Grounded in common haptic design practices, we evaluated the viability of crowdsourcing for both systematic parameter-based and metaphor-based Tacton design. We programmed the Tactons on the iPhone series as they provide control over all known VT design parameters. Study I focused on identifying the perceptual space of Tactons that systematically varied in duration, envelope frequency, and carrier frequency. Forty participants rated the pairwise similarities of 12 Tactons in the lab and online settings (20 participants per setting). The pairwise dissimilarities were strongly correlated ( R s = 0.93, p < 0.001), and the resulting perceptual spaces for the Tactons closely matched one another in the two settings. In Study II, we selected 14 Tactons from an existing vibration library, VibViz [ 36 ]. The Tactons represented seven metaphors (e.g., heartbeat) from VibViz. Forty new participants rated the pairwise similarities of the selected Tactons and then rated how well each Tacton represented the seven metaphors. Similar to Study I, the pairwise similarities were strongly correlated ( R s = 0.91, p < 0.001), and the perceptual spaces were similar in the two settings. The metaphor ratings showed higher variation and lower average ratings in the crowdsource setting. Furthermore, while 60% of the metaphor ratings were statistically equivalent, 10% were statically different in the two settings. Our results suggest that pairwise similarity ratings can be crowdsourced with iPhones for a wide range of vibration Tactons, but metaphor ratings require further research that control for various cognitive factors such as participant background, context, and mental imagery. We discuss possible reasons for the results and ways to leverage the power of the lab and crowdsource settings for Tacton design. We contribute the following:

  • Results showing that the similarity of VT Tactons from both systematic parameter-based and metaphor-based design approaches can be evaluated via crowdsourcing.
  • The first comparison of Tacton metaphor ratings in the lab and crowdsourced settings. We provide data revealing similarities and differences in the two settings and discuss factors that may reduce the variations in the future.

2 RELATED WORK

Haptic designers have compiled vibrotactile Tactons of the best information transfer for mapping target application information. One of the representative Tacton selection methods is analyzing perceptual dissimilarities with multidimensional scaling (MDS) and selecting the most distant VT Tactons in their perceptual space. This section reviews the relevant VT Tacton selection studies and several attempts to substitute crowdsourcing platforms in place of lab studies.

2.1 Vibrotactile Pattern Set Compilation

The most classical approach to designing VT Tactons involves the manual coding of vibration parameters or waveforms with low-level interfaces. Relevant research mostly controlled the sinusoidal parameter of a single envelope frequency [ 23 ] or multiple parameters of duration, amplitude, envelope, and frequency [ 44 ]. Other researchers designed Tactons using abstract but salient parameters of rhythm [ 1 , 39 ] or waveform patterns [ 26 ]. This approach enables control of a single-point-correct vibration waveform; therefore, a researcher with a deep understanding of haptics can design accurate Tactons. One negative effect is the large time cost from its hard-to-understand design interface.

To garner the benefits of accuracy with lower cost and difficulty, several attempts reduced the necessary VT Tacton design time by implementing intuitive interfaces. Prior research developed GUI-based interfaces for designing a single VT Tacton for prototype devices [ 33 ] and a web-based GUI editor for better accessibility [ 34 ]. GUIs for multi-actuator pattern design were also proposed for temporal [ 31 ] and spatiotemporal patterns in a helmet interface [ 12 ]. Metaphoric GUIs, such as a musical score [ 16 , 17 ] and user's touch events [ 10 ] were also assessed as easy-to-design interfaces for vibrotactile patterns.

While in the above approaches designers create patterns from scratch, haptic designers may also use large pre-existing sets of Tactons [ 35 , 36 ]. The Tacton sets are usually composed of several VT Tactons that were sorted by experts using subjective ratings and metaphoric labels, providing a way to search for vibrations of interest. The VibViz library [ 36 ] includes 120 vibrations, allowing users to use five search schemes: physical, sensory, emotional, usage examples, and metaphor tags.

2.2 Assessing the Distinguishability

When creating VT Tactons, designers carefully identify a subset from the selected Tactons to associate with target application information. To select the subset, the designers consider how differently the Tactons feel, i.e., perceptual dissimilarity. A well-known design method requires three steps: running a study to collect combinatorial pairwise ratings of Tactons, finding their perceptual space using MDS analysis, and selecting the most distant subset [ 23 , 26 , 32 , 45 ]. The pairwise comparison test requires all pairwise distances in analysis, so its time cost exponentially increases with the number of Tactons. Moreover, completing a large number of comparisons can induce inconsistent scoring criteria due to mental fatigue in users.

An alternative method, cluster-sorted rating, is known to reduce the time cost for a large number of patterns [ 26 ]. In this method, participants group Tactons in the same cluster if the Tactons feel similar. The process is repeated with multiple trials, and pairwise perceptual distances are calculated from their clustering results as discrete and indirect perceptual dissimilarities with a considerably lower study duration. For example, 84 Tactons took 2 hours for cluster-sorted ratings [ 39 ], while pairwise dissimilarity ratings for 8 Tactons took 1.5 hours [ 23 ].

2.3 Crowdsourcing in Haptic Studies

Haptic user studies control for environmental factors by requiring that participants come to a lab. On-site participation enables consistent stimuli and information for accurate perceptual responses with less noise. However, forced face-to-face participation limits the number of concurrent studies, so large-scale haptic studies have been rare in the literature. As a possible substitute to on-site studies, crowdsourcing allows designers to recruit participants over the network [ 5 , 30 ]. The use of crowdsourcing enables parallel user studies and shortens the time frame for data collection, but results from these studies cannot be trusted if the studies can not ensure consistent haptic stimuli.

Three recent studies focused on crowdsourcing using haptic actuators in mobile phones. Schneider et al. ran in-lab and crowdsourced studies with 10 Tactons to test whether low-fidelity Tactons in Android phones can receive ratings (e.g., intensity, speed) in crowdsourced studies similar to ratings for Tactons rendered with high-fidelity actuators in the lab. Yet, the authors reported several inconsistencies between the lab and crowdsourced results [ 35 ]. Demers et al. used crowdsourcing to actively sample Tactons from the design space of possibilities with Android actuators, but the authors did not assess whether the results matched with in-lab data [ 8 ]. Abou Chahine et al. compared lab and crowdsourced results in pairwise similarity ratings on both iPhones and Android phones, but their Tacton design space was limited to 14 Tactons varying on 2 amplitudes and 7 rhythms [ 1 ]. To create Tactons for various applications (e.g., VR, automotive, healthcare), designers often need to evaluate complex Tactons that vary on several parameters or are sourced from existing libraries. Prior work provides initial evidence about the viability of crowdsourcing for simple Tactons, but the results do not adequately address designer needs.

We assessed the response consistency between lab and crowdsourced settings with high-fidelity Tactons designed by all known VT design parameters. We structured our studies around existing designer practices with systematic parameter-based (Study I) and metaphor-based (Study II) Tactons and assessed whether these practices can be supported with haptic crowdsourcing. We ran the studies on iPhones as they provide control over all vibration parameters. User Study II additionally collected seven metaphoric similarities of each VT pattern for assessing consistency in the cognitive mental process.

3 USER STUDY I: EVALUATING TACTONS BY SYSTEMATIC PARAMETER CONTROL

User Study I was designed to evaluate the consistency of crowdsourcing in a pairwise similarity rating of VT Tactons. We designed 12 Tactons by varying VT parameters that were not simultaneously assessed in prior haptic studies, and identically replicated the same experimental procedure in both lab and crowdsourced settings.

3.1 Vibrotactile Pattern Design

The most frequent parameters used in VT Tacton design are amplitude, carrier frequency, duration, envelope modulation frequency, rhythm, and body site [ 4 , 22 , 23 , 39 , 40 , 44 ]. One prior work already reported consistent responses from crowdsourcing using VT Tactons designed by amplitude and rhythm [ 1 ], whereas body site cannot be accurately controlled in crowdsourcing. In a recent paper, Yoo et al. [ 44 ] designed VT Tactons with the three parameters of frequency, duration, and envelope modulation. The authors collected affective ratings in the lab but they did not assess similarity ratings. Therefore, we ran several pilot tests to find perceptually distinct patterns and designed 12 VT Tactons by varying the carrier frequency (80, 155, 230 Hz), duration (200, 300 ms), and envelope modulation frequency (0, 8 Hz).

3.2 Device Configuration

Apple first introduced its Taptic engine in iPhone 6 to support the haptic features of iOS 10’s 3D Touch. After the release of iPhone 8 and iOS 13, the Core Haptics framework was released to allow developers to create their own haptic patterns and render them on iPhone devices of the later 8 series. Currently the Core Haptics framework and Taptic Engine can support all known VT parameters.

In comparison to iPhones, embedded actuators in Android devices range from eccentric rotating motors to wide-band linear resonance actuators, and Android APIs do not yet support frequency control. Also, one prior work already assessed that iPhone devices were relatively more consistent in crowdsourced settings than Android devices at rendering low-fidelity vibrations [ 1 ]. Due to the capability and consistency of our designed Tactons, we chose iPhone devices in both lab and crowdsourced settings.

To render vibrations in iPhone devices, it is necessary to convert the pattern signals to the Apple Haptic and Audio Pattern (AHAP) formatted files. This format is written in a JSON-compatible representation that defines frequency, amplitude, and duration of a sinusoidal vibration. Also, AHAP provides envelope control points that modulate intensities and frequencies of the sinusoidal vibration during the pattern playback.

In the calibration process, we attached an accelerometer (Analog Devices: ADXL354cz) to the back of the iPhone 11 Pro Max and measured 3-axis accelerations of all Tactons while the same experimenter was holding the phone. We tried to match the intensity of the strongest-axis acceleration to the target Tacton waveform by controlling AHAP parameters as shown in Figure 3 . The AHAP format does not support negative envelope so the spectrum is different to the original Tacton varying envelope frequency, but this difference was almost imperceptible in the pilot test when compared to using a voice-coil actuator (Haptuator Mark II; Tactile Labs). Acceleration and spectrum plots of all the Tactons for Study I are provided in the supplemental materials.

3.3 Participants and Procedure

Recruitment of Participants - In both settings, we required the participants to be at least 19 years old and speak English at a B2 level to understand the instructions in the application. We used Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) as a crowdsourcing platform and limited the participation requirement for recruiting quality participants who get approved for more than 5000 tasks with a success rate of 98% or greater. We finally recruited 20 users (6 male, 14 female) with a mean age of 39 years (25-71). Nineteen participants were American and one was Brazilian. Participants’ devices were uniformly distributed from iPhone 8 to iPhone 12, and only five participants reported iPhone 11, which are known to include Taptic Engines capable of rendering our Tactons. The experiment took 10.86 minutes on average, and we paid $ 3 USD as compensation.

In the lab setting, 20 participants (10 male, 10 female) with a mean age of 23.7 years (18-32) were recruited through flyers and online bulletins. All the participants were from the same non-western country. No participants reported any tactile sensory disorders, and they conducted the experiment using the iPhone 11 Pro Max and the same application used by the MTurk participants. To prevent them from hearing any noise from the vibration rendering, they wore noise-canceling headphones playing pink noise. The experiment took 10.52 minutes on average, and we paid 11.5 US dollars for compensation including transportation fees. Both settings were approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB-20220419-HR-66-03-02).

Procedure - We implemented an application consisting of four sessions: initial, training, pairwise comparison of VT Tactons, and feedback and submission. The initial session displayed two survey screens about participants’ demographic information (age, gender, nationality, and use of phone case) and haptic expertise level, an instruction screen on the experimental procedure, and a consent form screen for participation. The instruction screen guided participants with three required actions: to remove their phone case, hold the cell phone with their left hand, and use their right index finger to respond to the experiment.

Next, the training session screen displayed 12 buttons randomly assigned the 12 VT Tactons. Participants clicked a button to feel its corresponding Tacton as many times as they wanted. The NEXT button was enabled after all the vibration buttons were clicked at least once to prevent incomplete training.

In the pairwise comparison session, participants rated perceptual similarity of 66 combinatorial pairs from 12 VT Tactons. We added one additional pair of two identical Tactons ( F80 − E0 − D2000 ) as an attention test. The final 67 pairs were randomized without repetition, and Tactons in a pair were randomized in each trial. On each rating screen, the participants felt two Tactons by clicking on their buttons, and a similarity rating bar was enabled if each button was clicked at least once. Participants evaluated the perceptual similarity of the Tactons between 0 (totally different) and 100 (the same) using the rating bar.

After rating all the pairs, the participants could provide subjective comments in an input textbox by using their keyboards and send the results by clicking SUBMIT button. All results, including demographic data, dissimilarity ratings, time stamps, and subjective comments, were uploaded in a Google Firestore database.

3.4 Results and Discussion

All the participants passed the attention test with ratings higher than 85 points for the identical Tacton pair. We converted the measured similarities to dissimilarities by subtracting them from 100 and averaged them over 20 participants in each setting. Because the perceptual dissimilarities are usually considered as ordinal [ 41 ], we chose two analysis metrics: Spearman's rank correlation coefficient ( ρ ) for showing consistency of ratings between two settings and non-metric multidimensional scaling (nMDS) to visualize the perceptual structure of VT Tactons in each setting. To check if the crowdsourcing participants responded with larger variance, we calculated the averaged standard deviations of pairwise similarities (20.66 ± 6.95, Lab; 24.78 ± 7.71, MTurk), but they were not significantly different. Because of its symmetry, we used only the upper triangular elements in each dissimilarity matrix for calculating the correlation. The Spearman's ρ showed a strong correlation between the pairwise dissimilarity ratings in the lab and MTurk ( R s = 0.93, p << 0.001)). The Spearman's ρ value is interpreted as a strong correlation between two datasets if it exceeds 0.90 [ 6 ]. This result means the ratings from the participants in the crowdsourced setting were in line with the ratings from the lab participants.

We further verified the consistency of the two settings by visualizing perceptual spaces using nMDS analysis. First, we calculated Kruskal's stress values for every dimension in each setting, where a stress value below 0.05 is considered as the good fitting dimension with small errors [ 13 ]. The stress values showed that two and three dimensions fitted well in lab and MTurk settings, with respective stress values of 0.028 and 0.027. For a fair comparison, we selected three dimensions in both settings and plotted them in 2D plots in Figure 4 . One of the most notable observations is that three VT parameters were aligned with the dimensions clearly: D1 for envelope frequency (0, 8 Hz), D2 for duration (300, 2000 ms), and D3 for carrier frequency (80, 155, 230 Hz). These dimensional properties were consistent between the two settings.

Also, we collected subjective responses submitted in the feedback session. Seven out of twenty participants left comments and consisting of four requests of providing perceptual difference rating guidelines, two reports of their rating tendency, one subjective feeling to the Tacton feedback. One notable comment was Rating criteria became clear while the pairwise ratings proceed, so I'm not confident about the early trial ratings . A total of four participants had a similar comment. Eleven out of twenty MTurk participants left two requests for the rating guidelines while the other nine comments were simple goodbyes.

In conclusion, a strong correlation of perceptual dissimilarities and almost identical perceptual spaces showed that crowdsourcing is a viable option for estimating perceptual similarities of systematically designed VT Tactons. Also, this consistency was maintained between environments that have participant groups of different ages (39 vs. 23.7 years old) and nationalities (US vs. non-western country). Since the pairwise dissimilarity rating directly compares only two tactile sensations in a pair, these findings imply the possibility that the results of perceptual tasks may not be negatively affected in crowdsourcing.

4 USER STUDY II: EVALUATING TACTONS FOR TARGET METAPHORS

User Study I supported our prediction that crowdsourcing may not significantly interfere with perceptual tasks. This assumption, however, was derived from the 12 systematic VT Tactons representing a part of the VT design space. Therefore, we designed User Study II to assess the consistency of the responses in the pairwise similarity ratings for complex VT Tactons in both settings. The complex VT Tactons were compiled from the VibViz library using seven metaphoric labels. Also, we added a metaphor similarity rating task as a probe to cognitive task consistency.

4.1 Vibration Stimuli and Metaphors

We compiled 14 VT Tactons using metaphor searching functions in the VibViz library [ 36 ]. VibViz includes 120 VT Tactons with their user-annotated metaphors, and we selected the most distant seven metaphors in the metaphoric space, namely Alarm , Animal , Engine , Heartbeat , Jumping , Walking , and Tapping . Two Tactons were chosen for each metaphor, with the aim of overlapping as little as possible with other metaphors (Figure 5 ). We limited the number of Tactons and metaphors to fit the maximum experiment duration of one hour.

The iPhone series were also chosen for their haptic capability, as in User Study I, and the Tactons should be converted to AHAP files. In contrast to the Tactons in Study I, the compiled 14 Tactons were not describable with systematic VT parameters. Thus, we iteratively modified the corresponding AHAP files to generate similar accelerations to the waveform of the original 14 Tactons. In the first step, we used Lofelt Studio [ 19 ] to convert the VibViz wave files to AHAP files. We played the resulting vibrations on an iPhone 11 Pro Max and measured their accelerations at the strongest axis using a 3-axis accelerometer (Analog Devices; ADXL354cz) attached to the back of the iPhone. By comparing the waveform, spectrum, and spectrogram of the Tactons and the corresponding accelerations, we iteratively modified the AHAP files to make the accelerations resemble the target VibViz wave file as much as possible. Figure 6 shows the calibrated accelerations of the Alarm1 Tacton that we measured with the same procedure as described in Section 3.2 (see the supplemental materials for plots of all the Tactons).

4.2 Participants and Procedure

Participants - We recruited a total of 40 participants in the lab and MTurk settings. In the MTurk setting, 20 participants (11 male, 9 female) with a mean age of 35.7 years (23-50) finished the experiment. All the participants had completed over 5000 hits on the MTurk platform with a success rate of 98% or greater. The participants reported their nationality as USA (16), India (2), Brazil (1), and Columbia (1). Participants’ devices were uniformly distributed from iPhone 8 to iPhone 13, and the most frequent devices were iPhone X and iPhone 13 with three occurrences each. All of them were known as capable to render the designed Tactons. The participants took 24.15 minutes on average in the experiment and received $ 4.5 USD in compensation. The lower compensation rate in this setting is consistent with prior work [ 1 , 8 ]. This rate reflects the flexibility of MTurk participants to multitask and the absence of commute time to the experiment location.

In the lab setting, we recruited 20 participants (14 male, 6 female) with a mean age of 21.95 years (18-28) through flyers and bulletin boards at the authors’ university. All the participants were from the same non-western country. To complete the experiment, the participants used the same iPhone 11 Pro Max. The study took 20.13 minutes on average and the participants were compensated with $ 15 USD. As in Study I, both settings were approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB-20220419-HR-66-03-02).

Procedure - We modified the iOS application of User Study I to five sessions: initial, training, pairwise comparison, metaphoric similarity rating, and feedback and submission. The metaphoric rating session was newly added. The other four sessions were designed similarly to User Study I using 14 complex VT Tactons. For the attention test, we used the identical pair of the Animal 2 Tacton.

After completing the pairwise similarity ratings, an instruction screen for the metaphor similarity rating task was displayed. Until the metaphor instruction screen, participants were not exposed to the metaphor words. The following screens showed a button assigned to one of the 14 VT Tactons and seven sliders displaying seven target metaphors. The participants needed to feel the Tacton at least once and rate how well it matched each of the seven metaphors between 0 (totally different) and 100 (same). After providing all the seven similarity ratings, they could proceed to the next Tacton. The metaphoric rating also included an attention test that generated a 1-second silent Tacton for the button click. To pass the attention test, the participants were instructed to rate all metaphors below 5 out of 100. The representation order of 14 vibrations and seven sliders was randomized for each participant.

4.3 Results and Discussion

In both settings, all the participants passed the attention tests. We present the results of the pairwise similarity rating task followed by the metaphor rating task. In both cases, our analysis focused on whether the results from the MTurk setting matched the lab setting outcomes. Prior to the main analysis, we calculated the averaged standard deviations for comparing the response noise. The standard deviations for pairwise similarities (20.51 ± 4.63, Lab; 21.02 ± 5.12, MTurk) and metaphor ratings (29.88 ± 3.03, Lab; 32.33 ± 4.47, MTurk) showed no significant differences in the two settings.

Pairwise Similarity Ratings - We created two dissimilarity matrices from the lab and MTurk data and computed their correlations using the upper-triangular values as in User Study I. The Spearman's ρ showed a very strong correspondence ( R s = 0.91, p << 0.001) between the data from the two settings. This coefficient is slightly lower than the coefficient in User Study 1 ( R s = 0.93), but the value is still higher than 0.90 indicating the strong consistency of the crowdsourced perceptual similarity ratings.

To clearly and qualitatively determine the consistency, we visualized the perceptual spaces for the two matrices using nMDS. We used the 0.05 goodness-of-fit threshold similar to User Study I and obtained four-dimensional solutions for both the lab and MTurk settings with respective stress values of 0.032 and 0.049.

Four-dimensional coordinates were represented in two plots of D1 vs. D2 and D3 and D4 in each setting in Figure 7 , and we noticed similar perceptual structures emerging from the lab and MTurk data. Along the first two dimensions (D1/D2), the spatial configuration of the VT Tactons was largely consistent between the two settings. The Tacton configuration along the third and fourth dimensions (D3/D4) in the two settings was consistent for the majority of VT patterns but differed for a few patterns. Specifically, Heartbeat 1, Heartbeat 2, Walking 1, and Walking 2 were close to one another along D3/D4 in both the lab and MTurk plots. Similarly, the spatial relationship of Tapping 1, Jumping 1, and Jumping 2 as well as Engine 2, Animal 2, Tapping 2, and Alarm 2 were comparable in both spaces. In contrast, the locations of Animal 1 and Alarm 1 in relation to other patterns differed in the two plots. Furthermore, Alarm 1 and Tapping 2 were located farther from the rest of the patterns along D4 in the lab plot. Overall, the two perceptual spaces showed consistent configurations with a few differences.

Metaphor Ratings - We calculated summary statistics and applied equivalence testing to metaphoric similarity data in both settings. Overall, the metaphor similarities from the MTurk setting had a lower mean and higher standard deviation ( M = 31.3, Std = 32.3) than the lab similarities ( M = 38.7, Std = 29.9).

We followed the procedure in Schneider et al. [ 35 ] to test whether the metaphor ratings from both settings are statistically equivalent. The equivalence testing is a technique that tests whether two sample means are within a threshold of δ against the null hypothesis of being outside this threshold. In other words, one can test whether the means are equivalent within an error threshold (i.e., δ ). The error threshold is defined based on the domain. Similar to Schneider et al., we first calculated the least square means of the metaphor ratings for each Tacton and setting. The 95% confidence interval (CI) of the means for the lab data ranged between 27.7 ( Heartbeat ratings) and 28.8 ( Animal / Jumping ). The estimate of the sample means cannot be more accurate than the lab ratings where we could control the haptic hardware and the experimental environment (i.e., the lab setting results were considered as the gold standard). Thus, we set the error threshold for the equivalence testing ( δ ) to be the same as the 95% confidence intervals from the lab results. The equivalence testing procedure determined if the mean ratings for each Tacton from both settings are statistically equivalent . We also performed typical hypothesis testing to examine the statistical difference between the ratings in the two settings. The results indicated whether the metaphor ratings from the lab and MTurk experiments for each vibration were statistically different , uncertain , or equivalent .

Figure 8 shows the results of the equivalence testing on the metaphor ratings. Each data point presents its corresponding least square mean using a dot, its confidence interval (CI) with the whisker heights, and the results of the equivalence testing with its color. A notable subset (0%) of the metaphor ratings were statistically equivalent (dark green) across the lab and MTurk settings. The Tactons with the highest number of equivalent ratings across the two settings were Walking 1 with 6 (out of 7) equivalent metaphor ratings, followed by Alarm 2, Animal 1, Jumping 1, Walking 2, and Engine 1 with 5 equivalent metaphor ratings. A subset of the metaphor ratings was statistically different (0%). The Tactons with the most statistical differences included Alarm 1 and Engine 2 with 2 (out of 7) statistically different metaphor ratings, followed by Animal 2, Engine 1, Heartbeat 1, Heartbeat 2, Tapping 1, and Tapping 2 with 1 different rating. In the majority of cases with statistical differences, the lab participants provided higher ratings for a given metaphoric word. This observation matched the lower overall mean of the metaphor ratings in the MTurk setting. Only for the Tacton Heartbeat 2 did, the MTurk participants provided a higher rating for Walking than the lab participants. The reason for this exception is unclear. The results of the statistical test were uncertain for the remaining ratings (0%), meaning that equivalence or difference cannot be established from the current samples.

As in User Study I, we asked for subjective comments in the last screen. In the lab setting, only one participant left a comment but it was a simple good-bye comment. Meanwhile, five out of twenty MTurk participants left comments, but only one comment was valid as saying, I felt two Tactons were identical with different lengths .

Overall, these results suggest that the metaphor ratings were statistically equivalent in 60% of the cases, the results are statistically different or uncertain for 40% of cases. While the perceptual spaces vary to some extent, crowdsourcing appears to be an appropriate and effective method of data collection for pairwise dissimilarity experiments with complex VT Tactons created via a metaphor-based design approach. This is evident from the high correlation between the dissimilarity matrices and the similarity of the perceptual spaces.

5 DISCUSSION

Our work enables crowdsourced studies for Tacton similarity perception. Haptic designers often need to evaluate Tactons that vary on several parameters or have a complex waveform. Yet, prior haptics crowdsourcing studies provide data on a small set of Tactons (e.g., 7 rhythms, 2 amplitudes). Our studies are grounded in common haptic design practices and proved the viability of crowdsourcing similarity perception studies for both systematic and metaphor-based Tactons. Thus, this paper provides an enabling contribution to haptic crowdsourcing that sets it apart from prior work and has the potential to support evaluation practices for many HCI researchers who experiment with VT Tactons. Also, we present the first comparison of metaphor ratings in the lab and crowdsourced settings. Rating vibrations on words and metaphors are common in the lab, but the literature lacks data on haptic crowdsourcing for metaphors. We provide results showing both similarities and differences in the two settings and discuss factors that may reduce the variations. Below, we discuss our results on the viability of crowdsourcing in haptic studies and present implications for future work based on our findings.

5.1 Viability of Crowdsourcing: Perceptual Similarity and Metaphoric Ratings

The lab and MTurk participants varied in their cultural backgrounds, mobile phones they used for rendering the VT Tactons, and environmental factors. The participants for the lab setting in both studies were from a non-western country. They completed the experiments with the same iPhone 11 Pro Max, without any phone case. Furthermore, the lab studies controlled for all other environmental factors, such as the vibration sound and other distractions. In contrast, the majority of MTurk participants were from the USA (n= 19 in Study I, n= 16 in Study II). They used a variety of iPhone devices to complete the experiments, and we had no control over or data on their environments. All of these factors are considered to impact haptic perception and task performance over two user studies, yet their relative impact is unclear in the literature.

Researchers can use Amazon MTurk to quickly identify the most distinguishable patterns in a set of VT Tacton. The VT Tacton in our studies had a diverse range of design parameters. They included systematic variations in the most prominent VT parameters as well as Tacton with complex waveforms. Data from both studies showed strong correlations ( ρ > 0.93, p < 0.001; ρ > 0.91, p < 0.001) between the lab and MTurk conditions. Furthermore, the perceptual spaces from nMDS analysis showed consistent Tacton configurations across the two settings. The participant group differences and the crowdsourcing had little effect on the outcomes of pairwise similarity ratings. One notable issue is the lack of technical specifications on Taptic engines in various iPhones. The participants in our study used various phone models ranging from iPhone 8 to 13, and the results showed high consistency with the lab results using iPhone 11 Pro Max. Thus, we anticipate that crowdsourcing on various iPhones from series 8 to 13 can provide similar results to lab data for pairwise ratings. For future iPhone models, researchers can collect acceleration measurements from the new iPhone models with their Taptic engines and compare them to data from a prior model (e.g., iPhone 11 Pro Max) to judge the degree of similarity for future phones.

In contrast, metaphor ratings may require a more controlled study design to obtain consistent results in the lab and MTurk settings.

Our results suggested that 60% of metaphor ratings were statistically equivalent while 10% of them were different. High-level cognitive ratings can depend on many factors such as the participant demographics and background (e.g., gender, age, culture), context, and study design. We conjecture that a metaphor rating estimates the similarity between a Tacton and the reminiscent sensory imagery (e.g., visuals, sounds) which can be influenced by the above factors. The metaphor rating interface in our study provided seven words on the screen, allowing the participants to freely imagine the metaphor. The variation in metaphor ratings may be reduced by controlling for the user demographics (e.g., nationality, age, gender), providing context information (e.g., usage scenarios), or adjusting for sensory imagery (e.g., visual icons or Earcons). The latter approach would be similar to the effective use of visual icons in the self-assessment manikin (SAM) scale for emotion ratings [ 3 ]. To assess the impact of participant background, we conducted a preliminary test on the effect of gender in metaphor ratings. We ran separate equivalence tests on metaphor ratings from male and female participants in Study II. Our preliminary results suggest that gender may be an important factor for metaphor ratings. We do not include the preliminary analysis in the paper due to the imbalanced sample sizes, the low number of female participants, and the need for further verification in future studies. Instead, we provide the initial gender results in the supplemental materials. Future studies controlling for the above factors may obtain more consistent results between the lab and crowdsourced settings.

One interesting result of our metaphor ratings was the low values of the estimated least means of squares. Except for several cases including Tapping1 Tacton to Jumping metaphor rating, most of the estimations are below 50 which implies the low perceptual matching to metaphors regardless of consistency between two settings. This result might come from the low exposure to abstract metaphors without detailed descriptions; the participants faced the metaphoric words after the pairwise ratings without prior descriptions. One relevant work reported high least means [ 35 ] to measures of speed, duration, energy, roughness, urgency, and pleasantness, which were less abstract than our seven metaphors.

5.2 Implications for Future Work

Our results inform future work on VT Tacton design and research. Specifically, future studies can build on our methods to evaluate the viability of crowdsourcing for other haptic experimental tasks and consumer devices. In addition, they can use our results to build large-scale datasets for haptics.

Future work can further compare crowdsourced and lab data for a range of perceptual, affective, and metaphor ratings. Our work focused on metaphor ratings since our selection of the VT Tacton set was based on metaphors in the VibViz library [ 36 ]. Yet, metaphor ratings are sensitive to the participant's backgrounds. Previous work examined user ratings for a vibration's duration, energy, or speed, but with Android phones. Compared to metaphors, these ratings mainly rely on the perceptual assessment of the vibrations and may be less sensitive to the participant's backgrounds. Thus, they could be the next target for assessing the feasibility of crowdsourcing with iPhone devices. If validated, the researchers can move to test the feasibility of using online platforms for affective ratings such as pleasantness and urgency as well as a variety of metaphor ratings while controlling for the participant demographics and other cognitive factors as described in the previous section.

Researchers can build on our results to collect a large dataset of Tacton similarities using crowdsourcing and develop models of VT similarity. The design space of possible VT Tactons is large. A model of Tacton similarity allows researchers to efficiently sample this space for the most salient VT patterns. Some studies have proposed objective metrics of VT similarities between 3D and 1D vibration signals captured from the same texture [ 14 , 15 , 25 ]. Yet, validating and extending these models to a variety of VT patterns require large data. Given the promising results in our studies, haptic researchers can crowdsource the collection of a large dataset at a faster rate and lower cost than in the lab. Similarity data from multiple small sets of VT patterns can be combined to create a large dataset [ 20 ]. Furthermore, techniques such as active sampling can be used to further improve the efficiency of data collection via crowdsourcing [ 8 ]. The resulting dataset can help train and validate machine learning or neurologically inspired models of tactile similarity.

Future studies can investigate the validity of crowdsourcing for other consumer haptic devices. The most immediate use case would be haptic design for virtual reality (VR) and game controllers. Researchers have validated crowdsourcing for visual interactions in VR using platforms such as Prolific [ 28 ] and XRDRN [ 43 ]. VR controllers can provide VT feedback, but little work has examined VT playback across different controllers and the impact of user posture and movement on the perception of vibrations. Such a study can open new avenues for designing and evaluating VT effects in VR and complement recent efforts in defining a framework for remote VR studies [ 29 ].

6 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we assessed the response consistency between lab and crowdsourced settings with high-fidelity vibrotactile Tactons. We compiled two Tacton sets from systematic control of VT parameters in User Study I and metaphoric selection from a large-scale Tacton library in User Study II. We chose the iPhone devices in both studies to guarantee the consistent VT Tacton rendering and iteratively converted two Tacton sets to the iOS AHAP format files. To evaluate the consistency of crowdsourcing, we measured pairwise similarity ratings in two user studies using the converted Tacton sets. Our results showed that perceptual dissimilarities were highly correlated between two settings in the systematic Tacton set ( ρ = 0.93) and the metaphoric Tacton set ( ρ = 0.91) while keeping similar perceptual spaces. Therefore, crowdsourcing was consistent in the pairwise-similarity rating task and implied the strong potential of crowdsourcing in perceptual tasks. These results enable haptic designers and many HCI researcher who experiment with haptics to use crowdsourcing to select a perceptually-salient subset of rich Tactons for their target applications.

To extend our assumption of crowdsourcing consistency in cognitive tasks, we added metaphoric similarity ratings in User Study II. The results showed that 60% of metaphor ratings were statistically identical, 10% were statically different, and 30% were statistically uncertain between the two settings. While metaphor ratings were not sufficiently consistent in our studies, our results open new directions and questions for future work to investigate the impact of various factors on variability in cognitive ratings for haptic stimuli.
