Postpartum depression (PPD) is one of the most prevalent mental health disorder following childbirth. Mothers utilize social media and online forums throughout postpartum to seek mental health support. In this paper, we aim to gain a comprehensive understanding of the topics of naturally occurring online discussions and the online social support dynamics associated with PPD. We qualitatively analyze posts and comments from three postpartum depression support communities corresponding to well-known online social platforms (Reddit, What to expect, and Babycenter). We discovered that the three communities share common themes of discussion that include the causes, stressors, symptoms, and coping mechanisms for PPD. Seeking both informational and emotional support through venting and storytelling was prevalent, and emotional support seeking was more common in Reddit than the other two communities. We provide recommendations for future postpartum depression research and design considerations based on pervasive help-seeking approaches.

1 INTRODUCTION

Postpartum depression (PPD) is a type of depression that happens after someone gives birth, and it is often brought on by a combination of hormonal changes, psychological adjustment to motherhood, and weariness [ 28 ]. Symptoms of PPD, inter alia, might include insomnia, loss of appetite, intense irritability, and difficulty bonding with the baby. It is the most discussed perinatal mental health disorder with a high prevalence all over the world [ 70 ]. Approximately one in seven women in the United States are diagnosed with PPD [ 1 ]. Postpartum mental health problems, if left untreated, can damage mothers as well as their children's health and development [ 11 ]. Also, over a 5-year period [ 74 ], women who have had postpartum depression are twice as likely to develop recurrent episodes of depression. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists reported that up to 25% [ 45 ] of women have postpartum depression (PPD) within the first year following childbirth, and more than half of the cases are estimated to remain undiagnosed. Societal standards regarding appropriate mothering and practical problems in getting treatment with a baby may operate as distinct obstacles to accessing mental health care [ 7 ]. Limited identification of symptoms by the woman, her family, and healthcare professionals is a considerable barrier to receiving postpartum mental health care, even in many high-income countries [ 50 , 97 ]. In resource-poor nations, on the other hand, PPD is more widespread and related to higher morbidity for mothers and children [ 50 ].

Social support is considered one of the most vital aspects for managing illnesses, maintaining sound health, or even overcoming chronic diseases such as cancer [ 55 , 71 ]. Social support, like many other mental health difficulties, plays an important role in managing with postpartum depression symptoms [ 12 , 70 , 80 , 89 , 99 ]. Women without adequate social support during pregnancy and the postpartum period are ଁve times more likely to experience PPD [ 24 , 89 ]. On the other hand, welcomed social and peer support are considered a preventive strategy for PPD among at risk women and provide them with various mental health benefits [ 34 ]. Women in postpartum primarily expect social support from their partners and family members [ 76 ]. Furthermore, with the rise of online social media platforms and health communities, new mothers have started to discuss multiple pregnancy-related difficulties online (through Facebook, Twitter, Reddit, online PPD peer-support groups, and blogs). The use of various online forums for sharing perinatal mental health issues seems promising, as they are usually free of cost, available 24/7, and allow women to receive information and validation from others with similar experiences [ 35 ]. Many online groups’ anonymous (or pseudonymous) nature makes these women dealing with health-related stigmatization feel safe and creates an environment for open and honest discussion and opinions that others may perceive negatively. Social support obtained from these platforms can affect women positively throughout the postpartum period and motivate them to fight against depression. A few recent studies leverage online content shared in PPD communities to understand how clinicians and nurses can better support new mothers’ needs [ 8 , 10 ].

Identifying support needs and expectations of new mothers is important for their physical and emotional recovery after childbirth. Online communities for PPD offer researchers a rich source of data to understand these needs from content created by users with diverse demographic backgrounds. In HCI, CSCW, and health informatics, researchers have leveraged data from online mental health communities and social media to gain insights on the ecosystem of technology use [ 22 , 47 , 86 ]. They also characterize [ 22 , 47 , 86 ] online social support to capture users’ attitudes and preferences towards self-disclosure and anonymity for the automatic detection of mental health illnesses. In the context of online PPD communities, research has focused on classifying support types in different public and private platforms [ 35 , 93 ]. Those studies also focus on understanding the acceptability and feasibility of participating in these communities, investigating the accuracy and effectiveness of applying automated machine learning and natural language processing techniques to characterize the themes of discussion or to predict postpartum mental illnesses [ 31 ].

The psychological and psychiatric literature primarily focus on the facilitation of offline social support for PPD, while the HCI/health informatics literature on online support groups show numerous advantages of online social support for different mental health conditions. We contribute to bridge the gap in these two domains by surfacing how the support dynamics in online communities can be leveraged to facilitate offline support. We aim to conduct a systematic inquiry of multiple online PPD communities to gain a comprehensive understanding of women's nuanced support needs during postpartum. Contents from online PPD communities can illustrate the key themes regarding women's concerns after childbirth and increase our understanding of the strategies they use to elicit support. The findings may inform the redesign of healthcare interventions better suited for them. In this work, we aim to gain a holistic understanding of online social support dynamics for PPD by answering the following research questions:

  • RQ1: What are the key topics of discussion in the online PPD communities?
  • RQ2: What kinds of social support are sought and provided in the posts and comments of the online PPD communities?

We approach these questions by a qualitative thematic analysis of the textual content of the posts and comments from three prevalent online support groups for postpartum depression. We found out that women use these communities both for venting, and for seeking different PPD related help, ranging from dosage of medication, bonding with baby, validation about their feelings, and so on. The social support dynamics is different for different communities. The contribution of this work is different from other psychiatric/ psychological studies on analyzing online PPD communities, as they either focus on only one community, or primarily provide implications for specific health promotion strategies that clinicians can readily incorporate into antepartum and postpartum care [ 36 ]. On the contrary, we conduct a cross-community study to increase the generalizability of our findings and provide specific implications for researchers, designers, and policy makers of online PPD communities and similar online PPD intervention platforms. We contribute a nuanced understanding of the topics of naturally occurring online discussions related to PPD and the application of a social support model to understand the online PPD support ecology. In our discussion, we outline the implications for online health community researchers and policymakers, as well as opportunities to integrate storytelling and automatic crisis detection techniques to provide timely support for PPD.

We begin by situating our work in the context of previous research on offline and online sources of social support during postpartum period, and existing sociotechnical systems developed for supporting the needs of new mothers. Next, we discuss the methods for data collection and analysis and report the results of our investigation. Findings include descriptive statistics of the users, posts, comments, and three key discussion topics: stressors, symptoms, ways to cope up, and the online social support dynamics. We conclude with a discussion providing implications for PPD researchers, community designers, and health policy makers.

2 RELATED WORK

We discuss offline and online postpartum mental health support needs as illustrated in previous work. Additionally, we provide an overview of the research landscape on sociotechnical solutions and online health communities for PPD.

2.1 Social Support During Postpartum Period: Benefits, Sources, and Barriers

Research on maternal mental health indicates that social support is effective in helping women cope with various psychological stressors and illnesses during postpartum [ 42 , 56 , 73 ], and this applies to women irrespective of the sociocultural differences [ 23 , 56 ]. According to the theoretical framework of social support constructed by Leahi-Warren et al. [ 63 ], social support is one of the influencing factors that help predict and reduce the risk of postpartum depression. Access to emotional, tangible, and informational support and social interactions has been linked to better maternal-child health outcomes as well [ 46 , 76 , 85 ]. New mothers value instrumental help the most immediately after childbirth; spouses were most frequently listed by mothers as sources of support: both for emotional and instrumental support. Closeness to husband and baby were also found to be related to feeling supported in various life situations [ 92 ]. Close family members were also identified as sources of instrumental support in many studies [ 85 ]. Women perceived emotional support as being able to talk to someone about what they were going through, receiving words of encouragement, and pampering. Many women rely on girlfriends, cousins, godmothers, and other new mothers from in-person support groups to talk about their feelings and experiences [ 47 ]. Spiritual support, such as prayer and self-reflection, also helped [ 76 ] cope with negative feelings. Participants in prior studies believed that support from various sources should be provided without asking, and thus, there is a gap in expected and received support for new mothers [ 85 ].

Researchers have previously used the Health-Belief model to identify the barriers that prevent women from seeking psychological help for symptoms of PPD [ 45 ], underscoring the lack of knowledge about the prevalence, symptoms, and treatment of PPD as the top reason for not seeking help [ 39 , 53 , 60 ]. Perceived stigma is another significant reason; stigma associated with PPD is twofold, women are stigmatized for having a mental illness and for not meeting society's expectations of a good mother [ 72 , 83 ]. Researchers pointed out that new mothers even avoid mentioning PPD related symptoms to doctors in the fear that their babies would be taken away from them [ 15 ]. Women often believe that their healthcare providers avoided topics related to symptoms and screening of PPD due to their lack of knowledge or training, discouraging patients from mentioning their conditions [ 17 , 52 , 69 ].

2.2 Online Social Support for Mental Health

With the emergence of technology for healthcare support, patients frequently seek health-related resources, information, and support online through social media and online health communities [ 33 , 64 , 90 ]. Online communities or support groups dedicated to mental health illnesses, such as depression, PTSD (Post-traumatic stress disorder), ADHD (Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder), and others, allow patients to exchange social support through communicating with each other via posts, comments, blogs, forums, or direct messages [ 40 , 81 , 87 ]. These groups benefit mental health patients subjected to stigma and other social barriers to treatment [ 81 ]. In a meta-analysis [ 88 ] of computer-mediated support groups, it has been highlighted that ongoing membership in these communities was linked to lower levels of depression, higher quality of life, and increased self-efficacy in managing one's mental health difficulties. Similarly, De Choudhury et al. [ 32 ] assessed how effectively receiving help from online mental health communities lessens depression and enhances mental wellness. Research focusing on the analysis of content from these communities identified that positive comments reinforce posting more in online mental health communities. Users who obtain social support on their first post are more inclined to post again and provide support for others [ 22 ]. Another body of work utilized textual and visual content to predict mental health state [ 18 , 31 ], to assess the quality of support received. Researchers also explored members’ perceived sense of virtual community [ 86 , 91 ] to evaluate the effect of factors like anonymity [ 25 ], self-disclosure [ 25 , 82 ], culture [ 84 ] on supportive interactions, and to provide taxonomies of different kinds of support [ 86 , 91 ]. This body of work highlighted the importance of gaining an understanding of the dynamics and processes of social support in order to provide an supportive environment to foster interactions.

2.3 Online Communities and Support Groups for Postpartum Depression

Women's support-seeking activities during postpartum recently expanded to online communities and support groups that provide advice and insights on parenting and dealing strategies for PPD and postpartum anxiety (PPA) [ 103 ]. In addition to gaining understanding of women's offline support-seeking behavior, researchers have employed different techniques to utilize online traces of users in PPD communities. Online forums can facilitate sharing of information, act as a source of entertainment, and provide a sense of community for participants who might otherwise feel isolated due to the demands of new motherhood [ 95 ]. Research suggests that the users of the PPD online support groups experience significant improvements along the three dimensions in the recovery process: symptoms, life satisfaction, and self-esteem. The anonymity of online support groups is particularly appealing to individuals diagnosed with a stigmatized disorder like PPD [ 6 ].

While plenty of research points to the benefits of adequate social support during postpartum, and the potential of analyzing online discussions in mental health communities, little research connects these two in the context of postpartum depression. A small body of work analyzed social media data from Facebook, Twitter, Reddit, and so forth. for characterization of predictors of PPD, identification of changes in emotions expressed following childbirth, and determining factors contributing to various levels of responsiveness and engagement online, etc. [ 37 ]. Research effort for detecting and predicting onset of PPD using emotion and linguistic style on Facebook posts showed that increased social isolation and lowered availability of social capital on Facebook are the best predictors of PPD in mothers [ 31 ]. Automated extraction of the main topics discussed among PPD forum users includes exposures (e.g., medical treatment, non-medical treatment), phenotypes (e.g., symptoms, clinical features), health conditions (e.g., present health condition, previous health condition), behaviors (e.g., social behavior, individual behavior), and timing (e.g., timing of symptoms) [ 26 , 48 ]. Additionally, online anonymous platforms have been shown to encourage information sharing, serve as a source of enjoyment, and provide members who might otherwise feel alone owing to the rigors of new parenthood a feeling of community [ 95 ]. A thorough analysis of the topics of discussion and the types of social support commonly exchanged in online communities can allow researchers gain a comprehensive understanding of women's mental health needs and inform the design of sociotechnical solutions responsive to those needs. However, previous research has also shown that each community's norms [ 19 ], affordances [ 44 ] and features [ 66 ] often influence those community participants’ recurring online discussions in online mental health communities. For example, different modes of communication (e.g., text vs multimedia) influence the social support dynamics and member engagement [ 100 ]. Chancellor et al. [ 19 ] discovered that diverse online communities demonstrate similarities in their linguistic content. Exploring the context of these linguistic cues as norms reveal significantly divergent behaviors despite the groups’ supposedly same purpose. Moreover, members’ perceived social identity and extrinsic and intrinsic motivations largely influence support seeking and sharing behaviors in online discussion communities, which in turn affect satisfaction within the community [ 21 , 61 ]. These findings led us to consider a cross-community research study including three different PPD support groups to gain a holistic understanding of the online social support dynamics for PPD.

2.4 Technologies for Postpartum Mental Health and Wellbeing

Although there are significant barriers for new mothers to seek help for PPD, studies have found out positive experience and impact of using technology for postpartum wellbeing. Many technological interventions have been developed over the past years to address women's depression, anxiety, baby blues, stress, insomnia, parenting problems, and general mental health and wellness [ 9 , 31 , 49 , 67 ]. These interventions facilitated therapy, information, advice, and social support. For example, Sleepio contained sessions on sleep restriction, progressive muscle relaxation, and sleep hygiene directed by animated virtual therapist, and it seemed to help suicidal thoughts among women with PPD [ 59 ]. Similarly, web-based system (e.g., MUMentum [ 68 ] and Be a Mom [ 41 ]), and apps (e.g., Motherly [ 104 ] and Happy Mom [ 57 ]) delivered online sessions, often through storytelling, focusing on psychoeducation and self-care techniques through Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT). Mindfulness was utilized in the Spirits Healing app that led to a lower rate of depression at post-intervention [ 94 ]. For both emotional and informational support and self-efficacy, many smartphone apps such as “We'll” and “Mommy go” were proved effective during pregnancy and postpartum [ 67 ]. “Mamma Mia”, an interactive website was useful in changing negative affect related to subjective well-being [ 49 ]. For day-to-day advice and information regarding newborn care, maternal self‐care, normal postpartum physiology, and psychology such as baby blues and postnatal depression, Fealy et al developed the SFNM app [ 38 ].

In most cases, digital platforms for providing self-help training, peer support, and treatment are feasible, well-accepted, and may serve as a simple, inexpensive adjunct therapy well-suited to cross socioeconomic boundaries [ 9 , 16 , 31 , 49 ]. In many feasibility studies, women expressed interest in web-based PPD treatment and identified desired characteristics of such interventions [ 79 ].

3 METHODS

3.1 Data Collection

To identify the well-known PPD-specific online communities, we started with two sources: a comprehensive online search [ 98 ] and previous research articles that analyzed data from PPD support groups [ 93 , 95 , 98 ]. To find the popular online PPD support groups, a Google search in incognito mode using the keyword “online postpartum depression community” was carried out. The search yielded a list of fourteen online communities. Next, we compiled a list of relevant research articles that utilized data from online PPD communities [ 93 , 95 , 98 ]. This work was already done as a part of an ongoing systematic literature review study by the authors. From these articles, we found out five other online communities. The list of communities found from these two sources and the research articles are provided in the supplementary document. We selected three communities from this list that are a) public platform (does not require registration for collecting data), b) relatively large in terms of the number of participants, and c) have been active for some period in terms of posts and comments.

  • “Postpartum_Depression” subreddit from Reddit (SR): Reddit had been ranked as the seventh most visited website in the US and nineteenth worldwide as of September 2021 [ 2 ]. The “Postpartum_Depression” 1 subreddit was created on May 6, 2014 and it has 5,300 members during the time of our data collection. The forum allows users to interact with each other through upvotes, downvotes, and comments.
  • “Postpartum Depression” forum from the website What to expect (WE) : What To Expect provides necessary information, engagement, and assistance at every phase of the parenting journey through its app and website. This platform is based on the book by Heidi Murkoff [ 75 ]. The website receives about one million new user posts every month, and has a user population of over 13 million women. We carefully read recent posts from different forums on the website and discovered that the forum “Postpartum Depression” 2 provided moms with a virtual space to discuss PPD specific issues. It has around 13,400 members who can interact through posts, comments, and likes.
  • “Postpartum Depression, Anxiety, and Related Topics” community from the website BabyCenter (BC) : Babycenter.com is an online media company including websites and apps which provides information on perinatal and parenthood journeys. It allows users to build sub-communities on certain topics [ 27 ]. We selected one of these sub-communities: “Postpartum Depression, Anxiety, and Related Topics” 3 . This forum has about 12,600 members who are allowed to post, comment, and like each others’ posts and comments.

The similarity among these three online PPD communities is that any web user may read the posts and comments on the three communities we studied. However, users must register for an account to post or comment to communicate with each other. We collected all the posts and comments from these three prevalent PPD specific communities that fell in the range from March 2020 to March 2022 (two years). For Reddit, we used Reddit's official API to collect posts, comments and associated metadata from the “r/Postpartum_Depression” subreddit, specifically using a Python Reddit API Wrapper (PRAW) [ 14 ]. After removing the posts with empty content (posts with no text, just attachments), we ended up with 992 posts and corresponding 6067 comments. To extract posts and comments from BC and WE, we created web scraping scripts using Python Selenium library. From the WE community, we collected 981 posts and corresponding 2629 comments. We gathered 177 posts from the Babycenter sub-community, along with 776 comments. Corresponding to each post we collected information on the title of the post, the body or textual content, timestamp, and author user id. We collected comment text, timestamp and user id from the comments.

3.2 Data Analysis

We first randomly selected a subset (n=50) of the extracted posts making sure these posts are uniformly distributed among the three selected communities. We began with a focused reading of posts in this subset, applying open-codes to capture individual units of meaning. Next, we clustered open codes based on thematic affinity. Through multiple such clustering passes we arrived at a set of themes that comprehensively captured the discussion topics in all the selected posts. We then developed a codebook where each theme was described by a single code and a long-form description of the sorts of posts which that code may encompass. For the discussion topics, each post was considered as a unit of analysis. As the authors applied the codebook, additional small refinements were made to the long-form descriptions to enhance consistency in applying the codes. Once the codebook was complete, two investigators independently coded a sample of 50 randomly selected posts (2.3% of total number of 2150 posts extracted) and discussed each answer and corresponding codes to establish a common vocabulary. Following this process of establishing a common ground, we coded another set of 50 randomly selected posts to establish inter-rater reliability. This yielded an overall Cohen's Kappa coefficient of 0.90 (over 2150 posts, coded in 13 non-exclusive categories), which is considered “almost perfect” agreement [ 62 ]. Given this high level of agreement, the lead investigator then assigned categories to the remaining 2050 posts.

For the support types, both posts and comments were analyzed following a closed coding procedure. We followed previous work on categorizing social support in online health communities [ 3 , 30 , 35 , 47 , 58 , 95 , 98 ]. We selected the framework introduced by Kalichman et al. [ 58 ], as it is the first of its kind, and seemed to be a good fit for analyzing our dataset. The model consisted of three types of social support: emotional, informational, and instrumental, and has been used in earlier studies for online content analysis from different online health communities (e.g., [ 35 , 47 , 95 , 98 ]). The themes developed for both the discussion topics and the support types are provided as supplementary material.

Although the focus of this work is to study qualitatively the topics of discussion and support dynamics, we compared and contrasted basic descriptive statistics from our data to provide insights about difference in users’ sharing behavior and interaction in different communities. All of these factors have been found to influence the social support ecosystem in online health communities [ 20 , 29 ].

3.3 Ethical Considerations

Women experiencing PPD are a vulnerable population. We took steps to consider the ethical implications of our work and to protect the rights of the community members. We ensured the posts and comments we collected are public and crawling activities did not violate these websites’ terms of service. Zimmer et al [ 102 ] add to this conversation of ethical implications by emphasizing that more than expected information should not be revealed through the combination of visual and textual elements. We ensured that by carefully refraining ourselves from publishing any identifiable information that were shared in the posts or comments, even though they were public. Furthermore, we altered the supporting posts and comments reported in this paper keeping the meaning intact, so that the community members cannot be traced back with their shared content.

3.4 Positionality

The research team consisted of members whose family members and close relatives have suffered from PPD and other mental health illnesses. One team member is an expert in intersectional stigma, online social support, and mental health care, while another is an expert in working with marginalized and vulnerable populations.

4.1 Descriptive Statistics of the Posts and Comments Collected

Our dataset comprised a total of 2,150 posts with 9,472 comments from the selected subcommunities of SR, WE, and BC. These posts and comments were made by 3,470 unique users. Although BC and WE PPD communities are almost double in size compared to the SR in terms of number of members, the number of posts in the two years range in the SR and WE communities were close in number, whereas the number of posts from BC was way fewer than those two (see Table 1 ).

It is clear from Table 2 that for the most part, PPD subreddit shows more activities and engagement in the two years of data we collected, in terms of the average word length in the posts, average number of comments on the posts, number of unique users who posted or commented, and average number of comments made by an user. We were interested in further investigations of these differences. We performed Wilcoxon Rank-Sum Tests (as some of the data did not show normal distribution) to calculate the differences in the word lengths of posts and comments and in number of comments for the three pairs: SR and WE, SR and BC, and WE and BC. To account for multiple comparisons, we adjusted the p -values using the Holm-Bonferroni method. Table 3 shows the results.

We measured another aspect of user activity: the temporal delay of comments from the corresponding posts. Figure 1 shows the distribution of comments over time following the corresponding post share (it only shows data from SR and WE, since we were unable to extract timestamp for BC posts). Reddit posts, on an average, seem to gather more replies in a short period of time time (i.e., more than 60% comments are made within 12 hours of post sharing). There were a few posts where mothers shared extreme negative feelings or suicidal thoughts which might indicate signs of mental health crises. However, the interval between the first comment and such posts has always been between two and seven hours.

To understand user engagement we calculated the Cumulative Density Frequency (CDF) of the percentage of users that generated posts and comments (See Figure 2 ). The distributions of percentage of the posters seem quite similar in the three communities: about 80% or higher (out of the unique users who have posted) making at least one post, about 2% making more than 10 posts, and majority of them less than seven posts. Given that a member will probably stay active only for a fixed period of time (e.g., one or two years after childbirth). However, the distributions of percentage of the commenters show that a higher percentage of SR members (8%) account for more than 10 comments compared to WE (6%) and BC (4%). This is another evidence pointing that subreddit users may be more active than the members of the other two communities.

Figure 3 shows the most common word used in the posts (a) and comments (b). The term “I” appeared the highest number of times in both the posts (n=18670) and the comments (n=14195) in all three communities. It indicates that women predominantly discussed their personal experiences with each other. We left “I” out of Figure 3 to better observe the nuances in the other words. The frequent use of the verbs “feel”, “help”, “get”, and “know” in the comments section of the online communities implies that these women are searching for support and validation for their sentiments (more details in the following subsection). This is consistent with the findings from previous work on self-disclosure in online mental health communities that members frequently use first person pronouns to share personal stories and seek support [ 25 , 82 ].

4.2 Discussion Topics in the Posts

Several previous researchers [ 4 , 5 ] have identified that women generally suffer from a range of mental issues and hence, seek support during their perinatal period. We wanted to gain a comprehensive understanding of what women share about their mental health statuses (factors affecting mental health, stories of mental disorders, and negative pregnancy experiences) in online platforms. Table 4 shows the themes of the discussion threads as found from our analysis of the posts with the numbers and percentages of posts included in each theme. The themes are not mutually exclusive, and thus the percentages for a particular community add up to more than 100%. The three prominent overarching themes were: causes/stressors, feelings/symptoms, discussion on ways to cope up.

4.2.1 Causes and stressors. Mothers have voiced a wide range of mental health concerns, categorized here as causes and stressors, in multiple posts, which we have categorized into four major themes: social factors, constant concerns about the baby, emotional and physical distress, and overwhelming changes in life after childbirth . 60.69% (n=602) of the posts on SR, 76.45% (n=750) of the posts on WE and 56.50% (n=100) of the posts on BC included at least one of the perceived stressors.

Social factors : We found mentions of having unhelpful spouses and family members, and lack of outside support in 11.90% of all SR posts (n=118), 19.78% of WE posts (n=194), and 3.39% (n=6) of BC posts. Previous research [ 42 ] indicates that the major source of social support for moms suffering from PPD is their significant others (SO). Moms on those online communities have discussed how unsupportive significant others and adverse surroundings have exacerbated their mental health difficulties. “The constant lack of support system they needed” triggered their anxiety. They often acknowledged that their spouses wanted to do more, but were unable to help due to full-time jobs. In other cases, they were not interested in helping out with the baby or household chores, as one of them mentioned, “My husband does not help enough and gives off the impression that because he has a job that he needs more sleep than I. I'm not sure how long I can continue living this way...”

Besides, unsupportive family members or not having family nearby to assist escalates the moms’ mental health stresses. In some cases, close family members were not only reluctant to offer help, but also discouraged the mother to get outside help from babysitter or daycare, or judged them for doing so. One mentioned, “My family lives far away. They came to help. I feel like crying since they left. I cannot do this alone.....”; Another mother stated, “My mother-in-law picks at me every day all day...then she goes to my husband and tells him how bad a mother i am...” .

Six mothers, on the other hand, revealed that despite having a supportive spouse, they suffer from underlying mental health difficulties. As one stated, “I have an awesome kid, my husband is a fucking rockstar with how supportive he is, and yet I don't feel happy. At least not happy enough.”

Constant concerns about the baby : 13.10% (n=130) of SR posts, 31.91% (n=313) WE posts, and 38.42% (n=68) of BC posts revealed that mothers’ deliberate concerns for their newborns to some extent amplified as the stressor. Baby's unusual food taking, breastfeeding issues and sleeping patterns were frequently discussed. The concerns amplified if the child suffered from any additional illnesses or showed any abnormal traits (e.g., autism). Some of the users described waking up multiple times to check their kid's heartbeat. Mothers’ worries did not only limit to their baby's current issues, they oftentimes felt anxious thinking about future when the baby grows up. One of the moms said, “With everything going on, I don't know how she will survive in this misogynistic world.”

Emotional and physical distress : Mothers who have a history of past mental health conditions such as PTSD, or ADHD, have reported that PPD impacted them hard because of their previous experiences. Situations including labor trauma, postpartum body image concerns, lack of sleep, prior miscarriages, or other medical complications and comorbid health conditions have been identified as crucial stressors of PPD in those online communities (SR=27.42% (n=272), WE=13.76% (n=135), BC=8.47% (n=15)). Oftentimes, women confused their PPD with previous mental illnesses. For example, “I am not new to depression, had it before in life, but this feels different. I am not sure it is just my depression talking, or PPD.”

COVID-19 affected new mothers’ mental health as well, particularly due to the isolation. One of the users in the subreddit described how COVID-19 made her PPD “ten times worse”.

Besides, it is not uncanny that moms experience intense torment during childbirth, which is exacerbated if the delivery is cesarean. Mothers indicated in all the online communities that they frequently experience emotional breakdowns in the postpartum period as a result of their painful C-section experiences. About birth trauma one mother noted, “Labor and delivery trauma messing with my head...I feel like a piece of me died that day.”

The postpartum changes in the body often did not allow women to feel positive about themselves. New mothers face these unavoidable changes, and social media's immaculate portrayal (e.g., “flat stomach” ) of others’ lives contributes to the agony to the extent that they start “hating their bodies” and, end up “hating themselves” .

Overwhelming changes in life after childbirth : Many listed unplanned pregnancy, financial challenges, or smoking/drug addiction as stressors. Every parent has to adjust to the change to their identity, to being responsible for another human life, and of course the massive changes to their day to day lives (SR=8.27% (n=82), WE= 11.01% (N=108), BC=6.21% (n=11)).

“I just miss my old life. I miss how carefree and fun I was before. I miss that I could go out of the house whenever I wanted. I miss being able to just feel like a human being.”

4.2.2 Symptoms and feelings. Women in general self-disclose postpartum depressive symptoms and feelings on online forums, as previous research identified [ 95 ]. Our qualitative analysis revealed the same, as this theme comprised the largest number of posts (SR=75.30% (n=757), WE=96.43% (n=946), BC=63.84% (n=113)). Four diverse, but closely related aspects were described: psychological and physical symptoms of depression , fear and guilt about being an unfit mother , co-occurring feelings attributable to life changing events , feeling of not being understood or cared for , and other mood issues and mental health symptoms .

Psychological and physical symptoms of depression : All community members expressed their difficulties with depression-related symptoms such as loneliness, exhaustion, sleep deprivation, and so forth. Eating and sleeping patterns were affected by anxiety and “racing thoughts” . Some of them said they felt lonely even amidst people, or even when everyone around them was being very supportive. Physical pain was also described as a symptom of PPD and anxiety. These women were usually in denial that their PPD could cause physical pain. Therefore, in most cases, these posts were looking for validation of their assumption.

“Did anyone's PPD/PPA cause them physical pain? I have pain in my shoulders neck hips thigh bone. Nerve pain in my arms and leg, tingling numbness in the night..feels like burning sick pain. I don't believe any of this is bc of my depression or anxiety, but my doctor tells me that”

Fear and guilt about being an unfit mother : Mothers addressed their sentiments about continual baby concerns in 4.74% of all SR posts (n=47), 9.17% of WE posts (n=90), and 5.08% (n=9) of BC posts. They emphasized that their “Mom guilt” that stems from their thought of not being able to “take care of their children properly” due to mental and physical conditions affected their bond making process with their children. Many of them asserted that they had been emotionally disturbed as a result of their constant worry over losing their kid or hurting them by any means. They even refrained themselves from disclosing their symptoms and thoughts with their doctors or nurses in the fear that their babies would be taken away from them. One illustrative example is: “I didn't share my thoughts with the midwife even though she asked. What if they call CPS!”

Co-occurring feelings attributable to life-changing events : Mothers on the online communities have regularly emphasized the co-occurrence of numerous sentiments such as “being jealous of others’ lives”, “identity crisis” . for the transformation of motherhood (SR=15.42% (n=153), WE=21% (n=206), BC=6.78% (n=12)). They even envied their spouses as “they can hangout with their friends whenever, and always pass baby back to them because the mother have to feed the baby.”

Feeling of not being understood or cared for : Doctors or healthcare providers often tend to invalidate their feelings. What is worse is close relatives and family members would not understand their feelings and actions, and judged them. Women also expressed dissatisfaction sharing that often their OBGYNs or nurses invalidated their feelings, and even physical pain. One of the primary reasons of women joining support groups is that they feel “unheard, misunderstood, and completely uncared for” . To elucidate one mentioned, “They don't care about what I need. I can't wait for this life to be over and jump off a tall building.”

Other mood issues and mental health symptoms : Other mood related issues and extreme symptoms were described in approximately 3.53% percent of all SR posts (n=35), 1.73% percent of WE posts (n=17), and 1.13% percent of BC posts (n=2). Rage ( “I've been so triggered and filled with rage. I don't know how to stop the internal abusive monologue” ) and self-loathing ( ”I hate myself, I have resentment towards myself. I feel useless” ) were the most common ones. Women described experiencing auditory and visual hallucinations: “I have panic attacks and hallucinations all the time (a person trying to kidnap my baby)” . A few mothers discuss their struggles with suicidal thoughts and mention that they have even “planned it in their heads” ; “I feel really hopeless and suicidal. I don't know where to go for help. I can't keep waiting for months for an intake appointment and then have the same experience I just did.”. Some of these posts represent critical sentimental agonies and signs for potential crisis that should be given immediate attention.

4.2.3 Discussing ways to cope up. A significant percentage, 24.66% (n=242), of the users of the postpartum online community on What to expect shared their stories about handling postpartum depression using treatment, medication, self-care techniques, and technical assistance. On the other hand, in 13.91% of SR posts (n=138) and 19.21% (n=34) of BC posts, mothers have discussed such coping mechanisms. The four different successful mechanisms to cope up were treatment/medication/therapy , physical activity, exercises, and self-care , apps and other online resources , and asking for help from others.

Treatment/Medication/Therapy : Respectively, 0.60%(n=6), 3.06% (n=30), 2.26% (n=4) posts on Reddit, WhatToExpect, and Babycenter's postpartum-related online communities shared what particular treatment method worked for them with a description of their signs and symptoms. In those posts mothers recommended others to seek treatment, medicine, or counseling if required. For example, one mother wrote, “I am feeling a million times better now after therapy and medication, and you will feel better too....”

Physical activity, exercises, and self-Care : Practicing self-care or yoga, as well as engaging in physical activities benefited moms in dealing with postpartum distress. Mothers mentioned few examples of such activities: “I danced today and felt so good ” ; “If you're ever feeling depressed and lethargic, force yourself to walk around outside for at least 20 minutes.” ; “My happiness triggers include a quiet walk alone, meditation, yoga, reading & cuddling with my kids” . To explain the positive effects of self-care, one of the mothers wrote, “I felt like I was lifted out of a fog I didn't know I was in. I was able to function much better...”

Apps and other online resources : A few mothers in this subreddit shared their experiences of using several online and offline resources, including books, apps, personal digital assistants, websites like 7cups, self-care bingo, and genesight. They cited other generic online media and websites (e.g., YouTube or exercise videos) as a way of distracting themselves from negative thoughts.

Asking for help from others : Mothers have recommended others to get assistance immediately rather than waiting. For instance, “please seek help” , “There is no need to suffer in silence!” , “Please ask for help, for all the stubborn ladies out there!” , “don't be afraid to ask for it” .

4.3 Social Support Dynamics in the Posts and the Comments

What kind of social support do members in postpartum depression related online communities exchange? We incorporated in our codebook a characterization of support sought or provided in the extracted posts and comments. The taxonomy of social support we applied distinguishes three categories inclusive of emotional, informational, and instrumental support [ 35 , 58 , 98 ] . Emotional support consists of concern, affection, comforting, and encouragement resulting in sense of belonging and self-worth. Informational support involves advice giving, information sharing and personal knowledge development. Instrumental support reflects tangible assistance such as practical help with daily living. These three aspects are consistent with Oakley's characterization of social support in pregnancy which involves active listening, being non-judgmental, providing information and relevant help [ 77 ].

4.3.1 Types of support exchanged in the posts. We have been unable to discover any posts that had instrumental support dynamics. This makes sense, as in most cases women would require to disclose their address and look for others living nearby. However, both informational and emotional support have been sought frequently in such online forums. Mothers sought or provided informational and/or emotional support in 95.06% of the SR posts (n=943), 104.99% of the WE posts (n=1030), and 98.87% of the BC posts (n=175). Table 5 illustrates example quotes of each support type. Figure 4 shows percentages of posts containing one or more types from this table. Although majority of the posts were asking questions and looking for support, a few members simply shared their stories and experiences to provide support to other members.

However, no such posts were shared on BC or WE, and this is understandable, considering the fact that those communities are particularly designed for the mothers. This finding is related to the discussion topic described in 4.2.2 about women feeling not being understood properly. Some spouses and FnFs were aware of this communication gap, and were empathetic enough to ask for help on subreddit to understand the mother's journey better.

In terms of the percentages of posts by support type, reddit was the topmost platform in our data for seeking emotional support, and BabyCenter was the topmost for seeking informational support.

Since two different codebooks were used to find the themes of discussion topics and to classify the social support types, we wanted to understand if there are any overlaps between these two classifications. This overlap has been illustrated in Table 6 : while seeking emotional support, mothers usually portray their stressors and feelings, whereas for seeking informational support, they often discuss their ways of coping with postpartum depression in the posts (e.g, asking about the side effects of the medications she is taking.)

4.3.2 Support exchanged through comments. Similar to other online mental health communities, replies were usually positive. Other mothers expressed their support and solidarity with the posters. Although we did not see any posts looking for instrumental support, the comments illustrated provision of all three types of support:informational, emotional, and instrumental (see Table 7 ).

The comments providing emotional support incorporated validation, reassurance, and empathy (e.g., “in the same boat”, “this too shall pass”, “hang in there”, “don't give up” .). We discovered that providing emotional support was the most prevalent type in the comments (n=4751, 78.31%) of SR and (n=1269, 48.27%) of WE. ‘I’ being the most frequent word in the comments (section 4.1) depicts that mothers tried to reassure or validate struggling mothers’ feeling and empathize with them by sharing their own stories, whether they were successful or unsuccessful in combating PPD. Sometimes, the comments are brief yet inspiring, and occasionally, the words asserted their side of the stories. For instance, “I had terrible PPD and went thru similar feelings after a lot of birth trauma. These feelings are passing. Reach out to your OB. They can help.” On the other hand, 48.27% (n=269) of BC comments contained emotional support. One of the mothers in the comment section said, “Let yourself have your moments when you need them and know that it will get better and that you are not crazy nor are you alone.” Informational support providing occurred through discussing ways of coping up with the symptoms, medication, therapy, and treatment associated advice and information in those online communities. About 7.48% (n=454) of SR comments contain informational support whereas WE and BC respectively has 39.98% (n=1051) and 41.88% (n=325) comments regarding informational support. Information about different websites and online resources that help find offline help was frequently mentioned. Commenters also recommended resources for finding remote/virtual support during COVID-19. One of them mentioned “Medication helps, but I'd suggest finding a therapist, too. Search Postpartum International, they will match a therapist with you. Also, in my area therapy is telehealth right now, so you don't have to leave your home”

The only aspect of instrumental support we observed as provided by the commenters (SR: n=144, 2.37%; WE: n=163, 6.20%; BC:n=18, 2.32%) are encouraging the posters to use either the private message/email option to communicate with them or to refer other websites for help. This is understandable, as mentioned earlier, since most members used reddit anonymously, did not know each other, they could possibly not offer any other tangible assistance.

We cannot surely claim that the posters received the expected support they were looking for from this community. However, our analysis revealed that in many of the posts the post sharer replied and expressed gratitude towards the comment(s), and they acknowledged that they had received assistance: “I appreciate you sharing ur story and ur support. 5 DISCUSSION

In this section, we discuss the implications of our findings and highlight design opportunities for future technologies to better support women facing postpartum mental health challenges.

5.1 Implications for Future Research on Online Support-Seeking for Postpartum Depression

5.1.1 Leveraging online communities for facilitating offline support. Through the analysis of comments, we found that members of online PPD communities ‘cared’ for each other by providing a ‘safe space’ for others through reassurance and validity of feelings and symptoms, consistent with prior work in online mental health space [ 72 , 83 ]. We frequently observed comments such as “these feelings are normal” or “you are not alone” . Consequently, mothers acknowledged that they received the social support and solidarity they were looking for: “Thank you for the comment– it's encouraging to know other people felt the same and got through it” , or “I am so thankful you wrote this. I do not feel so alone and like such a horrible mom when I come read experiences on here” . Many mothers discussed how often they lack such instances of reassurance in real life from the people they expect it the most. As evident from our findings, spouses and family members judged, misunderstood, or invalidated their feelings (section 4.2.2). They even wished for offline support groups in their area: “I wish there was support groups like AA where you go and talk about your depression....” .

Previous research indicates that mothers most value instrumental support: physical help in tasks, such as laundry and cooking [ 85 ]. This type of tangible assistance is in most cases not viable to obtain from an online platform with unfamiliarity and lack of proximity with other members [ 96 ]. Although the community members discussed the lack of instrumental support in real life and how that affected their mental health symptoms (section: 4.2.1), asking for instrumental help (e.g., babysitting) was rare. We observed only a few instances of providing or interest in providing instrumental support (e.g., Idk if you have help but ask for it. Or hire someone for just a few hours. I'm a message away if you feel like you are drowning ). This is natural since the community members are not likely to be in each other's close social network.

The potential of online communities to meet offline emotional and instrumental support needs more investigation in future, as these types of support are crucial during postpartum. Additionally, more in-depth and user-centric research should be conducted to understand women's support expectation from offline and online sources. The online communities can be leveraged to build an offline support network for women who need it. For instance, these online platforms can facilitate and encourage offline interaction for members who live in the same city and are comfortable to share on their online profiles the city where they live [ 96 ]. This may also facilitate the exchange of tangible assistance, such as babysitting and play-dates among the online members living in the same area.

5.1.2 Implications for machine learning and NLP researchers. According to the descriptive statistics, SR posts were longer in average compared to the posts from the other two communities, and gathered more comments in less time. It does not provide us any clear indication of higher user engagement or interest for involvement in the subreddit, as we did not compare the PPD subreddit against any other subreddit (e.g., this may be a common pattern for all mental health subreddits). The three community's infrastructure may be inherently different. Furthermore, the members’ level of perceived stigma could also have affected their sharing behavior, as prior work has shown association of lower participation and adherence to treatment with perceived stigma [ 13 ]. However, this finding, combined with the insight from qualitative data about the prevalence of emotional support on reddit, has major implications for researchers interested in developing machine learning models for automatic detection of illnesses or for categorizing support types from online textual content. The specific patterns and language used in posts can inform the design of features for machine learning models [ 43 ]. For example, if a model is built to characterize social support by analyzing linguistic features observed in reddit posts it may lack words and expressions related to informational support. Expressions recognized by this model may not be representative and meaningful for other communities, and thus may not achieve high accuracy when applied to a community where informational support related posts are prevalent. However, in order to determine the most discriminative linguistic features between different communities focused on the same condition, additional work is required continuing from the results shown here.

5.2 Implications for Design of Online Communities for Postpartum Depression

5.2.1 Design considering close others. We observed posts about spouses and relatives not understanding the feelings of the mothers, and how the lack of social support worked as a stressor for their PPD. On the contrary, although not very frequent, 7.86% of the SR posts were shared by husbands, friends, and family members of women, asking for advice on how to provide support to the mothers experiencing PPD. In light of these findings, we recommend that designers thoughtfully leverage the broader social network of users who serve to support those individuals who are experiencing PPD. They may consider how family members and friends can be involved and informed, and their mental health literacy strengthened to provide support. Online platforms can aim to make the partners more aware of the consequences of their interactions on the new mothers’ mental health. One way to accomplish this is to utilize the supportive posts shared by others’ family members to create empathy and accountability in those who lack them. In many of the posts, women recognized that their partners wanted to do more; however, interventions for women with PPD have not focused on supporting fathers as they care for their partners affected by PPD. Previous work on support for partners of PPD patients suggest that much work is needed to educate new fathers about PPD and to include them in treatment planning for the affected mothers [ 65 ]. Online communities can allow husbands/partners/close family members to see the mother's posts and educate them about how to better support her specific needs. Furthermore, these posts can be utilized for collaborative tracking of mothers’ mental health symptoms. For example, if a mother shares that she is in bad mood or experiencing sleep issues, her partner may be recommended with techniques to help them improve these symptoms.

5.2.2 Design for social support through “storytelling”. Designers of online mental health communities can utilize “storytelling” to effectively engage members. Digital storytelling has been proven effective in critical health contexts (e.g., cancer) [ 54 ], and healthy behavior change [ 51 , 101 ]. Past research on breast cancer support [ 54 ] community found out that women felt empowered by the exchanges of knowledge and experience within the support group. In the online PPD communities, the number of posts and comments containing mothers’ journeys and experiences of perinatal mental health was much higher than that which shared digital or offline resources (e.g., websites, apps, books, and other technologies). In our dataset, about 10% posts contained more than 400 words. Many long posts described women's PPD journey spanning for multiple years, and and many others discussed the advantages or the disadvantages of particular ways to cope up, in a thorough manner. Some common examples include delivery trauma, previous mental health illnesses worsening PPD, and continuous change in medication due to side effects. These experiences can be used as educational material for others looking for information or reassurance, making these communities a potentially vital “storytelling” resource. Digital storytelling can be particularly useful in this context, since women mentioned “feeling overwhelmed” while looking for information online: “I'm battling PPD/PPA and a huge contributor has been going down rabbit holes on the internet especially regarding baby sleep.” , but at the same time they “enjoy reading the stories” . Online PPD support groups can consider designing the interface for creating posts in a way that facilitates storytelling. Textual content can be converted to short stories with visual elements to support better time management for new mothers. This can also be leveraged to provide education to remove the social barriers and stigma to participation.

5.2.3 Design for providing verified information. Our finding shows that in the communities mothers frequently seek informational support regarding medication and clinical details. Around 72% of the BC posts indicated some informative assistance, which strengthens the case for the efficiency of online forums in disseminating PPD-related advice and knowledge. In addition to the already existing distrust, our findings revealed that women hesitate to share PPD symptoms even with their OBGYNs and nurses, as they often felt that their feelings got dismissed or judged. Moreover, mothers constantly worry that the healthcare providers might call CPS (Child Protective Services) if they are proven unfit or dangerous. Mothers frequently turn to online forums for informational assistance due to these worries. We observed comments suggesting change in dosage of medicines, sharing links to find professionals and therapists, and medical solutions for the babys’ health related issues. However, it is hard to make sure that they always receive verified information through these platforms. In an attempt to offer well-researched helpful, and verified information, associations that are dedicated to pregnant women or parenting should consider funding their own online mental health support groups, such as WE and BC. These communities can work as a major source of providing education about vetted and non-vetted information on the Internet along with the importance of scrutinizing the accuracy and validity of these information.

5.2.4 Design for crisis support. Online mental health communities can help encourage mothers to seek help and ensure they get the proper support when needed. O'Leary et al. [ 78 ] discovered that individuals with mental disorders frequently seek “just-in-time” care in times of crisis. Women contributing to these forums often suffer from mental health complications and concerns such as PPD, and they probably expect their posts to be adequately and promptly answered [ 46 ]. Yet our analysis showed that only some posts receive immediate responses, particularly in the BabyCenter sub-community. That would be a reasonable expectation if users were on a mental health platform. We did not observe any urge for immediate crisis support in the posts. However, a few posts indicating suicide ideation do not appear to receive an immediate response, which may be alarming. For example, after posting, “I'm feeling suicidal. I called the suicide hotline but they don't have any good info to tell me other than they hope I feel better” the mother received the first comment after two hours, demonstrating a lag to respond to a potential crisis event. Developers can incorporate automated methods of curating previous occurrences of similar posts and corresponding helpful comments made in the past. The interface can show these past comments immediately if “warning signs” or “crisis events” are detected in posts, and other members do not reply immediately. However, this design consideration poses some open research questions. Reddit is not a self-help platform. Who would define a warning sign or crisis event within a self-regulated open space? In a totally-volunteer community, the platform cannot force interactions. So does Reddit become a formal online health community, or are there ways to better temper people's expectations in the forum? When people use Reddit for sharing mental health challenges, is a timely response what is wanted, or does just sharing the information has benefits? Future work can look at addressing these tensions and pressure-testing these assumptions.

5.3 Implications for community guidelines design and for policy makers

We observed that the salient type of support provided and received in the SR community was emotional support, in the BC community was informational support, and in the WE was a balanced combination of these two. There is mixed evidence in literature about the type of support exchanged in online PPD related forums. Stana et al [ 93 ] have found out that informational support was the most frequently used type of support in a closed online community for PPD (where creating a profile and permission are required to gain access to the forum), followed by emotional support and network/community support. On the other hand, research on public online PPD communities show that emotional support is the most frequent type of support sought [ 35 ]. In our study, the infrastructure of the communities may have an impact on this difference in support types in different communities. The three groups were similar in terms of access: anyone on the web is allowed to read the posts and the comments, but need to create an account to be able to generate content. Participants are allowed to use pseudonyms instead of their real names. On Reddit, throwaway accounts can also be used for anonymity, although we found only two user ids from the throwaway accounts in the data we analyzed. However, WhatToExpect and BabyCenter online communities were designed specifically to support the maternal process of which PPD is a part of, and reddit is a general, anonymous platform where a space (subreddit) has been created for discussing PPD. We found instances in the reddit posts indicating that members prefer anonymous text communication over video platforms, or platforms where there real life identities may be revealed: “Can't open up to my doctor, also don't feel comfortable to do a Zoom group. My mom tried pushing me into a local mommy and me group which ‘No thank you’. This subreddit is a group I actually don't hate” . For BC and WE, even if people might not know each other in real life, profiles and people become familiar, as they share stories and ask questions in other communities part of the same platform (e.g., Baby Names or Pregnancy community on BabyCenter). Someone may not want to be remembered or associated with a particularly embarrassing, tragic or sad situation shared in the PPD community: “If a regular poster came on and said they caught their oh in a threesome with a goat and a donkey you will forever remember them as being the one with the husband who likes some ass!”

We understand that there are more nuanced reasons why the primary type of support varies from community to community. Future research should be conducted to gain additional insights from interviews and focus groups with the users of these online communities to identify these reasons. These findings bear practical implications for community guideline design and for health policy makers. There is significant stigma associated with PPD that needs to be addressed. Advocacy organizations should have websites dedicated to PPD, and they should facilitate advocacy activities, such as blogs for PPD education, promotion of self-care, or publicizing concrete actions that individuals can take for changes in local and national policies that affect people with PPD. Online communities can benefit from establishing ground regulations to ensure a “non-judgemental place to ask for help”. Users should be allowed to create anonymous posts easily while still logged in with their regular accounts. Also, future research by contrasting this subreddit to other mental health-related subreddit (e.g., r/depression, r/SuicideWatch, r/ptsd) might show the broader picture of how reddit communities promote peer support through detailed community backgrounds and guidelines.

6 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

Our study represents a small aspect of a developing research domain; therefore, it has certain drawbacks and unresolved concerns. Although the platforms we chose are massive online media offering pseudonymity, analyzing a subset of posts from these communities within a time frame may not represent the broader picture of online support dynamics of PPD. Moreover, we might be missing feedback from users who only lurk in this community and are not comfortable (or do not care) about writing their experiences online. Since we collected data from three familiar and large communities, we can assume that our findings represent similar discussion topics and support-seeking behavior obtained from analyzing other PPD-related online platforms and peer-support groups providing pseudonymity or anonymity as an affordance to its users. However, we believe that future research should collect self-reported data from additional sources (e.g., surveys, interviews) to understand better women's expectations and experiences of using online resources and communities.

7 CONCLUSION

Social support is necessary for effective treatment of PPD. Online communities offer vital networking opportunities for postpartum women, allowing them to strengthen their medical care with emotional and informational support. To explore the PPD related experiences shared online, this study qualitatively evaluated the textual content of the posts and comments from three online communities from Reddit, What to expect, and Babycenter. We discovered that mothers primarily seek emotional support on the PPD subreddit, whereas they tend to seek informational support in the other communities. The three communities share major topics of discussion including stressors, symptoms, and coping mechanisms for PPD. This is done mostly through sharing personal experiences and venting. We provide research implications for identifying the differences in women's offline and online PPD support network and for considering community-specific sharing patterns to inform machine learning models that aim to automatically detect mental health illnesses. We provide design recommendations for online PPD communities to facilitate social support and education through storytelling, to consider close others, and to provide adequate support during mental health crisis.
