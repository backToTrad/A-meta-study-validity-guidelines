Trauma-informed design is an important yet challenging area due to required expertise for careful engagement with the vulnerable and traumatized person, particularly with children. While technologies have the potential to generate value for the person with experience of trauma, the risk of exposing them to additional trauma due to research and design has ethical implications. We explore this space by engaging with therapists and social workers as dyads of proxies for parents and children with a history of trauma. We conducted a study as a participatory workshop with proxies to understand how technology could support parents in coaching social-emotional learning during episodes of high intensity emotion and difficult behavior by the child. Our findings identified design qualities to embed in SEL technology, and three roles that parents can play in implementing SEL strategies at home. We contribute a set of seven methodological guidelines for dyadic trauma-informed participatory design with proxies.

1 INTRODUCTION

Trauma-informed design is an important yet challenging field due to required expertise for careful engagement with the vulnerable person with history of trauma, particularly with children. Trauma may involve various forms of neglect, separation, or abuse, among other forms of violence. Trauma may be described in terms of personal identity trauma (e.g. sexual abuse), secondary trauma (e.g. parents’ experience of war) or collective trauma experienced by a group, as Kira and colleagues describe [ 49 ]. It may occur due to a single event or be cumulative [ 49 ]. Trauma is complex and situated. A child with experience of trauma is at risk because they may perceive many daily interactions as threat and therefore respond with high levels of emotional arousal [ 33 ], that is, the physiological and bodily manifestations of emotion, signifying a state of being alert [ 71 ] with expressions such as anger. Trauma can disrupt the developmental process of obtaining social-emotional competency in children that is typically important to learn, manage and express emotional responses to social situations and successfully manage life tasks [ 28 ]. The process of obtaining these emotional skills is called social-emotional learning (SEL) that centers learning and managing effective strategies for emotional expression [ 48 ]. Evidence based SEL programs are often conducted in a school context to promote emotional well-being [ 26 ] [ 29 ], while a therapy intervention may aid children who are “at risk of mainstream education failure due to significant mental health problems such as anxiety, depression, aggression, social and communication skills deficits, and poor emotional self-regulation both at home and at school” [ 87 ]. Within and beyond the therapy context, there remains a design potential to support the child as well as their parents and guardians.

Most therapy interventions occur outside the school environment in a clinic setting, and often focus on the child [ 39 ]. Research has shown that carers, guardians or family play a significant role in children's social emotional learning at home [ 67 ], for example by providing spaces and activities to develop a child's social emotional skills. Therefore parents and guardians may be engaged in therapy to address emotion coaching, for example, to help a child learn effective ways to deal with different emotions, like frustration, in social situations [ 37 ]. This is particularly important for children for whom trauma can cause learning difficulty [ 89 ]. In our previous research [ 54 ], we focused on therapy that aims to repair and align the relationship between parent and child, and to foster better support to the child. Working closely with therapists, we identified and framed multiple practical SEL strategies that parents of children with history of trauma might use for emotion coaching. These strategies can be employed in research and design to generate reflection on emotion and behavior (key components of SEL) in children in an environment where their needs for safety, relatedness, empathy and social resilience are fulfilled. We proposed the safety, connection and reflection framework to demonstrate such a social environment is needed to establish trust and readiness for reflection and learning for the child. While the therapy deeply explores the circumstances of trauma to identify how these goals can be achieved, our research focuses on whether and how technology can support specific SEL strategies suggested to children and their families for emotion coaching. Our research is informed by these complexities that are challenging to capture and design for in human-computer interaction (HCI) due to limited precedence in terms of research methods, frameworks and critical perspectives on technologies relevant to trauma treatment [ 54 ]. We investigate and contribute to this knowledge gap by adopting a trauma-informed approach.

In our follow up research presented here, we specifically examine effective ways of supporting parents and guardians in emotionally charged situations (e.g. when the child experiences a difficult emotion and responds with anger) where immediate actions of the adults are likely to frustrate or assist a child to reconsider their reaction. This type of situation can be challenging for parents who will need to manage their own reaction to the situation, while recalling a learned strategy to appropriately coach the emotional expression of the child. While technologies can generate value for the person with experience of trauma, the risk of exposing them to additional trauma due to research has ethical implications [ 54 ]. So how might we conduct participatory design research in such a sensitive space? Our previous research identified the immense potential of working with therapists as informants and proxies in the research and design process. In the study presented in this paper, we further explore ways of engaging with therapists as proxy stakeholders [ 20 ] with intimate knowledge of the trauma context and experience (Fig 1 ). This is established practice with a history in a number of research areas, such as dementia research when the person with dementia diagnosis is experiencing cognitive decline or challenges with regards to their communication abilities [ 20 ]. This type of proxy engagement often involves carers and families of the person, or it may involve dyads including both the patient and their carer. In some situations, such as working with children with experience of trauma, engagement with a dyad of child/parent might not be feasible. This was the case in the project presented in this paper.

Therefore in the study we present in this paper, we explore this space by engaging with therapists and social workers as proxies. We address two research challenges: one to avoid further exposing participants to trauma when the research study is asking participants to recall and discuss highly charged emotional situations that may trigger underlying trauma, and another to address the challenge of recruiting participants in sensitive settings such as home. Ultimately we aim to engage in mutual learning to converge our multidisciplinary perspectives in therapy, design, and HCI. The study addresses two research questions (RQ):

  1. How might we design technologies to support children and parents to put into practice emotion coaching strategies after they have transitioned out of the therapy context and into the home environment?
  2. How might we effectively conduct participatory design research with proxies in the mental health space, when therapists play the roles of parents and children?

We aim to extend the knowledge in two critical areas of design for SEL and design with proxies to support care, healing and skill building in children with history of trauma. We first identify design qualities and propose three design guidelines that embed SEL at home through pre-reflection, that is, preparing the child to reflect. The guidelines account for (i) different reflective purposes , (ii) multiple parental roles as peer, supporter and carer of the child, and (iii) different states of reactive and proactive reflection . Second, we contribute seven guidelines for conducting dyadic trauma-informed participatory design (PD) with proxies based on scenarios embedded in their real-world practice. This is an important methodological contribution, which can offer new opportunities in participatory design. Our study had started with a clear focus on RQ1, and yet it was not possible to adequately and ethically fulfill our objective without shifting our focus to working with proxies. Therefore, it became apparent that our approach has methodological merits that might help others in the community who conduct research in sensitive environments. As such, we first present our research method in detail with the findings section focused on investigating RQ1. A separate section then follows to locate our reflections on engagement with proxies to examine RQ2. We hope the suggested method can contribute new approaches to ideating technologies to minimize the risk of traumatizing experiences for vulnerable individuals in a participatory process.

2 RELATED WORK

In the following sections, we first review how social-emotional learning takes place in educational and mental health contexts. SEL is particularly resonant with trauma-informed approaches in care and design that highlight the risks of inadvertently generating re-traumatizing experiences. Such approaches aim to minimize harm and support safety, well-being and healing. Whilst designing with proxies for the vulnerable, traumatized person or group has been used before, practical guidelines for specific contexts such as parents and children with a trauma background is lacking.

2.1 Design for social-emotional learning (SEL)

Social-emotional skills have long-term impact on quality of life due to relevance of core competencies such as self-awareness, self-regulation, social skill, empathy and motivation [ 37 ]. These skills are important to enable the child to identify and manage emotion; demonstrate empathy; set and achieve positive goals; develop, and sustain positive relationships; and make responsible decisions [ 25 , 53 , 85 ]. With the continued emphasis on health and wellbeing in education, SEL is widely used in various educational settings [ 9 , 22 , 29 ], for example, in the United States [ 25 ] and Australia [ 84 ]. Consequently the potential of using technology to support the learning process of social emotional skills has received attention in HCI [ 31 , 32 , 35 , 54 ].

Early childhood to adolescent period is a critical stage in the development of important social-emotional skills. The disruption of skills acquisition by trauma during this period can have significant impact on education [ 35 ], relationships, work impairment, uptake of health risk behaviors such as drug and alcohol abuse, and mental health problems such as depression[ 3 ]. Research has shown that trauma can impair children's memory and the degree of trauma impact can create difficulty for the child's learning process [ 89 ] that may lead to difficulties in understanding and learning SEL and require additional assistance and intervention.

Evidence suggests that design can play a unique role in SEL implementation. The SEL Transition Wheel is an interactive artifact that articulates social emotional skills for children aged 3 to 6, and supports caregivers in mediating the SEL process [ 77 ]. Another example is Inside out , an online tool used for middle school education that assists teachers in monitoring and facilitating SEL activities, to help students assess how they experience and express emotions [ 51 ]. Beyond the classroom, Slovak et al. [ 76 ] designed a technology probe, including a magnet card leading to an online portal with SEL activities to support parents and children at home. Now more than ever, there is a need for supporting parents to nurture SEL of children at home, given the disruption of school based programs due to the COVID-19 pandemic [ 35 ].

In the context of mental health, SEL is often coupled with emotion regulation. Previous research highlighted the potential of using technology to support self-regulation of emotions in children [ 79 ] and youth [ 21 ]. Theofanopoulou and Slovak [ 79 ] explored an embodied intervention for emotion regulation in children using a smart toy that supported parental reaction to child's emotion and parental discussion of emotions with the child, while also highlighting a need for supporting the family beyond the “in-the-moment soothing support”[ 79 , p. 11]. While the study [ 79 ] did not explore trauma or SEL specifically, the findings on eliciting parent-child interaction highlighted the potential of situated technologies at home. Our previous research [ 54 ] also highlighted the differences in how parents and children contextualized affective experiences, and the potential of technologies to address a perceived gap. Theofanopoulou and Slovak [ 79 ] similarly note future directions for guiding parent-child interactions through dyadic experiences that recognize the relational underpinnings of SEL, an emergent yet less explored technology design direction.

The use of technology for emotion regulation of children particularly those with history of trauma should be approached with care. Some studies have demonstrated the potential risk the technology might pose for trauma in children. For example, social media might put teens with a history of mental health conditions at risk of being re-traumatized due to cyber bullying [ 72 ]. George and colleagues [ 34 ] discovered that daily use of digital technology may be positively associated with mental health symptoms in the same day. They showed a significant correlation exists between time spent texting and on social media, and ADHD symptoms within the same day. In the context of a person with a history of trauma, technology might create triggers, and feelings of powerlessness especially in situations that are unexpected. Evidence suggests that those suffering from intimate partner violence (which is often viewed as a traumatizing experience) may be at risk of being re-traumatized through the technology, for example, being monitored or harassed [ 30 ].

In our research, we do not view technological intervention as the only solution. Indeed, technology may be unsuitable for some users due to the risks outlined above. In the project presented in this paper, we explore technology imaginaries that parents and guardians may use to support traumatized children in learning and practicing social emotional skills. Through speculated outcomes, we outline potential directions for further research, which may be validated through empirical evidence in consultation with healthcare professionals.

2.2 Trauma-informed approach: applications and interpretations in care and design

Trauma is a term used to describe experiences that result in mental and physical harm to a person [ 23 ], for example, harmful emotional impacts due to abuse, violence, war and conflict [ 45 ]. Schimmenti describes trauma in terms of the psychological wounds inflicted upon a person, impacting the individual's psychological processes at multiple levels of functioning, ”leading to significant alterations in self-concept, relationship patterns, and belief systems” [ 73 , p.553]. Acute trauma occurs due to exposure to a single oppressive event, which is different to complex trauma resulting from prolonged (and cumulative) exposure to a traumatic environment [ 8 ]. The source of trauma [ 8 ], and extent of exposure to traumatic events, could have long term impact on a person's life [ 27 , 38 , 74 ]. Studies show that 70% of people will have at least one trauma experience in their lifetime [ 61 ]. However, not everyone who has experienced a traumatic event will be traumatized. If the traumatic memories are not processed by the person affected, then they would be stored as physiological responses to stimuli, situations, or states that evoked those memories [ 83 ]. This is central to the experience of traumatized children, which negatively impacts their emotional development and harms their ability to manage their responses to social and environmental stimuli. SEL and holistic programs (such as the one presented in this paper) can embed trauma-informed care to help family or guardians learn about emotion coaching of children in emotionally charged and difficult situations. This occurs in an environment that is sensitive to their trauma triggers, demonstrating a trauma-informed approach.

The increasing advocacy for trauma-informed approaches aims to reduce potential re-traumatizing of people involved in a process such as a research or design study [ 16 , 45 , 63 ]. The trauma-informed approach is used to realize, recognize, respond to and resist potential traumatic experiences in many situations (and not specifically in trauma-focused services) based on the assumption that trauma is prevalent and a trauma-informed approach should be implemented in every possible domain [ 64 ]. Huang et al. [ 45 ] suggest that re-traumatizing is prevalent in many public service systems, and that we need to better understand these traumatic experiences to help mitigate them. To this end, they propose trauma-informed approach principles of safety, trustworthiness, peer support, collaboration, empowerment through voice and choice, and moving past stereotypes and biases pertaining to cultural, historical, and gender issues to cultivate healing [ 45 ]. There is an implicit presence of these principles in our research design, as we set up a safe and trustworthy environment of peer collaboration among proxies (therapists), and empower them to enact emotionally charged situations based on real client cases to avoid stereotyping and bias.

Similar to the principles of a trauma-informed approach, trauma-informed care has been applied to many contexts, such as mental health support [ 13 , 46 , 58 ], adverse childhood experience [ 24 , 36 , 42 ] and addiction treatment [ 18 ], predominantly situated in healthcare. Trauma-informed care is developed based on trauma theory [ 65 ], with a goal to not treat trauma, but to provide services to those who have experienced trauma in the most appropriate way to avoid the possibility of re-traumatizing [ 43 ]. Rosenberg states that trauma-informed care aims to meet the unique needs of trauma survivors [ 66 ]. Patients with a history of traumatic life events may become re-traumatized as a result of a medical experience, and these patients can benefit from trauma-informed care that is sensitive to their unique needs [ 65 ]. We bring a trauma-informed approach to a project situated in the trauma-informed care context, at a mental health clinic for children and youth. Our approach informs our methodological choices as well as our findings.

Trauma-informed care has inspired trauma-informed design in architecture and spatial design [ 68 ] to embed the care principles in practice including safety, trustworthiness, choice, collaboration and empowerment [ 10 ]. Gill [ 59 ] explains that the goal of trauma-informed design is to create physical spaces that promote safety, wellbeing, and healing by incorporating the principles of trauma-informed care into design. The application areas of trauma-informed design include, but are not limited to, architecture, interior design, landscape design, and user experience design [ 10 ]. Specific applications of trauma-informed design are seen in creating safe classroom environments to guide students [ 50 ], and creating school environments (including student-teacher relations) that centers SEL [ 6 ]. Additionally, Bollo [ 10 ] explores trauma-informed design of supportive housing in the justice system, such as the Hope Street women's residential center that incorporates the value of safety in design at all stages of the counseling services [ 62 ].

In HCI, technology to support users with trauma has been discussed in recent years. For example, Mythos is a video game designed for improving communication between social workers and children who experience difficulty to express themselves due to a history of traumatic experiences [ 69 ]. While there is tremendous potential for technology to support users with traumatic experiences, there are significant challenges in trauma-centered design. Chen et al. [ 16 , p. 1] noted that “digital technologies can create or exacerbate traumatic experiences”, proposing a technical framework for trauma-informed HCI. Adapting SAMHSA's trauma-informed approach principle [ 45 ], Chen et al. urge experts to design systems to avoid exacerbating injuries such as trauma. Elizabeth et al. [ 1 ] also noted in their study that the sensitive traumatic experiences of patients can be difficult to recall and talk about, and that the need to obtain information about traumatic experiences while being respectful and caring for participants poses a barrier for researchers and designers. Therefore, during the research and design process for trauma-informed design, we needed a method that prevents potential re-traumatizing and harm to those we design for.

2.3 Designing with proxies

Co-design [ 12 ] and participatory design [ 52 ] approaches provide flexibility to engage with vulnerable participants in co-production of knowledge and design outcomes. For example, Makhaeva and colleagues proposed balancing structures and freedoms to tailor creative spaces for children diagnosed with autism and designers to collaborate and overcome the normative expectations in co-design experiences [ 55 ]. However, working with traumatized people remains a challenge for collaborative design and evaluation due to risks of re-traumatizing participants with unprocessed emotional experiences [ 41 , 86 ].

Working with proxies is an alternative method to enable engagement with stakeholders who have deep insight into a person's experience. A proxy is a person or group of people who provide critical input to generate an understanding of another person or group's experience [ 60 ]. A number of examples provide context for this approach. In the hospice systems and services, family members may act as proxies to assess the patient's quality of life [ 78 ]. Moreover, parents as proxies are identified in medical reports [ 75 ]. In the criminal justice system, in the process of investigating and helping survivors of assault, proxies may participate in the investigation to avoid additional harm through re-traumatizing of victims or when victims are being treated for acute trauma [ 5 , 19 ]. Campbell et al. [ 14 ] noted that considering the challenges inherent to such contexts, working with a proxy is an alternative option to working with participants for researchers or service providers (e.g. doctors). Working with a proxy therefore fits well in trauma-informed research. An important consideration in working with proxy actors is developing an ethical paradigm for representation.

Research with proxies has also been explored in HCI in relation to dementia and older adults [ 20 ], children with severe anxiety in hospitals [ 2 , 90 ], mental health interventions [ 54 ], and more. However, there is still a need for concrete practical guidelines suited for various contexts due to complexity of this approach [ 20 ]. PD with Proxies (PDwP) is a method that involve proxies such as parents and teachers in a participatory design process [ 11 , 41 , 44 ]. Hamidi et al. [ 41 ] used PDwP to collect input from multiple stakeholders such as speech-language pathologists, to design a therapeutic and learning focused digital media system for children with limited communication means. Their findings indicated proxies who were familiar with children and had built intimacy over time, contributed to the project by mediating communication and interpreting the child's cues. However, Hamidi et al. [ 41 ] point to the issue of representation, noting that input from proxies is not the same as input from actual users, and data is perceived and translated at multiple points by proxies as well as researchers. Additionally, Dai and Moffatt [ 20 ] propose a number of guidelines for research with proxies based on evidence from working with dementia dyads including carers and the person diagnosed with dementia. The authors suggested extensive engagement with communities to establish trust and deep understanding, thereby involving relevant proxies and choosing appropriate approaches for the context, triangulating multiple sources of information, assessing the power dynamics (in the context of dyads), and using multi-sensory prompts. The researchers also offer practical advice for working with dyads of dementia patients and carers [ 20 ]. As illustrated in our methods section, our approach highly aligns with these guidelines even though our study was conducted before these results were published. We intend to extend the existing guidelines for dyadic research with proxies by providing insight into the context of trauma-informed care and SEL for children.

3 BACKGROUND

The study presented in this paper builds on previous research [ 54 ] that examined the key characteristics of reflective experiences typically offered in the context of SEL therapeutic processes to children with a history of trauma. We conducted an extensive ethnography using methods such as contextual inquiry at a mental health clinic, followed by co-designing a set of tools for deep exploration through reflective narrative building and visual storytelling. We validated our findings through a case study with a parent and a child. Throughout the process, therapists at the clinic were engaged as informants (providing input about the process and philosophy of care) and proxies (when piloting the generative tools designed for the workshop). The site of our research is a mental health facility in Western Sydney (NSW, Australia), offering treatment to children, many of whom present with a history of trauma of the type the therapists describe as relational trauma, developmental trauma, or attachment disruption (with parents or guardians). Children referred to the clinic often have difficulty managing their emotion and behaviors in social situations (at home or school) where they can be triggered to feel unsafe. They are often impacted by cumulative trauma and/or intergenerational trauma (where both parents and children are affected). Occasionally there may be a specific trauma (such as a particular event or series of events), but it is rare for this to be the only presenting issue. The therapists in the clinic focus on supporting repair or strengthening of the parent-child relationship.

Our research contributed the safety, connection and reflection framework [ 54 ] for designing reflective SEL experiences for children with trauma, see Fig 2 . The framework suggests that interventions must fulfill the needs of a child (safety, relatedness, empathy and social resilience) and generate an SEL experience that guides the child through pre-reflection, reflection and post-reflection . This can be achieved by mapping the SEL therapy goals across a reflecting process: starting with adults signalling safety to the child, building trust, facilitating emotional expressions (validation of feeling, articulating emotions through labels and identifying ruptures in social relationships caused by the behavior), exploring alternative worldviews and behaviors, and ultimately transferring the learning to repair the relationship. The framework provides a scaffolding for designing technology mediated interventions or design activities in a research context.

We identified three technology directions for supporting emotion coaching of children with history of trauma (rather than addressing the trauma itself): technology to support existing therapy activities and SEL goals, technology for children to transfer their SEL learning from therapy in clinic to everyday life, technology to help parents emotion coach their child [ 54 ]. In a follow-up study presented in this paper, we continue our research by focusing on the latter direction, as parents, carers and family of children with history of trauma are often regarded as resources for emotion coaching of children.

Our first study showed that creating an environment for pre-reflection is critical for establishing safety and connection with the child, both in moments of high emotional arousal (e.g. anger), as well as in the long term. Specifically, we identified 10 practical strategies for pre-reflections to guide actions by therapists, parents and carers: physical proximity, creating predictability, building routine interactions, taking space, filtering triggers, physical holding of the child, signalling safety, using visual timetables, building trust in interactions, and guiding response. These are particularly important actions to put into practice when families transition out of the therapy context and into the home environment. For this reason, we focused our follow up study on investigating how potential technology directions might support pre-reflection at home. Because demonstrating or discussing an experience of high emotional intensity can be triggering to children and their families, a methodological challenge emerges. We therefore develop a specific dyadic trauma-informed proxy approach suited for our PD process. We present our extensive reflections on our PD research with proxies, to contribute key lessons learned. We believe this approach can be helpful in design and HCI research in similar contexts that involve working with vulnerable individuals and therefore we further generate a set of guidelines in our discussion section to put it in practice.

4 METHOD AND APPROACH

We organized co-design workshops [ 80 ] at a mental health clinic in Western Sydney, Australia. The clinic offers trauma-informed services on social emotional learning to children and families. We recruited participants through the research team's contacts and advertisement at the clinic, predominantly engaging psychologists and social workers, who are collectively referred to as therapists in this context. We prioritized creative workshop activities to speculate how technologies may support pre-reflection in parent-child relationships at home. Specifically, we used co-design due to its flexibility for open-ended interactions, role playing and exploring ambiguous situations using generative tools [ 70 ] such as props and templates. In the first phase of our research, we found the therapists at the clinic were familiar with role playing and improvisation techniques through both their training and practice (e.g. they often role play with families during therapy sessions). Role playing was deemed particularly appropriate in this research as it allows proxies to voice values, assets and needs in relation to a given situation [ 12 ]. We collected data through audio and video recording and artifacts made during the sessions. The project was reviewed and approved by the Sydney Children's Hospitals Network Human Research Ethics Committee, reference LNR/18/SCHN/271. Written consent was obtained from all participants.

4.1 Adapting the Safety, Connection and Reflection framework

For the co-design workshops, we considered dyads of proxies, representing parents and children through enacted role play. We wanted to see how parents might use technologies to demonstrate important pre-reflection strategies while also embedding SEL norms and rules such as not passing judgement/shame, not avoiding emotions, taking time to listen, and being patient to fulfil the child's need for safety and relatedness in an emotionally difficult situations (Fig 2 ). Based on our framework, this requires signalling safety, building trust and facilitating emotional expressions. Our previous work identified 10 specific strategies to achieve that. Therefore, for co-design sessions we planned for dyads to role play and implement these strategies whilst we observed how the scenarios unfold, and how specific technological qualities emerge. This necessitated developing instructions, resources and generative tools for the co-design sessions.

4.2 Workshop protocol development and iteration

A workshop protocol was developed using the 10 pre-reflection strategies, and the scenarios to illustrate each strategy. We used open-ended and flexible generative tools; 10 strategy descriptions and scenario cards to prepare proxies for role playing, props such as paper prototypes to use during the role play, and reflection sheets for after the role play. The Scenario Card (Fig 3 ) is used to assist participants in role playing, and consisted of elements based on the framing of who (proxy roles), what (technology prop), when (time of interaction), where (place of interaction), and how (strategy embedded in interaction) [ 56 ]. We used three props in the form of paper prototypes for smart-phone, tablet, and a toy, all of which looked ambiguous and blank, as shown in Fig 3 . Inspired by the body-storming technique [ 80 ], we planned to use the props as surprise additions that are thrown into a role playing scenario by the facilitator, to compel the participants to discover the object's potentials. Finally we prepared reflection sheets with two questions to facilitate a discussion at the end of the workshop; (Q1) What were the challenges when using the tool, and (Q2) What do you want the technology to do to support the proxy role with the strategy. We printed the cards and reflection sheet, and provided post-it notes to participants.

The workshop protocol went through a number of iterations, first within the research team, and then in consultation with two therapists at the clinic. During a pilot testing of the workshop protocol, we found that the 10 scenarios we had developed were challenging to adopt and enact. Participants had a difficult time getting into the roles and relating to the situation. They suggested to instead, create open-ended scenario templates. Dyads of proxies would first have a conversation about their real-world experiences, and then develop a scenario based on a real-world client case linked to a given strategy suggested to the client (e.g. parent managing physical proximity in a difficult emotional situation with the child), which they would then enact. The assumed roles by participants would be negotiated between the participants. This worked well in a second pilot test.

4.3 Workshop Protocol

The final workshop was facilitated by four HCI researchers and included four activities, see Fig 4 . We aimed to help participants slowly transform out of their therapist role and into the proxies. Activity 1 “warm up” invites participants to form a circle and walk around the room in pairs while talking to each other. The facilitator would then instruct participants to walk slower/faster, or talk slower/faster, with the aim of helping participants relax and ease them into a collaborative group activity. Activity 2 “develop the scenario” invites participants to form dyads to then reflect on a given strategy and share a recent client case (while maintaining client anonymity) for whom they suggested the strategy. Using the given scenario card, the pair would write down attribute descriptions of the parent (e.g. anxious about doing a good job), child (e.g. aged 6, anxious and easily overwhelmed), a back story of the case, and how they advised the parent(s) to implement the given strategy to create a sense of safety and relatedness.

Activity 3 “role play” invites the dyads to enact the scenarios (each dyad goes to a separate room to minimize distractions). Participants in each room covered 3 to 4 strategies, and in one case the role play involved three proxies (parent, child with history of trauma, sibling of the child). During the role play session in activity 3 (see Fig 4 ), the facilitator would randomly pause the enactment and provide the parent proxy with a prop, asking them to now improvise as if they have a technology in hand (smart phone, tablet, smart toy). Fig 1 illustrates a summarized sample scenario unfolded in one of the enactments. Activity 4 “reflect” invites all participants to meet back in the original room where they had started. Groups from each of the three rooms reviewed and shared their reflections on the scenarios, and the potential technology concepts that emerged through role playing. These were written on the reflection sheet (A3 paper) and post-it notes. Facilitators from each room moderated the reflection discussions.

4.4 Participants

We recruited nine participants (n=9) from the Under 12 y/o Mental Health team located in two clinics. All participants identified as therapists with backgrounds including therapy care worker, clinical psychologist, senior clinical psychologist, and senior social worker, all with extensive experience in trauma-informed care and SEL. The participants’ working experiences ranged from 11 to 31 years (median 17 years). All participants had experience with children within the age range of 3 to 12 years and collaborated on multiple cases together. The entire workshop lasted for 4 hours.

4.5 Data analysis

The recordings from the workshop were transcribed. Two co-authors analysed the data collected using reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) process [ 17 ] by following the six steps of dataset familiarization, data coding, initial theme generation, theme development and review, theme refining and defining, and report write up. The themes were reviewed by the other authors and refined through discussion. A typical RTA is guided by a set of assumptions and values, which for us were represented in our framework (Fig 2 ).

The outcome of the analysis included two high level themes: design qualities to embed in SEL home technology, and roles of parent in implementing SEL strategies at home. The use of thematic analysis enabled us to develop narratives, and hypothesize design attributes and qualities that are critical for supporting various parental roles. Additionally, we used narrative vignettes to unfold the complexities of parental roles. Each vignettes was constructed based on data from multiple sessions with illustrative parent/child dialogues woven into them. Finally, we reviewed our observation notes and participant reflections communicated in the activity 4 (i.e. reflect, see Fig 4 ), in order to provide a critical analysis of barriers and facilitators in working with proxies. This enabled us to locate the lessons learned and develop a set of guidelines for working with proxy dyads in the context of SEL support for children with a history of trauma. As we found great value in both developing and implementing a dyadic proxy engagement grounded in real-world case scenarios, we present these findings separately in section 6 as “reflections on working with proxies” to draw attention to the nuances of the proposed method. These reflections are based on data obtained from the workshop transcripts, especially in activity 4 (reflect), Fig 4 and the facilitators’ observation notes.

5 FINDINGS: DESIGNING TECHNOLOGIES FOR EMOTION COACHING OF CHILDREN

5.1 Design qualities to embed in SEL home technology

In total, 12 conceptual ideas emerged from the role playing corresponding to the 10 strategies. The concepts are summarized in Table 1 (for example S1 for physical proximity) and include 5 phone apps, 3 tablet apps, and 4 tangible interactive toys/objects. The variation of concepts across the 10 strategies is due to some props not generating satisfactory ideas in some sessions or participants asking for an additional prop spontaneously. For example, in a role played scenario for S5, proxies responded well to the toy and tablet props but not the phone prop because the small phone screen was not appropriate for sharing between multiple users, hence the two concepts instead of three. All concepts aim to support pre-reflection in parent-child interaction during difficult situations, e.g. when the child experiences tensions but finds it challenging to form a proportionate response. As an example, one scenario demonstrated a proxy parent attempting to manage tensions between siblings (as shown in Fig 1 ). The parent is met with the proxy child's refusal to communicate. The proxies implemented a strategy for filtering triggers (S5 Table 1 ) using a squeeze toy to shift the child's focus and then a tablet app with a personalized character to mediate the emotion coaching and conversation. In another example, an anxious pair of parent-child with a history of trauma (domestic violence) experience a difficult situation when physical affection was not received as expected. This triggered aggression in the proxy child (running, screaming). In a moment of frustration, the pair used a squeeze toy to shift focus, after which the proxy parent coached the child to expel energy before moving towards a safe physical holding strategy (S6).

Based on the observation of role played scenarios and participant reflections obtained during the workshop, we identified four design attributes that appear critical for implementing SEL strategies in transformative and meaningful ways. These attributes represent patterns of social interactions and quality of experiences that were repeatedly demonstrated across role played scenarios, regardless of the specific SEL strategies or technology props used. As we outline these attributes, we refer to specific strategies numbered on Table 1 and scenarios that unfolded around those.

Using the technology to mediate conversation through non-direct shared interaction . Across various strategy implementations, participants wanted the technology to provide a non-direct shared interaction between the parent and the child, “[P5] sometimes our facial expressions can be triggering, particularly when we're experiencing high levels of stress, we misread each other, and see threat... [so in this situation] we don't have to have direct contact.” This typically involved shifting the focus away from the child (who is experiencing strong emotions) to something external, e.g. sharing media or tangible objects. This externalizing of emotions was relevant to nearly all enacted strategies, to enable mediation of conversations without raising the tensions in the situation. As an example, in attempting to create predictability (S2) in a situation, participants suggested a toy to touch, squeeze and look at together, which created sensory stimulation and an avenue to communicate and converse.

Shifting the child's attention away from the trigger is key to effective emotion coaching. From the child's perspective, “[P4] I had a break from having to talk about going to school [...] it was like a break from the trigger. Like I feel this sense of live animation of something that's connected to my parent [...] like it was really important that it was my mom's heart [visualized, externalized and simulated through the prop], and that I could see her moving in reaction to the way that I was interacting with it.” Proxy parents felt this would also provide enough time for them to think of an emotion coaching strategy to use and help create a safe space for connection. For example when using physical proximity (S1), a child proxy noted, “[P6][the prototype] actually allowed mom to be close to me [child] without physically hurting. It can be intense.” Parent proxies therefore used the technology to shift the attention from face-to-face and create a situation where they can interact with something in parallel. The technology here alleviates the emotional tension to prepare the pair for communication and pre-reflection.

Enabling emotion expression through visual aids . Proxy parents surfaced, through role playing, challenges they faced in understanding the proxy child during emotional outbursts. Here they explored barriers to implementing emotion coaching. To prepare a safe space to reflect on the relational and affective experiences as they unfold, interactive technology can support emotional expression by both parent and child. This contributes to the pre-reflection and preparation to communicate in effective ways, a key component of SEL (as shown in Fig 2 ). During the role play, one proxy parent used the tablet prop as a drawing board to communicate with the child proxy who then noted, “[P5] I couldn't communicate [...] So I had to just draw.” The intervention demonstrates the value of an open-ended medium for creative and personalized expressions of affect. Building trust and interaction were found inextricably intertwined, “[P9] in order to build trust and interaction, they [parent] need to interact with him [child] in a different way, around being able to see his behavior as a form of communication, rather than responding to him as a source of stress or a source of disappointment,” while also pointing out that technology is not a magic bullet, “[P1] One thought that I had [about the strategies] is... cause I think parents might think that it's like a magic bullet, [if they think in this way] it's set up to fail... You don't just be like, okay, here you go, do your feelings, come and tell me when you're calm with the iPad.” A calm and non-judgmental space is essential for emotional expressions and coaching.

Offering resources in or after moments of disruptive behavior . Proxy parents noted that the mental capacity to recall specific SEL strategies is compromised during or after intense situations fueled with tensions, disruptive behavior and outbursts. Here access to resources can be offered through technology as refresher for parents who have already learned about the 10 strategies (during a therapy period) and are in need of a pre-reflection moment to recall and strategize. In one example, the proxy parent experienced an intense moment of frustration when they felt they were not connecting well with the proxy child and grabbed a phone prop to look up information about emotion coaching, “[P1] there might be some prompts that I received from the therapist around what might be really going on [with the child] when he's rejecting me,” adding the information may also “[P1] help her [parent] realize what will help them [child], compared to what they [parent] think will help them [child]” . Additionally, parents may also need information and resources after the event, “[P2] Something that a parent could go to after there had been a moment of rupture with the kids later on,” adding that “[P2] having something on the phone is going to actually be the most likely thing to fit in rather than reading a book” , as it is the most convenient way to obtain information given the ubiquity of smart phones.

Assist with down regulating emotional arousal level . Down regulating the emotion is key to signaling safety and building trust in the relationship in the pre-reflection stage. This does not mean avoiding the emotion, rather creating an environment for safe expressions from a low arousal state. Participants suggested technology can be used as a platform to help expel energy (note this was used as a strategy to create a reflective research environment in the previous study, for example by allowing play time before a workshop [ 54 ]). Exerting energy was shown through physical movements and active play by participants in the scenario enactments. As an example, a proxy child expressed intense emotions when the proxy parent attempted to communicate an emotion coaching strategy is a scenario taking place in a car while the proxy parent was driving. Participants noted this to be an urgent situation that necessitates intervention to down regulate the emotion. Both proxy parents and children were drawn to the tangible toy, for example throwing it. This appeared to help them both down regulate their emotion in a heightened scenario, “[P4]...in terms of getting to the point where I could think about doing physical holding [S6] that was helpful, particularly when I throw it too.” The proxy child noted that the toy helped them to “[P6] be able to use all that energy in that force in a way, and it was good when mom joined in.”

5.2 Roles of parent in implementing SEL strategies at home

Our observations revealed a change in parental role during the role play, switching between being a peer , supporter or carer . When the child's emotions became heightened, the proxy parent's first reaction usually involved taking the role of a carer or peer, followed by changing into the role of a supporter after the introduction of the prop in role play. The technology intervention here becomes an anchor that encourages support in the form of an emotion coaching strategy to respond to the situation which can then continue on in other forms. We present three vignettes next to capture the nuances in definitions and details of the three parental roles of parent as peer, parent as supporter, and parent as carer. Each vignette is constructed based on our analysis of all the role playing data combined. As such, the vignettes present reconstructed narratives rather than specific quotes by individual participants.

Vignette A. Parent as peer . The parent may behave as a peer with an equal social or relational standing as the child, keeping themselves calm while also acknowledging the child's responses towards the trigger. This can help the child to feel safe enough to be vulnerable. During the role play, proxy parents usually switched to the role of the peer when they wanted to express trust and understanding. For example a child exhibits an avoidance behavior and does not engage in communication. The parent offers to accompany the child, “I'm just going to stand closer. Because I know I don't like to be far away when you are upset.” When the child is experiencing high levels of emotional arousal, the parent acting as a peer engages in a calm conversation, to explore what happened from the child's perspective and understand the child emotion. For example, a child is frustrated due to conflicts with a group of friends at school, the parent acknowledges the emotion, “It's going to be really hard. Because I know you really like the group”, or when the child expresses dislike of something, “I know you like biscuits, but that is not fruit and veggies. Oh, I can see you're upset by that.” Parents as peers may also express their own emotions and explain situations from a peer's perspective, “I'd like to be your friend, and that's good that we spend time together but it's going to be tricky because I need to spend time with the other mums as well.”

Vignette B. Parent as supporter . Parents play the role of a supporter to the child, by accepting and empathising with the child's emotions, for example by normalizing the experience of high arousal level emotions while showing the child alternative ways of reacting or behaving based on the emotion. For example, a child is crying and does not want to do their math homework. The parent offers support, “So you're being really brave and you are going to give it a go. So I'm thinking I really should say that, but you have been as brave as you can be when you're doing your math.” In the workshop, we observed a proxy parent in a similar situation trying to visualize the emotion to help the proxy child understand their own feelings, “Sometimes you do get angry but you don't get angry all the time. Sometimes you get angry and it doesn't become a big, big mountain of anger it gets a bit smaller [shows the speculative visualization using the phone prop]. Remember we saw this thing on the smartphone about a volcano blowing up? Like it went everywhere. All over the houses in town. And small volcanos, sometimes your anger is smaller. Sometimes when you play, when the anger gets bigger, scares everyone, and I think that's what they don't like.”

Vignette C. Parent as carer . When parents take the carer role, they develop a keen awareness of the child's key triggers (based on their observations) and respond to the child accordingly. Here the parent tries to communicate and slowly start a conversation, preparing the child and shaping the pre-reflection experience as a stepping stone towards reflection. In one example, a participant took the role of a proxy teacher while two others represented a proxy parent and a child. The teacher tells the parent that her child had a conflict with another student. When the parent tries to ask what happened, the child refuses to talk about it. The parent leads in a carer role, “Yeah, I know. It's unfair. But you know, if you tell me what happened, then I can help you, honey. Come on honey, you need to talk to me, try it: ’this is what the therapist said to me... just try!” It is in a carer role that the parent closely engages with de-escalating the intense situation. In the example, the parent de-escalates by offering a toy to provide a sensory experience and help the child down regulate their emotion, “I'm wondering if right now to help you feel better, you might want to play with this? Just have a little feel about it and try it. Tell me if it helps you feel a bit better.”

6 FINDINGS: WORKING WITH PROXIES

In this section we reflect on our observations of working with participants (therapists) who role played scenarios as dyads of proxy parents and children. Our reflections are supplemented with feedback from participants as the final activities invited them to look back at their role playing and discuss their thoughts and reactions. It is important to note that the enacted scenarios were based on real cases that multiple therapists had already collaborated on or were familiar with. This ensured the ecological validity of the outcomes and feasibility of the approach that were grounded in actual events experienced by clients in real life situations (all identifier details were omitted from the scenarios). At the same time, the real case scenarios only formed the basis for the design activity while leaving open possibilities for speculation such as using technology to aid the parents/guardians with implementing a learned strategy in a heightened situation.

Proxy dyads enacted a network of embodied interactions and affective responses to the facilitator's prompts. They used the space in creative ways; moving, using the existing furniture and objects, reaching out to other participants in the room and inviting them into other proxy roles such as siblings and teachers. At times they blurred the boundaries between participant and facilitator roles. Our participants had deep insight into the scenarios. Through role negotiation and establishing of safety, they experimented with various and intimate forms of emotive interaction using verbal and physical cues while discovering new technology imaginaries. Our thematic analysis of workshop transcripts and our own observation notes resulted in three broad themes: need for intimate knowledge to shape the proxy roles, need for preparation to ground the proxy roles, and need for a safe environment to enact proxy roles.

6.1 The need for intimate knowledge to shape the proxy roles

Our participants believed that shaping the proxy roles required intimate (and in their case professional) knowledge of the complexities involved in the roles, circumstances, and context. They believed such knowledge was needed to complete the proxy profiles in activity 2 (develop the scenario, see Fig 4 ), while leaving enough ambiguity in the roles to be able to respond to other proxy roles in a scenario, [P1] “It's all in how you do it... And the level of skill that is required to actually use any of those things.” Interestingly, participants believed that the workshop activities even have potential to be used as training for the parents including co-parenting “[P1] If it's just one parent that has the App, or where the capacity is for if you [are] co-parenting and there are two of you who handle the situation differently, then where is the opportunity [of] connection for the two of you to reflect together about what you both did, when disagreement comes, and is there a role for that in this, or is it actually each parent does it separately.” Others mentioned another potential to embed a scenario-based training in group therapy meetings for parents whose children might face similar challenges, [P2] “..you know, parents did a similar training to this, but I would give them [this] role plays to begin with...there's an opportunity to do that with other parents and talk training, because I think of the parents with similar kids, similar needs, they gonna be able to role play it.”

We note that while parents have intimate knowledge of the complexities involved in their situation, our participants share an intimate perception of what the parents and children might face at home. Combined with their insight into the application of SEL strategies for pre-reflection and observations of therapy experiences, our participants role play a representation of the scenarios rather than depicting an accurate picture.

6.2 The need for preparation to ground the proxy roles

Participants pointed to the need for preparation for bodystorming component of the activity 3 (role play, see Fig 4 ) in order to ground their speculation of the technology props. For example, they identified an opportunity to receive clear introductions so that they can identify the possibilities that each technology form offers, [P1] “I found [it] very hard because there hadn't been any prep [...] like there had been no introduction to any of the things outside that incident, so it would be different.” Additionally, participants noted that the notion of bodystorming (involving instructions to pause, take a prop and use it immediately) did not quite align with how they would potentially approach technology use in their practice, [P1] “I would imagine if I was doing it with [a] kid, I would do it differently. Like... we would sit [there] with some variety of different things and the kid could choose which ones they wanted to try, kind of [like] that. There [the child] would have had a familiarity with it [the technology] outside the moment... that would really [be] important.” Therefore, providing the proxies with a clear introduction to the technology props, the freedom to choose from a range of props and the freedom to perform as they normally do in their practice, is important. This enables them to ground the role in their reality and a familiar setting. As a result, the workshop environment would be more comfortable for role playing. Additionally, participants suggested that preparation may extend to opportunities to personalize the technology prop in a meaningful way so that they would ground their understanding of the purpose of the props, [P2] “preparation on personalizing it and [their] difference. Understanding what is it, and why we using it.”

6.3 The need for a safe environment to enact proxy roles

We found that creating a safe environment for participants was important to enable the enactment. In our process, this was implemented through the opportunity for the proxies to communicate the scenarios and negotiate the roles in advance. This was essential because the enactment centered intense emotions and in some instances physical contact. We believe the warm up in activity 1 and initial communication during activity 2 (develop the scenario, see Fig 4 ) before the role play helped the proxies to feel prepared, which created a safe environment for them to role play. In one scenario, the proxy child role involved outbursts, which might be characterized as aggression. The proxy child discussed what might happen, [P6] “It can be, I guess, kicking the seat, saying I'm not getting out of the car. And then, mom trying to just sort of cozy him.” In contrast, there was an instance with a different set of participants, which involved intense emotions. Because the initial communication was not effective, the proxy parent was taken by surprise and was lost for words during the enactment. Negotiating the details of the roles beforehand was also helpful for proxies to identify which role was appropriate for them to enact, and what felt comfortable and safe for them.

7 DISCUSSION

From our findings we derive and contribute two sets of guidelines. The first is a set of three design guidelines (DG) for exploring trauma-informed technologies for parents and children to engage in pre-reflection at home (RQ1). A set of seven research guidelines (RG) then contribute new directions for dyadic trauma-informed PD with proxies in early childhood and adolescent mental health context (RQ2) and potential applications to other contexts.

7.1 Design guidelines: Trauma-informed reflective technologies for parents and children

  1. Designing for pre-reflection across multiple purposes
  2. Designing for pre-reflection across multiple roles
  3. Designing for pre-reflection to support multiple states

Our findings suggest a holistic view of affective interactions is essential for embedding SEL strategies in home technologies and support parents in emotion coaching of children with history of trauma. Affective Interaction encompasses relational interactions with artefacts, technologies and systems and is underlined by emotional responses [ 47 ]. Affective interactions form part of the human capacity for reflection and meaning-making, and can motivate, shape and inform our behaviors and responses to the world around us [ 54 ]. Affective interaction in the context of work presented in this paper necessitates a situated, temporal and relational understanding of affect as an embodied experience: a perspective that is central to SEL strategies as outlined in our research. This view follows a tradition of affect as an experience that can only be interpreted by people due to its situated quality [ 7 ]. The interpretation aspect is critical in our work due to the presence of trauma, which changes the individual's responses to the emotional events, and their interpretation of relationships and safety. Taking on the multi-faceted lens of affective interaction, we examine the findings in our research and propose three design guidelines to foster emotion coaching and SEL strategies in home technologies for parents and guardians.

DG1. Designing for pre-reflection across multiple purposes . Theofanopoulou and Solvak [ 79 ] summarize three key aspects for emotion socialization technologies (reacting to the child's emotion, discussing the child's emotion, and parents expressing emotions), of which only the first two were evident in their empirical data. All three aspects were raised in different ways by our proxies who also extended the functionality of pre-reflection technologies to information and resource seeking. Specifically, our participants noted technology features such as using media for focus shifting and down-regulating emotions through activities or games to expel energy (reacting to the child's emotion as well as helping parents express emotions), mediating conversations (discussing the child's emotion), visualizing emotions (to both discuss the child's emotion as well helping parents express their emotion) and educational resources and refresher modules to support parents in transition out of therapy context. We propose flexible yet multi-purpose design to seamlessly combine the identified technology features and qualities across the 10 SEL strategies in order to support various parental roles. Such technology must provide opportunities for parents and children to collaborate and prepare to reflect together. For example, we speculate a phone/tablet app paired with a soft interactive toy (see Fig 5 ). The app may display resources for parents (in form of learning modules with descriptions and examples of the 10 SEL strategies), scheduling features to create predictability of daily plans and chores for the child (e.g. doing homework), and a gamified feature for visualizing emotions through shooting stars (to offer a shared interaction to aid discussion and mediation) when the interactive toy is squeezed to activate embedded sensors (to expel energy through a tangible and embodied interaction). Nearly all these features were proposed by proxy dyads in our study, however this speculative concept is not evaluated and serves as a demonstration of possibilities identified through our research. Further research is needed to not only examine its feasibility, but also examine the suitability of technology as trauma is complex and the needs of children with history of trauma are situated.

DG2. Designing for pre-reflection across multiple roles . Our findings illustrate that emotion coaching of children with history of trauma at home demands enacting multiple parental roles: as peers, supporters, and carers of children. Flexible reflective technologies are therefore needed. This view is inherently complex, acknowledges vulnerability and makes space for it in different ways. To value vulnerability in children with changing developmental needs, we must make space for the parent to be vulnerable with the child by enacting different levels of social standing towards the child, ranging from equal standing of a peer, to empathetic role of a supporter, to carer with a keen sense of intention and awareness of trauma triggers. We understand vulnerability here as a social and relational construct that is temporally and historically situated, changing over time and through relationships. We do not take lightly that safety and trust are pre-requisites for vulnerability that must be nurtured through trauma-informed processes. Our findings show that the parental roles are dynamic and evolving when faced with intense emotional conflicts and tensions at home. Parents also employ different coping strategies as they move across these roles. In this process, technology interventions can offer prompts to shift between roles. For instance, in one scenario (S6 Group 2 Physical holding in Table 1 ), an emotionally overwhelmed proxy parent facing the proxy child's outburst used the technology prop to switch into the role of a supporter, to begin emotion coaching. We propose technologies should take into account the process of changing roles while cultivating opportunities for multiple users or dyads (of parent/child) to work together. We view such technology as a platform to remind, visualize and provide resources to ground the parental roles rather than shaping them.

DG3. Designing for pre-reflection to support multiple states . Our proxy parents speculated new technology imaginaries to use in reaction to intense emotional events centered around the proxy child. We term these as reactive technologies to help parents manage a difficult situation. As an example, sometimes parents need a moment to reflect and identify a strategy to engage with the child, and technology has the potential to provide that moment. As illustrated by our findings, technology can also aid mediation, emotion expression, offer resources and assist with down regulating emotions. This reactionary state, while necessary, was extended by dyads of participants who suggested technologies can be used for proactive pre-reflection as well, for example during parental training (during therapy) or at home to facilitate co-parenting. Parents may also experience intense emotions and require emotion coaching in parallel with their children in order to create or maintain a safe environment, where a sense of relatedness can occur (see the framework in Fig 2 ). As such, considering both child-facing and parent-facing features are appropriate. The proposed holistic view extends previous research findings and acknowledges the complexity of affective experiences and multifaceted engagements and roles needed to care for children with history of trauma. The roles expand across sites (clinic, home) and people (parents, siblings, therapists). We argue consideration of reactive and proactive states of care are inherent to the trauma-informed philosophy that aims to prevent re-traumatizing of individuals.

7.2 Research guidelines: Dyadic trauma-informed participatory design with proxies

  1. Set up a flexible study with open ended instructions, generative tools and props that can be used in multiple ways and cultivate ambiguity
  2. Recruit proxies with intimate knowledge of the individuals and contexts they represent
  3. Co-create enactment scenarios based on real-world scenarios and use generative tools that are in line with participant's existing practice
  4. Negotiate proxy roles to create space for the proxies to prepare for enactment
  5. Create a safe environment by providing agency and flexibility for proxies to take the enactment to new directions
  6. Encourage reflexivity by opening up time during and after enactment for reflection and discussion
  7. Provide trauma-informed debriefing, counseling information and resources to researchers and proxies

Through careful consideration underpinned by ethical intention, we examine the reflection on our research process to propose seven guidelines for co-designing with proxies using a trauma-informed approach. We echo Dai and Moffatt's [ 20 ] recommendation for tailoring dyadic research guidelines to different contexts of working with proxies.

Setting up a flexible study (RG1) should prioritize striking a balance between familiarity (e.g. of the workshop context) and ambiguity of the generative tools that are shaped creatively by participants. For example, writing a case report is a familiar task for many therapists (and healthcare workers) yet turning them into open ended prompts for role playing or using blank technology props without any features, affords the right degree of ambiguity. Caring for children living with the impact of trauma is complex. We are particularly interested in understanding better the experiences of parents transitioning out of therapy and managing highly intense emotions in-situ at home. Engaging proxies with intimate knowledge of the individuals and contexts they represent (RG2) requires a keen awareness of both researcher and proxy positionality. This study followed a year-long ethnographic and participatory research at the clinic [ 54 ] that informed researchers’ positionality. Participants were therapists with specific training that involved enactment techniques, a key asset central to our approach, both to make it feasible and safe to avoid risk of trauma to participants. They had integrated in their care practice a non-judgemental philosophy and had developed sensitivity to attend to their own affective responses to the cases. More research is needed to explore how this can be cultivated in other settings, as there is inherent ethical risk to overlook the conditions that allowed us to operate with sensitivity needed in representing a trauma context. Tonkinwise [ 81 ] considered ethics to be ingrained in design through the act of making. We extend this notion to enactment that embedded making of generative tools by researchers and making of speculated ideas by proxies.

The co-creation of the enactment scenarios based on real world scenarios (RG3) was an essential step for proxies to generate empathy with the roles [ 20 ]. We highly recommend warm-up activities to enable an embodied and spatial awareness in proxies. When physical exertion and emotion simulation is expected in enacted scenarios (such as ours), it would be beneficial for participants to partake in a more physical warm-up. Additionally, warm-down of the body and calming of emotions and the nervous system is advisable, as is common in somatic body awareness practices, and especially in the somatic experiencing approach for treating trauma developed by Peter Levine [ 88 ].

Integration with real-world cases aimed to mitigate the risk of stereotyping the represented dyads (parents/child), by making visible the social, situational, and embodied facets of their trauma. These intricacies must be negotiated carefully (RG4) in order to develop and enact authentic, meaningful and nuanced scenarios. We chose the proxy approach to avoid the risk of re-traumatizing parent/child dyads during our research. This remains a concern even during engagement with proxies. Creating a safe space (RG5) is key as enacting scenes with high emotional intensity can impact proxies as well. Even though our participants were highly immersed in their proxy roles, the boundaries of representation were discussed in advance, which contributed to their feeling of safety. Proxies should be empowered to communicate details, and take the scenario to new directions (for example by asking for a prop when needed) and have time in between scenarios to reposition themselves. Time is the essence of reflexivity (RG6) both during and after the enactment, not only to reflect on details relevant to the research but to de-role (getting out of the proxy role) and then debrief on affective responses to the unfolding scenarios.

Finally, trauma-informed debriefing, counselling information and resources must be provided (RG7) to mitigate the risk of secondary traumatization, which is common among mental health workers as they make sense of clients’ trauma [ 15 ]. Similarly, researchers may be impacted by secondary trauma. Resources provided in our study included information about seeking mental health support (distributed during the consent process), brief researcher training about managing emotions (extended over the entire study period) and holding debrief sessions within the research team. While this approach can be time consuming, it is necessary for risk management. This trauma-informed approach, combined with recruiting proxies who are skilled in enactment (through professional training) and managing the impact of their work, contributed to zero report of adverse effect in our research.

7.3 Ethics, politics and care in dyadic trauma-informed PD with proxies

Our method for dyadic trauma-informed PD with proxies addresses the ethical risks of engaging families with history of trauma to capture emotionally charged situations. The value of this method should be gauged in relation to the politics of participation in PD. We demonstrated the relevance of researcher/designer and participant positionality in our discussion. Research on ideating reflective technologies has already shown that plurality of positionality is an asset in PD, particularly in early stages of technology exploration and critique [ 40 ]. PD with proxies presents a unique situation: it leverages intimate knowledge and embodied experiences of proxies (therapists) whilst it may leave out primary users of the designed outcome (children and families). We do not condone this as a holistic approach, rather we advocate for proxy representation in early phases of discovery to prioritize the safety of vulnerable participants in research or design stages that can harm them. We emphasize this point as we recognize that politics of power and participation have always been part of PD, for example, PD was first used in Scandinavia in reaction to introduction of computer systems in the workplace and changes in distribution of power [ 40 ]. Such recognition is a necessary condition of using our proposed method.

Muller and Druin note “the PD tradition has, from the outset, emphasized mutuality and reciprocity—often in a hybrid space that enabled new relationships and understandings” [ 57 , p. 1131]. As we build on our mutual learning with proxies to deeply explore design spaces we were unable to directly (and safely) access, we propose an active engagement with our power to shape and change care through design. We set an ethical paradigm (see related work) to hold space for representing voices of those who would be omitted due to their vulnerability. We did so by building proxy representations based on real-world client cases, although we acknowledge that what is embodied in enactment is not the same as what is authentically experienced by children and their families. We supplement the enactment with direct accounts of participants in the prior phase of research [ 54 ], which established a broad context of their home life. Our ethical paradigm aims to integrate an ethic of care philosophy that according to Joan Tronto, encompasses “activity that includes everything we do to maintain, continue, and repair our world so that we can live in it as well as possible.”[ 82 , p. 16] Tronto provided a number of important questions that can guide caring judgement including “ how distant from those who give care are those who hold responsibility? ” [ 82 , p. 18]. We interrogate this question as we recognize the limitations and merits of the PD method with proxies to bring us (as those who assume a responsibility of care) closer to those who give care (the therapists) as well as care receivers (families).

8 LIMITATION AND FUTURE WORK

Our study was limited to exploring social situations between one parent and one child. This decision was motivated by the logistics and a study design for dyadic proxy enactment. Future work could expand this line of research to include more complex scenarios, for example co-parenting scenario, family/siblings, or experiences of tension and high intensity emotional episodes outside the home environment. Additionally, the choice of some material and props (phones and tablets) were familiar to our proxies, while one prop item (toy) was more ambiguous in functionality. The familiarity of the former might have limited the participant creativity, a core quality in generative techniques and co-design [ 4 ]. It is possible that familiarity with the affordances of the technology props would lead to proxies to feel a sense of pressure to “design” (with detail) rather than speculate or dream, as Liz Sanders envisioned [ 70 ]. Within this process, we did not co-create or test any specific design concept and therefore the app speculated in Fig 5 remains an example of possible design directions to explore in the future. Finally, a future direction for this project may involve confirming the four design attributes for future technologies with potential users like parents or children. This will require careful planning of the set up, managing expectations and risks of harm or trauma. Note that we do not view technology as a sole solution nor do we believe it is a necessary condition for emotion coaching of children with history of trauma. Rather we explore how we might collaborate with healthcare professionals to examine technology's potentials for supporting existing SEL practices suggested to families for emotion coaching of their child in everyday life.

We identify a number of potential directions for further research. As suggested by our participants, there is potential for exploring educational modules for parents by integrating the safety, connection and reflection framework with our guidelines for role playing to co-create scenarios for pre-reflection. There is immense potential in providing personalized and trauma-informed resources to parents particularly when preparing them for transitioning out of the therapy context and into the home environment where they put SEL strategies into practice. Additionally, participants suggested that the framework and guidelines would be useful assets for mental health workers for internal discussions at the clinic or across teams. The guidelines for dyadic trauma-informed PD may have applications beyond the scope of our project for research and design in other sensitive environments where traditional PD methods might cause re-trauma or unnecessary distress in participants and stakeholders.

9 CONCLUSION

Research with vulnerable participants with a history of trauma poses ethical challenges, which may be addressed through holistic engagement with proxies. Our dyadic trauma-informed PD with proxies illustrated technology imaginaries to aid healing and care of families with trauma background. This is an important opportunity for trauma-informed HCI, however the notion of representation in participatory research with proxies demands careful considerations of proxies’ positionality, as well as depth and situatedness of their knowledge. A commitment to advocacy for those who are being represented by proxies is critical. To ensure authentic representation, proxy roles must mirror real world situations while considering temporal, relational and embodied experiences of affect in trauma cases. A synchronous effort must also embed a trauma-informed approach in every engagement with proxies to secure their safety and comfort. While complex, we speculate that this type of research might have implications beyond designing technologies for children. A trauma-informed participatory design with proxies can surface key hypotheses and questions in HCI, and serve those who may be marginalized due to lack of meaningful engagement or risk of re-traumatizing.
