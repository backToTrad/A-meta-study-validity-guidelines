Humorous Robotic Behavior as a New Approach to Mitigating Social Awkwardness

Viva Sarah Press , Media Innovation Lab, Reichman University, Israel, vspress@gmail.com
Hadas Erel , Media Innovation Lab, Reichman University, Israel, hadas.erel@milab.idc.ac.il

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3580821
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Social awkwardness is a frequent challenge to healthy social interactions and can dramatically impact how people feel, communicate and behave. It is known that humor can invoke positive feelings and enable people to modify perspective of a situation. We explored whether using a non-humanoid robotic object performing humorous behavior can reduce social awkwardness between two strangers. The robot was peripherally incorporated into the interaction to ensure the natural social flow. We compared the impact of humorous and non-humorous robotic gestures on the human-human interaction. Objective and subjective measures indicate that despite being peripheral to the human-human interaction, the humorous robotic gestures significantly reduced the intensity of awkwardness between the strangers. Our findings suggest humorous robotic behavior can be used to enhance interpersonal relationships hindered by awkwardness and still preserve natural human-human interaction.

1 INTRODUCTION

From sharing a morning commute to networking at events, social interactions are not only a fundamental part of our daily lives, they are also important for mental and physical health [ 13 , 82 , 118 , 121 ]. In encounters with strangers specifically, talking to people we do not know has been shown to improve our quality of life [ 39 , 118 ], sense of belonging [ 61 , 82 , 84 ], happiness [ 43 , 61 ] and stress levels [ 65 ]. Yet, interactions with other people can be awkward [ 32 , 56 , 73 ]. Studies have shown that people may avoid initiating new encounters because of possible awkwardness [ 43 , 65 ].

Awkwardness implies a contradiction between what is actually happening and what individuals in the situation perceive should happen [ 31 , 32 , 33 , 73 , 88 ]. Awkwardness can bring about discomfort and embarrassment [ 73 ], as well as self-consciousness or apprehensiveness [ 28 ], negatively impacting communication, perception of the social interaction, individuals’ feelings, and the interaction itself [ 28 , 31 , 73 ].

Whereas awkwardness is often studied in the clinical population, it is a frequent challenge to social interaction in the nonclinical populace as well [ 73 ]. Moreover, awkwardness does not dissipate on its own. Avoiding, ignoring or attempting to control situations of awkwardness typically backfires, causing the awkwardness in an interaction to emerge as even more awkward, embarrassing and uncomfortable [ 32 , 73 ].

Approaches intended to reduce awkwardness in face-to-face human-human interactions include games and ice-breaker activities that have been shown to encourage individuals to meet awkwardness head on and transform this social breach into a better interaction [ 62 , 66 , 124 ]. While such actions have been shown to enhance relationships between strangers, they typically involve challenges including a disruption in natural Human-Human Interaction (HHI) and the need of time to achieve the desired outcome [ 62 , 68 , 124 ].

A more accessible strategy involves the use of humor, which can invoke a positive affective state [ 22 , 24 ], and enable people to quickly distance themselves from a negative scenario (such as social awkwardness) and modify their perspective of the situation [ 32 , 74 , 80 ]. Researchers across many fields, including psychology [ 51 , 134 ], sociology [ 27 ], medicine [ 34 , 42 ], and education [ 142 ] have shown broad importance of humor as a communication tool [ 26 , 42 , 78 , 109 ].

Ancient philosophers to contemporary thinkers agree that humor plays various roles in different domains [ 26 , 27 , 78 , 109 ], and can be applied in myriad ways to serve multiple functions [ 76 , 137 ]. Among humor's key functions is its role in aiding participants of small groups to engage in smooth social interactions [ 27 ]. Humor can regulate conversations [ 21 , 27 , 58 , 105 ], give people the feeling that they are being seen (social presence) [ 12 , 131 ], shape people's interpersonal connections [ 21 , 27 , 76 , 133 ], and relax tensions during social interactions [ 140 ]. In particular, humor can act as an external cue to prompt positive feelings [ 24 , 54 ] and shift perspective [ 74 , 80 ].

Humor is already being employed in the fields of Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) and Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) with the goal of improving interaction experiences [ 91 , 102 , 111 ]. Relevant HCI studies have shown that systems mimicking humorous human emotions as part of their communication mechanisms can improve HCI [ 11 , 35 , 105 ], readiness to cooperate with an agent or system [ 91 , 92 ], and better user experience (UX) [ 10 , 104 ]. In HRI, researchers have tested how humor (via jokes, improvisational humor, gestures and sounds) can influence, improve and ease the quality of interaction and level of enjoyment with robots [ 89 , 99 , 111 , 136 , 139 ]. These studies indicate the potential in using different elements of humor for enhancing interaction between the human and the technology.

The impact of humorous interactions with robots suggests that robotic humor could also positively affect and assist in enhancing awkward situations. This is supported by previous studies indicating that technologies (in general) can be leveraged to support HHI. For example, technology has been shown to enhance conversational dynamics [ 63 , 64 , 67 , 95 ], collocated social interactions [ 112 ], human-human relationship building [ 96 ] and human-human interpersonal evaluation [ 120 ]. Moreover, both human-like and non-human-like technologies were shown to influence face-to-face interactions between people [ 48 , 67 , 123 ].

Within the HRI space, specifically, the study of robot impact on HHI has been termed by some as human-human-robot interaction (HHRI) [ 120 ]. These studies indicate the potential of incorporating a robot in HHI to positively influence family social dynamics [ 50 ], promote trust within a team [ 129 ], promote inclusion in a group context [ 60 ], promote social support between two humans [ 48 ], and more. The positive impact of integration of robots in HHI positions them as strong candidates for enhancing interpersonal humorous experiences in a socially awkward human-human interaction. This positive impact is associated with the social nature of autonomous robotic behavior. Previous studies have indicated that humans automatically interpret the autonomous behavior of (even very simple) robots as social cues [ 40 , 47 ]. This automatic social interpretation affects the perception of the robot as relevant for a social context, making it easy to integrate robotic objects into social HHI and impact an interaction.

Indeed, robots are becoming an integral part of our daily landscape in schools [ 3 , 19 ], homes [ 50 ], healthcare centers [ 7 , 57 ], restaurants [ 106 ] and beyond. Exploring their impact on social awkwardness may position them as a novel strategy to support challenging human-human interactions. Robots can be added to HHI in several ways ranging from leading the social interaction [ 94 , 95 ] to taking a peripheral role [ 48 ]. The latter approach involves different levels of robotic integration into the HHI, always ensuring that the robotic object does not take an equal part in the human-human-robot interaction. A robot can be peripheral to an interaction when designed to be barely noticeable and with an implicit impact on the interaction; or designed to be noticed but to introduce minimal behavior limiting it from becoming an active and equal participant that would alter the dyadic nature of an interaction (by making it a three-party interaction) [ 63 ]. Hence, a peripheral robotic object can be noticed and may become part of the human-human conversation; however, its inability to talk coupled with its minimal gestures ensure that it cannot become an additional active or equal participant in the HHI [ 63 ]. This approach is typically applied by employing non-humanoid robotic objects designed to impact the interaction using minimal communication modalities that commonly involve nonverbal gestures [ 120 , 132 ]. Studies have shown that even with limited communication modalities and using nonverbal gestures to communicate their social intent, non-humanoid robotic objects can positively shape and enhance HHI [ 48 , 63 , 120 ].

In the case of social awkwardness between strangers, it would seem that applying humor to assist and improve an HHI should be done in a peripheral manner so as to avoid interfering in the human-human nature of the interaction.

We present a study based on the accepted benefits that social interactions [ 13 , 118 , 121 ] and humor [ 23 , 27 , 76 , 79 , 93 ] can have on HHI, and the detrimental effects social awkwardness [ 32 , 33 , 43 , 73 ] may bring to these very same interactions. Our study involves two conditions: an Experimental condition, in which the robotic object performs humorous behavior during a stranger-stranger interaction in an awkward social situation; and a Baseline condition, in which the robotic object performs non-humorous behavior during a stranger-stranger interaction in an awkward social situation (see Figure 1 ). The robotic object (used with permission [ 63 ]) in both conditions was originally designed to be peripheral to HHI (see Figure 2 ).

The nonverbal robotic gestures used in this study were previously shown to evoke either a humorous or non-humorous experience [ 116 ]. Using both subjective and objective measures, we tested the impact the robot's gestures had on HHI and whether its humorous behavior, specifically, could serve as a new approach to mitigating social awkwardness between two strangers during a first conversation.

Our main contribution is within the field of technologies being designed to support and enhance HHI:

  • we contribute to research on human-human-technology interaction, showing that a robot performing humorous gestures can potentially influence HHI in the context of social awkwardness;
  • we provide a new approach for mitigating social awkwardness among strangers during a first conversation, showing that robotic humor can positively enhance the interaction without meddling in the HHI

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Approaches to assist in reducing social awkwardness among strangers

When a situation of social awkwardness is handled with a proactive approach, humans are able to enjoy better social interactions [ 32 , 62 , 66 , 124 ]. Team-building activities have been shown to reduce social awkwardness and uncomfortable feelings in group interactions [ 62 ]. For example, Hanrahan and Pedro [ 62 ] showed how taking part in mutual exercise sessions in close physical proximity, requiring interpersonal skills and cooperation, led participants to discover similarities. These common experiences were then leveraged to improve interaction among a group of strangers assigned a team task of dancing together. Participants who had first exercised together reported feeling less awkward during the intimate dance task and were even open to touching one another [ 62 ].

Humor can also be used as an intervention to overcome social awkwardness [ 32 , 33 , 73 ]. With deep roots in psychological research as a coping strategy [ 51 , 134 ], humor has been shown to deter effects of negative experiences or feelings through reinterpretation, reassessment and resolution [ 1 , 23 ]. Clegg [ 32 ] reported that by telling jokes to address a verbal blunder directly, participants quickly transformed an awkward conversation into a more comfortable situation [ 32 ]. Another study indicated that explicitly making the awkwardness of a situation the butt of a joke led to a more enjoyable social interaction [ 33 ]. Of course, not everyone is comfortable with being the joke-teller and results of this approach will vary due to cultural and contextual differences in verbal humor [ 107 ].

Meanwhile, in the HCI domain, Huggard et. al. [ 66 ] designed the Musical Embrace digital game to create an uncomfortable interaction of social awkwardness between players and allow them to revamp, repair and take control of this social phenomenon by participating in the game experience. The digital game is a tool to reduce social awkwardness in a new collaboration by breaching physical proximity norms as it requires two players (strangers) to hug a pillow controller in order to navigate a virtual world. Some participants improvised new behaviors to succeed in the game, thereby decreasing the overall awkwardness and creating a more engaging entertainment experience [ 66 ]. Technology can also be used to reduce social awkwardness through facilitating interactions. For example, Jarusriboonchai et al. [ 68 ] showed that a mobile multiplayer quiz game could aid in small group introductions, especially on the first encounter. The turn-based conversation facilitator created a comfortable way for participants to get to know the strangers in their group. They reported that the game helped create a relaxed and playful atmosphere among participants. However, the game also presented a challenge to the natural HHI, as participants were constrained by the game's predefined topics and switched topics when signaled to do so, even when they were in the middle of speaking [ 68 ].

In the context of HRI, Ohshima et al. [ 110 ] tested a robotic intervention to overcome awkward silences in conversations. They used a robovie-mR2 robot to say “ummm” as a vocal filler and stroke its chin as a physical filler during a human-robot conversation to improve social interaction, particularly during awkward silences [ 110 ]. Findings indicated that these fillers could help mitigate awkwardness in verbal human-robot interactions and prompt them to be more effective [ 110 ]. Meanwhile, Sadka et al.’s [ 123 ] robotic bar-stools, which turn inwards or outwards as a first move of a social interaction, have shown to assist in overcoming awkwardness during opening encounters between strangers. The minimal orientation change, turning participants toward one another, was sufficient to promote engagement. Some participants who had been turned inward, reported that the bar-stools’ movement helped convert feelings of awkwardness into a fun, social part of the experience [ 123 ].

While these and similar studies have offered varying interventions aimed at keeping social awkwardness from sabotaging social interactions, our study presents a peripheral approach that can help bring about a positive social experience and support the interaction in a context of social awkwardness, while at the same time enabling the social encounter to retain its natural character.

2.2 HCI and HRI integration in HHI

In recent years, HCI and HRI researchers have evaluated social experiences in human-human-computer interaction and human-human-robot interaction contexts. It has been shown that interface agents, computer systems, and robots (human-like, zoomorphic-like, and non-human-like), can assist in human-human dialogue [ 64 , 67 ], moderate conversational dynamics during team problem-solving tasks [ 132 ], increase social support between two humans [ 48 ], and positively influence human-human interpersonal evaluation [ 120 ].

Interface agents and computer systems have shown to impact HHI positively during conversation tasks. For example, an interface agent designed as an animated dog and that mimicked the role of a party host during cross-cultural conversations in a virtual environment, was shown to influence how participants cope with awkward pauses [ 67 ]. The animated interface offered topic suggestions, then faded into the background to allow the HHI to continue uninterrupted. Findings indicated that the communications assistant agent led to a positive effect on perception of the experience, self-assessments, ratings of conversation partners and ratings of a partner's cultural group [ 67 ]. Meanwhile, Hohenstein and Jung's [ 64 ] study tested the influence of an AI assistant mediating a text messaging conversation via suggested text responses in the Google Allo messaging app. Participants reported the agent may have influenced how and what they communicated, explaining it guided them in the conversation [ 64 ].

In the HHRI arena, researchers have shown how non-humanoid robots can influence HHI in group interactions. Micbot, a peripheral robotic object resembling a microphone, was shown to increase participant engagement in a problem-solving task and promote more balanced participation by turning toward passive group members through the use of nonverbal gestures [ 132 ]. Meanwhile, Jung et al. [ 70 ] reported that a standard robotic arm employing an unequal responsive behavior could negatively influence a collaboration task between two humans. Participants were assigned with building the highest possible tower from wooden blocks given to them by the robotic arm, which distributed blocks in equal or unequal conditions. The block allocation by the robot affected participants’ perception of one another, with participants in the unequal allocation condition giving a significantly lower evaluation of their partner [ 70 ].

Erel et al. [ 48 ] also indicated that a non-humanoid robot can enhance HHI. Using empathy-related, nonverbal gestures the robot increased the perception of emotional support in an intimate interaction between two human participants. The robotic object, integrated as a side-participant in the conversation, showed that it could significantly improve the quality of emotional support provided by one participant to another in the interaction even though participants perceived the robot as peripheral to the HHI [ 48 ]. Similarly, Rifinski et al.’s [ 120 ] study designed the same robotic object to perform gestures that would promote calm, non-aggressive human-human conversation. Participants’ interpersonal evaluation was higher and the quality of the interaction between them significantly improved when the robotic object showed attentiveness to their conversation. Results also showed that participants accepted the robot's assigned role and did not expect it to take an active or equal part in the conversation [ 120 ].

Collectively, these works show that agents and robots can shape HHI and influence how people interact with one another when technology is in a central or peripheral role. However, these studies have not tested the possible impact of a robot's humorous behavior on an interaction between two strangers during a situation of social awkwardness.

2.3 Humor in HCI and HRI

According to the Computers Are Social Actors (CASA) paradigm [ 97 ], the affective computing domain [ 115 ], and other studies [ 17 , 117 ], humorous computer systems, virtual agents and robots should be able to communicate similar benefits humor has in HHI in HCI and HRI as well. As such, HCI and HRI studies are testing and applying different elements from humor theories [ 93 ] to find what works best in human-technology interaction. There are studies simulating humorous emotions as part of their communication mechanisms to positively impact acceptance and interaction with a technology [ 11 , 35 , 36 , 99 , 111 ], alter perceptions of a technology [ 37 , 99 , 111 , 139 ], build a more trusting relationship between user and interface [ 15 ], ease and improve HRI and HCI [ 101 , 111 ], and enhance likability of the technology [ 87 , 90 , 91 , 92 , 139 ]. For example, Buttussi and Chittaro [ 19 ] indicated that an animated interface agent (human caricature) increased the perception of sociability and likability of the agent when it used humorous emotions (facial expressions, voice tone, body language) and humorous comments, puns and jokes during a tablet-based task. Participants watched the agent's lesson on how to sit during an emergency flight landing in either humor, fear, or neutral conditions. Results showed that participants in the humor condition liked the application more than those in the other two groups [ 19 ]. In the work of Morkes et al. [ 91 ], participants who received humorous comments from a computerized humorous interaction partner in two experiments, evaluated the “partner” as more competent and likable, and showed an increased willingness to cooperate with it. Moreover, participants reported the humor was not distracting to the tasks [ 91 ]. Similarly, HRI studies have shown humor's positive effect on likability [ 87 , 89 , 130 ]. For example, Mirnig et al. [ 89 ] tested the effect of different types of robot laughter and the level of likability. In the study, participants observed an iCat and a NAO robot laughing either with self-irony or Schadenfreude , and then rated the likability of the robot's performance. Participants rated positively attributed humor higher than gloating humor [ 89 ].

A majority of studies in HRI have used verbal humor (jokes, puns or witty texts) [ 6 , 135 ] and robotic behavior that mimics human behavior [ 99 , 111 , 128 ]. For example, Niculescu et al. [ 99 ] analyzed the effects of humor, voice pitch and empathy in a social interaction with an Olivia roboceptionist [ 100 ]. The researchers tested how the robot's joke-telling with different voice pitches influenced the quality of the interaction and found that participants’ perception of the humorous robot and the interaction with it depended on their own sense of humor [ 99 ]. Similarly, Menne et al. [ 87 ] used a NAO robot telling jokes and indicated that intelligent jokes could increase perceived intelligence of the robot, and that having a sense of humor could help encourage sociality in HRI [ 87 ]. Another example, Tae and Lee [ 130 ] showed that a NAO robot telling jokes reduced awkwardness for those not familiar with robots. Their study indicated that jokes used at greeting and during topic transition enhanced likability of the robot and reduced awkwardness in the HRI [ 130 ]. Other studies tested joke-telling by humanoid robots performing as stand-up comedians, showing that social signals such as facial gestures and verbal behavior between humans and robots could create more amusing interactions [ 71 , 101 , 103 , 136 ].

Few HRI studies have used nonverbal humor (comical actions or gestures that others recognize to be funny [ 108 ]) [ 59 , 139 ], and robotic behavior that does not mimic human behavior [ 116 ]. Wendt and Berg [ 139 ] created a prankster robot based on a Pioneer P3-DX platform, dressed as a butler. Participants asked the robot to bring them items from a list. In the non-humorous condition, the robot did what it was told. In the humorous condition, the robot brought funny objects (paper snake) or reacted with a cheerful sound or silly gesture (dancing, clapping). The study found that participants perceived the nonverbal humorous behavior to be more entertaining and less dependable [ 139 ]. Meanwhile, Press and Erel [ 116 ] showed that a non-humanoid robotic object performing nonverbal humorous and non-humorous gestures, which were designed according to five animation principles, could lead to an overall perception of humor. Participants reported a difference between the robot's non-humorous gestures and its humorous gestures, and perceived the robotic object's humorous behavior as playful and funny [ 116 ].

This background research shows advantages of incorporating robots in human-human interactions as well as adding humor to interactions with technology. In this study, we leverage these advantages to mitigate awkwardness between strangers while still preserving the natural social interaction. Adding humor to a simple, peripheral non-humanoid robot does not require an additional time-consuming activity, and also makes it the responsibility of the robotic object to add humor to the interaction.

3 GESTURE DESIGN

In this study, we chose to use a small non-humanoid robotic object resembling a desk lamp (used with permission [ 63 ], see Figure 2 ). Previous studies already established that the robot can influence HHI via specific nonverbal behavior [ 48 , 120 ].

We follow Press and Erel [ 116 ], who designed humorous and non-humorous gestures for this non-humanoid robot by integrating processes of movement into the design interface of the robotic system. They showed that when the robot moved in a surprising and unexpected moderate to quick way, participants perceived it as having humorous behavior and when it moved in predictable slow and very slow movements the robot was perceived by participants as displaying non-humorous behavior [ 116 ] (See Figure 3 ).

For our study, we created two new animation compilations for this robotic object by combining gestures from Press and Erel [ 116 ]. The animation compilations were designed to create two different robotic behaviors: (1) Humorous robotic behavior and (2) Non-humorous robotic behavior. The implementation of the animation worked as follows:

For the humorous robotic behavior, we used two of three humorous gestures designed by Press and Erel [ 116 ], preferring to dismiss one of their humorous gestures that had shown variance in participant perception [ 116 ]. We created an animation compilation that utilizes their Spark Laugh and Side Laugh gestures:

  • Spark Laugh gesture features a contradiction of tempo by mixing slow and fast movements with repetition, considered key aspects of humor [ 9 ]. The gesture begins with one slow vertical downward movement and then switches to one wide-angle incongruent back-forth action, a short pause, and another wide-angle speedy back-forth movement [ 116 ].
  • Side Laugh gesture juxtaposes slow and fast moments of movement, repetition and speed fluctuations. It also integrates humor elements such as surprise [ 9 ], playing with expectations [ 25 ] and incongruous exaggerated movement [ 26 ]. The gesture features two slow full-length up-down motions and then two surprise quick up-down shakes of the robot's upper section only [ 116 ].

For the non-humorous condition, we used two of the non-humorous gestures indicated in Press and Erel's study as being wholly non-humorous [ 116 ]. We excluded their third non-humorous gesture to keep a balance with our new humorous animation compilation which includes two distinctive humorous gestures. As such, our Baseline animation compilation utilizes Spark Neutral and Uprise Slow gestures:

  • Spark Neutral gesture features slow and very slow movements, starting with the robotic object slowly folding its body halfway down, turning its base slightly, and slowly unfolding back up to its starting position. Once at its starting position, the robotic object then slowly extends its top part to end the gesture. In Press and Erel's study, a majority of the participants had nothing to say when asked how they would describe their emotional response to the gesture [ 116 ].
  • Uprise Slow gesture consists of very slow movements. The robotic object folds its body down and then back up again very slowly. In Press and Erel's study, more than half of the participants did not associate this gesture with any robotic emotional response [ 116 ].

The humorous and non-humorous gestures were integrated into a fluent robotic behavior by compiling a gesture compilation that included both the gestures relevant for each condition and additional gestures (turning actions and up-and-down movements), which previous studies indicated to be subtle, to barely capture participants’ attention, and that do not alter the human-human nature of HHI [ 120 ]. Previous work also showed that these up-and-down movements were perceived as “breathe-like” and were not associated with a humorous context [ 120 ]. This four-minute gesture compilation was designed to maintain a fluent robotic movement throughout the HHI [ 75 , 119 ] as well as keep the robot's behavior in a peripheral role that would not take participants’ focus of attention away from one another due to the robot's movement (See Figure 4 ). The four-minute gesture compilation was integrated into a six-minute HHI, an interaction length that was specifically designed to trigger awkwardness (see Experimental Design).

We acknowledge that it is possible that even shorter humorous gestures could have similar impact on the HHI, since prior literature has shown that there is no one specific length of time in which to perceive humor [ 137 ]. However, in this study we decided to use the exact well-validated gestures, previously indicated to lead to a humorous experience [ 116 ] and to integrate them into an interaction that was designed to evoke awkwardness (which required approximately six minutes, see Pilot study).

The robotic object performed the following animation compilation (See Figure 4 ):

  • The robot unfolded from its starting position while facing the center of the space between the two participants (thus, not facing either of them).
  • The robotic object turned to the participant sitting to the right of the robotic object (P1) and performed a breathing movement, then turned to the participant sitting to the left of the robotic object (P2) and performed a breathing movement.
  • The robotic object then turned to P1 and performed Spark Laugh or Spark Neutral depending on the humorous or non-humorous condition.
  • The robotic object then turned to P2 and performed a breathing movement, then turned to P1 and performed a breathing movement.
  • The robotic object then turned to P2 and performed Side Laugh or Uprise Slow depending on the condition.
  • The robotic object again turned toward each participant in order and performed a breathing gesture (turn to P1-breathe-turn to P2-breathe).
  • The robotic object then turned to P1 and performed Side Laugh or Uprise Slow depending on the condition.
  • The robotic object then turned to P2 and performed a breathing movement, then turned to P1 and performed a breathing movement.
  • The robotic object then turned to P2 and performed Spark Laugh or Spark Neutral depending on the condition.
  • The robotic object then turned to face the center of the space between the two participants (thus, not facing any of them) and folded down midway to perform an additional one-minute “breathing” animation sequence.

Altogether, the robotic object performed a four-minute behavior-specific animation compilation and then a one-minute “breathing” sequence. The compilations were applied as a fixed time-based movement, ensuring that there are no differences in the robot's timing and movement behavior between participants.

The robot was activated and animated through the Butter Robotics animation platform which controls the motors [ 85 , 86 ]. A web-based graphical user interface extended the platform's range of capabilities and allowed the researcher to sequence and execute robotic commands.

4 METHOD

The study was conducted under strict COVID-19 safety regulations. It was reviewed and approved by the ethics committee of the research institute.

4.1 Participants

We recruited participants from the student population of the mid-size university through advertisements calling on students to help out with research in return for compensation. The ads only included a link to a Google signup sheet. A member of our research team checked the sheet for names and coordinated student participation through email and telephone contact. None of the participants had any background information about the robotic object before joining the study. 52 participants ranging in age from 19 to 28 (Mean age = 22, SD = 2.00; 15 males, 37 females) participated in the study. Each participant was compensated with extra course credits or a $ 14.36 USD gift card for local shops. All participants signed a consent form and were informed that recorded material would be erased after data analysis.

4.2 Experimental setting

The experiment was conducted in a quiet room at the research lab. The room was set to minimize associations to a specific environmental context (i.e., home or work).

On the left side of the room, we set up two chairs facing each other, set precisely 55-cm apart. The chairs’ distance was designed to evoke awkwardness as they were purposely positioned in violation of the 76-cm space indicated by Burgoon et al. as a comfortable conversation distance [ 18 ].

Also in the experimental setup, at the midpoint between the two chairs was a small table (70-cm in height), and in accordance with findings from a previous study, was placed at a slight offset to minimize interference of the human-human, face-to-face communication [ 120 ]. Centered on the small table was the robotic object, whose height reached that of a human's shoulder when seated.

Along the right wall of each room, we placed a laptop computer atop another table. The laptop was used after the interaction with the robot ended. A smartphone and tripod were placed for documentation via the Zoom platform on a shelf on the back side of the room (see Figure 5 ).

To ensure a uniform experience at the post-experience evaluation, which was conducted separately for each participant, we set up a twin-like room so that when one participant moved to the second room the surrounding settings remained the same.

4.2.1 Pilot study: Verification that the experimental settings evoked awkwardness. To verify that the setup (i.e., distance between the chairs) evokes social awkwardness, we conducted a pilot study, whereby we invited 14 students from the university into the experiment room in pairs, after the researcher verified that they had no acquaintance. Upon arrival at the lab, participants were given an introduction and were told that there would be a robotic object in the room without any additional explanation of whether or not it would move. The pilot study experimental setting matched that of the full research study (same room setup, robot placed at the same location) with one exception: the robotic object did not move. The researcher escorted participants into the experimental room where they were asked to sit on two chairs facing each other, and told by the researcher that she would return in the coming minutes.

We specifically designed the interaction (waiting for the researcher) to last for six minutes, a time frame comparable to previous work testing social awkwardness during unguided conversation among strangers [ 31 ]. We aimed to verify that this conversation duration evokes social awkwardness without becoming overly awkward. In addition, we verified that the mere physical presence of a robotic object in close proxemics to the participants did not provide a common ground that diffuses awkwardness.

Results of the pilot study showed that a majority of participants (10/14) described the experience using words such as “awkward”, “embarrassing”, “uncomfortable”, “uncertain”. The one couple who were mixed male and female dyads, said they “felt we were on a date”, while other participants noted that if they had been in a mixed couple it “would have changed the experience.” There were also 4/14 participants who did not describe the stranger-to-stranger interaction as awkward, saying they found common themes to talk about, that the “conversation flowed”, and described how they like to meet new people.

While we purposely set out to evoke awkwardness in the interaction via the chair setup, at the same time, the pilot study ensured through semi-structured interviews that the baseline distance was not overly uncomfortable and would not stress out the participants. The researcher debriefed the participants and verified an overall positive experience.

4.3 Experimental Design

We used a between-participants experimental design. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two conditions: humorous (Experimental condition) and non-humorous (Baseline condition).

In both conditions, the robotic object did not move or respond during the first minute of the experiment. This initial one-minute phase without robotic movement allowed for participants to face the socially awkward situation. At the start of the second minute of the experiment, the robotic object performed the animation compilation that was designed for the specific condition. This four-minute behavioral animation compilation involved the gesture sequence designed for the specific condition (See Gesture design). Finally, the robot performed another minute of slow breathing movements, which allowed the interaction to continue to play out naturally while keeping the robotic movement fluid. In both conditions, the non-humanoid robotic object was not addressed and was a non-participant to the conversation [ 30 ].

4.4 Measures

We used both objective and subjective measures to evaluate the influence of the robot's humorous behavior on the human-human awkwardness experience.

4.4.1 Objective measure. To assess participants’ implicit experience we used the lexical decision task [ 49 ], a reaction time-based measure to assess implicit processes [ 29 , 141 ], thus providing an objective evaluation of social awkwardness immediately after the interaction with the humorous or non-humorous robotic object.

The lexical decision task requires participants to decide whether a string of letters displayed on a computer screen makes a word or nonword by pressing on labeled keys on the keyboard (keys “S” and “L”). Participants were requested to respond as quickly and accurately as possible. Each word had a corresponding nonword consisting of the same letters as the real word but mixed up and with maintained length (e.g. word: "platform"; nonword: "atprmlof"). The task was made up of five awkwardness-related words (shy, discomfort, awkward, embarrassed, hesitant), five comfortable-related words (social, relaxed, comfortable, easygoing, confident) and five neutral words (platform, light, laundry, language, passenger). Words relating to awkwardness were selected from previous research on the topic [ 32 , 33 , 73 ]. Similarly, we used prior research on comfortable interaction [ 122 ] and the Merriam Webster dictionary [ 38 ] to find relevant comfortable-related terms. The neutral words had been used in a previous psychological study [ 138 ]. Participants remained unaware of the connotation of words used in this task, and only knew that they had to differentiate between word or nonword. Altogether there were 15 strings of letters that made up words and 15 strings of letters that made up nonwords, which were repeated twice in a random order (60 trials in total). The stimuli were presented at the center of the computer screen in non-capitalized, black letters on a white background. A fixation plus sign flashed on the screen for 500 milliseconds before each string of letters.

Previous studies have indicated longer lexical decision latencies to negative words, due to an inherent defense mechanism [ 2 ]. As such, when negative words are irrelevant to the participant's present context (e.g., classifying scary words after watching a comedy), there will be slower reaction time to the negative word in comparison to neutral or positive words [ 49 , 141 ]. When negative words are relevant to the participant's present context (e.g., classifying scary words after watching a horror flick), this slowdown in reaction time dissipates and the reaction time latency for negative words modifies to that of neutral and positive words [ 49 ].

Our hypothesis was that in the non-humorous condition, the negative context (the social awkwardness) would eliminate the slower reaction time to negative words (in our case, awkwardness-related words). At the same time, if the humorous robotic behavior in the humorous condition would relieve awkwardness (meaning, it would no longer be relevant) the slowing effect would be present (i.e., longer reaction time latency for awkwardness-related words in comparison to neutral or positive words).

The lexical decision task was programmed with E-Prime software, a gold-standard software for experimental (behavioral) studies [ 125 ].

4.4.2 Subjective measures. Semi-structured interviews were used as they allow the researcher to ask open-ended questions and allow participants to freely express their views during data collection while remaining in line with a particular framework [ 52 ]. This qualitative measure also provides a more general and rich description of the experience [ 127 ]. The semi-structured interviews took place in each of the experiment rooms, immediately after participants finished the lexical decision task. Each participant was interviewed separately. Interview questions were designed to assess participants’ experience, feelings about the stranger-stranger (human-human) interaction, and perception of the robot's part in the experience (e.g., “How would you describe the experience?”, “Would you say that the robot took part in the experience?”).

4.4.3 Godspeed questionnaire. Although not the main focus of the study, we also tested the participants’ impression of the robot using the Godspeed questionnaire [ 8 ], a five-item Likert scale measure commonly used in HRI studies to test participants’ impression of the robot. We used three relevant subscales from the questionnaire: Likability, Animacy, and Perceived Intelligence. We tested whether the robot's behavior influenced participants’ impression of the robotic object during the social awkwardness experience.

4.5 Procedure

One week before arriving at the lab, participants filled in a demographic form, a questionnaire assessing participants’ attitudes towards robots [ 20 ], and the self-enhancing humor subscale of the Humor Styles Questionnaire [ 81 ] (via Google Forms), to balance the groups and verify a valid range of individual differences.

When participants arrived at the lab, the researcher verified that they had no previous acquaintance. Participants were paired in same-sex dyads to not mix gender groupings and thus avoid variables which could affect behavior during the interaction between strangers (see pilot study and [ 53 ]). They were asked to leave phones, smartwatches and bags outside the experiment room. This directive ensured that participants must face the situation in the room. Participants were also told there would be a robotic object in the room. It was explained that participants would be recorded and that they could choose to withdraw from the experiment at any time without penalty. They then signed an informed consent form.

The researcher escorted participants into the experiment room and directed them to take seats. The researcher told participants that the experiment would begin soon and that they should wait, and then the researcher left the room. They received no other instructions.

During the first minute of the experiment, the robot did not move at all. The researcher then activated the robotic object using the dedicated interface to perform a four-minute condition-appropriate animation compilation together with another full minute of breathing movements.

The researcher reentered the room at the six-minute mark and informed the participants that it was now time for a computer task. One participant was led to the twin experiment room. Participants were asked to perform the lexical decision task while sitting 28-cm in front of a laptop computer, and the researcher left the room.

Following the computerized task, the researcher came back into the experimental room to conduct the semi-structured interview. Participants then filled out the Godspeed questionnaire on a tablet. For the final stage of the experiment, the researcher debriefed the participants and verified that they were leaving with an overall positive experience.

5 FINDINGS

We applied mixed methods of quantitative and qualitative analysis to the data. The quantitative analysis included a two-way ANOVA evaluating the interaction between the two robotic conditions and word type. In addition, we compared the ratings of the Godspeed subscales between the robotic conditions using T-tests. For the qualitative analysis, we used the thematic open coding process [ 14 , 55 ], a methodology commonly used in HCI and HRI research. The analysis included five stages: (1) Interviews were transcribed, and half of the interviews were read several times by two coders (one researcher and one research assistant) to develop an overall understanding of the data before the coding process began. The two coders each marked quotes (a quote was defined as a sentence with consistent emphasis on a specific topic), striving to identify meaningful statements. They then individually classified the quotes into a list of initial themes; (2) The coders presented their initial themes to a third researcher and the three of them discussed these themes in-depth, merging and narrowing where needed while resolving inconsistencies; (3) This process resulted in three mutually agreed upon themes with clear guidelines for both coders on how to proceed; (4) The coders then independently marked quotes and coded 20% of the data according to the themes. They then verified inter-rater reliability (kappa=82%); (5) The two coders then analyzed the rest of the data, comprising 497 quotes classified according to the three mutually agreed upon themes.

5.1 Lexical decision task

Analysis of the lexical decision task revealed a significant influence on participants’ awkwardness experience. The difference in reaction time (RT) between awkwardness-related words, comfortable-related words and neutral words were compared in the two conditions, revealing a significant interaction between word-type and the robot's behavior (humorous/non-humorous) F(2,47) = 3.28, p = 0.04 (see Figure 6 ). Planned contrasts indicated that a slowing effect (indicating that awkwardness-related words were not relevant to the situation) was observed in the humorous condition, but not in the non-humorous condition. In the humorous condition, the reaction time to awkwardness-related words was longer in comparison to the reaction time to the comfortable and neutral words, indicating that the awkwardness-related words were unrelated to the participant's current emotional context. Meanwhile, in the non-humorous condition, the reaction time to the awkwardness-related words was similar to the reaction time to comfortable and neutral words (i.e., there was no slowdown effect), indicating that the awkwardness-related words were relevant to the participant's current emotional context (see Table 1 ).

5.3 Thematic analysis of semi-structured interviews

In total, 497 quotes were analyzed, leading to three main themes that highlighted participants’ perspectives of the experience: Impact on the human-human interaction; how the robot made participants feel; and the robot's role.

During the study, many participants used the word “he” when relating to the robotic object. While the interviewer did not use any gendered pronouns when describing the robot (e.g., He, She, or It) and only referred to the robot as the “robotic object”, we note that the word “it” is not a common way to relate to objects in the participants’ native language and thus may explain the choice of “he”.

5.3.1 Theme 1: Impact on the human-human interaction. All participants in the humorous condition (26/26) and over half of participants in the non-humorous condition (16/26) explicitly mentioned that they felt the robotic object's behavior had an impact on the stranger-stranger interaction. Their responses differed between the conditions in terms of whether the influence on the HHI was positive, negative, or neutral.

Humorous condition : All participants in this condition mentioned the robot's influence on the HHI. A majority of participants, 23/26, said the robotic object had a positive influence on the HHI. 3/26 stated that the robotic object had a negative effect on the HHI. None of the participants perceived the robot's behavior as neutral.

Participants who labeled the robot's impact on their HHI experience as being positive, perceived the robotic object's humorous movements as contributing to the conversation's flow. A majority of these participants, 16/23, specifically mentioned that the robot triggered a better conversation: “Conversation can only get so far. When you meet somebody, you try to find common ground. I'm sure the conversation would have gone somewhere but the robot gave us a means of similarity” (P42, M); and infused laughter into the conversation, “Suddenly this robot started to move... We laughed about it together” (P44, F); “We were laughing at it. We actually had a lovely conversation. It was really fun. I was happy to meet a stranger” (P31, F).

7/23 participants explained that the robot's contribution was in breaking the tension, and making a more comfortable atmosphere for the stranger-stranger encounter: “He made us laugh and that relieved the tension and the silence. It lightened the atmosphere” (P35, F); “It even released some of the tension in the air” (P39, F); “It made the conversation and the situation comfortable suddenly. Instead of being super serious, it became funny and fun. That was pretty cool” (P46, F). 5/7 of these participants explicitly pinpointed the robot's humorous behavior as something that could mitigate awkwardness: “It was strange because I'm sitting with a stranger, I don't know how to talk to her, so the robot was there by necessity. If it hadn't been there, it could have been awkward” (P43, F); “It was uncomfortable in the first minute and then the robot moved. I think that was the switch that made it comfortable. The robot was a really big part of the experience in terms of making it actually less awkward between us, we were both kind of laughing. So that was great," (P8, M).

3/26 participants in this condition found the robotic object's movements to have a negative impact on the HHI. They explained that the robot commanded their full attention: “The robot became the topic of conversation; we focused on it. We stopped talking. When we started talking again it was only about the robot” (P10, F); “The second it started moving, I couldn't avoid it or not pay attention to it. It was very, very present” (P45, F).

Non-humorous condition : Participants gave mixed feedback of how the robotic object's non-humorous behavior impacted the human-human interaction: 9/26 said it had a positive impact, 7/26 participants said it had a negative impact, another 7/26 said the behavior had no effect, and 3/26 said nothing about the robotic object's impact on the HHI.

Participants who mentioned a positive impact on the stranger-stranger interaction explained that the robot became their topic of conversation and triggered a better conversation: “The robot looked really cute, we spoke about what it was” (P33, F). One participant described the robotic object's positive impact on the HHI as that of a facilitator: “We had some connecting factor; it created a bit of a topic of conversation” (P21, F).

Participants who perceived a negative impact on the HHI explained that it hogged their attention and made it difficult for them to focus on their counterpart: “It affected the conversation so that instead of getting to know each other in a somewhat awkward situation, we focused on the robot” (P6, F); “It interrupted our conversation. [Without the robot], the conversation would feel a bit more comfortable, easygoing” (P38, F).

Participants who perceived a neutral influence stated that the robot was peripheral: “He was repeating the moves from her to me, from her to me, then we barely noticed him. We were more interested in the conversation rather than in the robot,” (P22, F); “I looked at the robot. And it was like, it's not too interesting a robot. So we continued our discussion,” (P52, F).

5.3.2 Theme 2: How the robot made participants feel. There was a wide discrepancy between how the robot made participants feel in the humorous and non-humorous conditions. A majority of participants, 18/26, in the humorous condition reported positive feelings whereas just 6/26 cited positive emotions in the non-humorous condition.

Humorous condition : 18/26 participants in the humorous condition said the robotic object made them feel positive emotions, and used words such as “comfortable”, “less awkward”, “curious” and “interested”. Participants explained their feelings in relation to the awkwardness of the HHI: “He made us laugh and he lightened everything up. I felt more comfortable” (P35, F); “It makes me feel curious. It's cute. I'm curious to talk to the other participant now” (P46, F).

A minority, 6/26, reported that the robotic behavior made them feel uncomfortable: “It creeped me out” (P31, F); “He moves violently, I really don't like it, he makes it uncomfortable when he reacts negatively to you” (P41, M). The rest of the participants in this condition did not discuss how the robot made them feel.

Non-humorous condition : Participants showed a variance in how the robot made them feel. Just under half of them, 10/26, stated that the robotic object's behavior prompted negative feelings: “This thing stressed me out” (P34, F); “I felt it was testing me, as if he judged me and it's really strange, it's a robot, he shouldn't judge me” (P21, F); “It's an uncomfortable presence” (P38, F).

A minority, 6/26 participants, reported that the robot's behavior made them feel positive emotions: “It was very soothing. I noticed it had some cat patterns” (P29, F); “There was something comforting about that robot, very relaxing” (P30, F). 7/26 participants made no reference to how the robot made them feel. Another 3/26 participants said the robot did not evoke feelings: “I didn't really feel his presence” (P17, F).

5.3.3 Theme 3: Robot's role in the experience. A majority of participants, 36/52, stated that the robot's role in the experience was positive and helped the human-human interaction. However, their responses varied between the conditions. Participants in the humorous condition indicated that the robot made them laugh in the role of an icebreaker while participants in the non-humorous condition ascribed it the role of a conversation starter.

Humorous condition : Nearly all participants, 21/26, said the robotic object's role was to serve as an icebreaker: “It prompted laughs and maybe even released some tension because we were both laughing at him at the same time, and as soon as you laugh in front of a person, you feel that you open up to them” (P39, F); “It's like an icebreaker… helping with the awkward atmosphere” (P46, F). Of these participants, 5/21, also ascribed the robot's role as facilitating the conversation: “I feel like it could interrupt or it could be a conversation starter” (P36, F); “It really seemed like he was a mediator” (P43, F). Another 7/21 of these participants gave the robot the role of icebreaker and entertainer: “It was like a source of humor or laughter between us” (P28, F).

3/26 participants perceived it as playing a negative role: “It's an eavesdropper. Is it part of the conversation? Why is it butting in?” (P10, F); “It's like an attack dog, or a police dog” (P42, M). For 2/26 participants, the robot held a neutral role: “It scanned me but that's about it” (P23, F).

Non-humorous condition : Just over half the participants, 15/26, said the robot played a positive role as a conversation starter: “The robot was basically an icebreaker” (P7, F); "It gave us something to focus on rather than forcefully looking for what to talk about" (P30, F).

5/26 participants ascribed its role as negative, saying it pulled attention away from the HHI: “Part of the time, the attention was directed to him and for a moment we stopped talking, he seemed to interrupt the conversation” (P25, F).

For 6/26 participants, the robotic object had no discernable role: “I didn't really understand what he was doing, it's just an empty space, that's it” (P14, M).

6 DISCUSSION

Our study provides encouraging data indicating that a non-humanoid robotic object, performing nonverbal humorous behaviors, can serve as a new approach to mitigating awkwardness in a face-to-face social interaction among strangers. The objective and subjective findings show that when the robotic object performed humorous gestures, participants judged it as having a more positive impact on the stranger-stranger interaction compared to when it performed non-humorous robotic behavior in a similar situation.

Specifically, we sought to shed light on how a simple machine with no human-like features, using minimal nonverbal humorous gestures and staying a non-participant to the interaction [ 30 ], could be used to enhance the interpersonal interaction between strangers caught in a socially awkward situation.

Because awkwardness does not usually dissipate on its own, there are some strategies aimed at countering this common challenge to social interactions and enabling healthy HHI [ 73 ]. Although these interventions for lessening social awkwardness serve their purpose in different domains, for the most part, these approaches (both humorous and non-humorous) add activities or devices that change the natural interaction between two humans [ 32 , 62 , 66 , 68 ]. Many of these approaches also take time to bring the desired effect, especially when an activity or game must be completed. Humor, on the other hand, is both a coping mechanism and communication tool that can have more immediate effects in altering people's perspective of their situation [ 74 , 80 ].

Little is known about the possible impact nonverbal robotic humor can have on HHI and what its main role can be, especially when peripheral. To date, the majority of studies focus on verbal humor [ 101 , 104 , 111 ]. But because verbal humor can be complex and difficult to translate culturally or linguistically, we chose to evaluate the application of a robotic version of nonverbal movement humor (e.g., gestures, performance, pantomime, slapstick) [ 108 ], positing that in the same way prior studies have shown that robots, interactive objects and virtual agents can convey emotion via nonverbal social cues and behavior in HRI and HCI [ 16 , 83 , 114 ], similar results for nonverbal humorous robotic gestures should be possible in HHRI as well. In this study, we propose a method for incorporating humor in HHI in a minimal way that fosters a positive effect on interpersonal interaction. The peripheral non-humanoid robotic object performing humorous behaviors allowed participants to meet one another in a more comfortable manner, and puts the onus of creating humor to reduce the social awkwardness on the robotic object rather than on someone in the interaction.

Robotic humor as a new way for assisting HHI in the context of social awkwardness is important because many people avoid initiating contact with strangers even though they know the benefits of this social contact, fearing possible awkwardness and its related stressors of anxiety and uncertainty [ 41 , 43 , 65 ]. We suggest that a humorous experience with a simple robotic object can encourage the initiation of social interaction that under different circumstances would be avoided.

6.1 Robotic impact on HHI

Our specific choice to use a non-humanoid robotic object performing humorous movements is based on previous studies indicating that humans automatically interpret autonomous behavior of (even very simple) robots as social cues [ 40 , 47 ]. These works position robots as strong candidates for enhancing human-human interactions in other contexts [ 48 , 50 , 120 , 129 ]. Our findings extend the scope of robots’ positive impact to HHI that involves social awkwardness. The robot's minimal gestures and peripheral role in the interaction further support the possibility that very simple robots can have a meaningful effect on HHI without changing the nature of the interaction and without becoming another participant in the interaction. With previous indications that basic non-humanoid robots impact human-human interpersonal connections [ 120 ], emotional support [ 48 ] and prosocial behavior [ 44 ], our findings suggest that such robots have great potential in shaping and enhancing HHI without drastically changing the interpersonal interaction.

Interestingly, while the robot's behavior impacted the HHI, it did not influence the perception of the robotic object (as indicated by the null results in the Godspeed subscales). While it is possible that this is a general phenomenon, other HRI studies have shown that robots employing humor (verbal and nonverbal) were rated with higher likability [ 130 , 139 ] and higher intelligence [ 87 ]. Combining these considerations, we propose two explanations for our findings: (1) that a situation of awkwardness, which may require heightened attentional resources in order to overcome or deal with the present circumstances, may interfere with participants’ ability to construct a clear impression of the robotic object; and (2) the robotic object as a non-humanoid, peripheral non-participant to the interaction, may have received less attention in comparison to robots used in the abovementioned studies. With reduced direct attention, participants may not necessarily form a well-defined, explicit perception of the robot. This last explanation is somewhat supported by our qualitative data, which indicated that participants spoke about noticing the robot but not dwelling on it: “I'm, like, okay the robot is here. He's moving. But I wanted to talk to the person in front of me” P27, F.

Another implication of our results concerns the application of robotic gestures in different contexts. The gestures used in this study were initially designed in the context of a robot responding to sentences presented on a computer screen [ 116 ]. Our findings show that both the humorous (and non-humorous) gestures could be easily applied to a socially awkward situation among strangers and add a humorous (or non-humorous) experience to the interaction (even from a peripheral position). As such, these findings further validate the possibility that a non-humanoid robotic object performing humorous movements can be used in different contexts as a tool to bring about a positive experience. Indeed, a majority of participants (18/26) reported that the robot's humorous behavior made them laugh, feel comfortable or feel interested in the HHI (“It made it funny in some way, it was entertaining”, P4, M; “It was fun. It's something that breaks the ice a little without even knowing it”, P16, F). Participants explained that the humorous experience was the main factor in relieving the awkwardness (“It kind of made the conversation or even the situation much more comfortable suddenly. We were laughing, it's kind of like we needed it. Instead of being super serious, it became funny and fun", P46, F). A few participants said these same gestures made them feel uncomfortable (“It's strange because it's not something natural”, P10, F), however, this finding seems to mirror earlier research on individual differences in sense of humor [ 81 ].

More generally, humor applications in HCI and HRI tend to examine the impact on perception of the technology and the interface agent/robot's ability to evoke a humorous experience [ 104 , 111 ]. This mirrors a more general approach of studies focusing on participants’ emotional interpretation of nonverbal robotic gestures [ 4 , 46 , 77 , 139 , 143 ]. In this work, we suggest a more utilitarian approach as a possible next step of such studies. Once an emotional experience with a robot has been established, it is possible to leverage a robot's gestures to influence and enhance interactions or experiences that can benefit from such emotions. A few studies have already adopted this approach, using robotic attentiveness to enhance human-human conversation [ 120 , 132 ] and emotional support [ 48 ]. We extend this line of work by indicating that a humorous experience with robots can be utilized to reduce awkwardness between two strangers. With robots already showing the ability to trigger experiences involving companionship [ 143 ], inclusiveness [ 45 ], sense of security [ 77 ] and sense of being seen [ 126 ], it is possible that simple robotic objects performing minimal gestures could play a meaningful role and contribute to increasing human well-being when applied to relevant contexts. For example, robotic companionship can be used to reduce loneliness; being included by robots can be harnessed to empower a sense of belonging; increasing a sense of security in an interaction with a robot can encourage people to open up to others; and communicativeness with a robotic object can enhance a sense of being seen (social presence). Taken together, our work indicates that a non-humanoid robotic object's nonverbal humorous behavior is a promising approach to mitigate social awkwardness in an interaction between two strangers. Even from a peripheral role, a robotic object with a simple mechanical design and limited communication modalities can perform humorous gestures that positively influence interpersonal interactions in a socially awkward context leading to a smoother social interaction.

6.2 Scenarios in which a robot can enhance HHI

Interaction and communication with people we do not know can happen just about anywhere: at a business networking event, in a classroom, at an academic conference, in a waiting room, at the gym, among many other circumstances. While for some people it is natural to strike up conversation, for many others the social awkwardness of a situation can cause people to avoid interacting in the first place and even bring about stress and other adverse health effects [ 28 , 73 ].

What if a non-humanoid robotic object performing humorous gestures was placed on a table in a conference hall during a break? Could this robotic object possibly reduce social awkwardness and positively impact the interpersonal interactions among strangers in these circumstances?

We envision this tool of a non-humanoid robotic object performing humorous behavior as serving to mitigate social awkwardness in numerous contexts in which strangers come into contact with one another but do not necessarily engage in face-to-face interaction because of possible awkwardness itself. For instance, we can imagine the use of a humorous robotic object in schools, education facilities or at hobby meet-ups. If we think about a college lecture, for example, social awkwardness may keep people from speaking to one another in the classroom. In such a scenario, a humorous robotic object could help mitigate the social awkwardness and allow for the formation of healthier social interactions, which have shown benefits that include sense of belonging [ 61 , 82 , 84 ], closeness and togetherness [ 69 ], and as such could enhance an educational experience and its outcomes.

Workplace cafeterias are another possible use case for mitigating social awkwardness via a robot performing humorous gestures. Workers often direct their attention to their smart devices during mealtimes, thereby skirting the need to engage in a possible socially awkward interaction [ 43 , 124 ]. Using a robot to mitigate the awkwardness in initial encounters between employees from different departments who do not know one another could impact and enhance positive casual social interactions between them. Healthy social interactions between employees has shown to contribute to job satisfaction [ 5 ], sense of belonging [ 5 , 84 ], better productivity [ 5 ] and general well-being [ 5 ].

While we foresee many possible scenarios in which a robot performing humorous behavior could positively impact social interactions, every so often, awkward silences may serve a purpose and not need such an intervention. At times, awkward silences can work in favor of a social interaction by allowing participants to collect their thoughts and process emotions, even if awkward and uncomfortable [ 72 ]. For example, an awkward silence in conversation may serve to emphasize a point [ 72 ]. Future research should evaluate in which other scenarios awkward silences should be allowed to play out without intervention. Taken together, we suggest that when there is a clear need to mitigate awkwardness, a robotic object performing humorous gestures can encourage the initiation of social interaction and positively enhance the HHI.

7 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

There were several limitations to this study. First, we analyzed qualitative interviews, which may be biased by the interviewers’ expectations [ 113 ]. We minimized this risk by following a detailed protocol and made sure that the interviewer used neutral language. A related limitation is that the interviews may be biased by the “good subject effect" [ 98 ]. To limit this effect, we told participants that all answers were helpful and ensured that participants never knew which responses would “please” the interviewer. Indeed, our findings show variance in responses, with participants assigning the robot's impact on the HHI as positive and negative in both conditions.

We also acknowledge the lack of a non-socially awkward control condition. Without such a condition it is difficult to assess the full extent of the robot's impact on the participants’ experience of awkwardness. Future work should evaluate an extended experimental design that includes a non-socially awkward scenario. In addition, it is possible that the impact of the humorous robotic behavior on awkwardness is related to a more general positive impact of robotic humor in both HHRI and HRI. In this work, we specifically focused on indicating the robot's potential for mitigating awkwardness due to its challenging negative effects on HHI. We, therefore, verified that the HHI involved awkwardness and indicated that specifically humorous robotic behavior (in comparison to non-humorous behavior) mitigates awkwardness (hence, both our Experimental condition and Baseline condition are purposely set in a socially awkward scenario). Moreover, future work should further evaluate the generality of the impact of robotic humor and whether it can enhance interactions in other non-socially awkward contexts (e.g., HHI that involves conflict, educational stress, tension in therapy, negotiations and more).

Individual attributes offer a further possible limitation. Individual differences including personality traits, cultural background, a person's context, subjective beliefs and sense of humor may have affected the participant's reaction to and appraisal of the humorous robotic behavior. Similarly, individual differences may have impacted the perception of social awkwardness in the interaction. To lessen these limitations in our study, we verified a valid range of individual differences via pre-study questionnaires [ 20 , 81 ]. We also used humorous, non-humanoid robotic abstract gestures that did not mimic human-like behavior, which may provide a more universal humorous experience (see Press and Erel [ 116 ]). And we verified in a pilot study that our experimental settings trigger social awkwardness to ensure, as much as possible, a uniform social context from which to interpret the stimulus (humorous robotic behavior). Despite the reasonable number of participants and despite the use of a matching technique balancing gender, sense of humor and attitudes towards robots, such differences may still have impacted the findings. As such, we suggest that future research should employ empirical methods to provide causal evidence for a relationship between culture and social awkwardness, humor and personality traits, and how sense of humor affects perception of humorous robotic gestures.

A further limitation concerns our decision to use same-gender strangers, intended to reduce the influence of additional variables that could affect behavior due to physical attraction and shyness [ 53 ]. Similar to previous studies which evaluated HHI involving strangers, we overcame this additional variance by limiting our interactions to include same-gender pairs [ 48 , 120 ]. Hence, while our findings show the robotic object's ability to impact both men and women, this choice limits the ability to generalize our results to mixed-gender dyads. Future work should test the effect of humorous robotic movements during an initial mixed-gender awkward interaction, while controlling for physical attraction and shyness, as awkwardness is not limited to same-gender, two-person interactions.

A separate limitation concerns the specific design of the robotic object. We used one robot with a particular morphology and distinctive humorous gestures for this study. Future work could evaluate the impact other types of robotic humor may have in HHRI, and further afield from a peripheral role.

Another possible limitation is the use of diverging compensation. Participants could choose between course credits and a coupon for local shops. Despite being balanced across conditions, the different types of compensation could have added undesirable variance to the data.

Finally, we acknowledge the possibility that shorter and even more minimal robotic gestures could lead to a humorous experience that could mitigate awkwardness. While in this study we chose to focus on robotic gestures, which had been validated as leading to a humorous experience in a previous study [ 116 ], future work should further explore the extent to which nonverbal humorous robotic behavior can lead to this impact.

More broadly, we hope researchers, designers, and practitioners in the fields of HCI and HRI will leverage our findings and apply them to HHI by designing and implementing new humorous mechanism gestures for other non-humanoid robotic objects, or other technological objects.

8 CONCLUSION

Social awkwardness is a constant challenge to social interaction, and can bring about embarrassment, anxiety, discomfort, and other negative effects to interpersonal encounters. We introduce a novel technology-based approach for addressing awkwardness without adding additional time-consuming activities and while allowing people to interact naturally. Our findings suggest minimal humorous robotic objects as a potential tool for overcoming challenges in human-human interactions and enhancing human relationships without altering the nature of the interaction.
