Virtual exhibitions have long been regarded as an extension of information delivery for physical exhibitions. However, what virtual exhibitions can offer audiences as a novel experience independently from physical exhibitions has been largely unexplored. In this study, we aim to understand the promises and challenges of experiencing and curating exhibitions in VR by interviewing nine expert curators. Drawing from expert insights, we summarized a set of design guidelines to inform what we can learn and adapt from physical exhibitions when curating in VR. Then, using an autobiographical design approach, we curated an interactive exhibition in VRChat to explore novel interaction techniques. We also hosted an open tour guide in the user study to validate our design guidelines with thirty participants. Results show that our approach of curating an exhibition in VRChat provided the participants with engaging and novel experiences interacting with the exhibits and other audiences.

1 INTRODUCTION

Recent years have seen a rise in virtual museums (VMs) with the rapid advancement of virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR)  [ 75 ]. VMs have a long history to trace in terms of the concept and technologies such as hypertext and CD-ROMs that were applied in the early stage of VMs  [ 28 , 49 , 68 , 74 , 75 ]. Since the emergence of web technologies (e.g., World Wide Web and the user-friendly browser Mosaic), a large number of museum institutions have been distributing information via websites as a form of VMs  [ 49 , 75 ]. While web-based VMs have been recognized as an essential form of extension of physical museums  [ 70 , 81 ], they fall short of interactivity and embodied experiences for audiences  [ 54 ]. However, as suggested by previous studies  [ 42 , 69 ], embodiment plays an integral role in engaging audiences in digital heritage.

To provide more embodiment in digital environments, VR technology has been suggested to be promising  [ 53 ]. Currently, VR is being used as an umbrella term for two different technical approaches to imaging  [ 73 ], which are Cinematic VR (CVR) and 3DVR. While CVR does not provide much interactivity since users cannot navigate freely in the space, 3DVR allows users to have various interactions with the virtual environment, e.g., moving around scenes, grabbing objects, etc. However, while most VMs in 3DVR can provide embodiment and interactivity, they are mostly replicas of the physical venue without many adaptation strategies to highlight the distinctiveness of VMs  [ 52 , 54 , 81 ].

A body of prior work has suggested that virtual exhibitions should be more than simply a simulation, documentation, or promotion  [ 13 , 52 , 81 ]. Hence, an imperative task is to highlight the distinctiveness of VMs. Researchers have identified three key aspects to this task: (1) the performative quality of born-digital culture  [ 13 ], (2) the high audience engagement with the exhibits  [ 13 ], and (3) human connections  [ 54 ]. Yet, these streams of studies have not investigated the design strategies with the currently available technologies and platforms to explore practical suggestions for future VM design.

Combining the benefits of 3DVR and social interactions, social VR refers to a set of applications where users can socialize through VR head-mounted displays (HMDs)  [ 61 ]. The booming commercialization of VR applications has led to a growing research agenda in HCI, e.g., activities in social VR  [ 57 ], communication modes  [ 62 , 65 ], and social interaction consequences  [ 32 , 79 ]. However, to our best knowledge, there has been no research utilizing social VR platforms as virtual exhibition venues in HCI and CSCW research communities. As noted above, social interactions enhance embodied learning, which has motivated us to study the significance of social VR as a virtual exhibition venue. Therefore, in this paper, we aim to investigate three research questions (RQs):

  • RQ1: What are the promises and challenges of curating virtual exhibitions compared to physical exhibitions?
  • RQ2: What strategies do curators employ in response to the challenges of curating virtual exhibitions?
  • RQ3: What strategies can effectively engage audiences in virtual exhibitions?

To better understand the sociotechnical phenomenon of virtual exhibitions from experts’ perspectives, we first conducted an interview study with nine professional art curators. The interviews have revealed the promises and challenges of virtual exhibitions. Informed by our expert interviewees, we curated a novel VR exhibition through an autobiographical design study in a trendy social VR application– VRChat. The exhibition aimed to validate the design guidelines drawn from expert interviews. The results show that our approach of curating a virtual exhibition in social VR provided the participants with engaging and novel experiences interacting with the exhibits and other audiences. Thus, contributions of our research in the field of HCI are (1) design guidelines for virtual exhibitions informed by expert curators, (2) a novel VR exhibition prototype independent of traditional physical exhibitions, and (3) detailed autobiographical reflections on the design, development, and curation process that present practical recommendations through hands-on experiences.

2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK

In this section, we discuss prior work on the origin and development of virtual museums, virtual exhibitions in different mediums (e.g., CD-ROM, websites, and VR), and the applications of online multi-user virtual worlds and social VR as virtual exhibition venues.

2.1 Exhibitions in Virtual Museums

The application of virtual museums (VMs) has developed rapidly with the proliferation of interactive technologies in recent years  [ 75 ]. However, the concept of VMs itself is not new. Theoretically, the origin of VMs could be traced back to the French art theorist Andre Malrux's idea of “ Museum Without Walls ” in 1949  [ 27 , 47 , 49 , 58 , 74 ]. Technically, VMs originated when hypertext became popular  [ 75 ], empowering museums to organize information in a non-linear way  [ 75 ]. Later, interactive multimedia technologies such as CD-ROMs provided greater access to images, videos, and audio  [ 68 ], making CD-ROMs widely popular with museums  [ 28 ]. However, multimedia technologies were flawed in the user-friendliness of the interface, content organization, updating efficiency, etc  [ 71 ]. Compensating for the flaws of multimedia technologies, the emergence of web technologies enabled museums to deliver and archive information via websites  [ 49 , 75 ]. Up to date, many renowned museum institutions still utilize websites as their primary source to document artworks, e.g., the Metropolitan Museum of Art (see Figure  1(a))  [ 1 ], the British Museum  [ 2 ], Louvre  [ 4 ], etc.

Nevertheless, web-based VMs have several limitations. First, web-based VMs have been criticized for limited interaction and navigation techniques  [ 54 ], e.g., clicking, scrolling, and watching videos. Besides, audiences have little freedom of navigation in a structured order of content. With few interaction and navigation techniques, web-based VMs can provide limited embodiment  [ 54 ]. However, prior work highlights embodiment as a critical aspect for audiences to engage with cultural heritage in virtual worlds  [ 42 ], as embodied interactions allow audiences to actively participate, accomplish tasks, and be fully immersed in digital heritage  [ 69 ].

To provide more embodiment and immersion, VR is a salient technology  [ 35 , 53 , 76 ], since it immerses users in simulated multi-sensory environments through panoramic views  [ 53 ]. Besides immersion, VR provides users with a subjective psychological sense of presence, i.e., the experience of "being there"  [ 35 ], by embodying them in a virtual body  [ 53 ]. With the rapid development of full-body tracked technologies in recent years  [ 77 ], users have been gaining more control of their avatars, experiencing enhanced bodily sensations in VR.

VMs in VR are usually deployed via two technical approaches: Cinematic VR (CVR) and 3DVR  [ 73 ]. CVR refers to VR scenes with 360-degree videos or images captured by a series of cameras, and an application of CVR in VMs is The British Museum's Work with Oculus (see Figure 1(b) )  [ 8 , 23 ]. Although the CVR approach demonstrates realistic environments, it mainly adopts simple branching narratives and hardly offers interactivity for users to actively explore the virtual worlds  [ 73 ]. In contrast, 3DVR provides more interactivity and agency by simulating real-time 3D graphical environments, where users can freely explore and interact with virtual objects. The Virtual Dutch Men [ 63 ] and The Museum of Fossilized Internet [ 16 ] are two examples of 3DVR VMs. While these 3DVR VM examples have better interactivity, they simply replicate the physical venues without many novel techniques or strategies that best suit the VR medium.

In summary, VMs have a long history, both theoretically and technically. Currently, web-based VMs are extensively used as an extension of the physical museum  [ 75 ]. However, studies on VMs as an independent novel experience, instead of an extension or substitution of physical museums, still need to be further explored. Users gain little embodied experience when exploring cultural heritage and artifacts in web-based VMs. To provide users with better embodiment in the digital environment, we consider 3DVR an ideal approach. However, the current challenges and actual benefits of implementing VMs in 3DVR have yet to be thoroughly investigated in practice. Implementing VMs with the existing technologies and applications helps us experience real-world obstacles to provide practical strategies for future VM designers and curators.

2.2 Multi-User Virtual Worlds and Social VR as Virtual Exhibition Venues

Users can meet, play, and work together when they are physically apart in multi-user virtual worlds such as Second Life (SL) and Minecraft  [ 3 , 9 , 38 ]. Early research on multi-user virtual worlds includes collaborative virtual environments (CVEs)  [ 29 , 30 ], social activities in virtual worlds  [ 36 , 41 , 43 ], and ethnographic studies of virtual worlds  [ 33 , 34 , 60 ]. While this body of work has recorded a wide range of activities and behaviors in multi-user virtual worlds (e.g., education and learning  [ 44 ]), very few studies have discussed activities related to exhibitions and museums  [ 39 ].

As commercial VR applications have become more popular, social VR emerged, which refers to a set of applications where users can socialize through VR HMDs  [ 61 ]. Prevailing social VR applications include AltspaceVR  [ 5 ], RecRoom  [ 6 ], and VRChat  [ 20 ]. These applications offer different degrees of tracked avatars from head-tracked only to full-body tracked avatars  [ 57 ], providing embodiment to different degrees. The existing work on social VR has investigated similar topics to early multi-user virtual worlds, such as activities in social VR  [ 57 ], communication modes  [ 62 , 65 ], and social interaction consequences  [ 32 , 79 ]. With a series of features such as synchronous voice chat and anonymity empowered by avatars, social VR helps users enhance social skills and improve social connectivity  [ 57 ], which can also benefit VM experiences.

Social interactions play an critical role in VMs  [ 52 , 54 ], compensating for the weakened embodied experience compared to physical exhibitions  [ 54 ]. Besides, the lack of embodiment and distinctiveness due to simple simulation of the physical venue can be improved with the performative and interactive quality of born-digital cultural artifacts  [ 13 ]. Born-digital artworks and cultural artifacts that depend on the computer for the creation and showcase can be fully experienced only when enacted within complex ensembles of hardware and software  [ 13 ], which highly requires audience participation. In other words, exhibitions as a whole are social processes, and virtual exhibitions are social processes that play out via computer-generated graphics  [ 13 ].

Despite little scholarly work on multi-user virtual worlds and social VR as exhibition venues, some recent exhibitions and VMs in the virtual worlds have received great attention from the public. For example, there are many museums in SL, which could be categorized into two types: (1) SL replicas of physical museums and (2) museums particularly designed for SL  [ 59 ]. For the first category, an example is the Gemaldegalerie Alte Meister in Dresden [ 24 ], which mirrors its real-life counterpart on a similar scale. Although this example replicates its physical venue, it incorporates many interactive features, such as information cards that lead audiences to search for information. For museums particularly designed for SL, a well-known example is the Virtual Museum of Art (VMOA) [ 19 ], dedicated to contemporary art in SL. The navigation method at VMOA is by flying, creating its own rules that are distinct from real life. Besides SL museums, VRChat has recently become another popular site for virtual exhibitions. For example, a VRChat world named “Chinese Memes Museum” has aroused extensive attention since its initial release in 2022  [ 80 ]. This VM assembles a large number of 3D artifacts that demonstrate the development of Chinese Internet culture. The “Chinese Memes Museum” has many novel interaction techniques such as stereo audio guides that simulate real-life effects, a Super Mario warp pipe that teleports users from one exhibition hall to another, etc. Besides, its great sociality has been widely praised, e.g., allowing up to twenty audiences to visit the museum, take pictures, and have fun together in the same instance.

In summary, although the growing social VR research agenda in HCI has covered many facets of social activities, its practices in virtual exhibition venues have been largely unexplored. Since social VR supports social interactions that are beneficial for enhancing embodied knowledge in VMs  [ 13 , 57 ], we are motivated to study: (1) the unique affordances that museums in social VR provide for audience engagement and exhibition design; (2) the challenges for audience engagement and exhibition design in social VR, compared to physical exhibitions; and (3) the strategies to mitigate these challenges.

3 STUDY 1: EXPERT INTERVIEWS

3.1 Methods

This study consists of three phases (see Figure 2 ), one interview study, an autobiographical design study, and a user study. We first conducted expert interviews with nine professional art curators (E1-E9) to understand the promises, challenges, strategies of designing virtual exhibitions, and how virtual exhibitions can learn and adapt from physical exhibitions. We modeled this study on similar design research that has conducted expert interviews to develop a design framework  [ 46 , 50 , 51 ]. As suggested by McVeigh-Schultz et al.  [ 61 ], industry experience may fill in essential gaps in academic research by reflecting real-world practices  [ 61 ].

3.1.1 Participant Recruitment. All the curators in the interviews have been curating physical exhibitions in authoritative art institutions worldwide for over three years. The institutions they work for include state-owned art museums in the USA, municipal art museums in mainland China, Hong Kong, Britain, and South Korea, and private galleries and salons in the USA. Otherwise, they have at least one year's experience working with a virtual curatorial medium, including immersive AR and VR exhibitions, web-based residencies, and digital archiving programs of physical exhibitions (see Table 1 ). The selection of curators with diverse backgrounds can reflect a wide range of curatorial practices in both physical and virtual exhibitions.

Potential professional curators were purposefully approached through personal connections or reaching out to official accounts of their art institutions. Since there were limited resources regarding virtual exhibition design guidelines and our research adopted an exploratory approach, the purposive sampling technique allowed us to directly reach out to experts of our interest more efficiently  [ 45 ]. For example, E2 is the curator of the aforementioned “Chinese Memes Museum” , which has greatly inspired our work, so we directly reached out to him.

Four interviewees were recruited from two authors’ personal connections via messaging them on Instagram and WeChat. Three interviewees were recruited by reaching out to their affiliated institutions via email. Two interviewees were from an artist group whose artwork was exhibited in the VR exhibition we designed in this study's second phase. We found they had substantial expertise in virtual exhibitions during the communication process via email. Therefore, we also invited them to our expert interviews. Also, we distributed recruitment advertisements in Chinese and English through emails and private messages on WeChat and Instagram. All those who self-identified as professional art curators with at least three years of physical curation experience or at least one year of virtual curation experience were invited. Given their industry status as professional curators, they agreed to reveal their names in the acknowledgment section. This study has been approved by Duke Kunshan University's Institutional Review Board (IRB).

3.1.2 Semi-Structured Interviews. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with nine professional art curators. The interviews were conducted remotely using video or audio calls in July and August of 2022 via Zoom and Tencent Meeting. Each interview lasted approximately 60 minutes. The interviews included questions about the curator's working experiences, their workflow of curating physical or virtual exhibitions, preferred strategies to engage and educate audiences, etc (see the complete list of interview questions in A.3). Interviewees were asked specifically about the differences that lie in the process of designing physical and virtual exhibition experiences from a curator's perspective, including the selection of different artwork mediums, information acquisition methods, and audience engagement strategies. They were also primed to recall their first-hand experiences when navigating various virtual exhibitions on the market as a visitor, including their perceived advantages and drawbacks of the exhibitions, as well as their expectations for future virtual exhibitions. Interviews were audio-taped in Mandarin and English and transcribed by the transcription service of Zoom and Tencent Meeting.

3.1.3 Data Analysis. To analyze the interview transcripts, we conducted qualitative thematic coding, similar to Saldaña's approach to coding  [ 72 ]. Three native Mandarin-speaking authors first coded the transcripts individually using an inductive approach and then met weekly to reach a consensus on codes. All the codes were then translated into English and were discussed by the research team using affinity diagramming to find emerging themes. All codes were transcribed on an independent codebook and then arranged in a random order. We then iteratively rearranged the notes into a hierarchy of themes and reached a consensus on the themes related to physical and virtual curatorial workflow, audience engagement, interaction design, and information acquisition strategies in virtual exhibitions.

3.2 Findings

In this section, we present our findings mainly in two folds. First, to answer RQ1 ( What are the promises and challenges of curating virtual exhibitions compared to physical exhibitions? ), we report three themes about the promises and three themes about the challenges of virtual exhibitions identified by the expert interviewees, along with the strategies recommended by the interviewees to answer RQ2 ( What strategies do curators employ in response to the challenges of curating virtual exhibitions? ). These proposed strategies will be further validated in section 5.

3.2.1 Promises of Virtual Exhibitions. Our expert interviewees pointed out mainly three aspects of what makes virtual exhibitions promising compared to physical exhibitions. First, virtual exhibitions are free of limitations from physical properties such as time, space, money, Covid, and the number of participants. Second, virtual exhibitions can provide audiences with enhanced experiences in appreciating born-digital artworks such as VR paintings. Third, audiences can have novel social experiences in virtual exhibitions via VR HMDs that provide immersion and embodiment.

Free of Physical Limitations. One of the most significant benefits that virtual exhibitions offer is that they can avoid physical limitations (e.g., time, space, and money), which usually downgrade audiences’ experiences in physical exhibitions. More specifically, physical exhibitions tend to generate tremendous financial, time, and communication costs. As most (7/9) of our interviewees mentioned, virtual exhibitions can optimize the visual effect of large installation works without worrying too much about the budget. For example, E1 (Female, 27, Hybrid) mentioned,

"In terms of visual effects, virtual exhibitions have the greater potential [than physical exhibitions]...For example, once, we curated a virtual exhibition featuring a lot of large installations. If we had done this exhibition in real life, the budget and the chosen material would have been a great challenge, but we didn't need to worry about these problems since it was a virtual exhibition."

According to E1, in virtual exhibitions, curators are not necessarily concerned about the budget that might limit the effect of how artworks are arranged in physical exhibitions. Especially when it comes to artworks of large sizes or expensive materials needed in the exhibition, virtual exhibitions can be a lot cheaper and more flexible.

In addition to the artworks themselves, virtual exhibitions are also budget-saving in terms of interior decorations, e.g., fountains, waterfalls, fireworks, etc. As E2 (Male, 28, Virtual) noted,

"In my virtual museum, I put some waterfalls and streams, which are expensive to maintain in real life...Also, I put fireworks at the end of the virtual museum and some plants. You don't need to worry about maintaining these things in a virtual museum. All you need to consider is how to make the design look good because you are in a virtual space with unlimited available spaces."

For E2, an essential part of his virtual museum is the interior landscape design, which could be highly time and money-consuming to maintain in physical exhibitions. In virtual spaces, there are few limitations in time and budget. Admittedly, virtual exhibitions could generate costs of 3D models, pay for modelers, etc. It is still at a meager cost compared to physical exhibitions that involve high costs of venues, renovations, logistics, maintenance, etc.

On top of the budget limitation, the spatial properties of the original physical space (e.g., the spatial layout, the floor height, and the materials of walls) also constrain curation to a large extent. According to E3 (Female, 32, Hybrid),

"Curators usually have an ideal ordering of artworks in the physical space. However, brutally, when remapping works into the physical space, you have to compromise a lot. For example, if I want to have a large projection space for the work in the first space but the space is not sufficient, I can only rearrange the work somewhere else. Therefore, it is actually a mutual negotiation between the curator's planned ordering of the work in the space and the constraints of actual conditions."

In E3’s opinion, curators usually have a plan for the locations where artworks should be placed, crafting the spatial narrative that leads audiences to understand the theme of the exhibition. However, due to actual conditions of the physical venue, e.g., the spatial layout, curators usually need to compromise their ideal ordering of artworks.

However, virtual exhibitions have no such concerns about unchangeable physical properties. E4 (Male, 26, Hybrid) and E9 (Female, 26, Hybrid) described their experiences in arranging the same pieces of artworks in both a physical and virtual space,

"We have a few works of sculpture that have been physically exhibited at the Chinese National Academy of Arts. The limitation was the floor height, which was only 6-8 meters. However, in our virtual exhibition, the floor height was set to be around 12 meters, making up for the limitation of the physical venue."

In addition to money, time, and space that could limit the effect of physical exhibitions, another factor that currently hinders the accessibility of physical exhibitions is Covid. In the era of Covid, the accessibility of virtual exhibitions has been valued by our expert interviewees. Five of our expert interviewees described their experiences canceling offline exhibitions or turning offline exhibitions to online forms due to Covid. For example, one hybrid curator noted,

"During the lockdown in Shanghai, we did not stand a chance to make the exhibition project happen offline, but we were quite urgent, so we made it online instead." (E1)

According to E1 and the other four interviewees who mentioned Covid as a current obstacle to physical exhibitions, more and more virtual exhibitions have emerged as a backup plan for offline exhibitions. Covid has become a catalyst for the rapid development of virtual exhibitions.

Apart from Covid, virtual exhibitions also provide accessibility that can break free from the physical limitations of geography and traits of audiences. In the context of globalization, when international communications have become frequent, virtual exhibitions open up opportunities for cross-border discussions on art. People from around the globe can visit an exhibition at the same time. In particular, exhibitions on social VR platforms, e.g., VRChat, allow users to change their avatars and be whoever they want to be. Therefore, racism and discrimination toward physical appearances can be ameliorated to a large degree. E2 described such novel experiences in a VR museum,

"In particular, compared to physical exhibitions, what makes virtual exhibitions promising is that you can become anyone in a virtual world. If you are a male, you can use a girl's avatar or a muscular man. You can also have a tour around the museum with anyone from any part of the world."

Echoing E2’s viewpoints about audiences’ appearances, another interviewee also mentioned that curators need to pay special attention to audiences’ physical traits in physical exhibitions. According to E5 (Female, 39, Physical),

"For example, if I make an exhibition [in real life] again, we have to build a space to deliver experiences effectively. However, since the target audiences were blind children, we could not use materials that could hurt the children. We need to avoid variables or even accidents that could happen when audiences make interactions or engage with the artwork."

For E5, considering audiences’ physical traits is critical when curating a physical exhibition to avoid potential accidents such as harmful materials. However, in virtual worlds, audiences of any physical traits can visit exhibitions without worrying about getting hurt physically.

Enhanced Experiences in Appreciating Born-Digital Artworks. Another prominent promise of virtual exhibitions noted by our expert interviewees is audiences’ enhanced experiences when appreciating born-digital artworks via virtual channels. By born-digital artworks, we mean artworks created entirely through digital interventions, e.g., AI-generated paintings, VR paintings that use tools such as Google Tilt Brush, and 3D models created with software such as Blender. Two of our expert interviewees explained,

"It is natural for pure digital artists to exhibit their works via screens. Their works were born in the virtual medium, and thus virtual channels are their works’ natural language." (E3)

"...I think the [virtual] medium that maximizes the potential of the [born-digital] artworks is suitable and can also democratize that aesthetic experience. Virtual exhibitions definitely have potential." (E5)

According to E3 and E5, the best way to showcase an artwork is to use the medium in which the work was originally created. Therefore, to maximize the presentation of born-digital artworks, presenting them in virtual mediums would be the most ideal.

Creating Social Experiences via Novel Interaction Techniques. Over the past decade of the booming internet and social media, museums and exhibitions have gradually become social occasions. According to E6 (Female, 30, Hybrid), “There is [an emerging] concept of turning museums into a social place.” Echoing her view, E7 (Male, 40, Hybrid) suggests,

"It has been a reality that over the last five to ten years, more and more ’wanghong zhan’ (commercial exhibitions that draw audiences to take photos and post them to social media) have emerged. The development of the internet has brought such socialization into art exhibitions."

For E7, due to technological advancement, the booming internet and social media have drawn people to exhibition venues, taking photos of artworks and themselves appreciating artworks and posting them on social media. Such a social trend has become increasingly common in museums and exhibitions. Hence, there is also a trend that curators value and design for social interactions in exhibitions, especially in commercial exhibitions.

Another promise of virtual exhibitions is that they can create new social experiences via novel interaction techniques, e.g., immersive and embodied interactions enabled by VR HMDs. One of our interviewees, E2, who just released a virtual museum project in VRChat, received a large number of positive comments on his work on Chinese social media. We asked why he had chosen to build a museum in VRChat, and he responded:

"If you use VR headsets, your body language will be projected into the virtual environment. When visiting an exhibition, you can point to some artworks, and your friends will be able to see your movements...Besides body movements, the stereo effect can resemble real-life social experiences, providing users with immersion...These dynamic social mechanics embedded in VRChat were the reasons why I chose this application."

For E2, there were mainly two novel interaction techniques that made him choose VRChat for his virtual museum project. First, embodied interactions enable users to project their body language into the virtual environment. When touring around the museum, friends can point to artworks for each other and experience artwork together. For instance, he mentioned a 3D car model in the museum, “There is one artwork in which three people [models] shake heads in the car model. If you have VR devices, you can shake your heads with your friends in the car, taking pictures and mimicking the artwork together.” Second, interactions that mimic real-life social experiences can provide audiences with immersion. For instance, the stereo effect in VRChat makes users hear only people near them, which resembles real-life conversations, immersing them in the exhibition like a tour in real life.

3.2.2 Challenges of Virtual Exhibitions and Proposed Strategies. While virtual exhibitions have much potential in terms of their flexibility, the virtual medium that better suits born-digital artworks, and novel social experiences, our interviewees also cast light on mainly three challenges that virtual exhibitions are currently facing: (1) a lack of adaptation strategies to curate virtual exhibitions when learning from physical exhibitions; (2) technical issues such as curators’ lack of skills in programming, game engines, 3D modeling, and high demand for audiences’ network environments and devices; (3) a lack of highlight in the necessity and unique benefits of virtual exhibitions, compared to physical exhibitions. To mitigate these problems, our interviewees have identified some specific strategies, e.g., designing realistic aesthetics and using intuitive interaction techniques to avoid some technical issues for audiences.

Lack of Adaptation Strategies When Replicating Physical Exhibitions. One major challenge for curators who want to make their first attempt at virtual exhibitions is that they lack adaptation strategies when learning from their experience in physical exhibitions since virtual exhibitions are still in their infancy.

The first type of adaptation that needs to be made is the design of spatial layouts and moving lines. For instance, E3 mentioned, “When designing the spatial layout of virtual exhibitions, it is a big challenge to make the layout allow audiences to have plenty of freedom to explore, while not making them confused.” Similarly, according to E2, who has expertise in virtual curation,

“In 3D virtual environments, people tend to become disoriented more easily, especially if there are return routes or the captions on the wall are too small, and people have to be super close to the wall...This is an example of not making any adaptations according to the characteristics of virtual environments.”

E2 contended that when curating virtual exhibitions, many details must be adjusted when learning from physical exhibitions. For example, there should be no branching or return routes that make audiences easily lose their sense of direction. Also, to minimize the side effects of VR (e.g., disorientation and motion sickness), the captions on the wall should be larger than those in real life, allowing audiences to see the content clearly at a distance.

Another adaptation that curators need to focus on is their workflow of communicating with the team. When asking the interviewees about their workflow of curating exhibitions, physical curators generally mentioned communicating with the construction team, graphic designers, public relation departments, etc. For curators with years of experience in physical curation, they are familiar with the workflow and people they need to contact– however, a large part of the communication workflow changes when switching to virtual curation. Instead of communicating with the construction team, virtual curators need to know how to communicate with programmers and coordinate the whole digital process. For instance, E6, who works primarily on physical exhibitions, mentioned, “It (virtual curation) is kind of like exploring different possibilities with a web programmer...There is a steep learning curve.” Similarly, E7 suggested, “I think what makes virtual exhibitions different from physical exhibitions is the required skill sets...Virtual exhibitions require much technology literacy, and [the needed skill set] is totally different from physical curation. Sometimes, curators might have many plans, but due to the lack of technology literacy, they cannot realize those plans.” According to the interviewees, even for experienced physical curators, switching to the workflow of virtual curation requires different skill sets from most of their past experiences, which makes virtual curation a hard thing to pick up.

To help curators adapt to virtual curation, two virtual and hybrid curators mentioned two approaches. First, an increasing number of online exhibition platforms have emerged, such as Spatial  [ 12 ], Museum of Other Realities  [ 15 ], and Slime  [ 17 ]. Curators can use these platforms to curate virtual exhibitions without experience in game engines or 3D modeling. As E1 mentioned, "We hope to directly curate on the platforms [without modeling or coding], but we are still waiting for an appropriate platform builder…I also noticed Slime Engine. I feel like they are still experimenting." That said, for E1, even though these platforms are promising, a well-established one that can suffice curators’ needs and audiences’ experiences at the same time has not yet emerged. As E2 noted a drawback of these online exhibition platforms, "The lower the threshold of this kind of [online exhibition] platforms for curators, the lower their creative freedom may be." To address this issue, E2 suggests the second approach– learning some basics of the game engine and 3D modeling and curating on VRChat. He contended that curating on VRChat only demands a few technical capabilities, and learning some basics of Unity would suffice to curate a fantastic virtual exhibition.

Technical Issues for Both Curators and Audiences. As mentioned above, curators need to have at least some understanding of technology, e.g., web programming skills, to better facilitate the workflow of virtual curation. Our expert interviewees mentioned specific technical issues they had encountered during virtual curation, e.g., web page maintenance, limited storage, failure to access, etc. As E6 explained, “I think the maintenance part [is hard]. Because we want to make sure it keeps running, and especially there are many projects that also document things or collect data. Then the project will keep expanding, and that would cause problems like the server does not have enough storage. Then the whole section shuts down, etc.” For E6, technical problems arise quite frequently during virtual curation. It is hard to maintain a virtual exhibition because of expanding data, such as a growing document about the artworks and artists. For smaller curator teams, technical issues are even more prominent. As E1 mentioned, “If you focus on virtual exhibitions, you need to have a lot of IT support. For curator teams as small as us, it is still the technical support [that most matters].” That said, it is critical for virtual curation teams to enhance interdisciplinary collaboration between professional curators and technical experts.

Not only curators would run into technical issues, but also audiences. Several interviewees (4/9) raised one major concern: audiences’ availability of the network and devices. For instance, E6 noted,

“I have a lot of reservations about virtual [exhibition] projects...For me, going to a physical exhibition is like an easy scenario– we do not worry too much about people not coming to our exhibition. However, for virtual [exhibition] projects, I think people have to make an effort to know the type of websites.”

For E6, physical exhibitions are easily accessible– people just need to walk into the venue. However, the public is mostly unfamiliar with virtual exhibitions, and therefore curators are worried about audiences not being able to see the exhibition by themselves. Similarly, E6 mentioned, “Virtual exhibitions seem to be accessible and open because everyone can make a website, create content with Instagram stories, or WeChat Moments...However, how you direct them to the virtual exhibition and whether they have the right device and enough network capacity to access [are all important questions]. Especially if special devices [like VR headsets] are required, many people will not be able to see the virtual exhibition.” According to E7, there are mainly three constraints of accessibility to virtual exhibitions– (1) some audiences do not know how to enter the virtual exhibition, e.g., being confused about which link to click; (2) audiences’ lack of network capacity; (3) audiences’ lack of required devices, e.g., VR headsets. These constraints are mainly given rise to by audiences’ lack of understanding of virtual exhibitions, which are still very new to the public.

To address this issue of the public's lack of understanding of virtual exhibitions, it is crucial to familiarize the public with virtual exhibitions by making the design features of virtual exhibitions intuitive enough. For example, the aesthetic style should be realistic enough, and the interaction techniques should be close to interactions in physical exhibitions. As E2 explained,

“Although I could freely customize the aesthetic style in a virtual environment, I did not want it [the virtual museum] to be too surrealistic, which means I hoped it would look like a real museum. Because almost everyone knows what a real museum is like, and if I make it realistic, audiences will be able to understand the experience quickly. If the style is surrealistic, this would increase the threshold of audiences’ understanding.”

For E2, the concept of virtual exhibitions is still new to the public. Therefore if the aesthetics and interaction techniques are distinct from physical museums and exhibitions, it will take some effort for audiences to understand how to navigate the space and interact with artworks, etc. Therefore, in order to conquer the unfamiliarity with virtual exhibitions at the current stage, it is necessary for virtual curators to make the exhibition like a physical one, including its aesthetic style and interaction techniques.

Lack of Highlights in the Unique Benefits of Virtual Exhibitions. Another concern that most (6/9) of our participants mentioned is the lack of highlights on the unique benefits of virtual exhibitions. For them, some virtual exhibitions, e.g., those that only feature 2D image artworks, are not convincing enough. For example, as E6 mentioned, “It (virtual exhibitions) is definitely a trend, and NFT is a trend, but it does not mean everyone has to ride on it. I was not really initiating all these virtual [exhibition] projects because I did not see the match or why this [exhibition] project works best online.” For E6, there is a trend of exhibitions and artworks going virtual, e.g., NFT art. However, she is very cautious about such a trend and still waiting for virtual exhibitions that are convincing enough.

Echoing E6’s point, E4 noted, “Currently, there is no difference between virtual exhibitions and games to the public who have not deeply investigated digitalization. It is just that commercial games have more interactions and dynamic social mechanics, so that is why I think many virtual exhibitions are not convincing enough to the public.” For E4, virtual exhibitions are not convincing enough because they are not distinct from games per se, but with less interactivity and fewer social mechanics, making the current virtual exhibitions less known and popular with the public.

To highlight the unique benefits and distinctions of virtual exhibitions, our interviewees proposed several approaches. First, focus on the unique characteristics of the virtual medium. As E7 explained, “Virtual exhibitions should not be a direct documentation and reproduction of physical exhibitions. There are many things you can play around with virtual environments such as its unique spatiality and temporality...Curators need to craft novel sensory experiences according to the uniqueness of the virtual medium.” Second, it is important to demonstrate as many 3D models as possible. Compared to 2D images, 3D models can craft novel sensory experiences in VR even better because 3D models in VR are usually not limited in their size and visual effects by budget or physical space. Additionally, as we mentioned in 3.2.1 Promises of Virtual Exhibitions , compared to physical exhibitions, virtual exhibitions are more suitable for born-digital artworks to be exhibited, e.g., VR paintings and 360-degree videos. Such artworks that cannot be easily exhibited in real life could offer unique affordances that physical exhibitions cannot.

3.3 Design Guidelines Drawn from Expert Interviews

After interviewing the expert curators, we summarized a set of main design guidelines:

  1. To lower the threshold of VR and decrease the onboarding time, VM designers and curators should adopt interaction and navigation techniques similar to real life so that audiences can adapt to the 3D graphical environments with less discomfort.
  2. To highlight the unique promises of exhibitions in VR, mediums that can be best interacted with in the VR environment should be prioritized, e.g., VR paintings, interactive 3D models, etc.
  3. When adapting curatorial strategies from real life, VM designers and curators need to make sure some design features are optimized, in order to adapt to the new medium, such as avoiding branching or return routes that might cause spatial disorientation in VR and enlarging captions that might cause difficulty in reading in VR;
  4. For curators who wish to have more freedom in virtual curation, it is crucial to master some basics of game engines such as Unity.
  5. For curatorial teams, it is crucial to enhance collaboration between professional curators and technical personnel.

4 THE HITCHHIKER'S GUIDE TO THE DREAM: THE VR ART EXHIBITION

Informed by our expert interviewees, we built a prototype of a virtual exhibition using an autobiographical design approach to validate the guidelines proposed by the interviewees. To see our final outcome, access "The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Dream: The VR Art Exhibition" in VRChat  [ 22 ]. In this section, we describe the autobiographical design study method, our design goals, principles, process, and outcomes to reflect on the emergent insights on virtual exhibition design.

4.1 Autobiographical Design Study Method

To validate the design guidelines drawn from expert interviews, we adopted an autobiographical design approach, which is a study method "drawn from extensive, genuine use by those creating or building the system"  [ 66 ]. This method has several benefits for our study. First, as autobiographical design supports the early stages of design iterations  [ 40 , 48 ], it is highly suitable for our exploratory study of social VR exhibitions that have been largely unexplored  [ 78 ]. Second, our method allowed us to iterate on the VR exhibition prototype rapidly and gain insights into real-world practices. During the design process, we built the system from scratch, learned about the design workflow, used the prototype ourselves, evaluated, and iterated the design, making it possible for us to understand practical benefits and challenges.

While autobiographical design research has been criticized for heavily focusing on oneself and lack of generalizability  [ 40 , 66 ], we invited thirty audiences to experience our system as described in section 5, in order to understand the advantages and drawbacks from the users’ perspectives, as a counterbalance to the critique of the autobiographical design study method.

4.2 Design Goals and Principles

Our overall goal of the autobiographical design study and the follow-up user study (section 5) was to (1) validate the design guidelines drawn from study 1 and (2) explore effective exhibition practices from the audiences’ perspective in the medium of 3DVR to address RQ3 ( What strategies can effectively engage audiences in virtual exhibitions? ). Therefore, our design goals are (1) to bring audiences brand new exhibition experiences by providing immersion and new ways of interacting with artworks and social interactions, and (2) to help audiences acquire new embodied information and insights into the concept of virtual exhibitions via social interactions, considering that social interactions contribute to the edutainment of exhibitions.

Drawing on results from expert interviews, we conclude our design principles as:

  1. A Realistic Visual Style and Intuitive Interaction Design. According to the expert interview, due to the public's unfamiliarity with virtual exhibitions, the best approach to lower the learning curve is to make the design realistic and the interaction techniques similar to those in physical exhibitions. Therefore, the aesthetic style and the interaction techniques, e.g., delivering messages through captions (see Figure 3 ), should be realistic and intuitive.
  2. Featuring Affordances that Only Virtual Exhibitions Offer. To highlight the unique features of the virtual medium in the context of exhibitions and make virtual exhibitions distinct from physical ones, our exhibition should feature affordances that only virtual exhibitions can offer, e.g., free of physical limitations, innovatively present born-digital artworks, etc.
  3. Adaptation Strategies from Physical Exhibitions. With our lessons learned from physical curation in expert interviews, we aim to adapt and redesign some curatorial strategies in physical exhibitions according to the characteristics of virtual exhibitions, e.g., the tendency of disorientation.

4.3 Design Process

4.3.1 Curatorial Statement. According to our expert interviewees, a clear and meaningful curatorial statement encompassing a central theme is critical to audiences’ information acquisition about exhibitions. Therefore, we started with choosing an appropriate theme for our virtual exhibition. Considering that we need to feature affordances that only virtual exhibitions can offer, we brainstormed themes that could feature a lot of born-digital artworks which audiences cannot fully experience in a physical setting. To this end, we came up with the theme "The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Dream: the VR Art Exhibition."

In the curatorial statement, we referred to VR as a dream technology that can allow us to dream infinitely. We invited audiences to think about how VR impacts our relationships with technology and society by looking at art created entirely in VR or via digital mediums.

4.3.2 Call for Artworks. After finalizing the curatorial statement, we published a recruitment post on a WeChat account, calling for born-digital artworks that could be exhibited through VR. As of Aug 2022, we had received artworks from 22 groups of artists, including 3D models, VR paintings, AI-generated paintings, digital paintings, VR photography, VR films, and some physical works such as oil paintings and photography of physical objects in real life. See Table 2 in the Appendix for the detailed list of artists and artworks.

To organize the theme narrative and follow the design principles, we filtered the artworks and eventually chose 17 artworks. Our selection criteria were: (1) entirely created in digital mediums, e.g., through VR, AI algorithms, 3D modeling, and so on, (2) can fit into the narrative of our theme question: how can VR as a dream technology affect us as individuals and our society?

We designed three chapters for our exhibition. The first chapter is about the history of VR development and VR art. We aimed to first present audiences with an overview of VR and different types of VR art. Therefore this chapter features artworks created with different digital mediums, including VR paintings, 3D models, VR photography, and VR films. These mediums are not easily accessible in real life, especially for VR paintings that are both three-dimensional and dynamic. For example, there is a VR painting (see Figure  5(a) & 5(b) ) that was inspired by traditional Chinese painting Wuzhong Landscape by Zhou Shen from the Ming Dynasty. Audiences can walk into the painting and interact with the dynamic mountains, flowing streams, and vivid trees, experiencing ancient Chinese lifestyle from the first-person perspective. This experience would be extremely hard and expensive to replicate in real life because the dynamic 3D models are both big in size and hard to maintain.

The second chapter focuses on how digitization, such as VR, affects every individual. Therefore all of the artworks in this chapter deliver a message from personal perspectives, e.g., one artist's works are VR photographs taken in VRChat when she traveled to different virtual worlds with her friends, demonstrating that people can have as much fun as in real life through social VR. This work was chosen because we thought it might change audiences’ understanding of social experiences in VR.

The third chapter is related to the impact of VR and other technologies on society. In this chapter, we first presented artworks that showcase the relationship between humans and nature with the coordination of technology. For example, an artwork is a series of virtual ornaments that use materials resembling algae. Through this work, the artist wants to invite audiences to speculate about the future of symbiosis between humans and algae. Audiences can hold the ornaments, place them onto their avatars’ faces, and see how they look by turning on the mirror in front of them. In physical exhibitions, such artworks would be constrained in two senses: (1) 3D printing and fabrication are expensive and (2) it would be hard to maintain the virtual ornaments since staff would have to make sure audiences will not damage the artworks and will put them back. However, there were no such concerns in our virtual exhibition, breaking free from the physical limitations.

4.3.3 3D Environmental Design and Implementation. We designed our 3D virtual environment in Blender. We first designed a 2D floor plan layout (see Figure 4 ) for the exhibition and then built the model of the entire virtual exhibition venue in Blender. After the model was done, we matched it with different textures and materials for floors, walls, ceilings, and exhibition caption boards.

We implemented the exhibition in Unity using official VRChat Software Development Kit (SDK)  [ 11 ]. We took full advantage of the scripts empowered by VRChat SDK to design interaction techniques, e.g., VRC Station Script for audiences to sit on the chairs and VRC Pickup Script for audiences to pick up objects and interact with our artworks, etc. The exhibition was uploaded as a public world named "The Hitchhiker's Guide To The Dream VR Art Exhibition" (enter the virtual instance through  [ 22 ]) in VRChat.

4.4 Design Outcomes

4.4.1 Key features. Conforming to our design principles, our final design features: (1) a VR painting scroll that audiences can enter (see Figure  5(a) and 5(b) ); (2) a 360 degree immersive VR film theater (see Figure  6(a)); (3) interactive 3d models (see Figure  6(b)); (4) creative spaces that allow users to create freely, leave comments and socialize (see Figure 6(c) ).

4.4.2 User Flow. Following the route map (see Figure 4 ), audiences will go through the nine steps in their flow of visiting our exhibition: (1) audiences onboard and spawn at the entrance of the exhibition hall; (2) audiences enter the first exhibition hall; (3) audiences read the captions of our curatorial statement on the wall; (4) audiences watch videos and look at images; (5) audiences enter the VR painting scroll and interact with VR paintings; (6) audiences participate in VR art creation themselves; (7) audiences watch VR films in an immersive theater; (8) audiences continue to look at images and videos, and interact with 3D models; (9) audiences finish the tour and leave comments with pens on the wall in the closing space.

5 STUDY 2: USER STUDY

5.1 Methods

5.1.1 Participant Recruitment. We recruited 30 participants, including the artists, HCI researchers, and other audiences interested in the VR exhibition to participate in the open event through posting recruitment articles on social media (e.g., WeChat and Instagram). There were 23 males and 7 females with an average age of 23.37 (SD=6.30), who were also respondents of the online survey made on the Qualtrics Questionnaire Platform. The detailed information of participants can be seen in Table 3 in the A.2.

5.1.2 Procedure. Initially, We set up a WeChat group chat for our participants to inform them of the information about the exhibition and guides to VRChat. After the exhibition was uploaded to VRChat, all participants entered the exhibition. Participants needed to visit the exhibits and experience the exhibition's interactive features. The tour time was about two hours, and two researchers were guiding participants in the open event (see Figure 7 ). At the end of the exhibition, all participants filled out a questionnaire that included eight open-ended questions to collect their feedback (see A.4).

5.2 Findings

We collected participants’ answers to eight open-ended questions, ranging from general questions (e.g., after visiting this exhibition, please share with us your new perspectives about VR/virtual exhibitions; Is there anything else you want to say? Any suggestions/comments are welcomed.) to more specific questions asking participants to compare their experiences in our exhibition and other types of exhibitions, e.g., physical exhibitions and other types of VMs. We also asked participants to indicate the features they liked in the exhibition and features they wished to have in future versions.

General Positive Comments on the Novelty and Immersion. In response to the general questions (after visiting this exhibition, please share with us your new perspectives about VR/virtual exhibitions; Is there anything else you want to say? Any suggestions/comments are welcomed.), most participants (21/30) left very positive comments. For instance, 12 participants mentioned it was such a very novel experience that they had never had before, and they considered this exhibition "creative" , "innovative" , and "visually impactful" . In particular, one participant commented, "Compared to traditional interactions, VR enhanced my exhibition experience. While maintaining some realism and immersion just like being in offline exhibitions, VR also brought unique experiences. I believe it is going to be a trend in the future."

Comparison with Physical Exhibitions. Concerning the question of "Compared to traditional offline exhibitions, what do you like about our exhibition?" , participants’ answers could be categorized into (1) no cost of tickets, (2) convenience and no need to travel, (3) less social anxiety in an avatar-mediated communication environment where physical appearances are neglected, (4) free of physical limitation for the presentation of artworks, (5) more interactions with the artworks especially VR paintings instead of just looking at pictures in real life, and (6) the possibility of touring with oversea friends at any time.

Participants’ Favorite Features. For the feature that participants like most, the most mentioned one (11/28) is being able to enter the VR paintings and interact with the objects in the paintings (see Figure  5(a) and 5(b) ). What follows next (7/28) is our fourth key feature: creative spaces where participants are transformed to be creators and able to leave comments 6(c) . Several participants also mentioned they liked such social experiences where people could tour around with friends, especially those from overseas, and appreciated our tour guide with many people together.

Suggestions for Future Development. Meanwhile, participants also gave some suggestions for future improvements. In response to questions such as "Compared to traditional offline exhibitions, what do you dislike about our exhibition?" , participants mainly complained about three aspects: (1) it was difficult to find the right space and participate for those who had never used VRChat before, especially with VR; (2) there were network issues that led to lag and failure in playing video artworks; (3) there were some bugs in modeling and lighting, e.g., participants could walk through the walls, and the lighting was flickering to some VR users.

6 DISCUSSION

To answer our research questions, our study mainly consists of three parts: (1) the expert interviews that investigate the promises, challenges, and industry experiences in both physical and virtual curation ( RQ1 & RQ2 ), (2) the design and implementation process of a virtual exhibition in VRChat, and (3) a user study that validates effective design strategies to engage audiences in virtual exhibitions ( RQ3 ).

In this section, we use our findings from the expert interviews, autobiographical reflections on the design, development, and curation process, and user feedback to discuss how our work extends the prior work on understanding the nuances and trade-offs of VMs, a series of design, technical, and methodological implications, as well as constructive strategies for future research directions.

6.1 Understanding the Nuances and Trade-offs of VMs in Social VR Compared to Physical Museums

In this paper, we extend the previous studies on the nuances and trade-offs of VMs compared to physical museums to investigate the design strategies of VMs. To do so, we have conducted two studies: (1) one interview study that explored design guidelines for curation in both physical and virtual settings from experts in both fields and (2) one follow-up user study of our autobiographical design outcome that aimed to validate the design guidelines drawn from expert interviews.

Intriguingly, findings from the two studies demonstrate both convergence and divergence in terms of the trade-offs and design strategies of VMs. For example, in study 1, the expert interviewees suggested that virtual exhibitions tend to be more flexible in time, geographical spaces, and budget, which leads to better visual effects and more ideal narratives. Building upon the proposed benefits from the perspective of curators, participants from study 2 also noted that they could visit VMs with their friends remotely, free from spatial-temporal limitations. In addition, participants’ complaints from study 2 also echoed study 1. Participants in study 2 felt most unsatisfied with technical issues, including not being able to find the right space and networking issues, which were also mentioned in the findings of study 1, 3.2.2 Challenges of Virtual Exhibitions and Proposed Strategies .

Despite the convergence of study 1 and 2, there is also a divergence between them in the design strategies of VMs. There were some features in our exhibition drawn from expert interviews that participants wished could be different. For example, in study 1, experts noted that the aesthetic style and interaction techniques should be realistic and intuitive. However, one of our participants with VR HMDs from study 2 commented that he wished the aesthetic style could be bolder and more surrealistic to further highlight the unlimited potential of VMs. For some participants, this exhibition took place in the metaverse , and therefore they expected a more surrealistic style in virtual exhibitions  [ 61 ]. This indicated that audiences’ understanding of virtual exhibitions varies depending on their different understanding of the current technological affordances. With access to VR HMDs, the participant who wished to have more surrealistic effects demonstrated a more advanced use of technologies and hence a higher expectation. This divergence between expert guidelines and user feedback demonstrates the importance for virtual curators to have an accurate target population to better serve their aesthetic tastes and different capabilities of mastering interaction techniques.

Despite the convergence and divergence between study 1 and 2, both of them extend the current understanding of VMs, especially regarding the trade-offs and nuances of VMs compared to physical museums.

6.1.1 The Trade-Offs and Nuances of VMs in Social VR Compared to Physical Museums. Our findings are consistent with prior work on how social interactions can compensate for the weakened embodiment in digital environments compared to physical venues. As King et al.  [ 54 ] argue, it is evident that exhibitions in virtual venues do not provide the same social and embodied experiences as the physical museum, and one approach to mitigating this issue is through emphasizing human connections. Other researchers also echo King et al., suggesting that social interactions enrich virtual exhibition experiences because a satisfied visitor can share the experience with others  [ 52 ]. Besides having greater satisfaction while touring with others, it is also suggested that users can enhance learning in immersive environments such as VR  [ 31 , 67 ]. Similarly, in response to our survey questions, a large number of positive comments reveal that even though many of the participants noted that they generally felt more immersed in offline exhibitions, they particularly liked our tour guide with a group of people together in the open event. At the open event, participants could communicate with others about their understanding of artworks; therefore, such socialization enhanced learning and information acquisition. Besides social interactions such as participating in group activities that can enhance embodied knowledge, our expert interviewees and participants agreed that interacting through full-body tracked avatars greatly enhanced their embodied experiences, e.g., pointing to the artworks with non-verbal cues and taking selfies with VRChat cameras.

However, our findings also show that some expert curators from our interview study held different perspectives toward social interactions in VMs. Echoing a previous study  [ 31 ], the experts indicated they prefer designing a space where audiences can appreciate artwork at their own pace. According to our interviewees, social interactions could also be a double-edged sword that jeopardizes audiences’ concentration on the exhibition content. This also indicates a future research topic in virtual exhibitions, e.g., how to balance audiences’ focus on exhibits and their social engagement to achieve an overall balanced user experience? (More in section 6.3).

In addition to the controversies around the role of social interactions in VMs, another finding in this study that is prominently distinct from prior work is the accessibility of virtual exhibitions. As pointed out by researchers  [ 54 , 56 ], besides highlighting social interactions, another point to make up for the weakened embodiment compared to physical museums is the greater accessibility of VMs. However, in our study, breaking free from physical limitations does not necessarily mean greater accessibility to museum content. Many of our participants in the user study noted that using specific software (e.g., VRChat) and even advanced hardware (e.g., VR HMDs) took them a long time to access. In our expert interviews, experts also demonstrated similar technical concerns that audiences might need help accessing virtual exhibitions. With the technological constraints and threshold for audiences and curators, sometimes a physical museum is more accessible than a VM.

In summary, our findings suggest that while social interactions can provide our participants with more embodied experiences in digital environments  [ 42 ], some expert curators also raised questions about whether social interactions would interfere with audiences’ concentration. Also, even though a body of prior work has suggested that emphasizing the accessibility of VMs is another approach to making up for the weakened immersion compared to physical museums  [ 54 ], our studies suggest that both curators and audiences have concerns about the technological threshold and constraints, such as a lack of devices and internet capacities.

6.2 Autobiographical Reflections: Successes and Failures in the Design, Development, and Curation Process

In this paper, we have encountered several challenges throughout the design, development, and curation process. In this section, we reflect on the successes and failures in the hands-on process to better inform future work of a similar scope.

6.2.1 Curation and 3D Environmental Design. As noted above, we initially started our curation by writing a curatorial statement and calling for digital artworks. One problem we encountered during this stage was receiving a number of physical artworks or digital artworks that could not highlight the benefits of VMs. Because we were initially worried about receiving very few digital artworks, we did not specify the medium of their digital artworks, with only the minimum requirement that the work must be able to be exhibited via VR. As a result, we received a lot of unrelated works that were not considered born-digital artworks. Hence, a lesson learned from this is to specify the medium as accurately as possible. If the number of artworks received is insufficient, curators can consider reaching out to the digital artists who demonstrate their works online.

During the phase of 3D environmental design, we chose to build the scenes in Blender, a beginner-friendly interface that enabled us to prototype the scenes quickly. However, due to the limited time we had for 3D environmental design, we did not do any light rendering or UV mapping, which downgraded the quality of the 3D environment. As our participants also noted in their feedback, the entire 3D environment still needed to be optimized. Therefore, curators should ensure enough time for the team to do 3D scene construction since it takes a lot of time and different stages, e.g., 3D modeling, finding materials and textures, UV mapping, rendering lights, etc.

6.2.2 Technical Implementation. We implemented our virtual exhibition in Unity with the interactive script VRChat SDK  [ 11 ]. The development process in Unity went smoothly without many technical obstacles due to the easy use and flexibility of the script provided by the official VRChat development team. With the interactive script, we have made the exhibition much more interactive than simply allowing audiences to walk around. In the exhibition, our audiences can sit on the chairs, grab pencils of different colors to write anywhere they prefer, change the settings of video players, such as the volume, and turn on and off the mirrors. All of these features were achieved with the open-source prefabs from the game community. No coding was required during this process. Therefore, we highly recommend that curators without much coding experience consider curating within VRChat if flexible and interactive features are desired.

However, one significant challenge during technical implementation arose with the video players. Many of our participants noted their failures in playing the videos. We later found out that VRChat video players currently only support a couple of online video platforms  [ 18 ], and most of the URLs need to be resolved with third-party platforms such as ParseVideo  [ 7 ].

6.2.3 Conducting User Studies Through Open Events. A critical methodological implication of our work is conducting the user study by organizing open events to gather feedback from many participants. This was a very efficient way of collecting data without having to ask each of them to walk through the exhibition. During the exhibition, we live-streamed our open tour guide on Bilibili  [ 21 ], an online video platform that gathers many different communities. Eventually, over 40 people came to our open events, many of whom noted they were attracted to the open event from the live stream. Also, we put a large-size QR code of our survey and emphasized that everyone had a chance to win our compensation on a lottery basis by filling out the survey. In doing so, many people who came to our open events were motivated to fill out the feedback survey. That said, over the two hours of the user study, we successfully collected valid data from 30 participants.

In summary, during our design, development, and curation process, we encountered several challenges, mainly from the lack of artworks of the ideal mediums such as VR paintings and 3D modeling artworks, limited time in 3D environmental construction, and technical obstacles with the video players. However, after the autobiographical reflections, we also recommend curators who want to take a similar approach to curate in VRChat that provides more flexibility in designing and implementing interactive features without coding needed. We also recommend that researchers who need to collect a lot of data within a short period of time should organize similar open events, e.g., group workshops, focus groups, tour guides, etc.

6.3 Limitations and Future Work

Our user study has several limitations. First, our prototype and the VRChat platform have a few technical limitations. Our VM requires better-rendered lighting to improve users’ aesthetic experience. Besides, our participants also encountered network connection issues and difficulties navigating the VM due to VRChat's steep learning curve. Therefore, future work could explore other browser-based social VR applications (e.g., Gather  [ 14 ] and Mozilla Hubs  [ 10 ]), which are easier to access for most users. Second, we acknowledge that various VM media could have unique design strategies. While designing VMs in social VR is promising for embodied and interactive social experiences, our guidelines might not be generalizable to all types of VMs.

Additionally, our work has revealed two emerging directions in future work. First, while most participants indicated they enjoyed the social interactions in our VM, some comments from the user study and expert interviews revealed that content would be their priority focus. Therefore, future work should explore the balance between focus on content and social experiences, and between free exploration and fully guided tours. Second, since all participants from our user study were individuals without disabilities, our design guidelines did not cover potential accessibility needs. Although accessibility issues have long been valued in physical exhibitions  [ 55 , 64 ], especially for people with visual impairment  [ 25 , 26 ], accessibility challenges in VMs have been largely unexplored. Enabling people with accessibility needs (e.g., people with visual or hearing impairment, autistic children, and older adults) to experience cultural heritage and art in VMs can encourage their engagement with other audiences without disabilities  [ 37 ]. Thus, future work could explore the accessibility guidelines of VMs and virtual exhibitions for marginalized audiences.

7 CONCLUSION

Through expert interviews, we summarized the promises and challenges of virtual exhibitions. Based on the identified challenges, the interviewees also proposed several strategies to address the issues that might hinder audiences’ experiences in VR. Following the expert interviews, we used an autobiographical design approach to curate a virtual exhibition in VRChat based on the design guidelines drawn from the interviews. In general, our design conformed to 3 principles: (1) a realistic visual style and intuitive interaction design; (2) featuring affordances that only virtual exhibitions offer; (3) adaptation strategies learned from physical exhibitions. We held an open event to give participants a tour of the exhibition and collected their feedback after the event session. Their responses were mostly positive, especially regarding the design for VR painting scrolls, creative spaces, and the socialization in our exhibition. Participants also gave some suggestions and complaints, mainly about how difficult and troublesome it was to use VRChat for the first time, network connection issues that led to lag and failure to play videos, and some bugs in the models and lighting. We reflected on our successes and failures during design, development, and curation. Our design guidelines and autobiographical design reflections could provide validated guidelines and hands-on experiences for future virtual exhibition and VM design.

A.3 The List of Interview Questions

I: Demographics.

1. Can you briefly introduce your experiences as a curator? How long have you been a curator? What museum/gallery have you worked for? What exhibitions have you ever curated/produced?

II: Physical Curatorial Process.

1. Based on your experiences, could you describe the overall curatorial process when delivering a physical exhibition?

2. What are the major steps of curating an exhibition from start to finish?

3. How do you choose the theme of an exhibition?

4. What information do you consider essential to be included in an exhibition? What is your order of information delivery? Like your primary information, secondary information, etc.

5. What are some factors that you have most concerns about during the whole physical curation process? For instance, physical environmental limitations, curatorial logistics, transportation and restoration, etc.)

6. Does route planning matter to curators? How do you usually plan the visitors’ routes?

7. Do forms/mediums of artworks matter to you? How do forms/ mediums of artworks influence the way you curate an exhibition?

III: Digital Curation.

1. Have you had any digital curation experiences? If yes, can you describe the most memorable one?

2. If you were given a chance to curate an exhibition in VR, what would be different from your curatorial process in the physical curation?

3. What are the factors that you have most concerns about during the whole digital curation process? For instance, virtual space desigg, spatial influence, communication, modeling, etc.

4. What's the most challenging step in the digital curation workflow compared to physical ones? Why and how do you think it could be enhanced?

IV: Attitudes Towards Digital Curation as a Visitor.

1. Have you paid any visits to virtual exhibitions yourself? If no, can you imagine what you will like or dislike about it.

2. What do you think about some exhibitions held in VR or the metaverse?

3. What do you think is the primary motivation of attending a virtual exhibition?

V: Visitor Experience.

1. Can you describe your experiences when you try to make the audience engaged with the exhibits? How did you do it? Did it work out well?

2. Do you consider social interactions important to an art exhibition?

3. What aspects do you focus on to make sure visitors have good experiences?
