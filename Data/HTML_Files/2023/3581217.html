Shape-Adaptive Ternary-Gaussian Model: Modeling Pointing Uncertainty for Moving Targets of Arbitrary Shapes

CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

This paper presents a Shape-Adaptive Ternary-Gaussian model for describing endpoint uncertainty when pointing at moving targets of arbitrary shapes. The basic idea of the model is to combine the uncertainty related to the target shape with the uncertainty caused by the target motion. First, we proposed a model to predict endpoint distribution on static targets based on a Dual-Space Decomposition (DUDE) algorithm. Then, we linearly combined a 2D Ternary-Gaussian model with the newly proposed DUDE-based model to make the 2D Ternary-Gaussian model adaptable to moving targets with random shapes. To verify the performance of our model, we compared it with the original 2D Ternary-Gaussian model and a recent proposed Inscribed Circle model in predicting endpoint distribution. The results show that the proposed model outperformed the two baseline models while maintaining good robustness across different shapes and moving speeds.

1 INTRODUCTION

Moving target acquisition, as a fundamental research topic in HCI, has recently gained increasing attention  [ 16 , 21 , 26 ]. A considerable amount of studies have been conducted to model moving target selection to aid in designing user interfaces involving dynamic content  [ 2 , 19 , 20 , 24 , 25 ]. For user interfaces with moving targets such as games and video surveillance systems, targets could have different shapes, which is likely to have an impact on pointing uncertainty (i.e., the distribution of endpoints formed by users repeatedly pointing at a target). Understanding such endpoint distributions is important because they can determine the selection error rate (ER) [ 21 , 22 ] and partly affect movement time (MT) [ 4 , 43 ]. However, there are few attempts to model endpoint distributions for moving targets with specific considerations of the target shape. Establishing such models could quantify user performance in selecting targets with different shapes and provide design insights for user interfaces involving dynamic targets.

Existing studies have looked into the effects of target shape on MT prediction. Sheikh and Hoffmann  [ 37 ] conducted a Fitts’ law  [ 13 ] study on static targets with different shapes and identified significant effects of target shapes on MT. They observed a “shape factor” that related a bivariate normal distribution of hits (i.e., endpoints) on the target. Following the idea of improving MT prediction of Fitts’ law with regard to endpoint distribution, Grossman et al.  [ 14 , 15 ] have considered the factor of target shape and proposed a probabilistic model to predict MT for targets with arbitrary shapes by mapping endpoint distribution to the index of difficulty (ID) in Fitts’ law. Although these studies have associated target shape and endpoint distribution with MT, they did not directly predict the endpoint distribution of target selection. Some other studies assumed the endpoints as Gaussian random variables and predicted the distribution by estimating the parameters ( μ and σ ) of the Gaussian distribution. For example, Bi et al.  [ 4 , 5 ] proposed a Dual Gaussian model to quantify endpoint distributions for two-dimensional (2D) static targets on touchscreens. Huang et al.  [ 21 , 22 ] presented a Ternary-Gaussian model to predict endpoint distributions for moving targets in one-dimensional (1D) and 2D spaces. However, as the Ternary-Gaussian model was only verified with targets of regular shapes (circles or rectangles), it may lose its efficacy for targets of arbitrary shapes.

Recently, Zhang et al. [ 46 ] proposed a model that combines the Gaussian mixture model (GMM) with a Ternary-Gaussian model to predict the endpoint distribution of targets with arbitrary shapes. The model (we named it the Inscribed Circle model in this paper) achieved high predictive accuracy by using an inscribed circle algorithm to split targets into smaller circulars to obtain the sub-Gaussian distributions for the GMM. Nevertheless, there are at least three remaining problems in this preliminary work. First, the free parameters in this work are shape-specified, which means that the model needs to be trained specifically for different shapes. This motivates us to create a more robust model and conduct a thorough evaluation of the model. Second, the Inscribed Circle model splits the shape by iteratively finding the maximum inscribed circle in the rest parts of the shape. However, target shapes are usually composed of different parts with different semantic information, the maximum inscribed circle method breaks the connection between these different parts. Third, this work was limited to 3 target shapes and target moving speeds ranging from 96 to 384 px/sec, it did not investigate the potential effects of target orientations on endpoint distribution. It's still unclear whether the Inscribed Circle model still works well under more complicated conditions.

There are three major challenges to model pointing uncertainty for moving targets of arbitrary shapes. First, the proposed model should reflect the geometric features of targets to the fullest extent possible, as this would be of importance to model validity. A straightforward way to accomplish this need is to divide target shape into smaller parts. However, target shapes are usually composed of different parts with different semantic information, we need to preserve the connection between these different parts  [ 28 ]. Second, in addition to target shape, the model must concurrently consider other factors such as target speed and target size that may also significantly affect endpoint distribution. Our work is the first attempt to model pointing uncertainty for moving targets of arbitrary shapes by considering the relationship among these factors and their potential joint impacts on endpoint distribution. Third, the structure and coefficients of the proposed model should possess good interpretability to enable a straightforward interpretation of user behavior and also provide insights for interface design in the future.

To address the aforementioned challenges, we propose a model to predict pointing uncertainty for arbitrary shapes based on a Two-Component assumption that the pointing uncertainty for arbitrary shapes is a combination of pointing uncertainty associated with target shape and pointing uncertainty caused by target motion (Fig. 1 (a)). Specifically, for the shape component, we decompose an arbitrary shape using a Dual-Space Decomposition (DUDE) algorithm  [ 28 ] that is more aligned with human perception (Fig. 1 (b)). Then we determine a rectangular simplification for each decomposed part (Fig. 1 (c)), and create an endpoint distribution for the target shape based on the rectangular simplifications of all the decomposed parts (Fig. 1 (d)); for the motion component, we build a 2D Ternary-Gaussian model  [ 22 ] with the bounding box size of the shape that accounts for the pointing uncertainty of target motion (Fig. 1 (e-g)); finally, the two components are combined with a linear function related to target size and speed, so as to establish the final distribution (Fig. 1 (h)). Our results show that the proposed model outperforms the 2D Ternary-Gaussian model  [ 22 ] and the Inscribed Circle model  [ 46 ], as evidenced by smaller Hellinger Distance [ 18 , 34 ] between the predicted and the actual endpoint distributions (the proposed model = 0.2534, 2D Ternary-Gaussian model = 0.3040, Inscribed Circle model = 0.3135).

2 RELATED WORK

In this section, we review related work and summarize them into three categories: moving target selection, effects of target shape on pointing performance, and human perception on objects with arbitrary shapes.

2.1 Moving Target Selection

Fitts’ law  [ 13 , 29 ] is one of the most widely accepted models which can mathematically predict MT of pointing at a static target. Fitts’ law implies a speed-accuracy trade-off of such pointing tasks, that is, tasks are performed slowly with high accuracy or fast with high error rates. Jagacinski et al.  [ 24 ] extended the rule of speed-accuracy trade-off to moving target selection by adding a term of speed to Fitts’ law. Hoffmann  [ 19 ] presented a MT prediction model for moving target selection by introducing a type of steady-state position error. Although these models have provided insights into moving target selection, they focused on predicting MT, rather than the endpoint distribution.

One work that used endpoint distribution to predict user performance in moving target selection is known as Temporal Pointing  [ 26 ]. The study presented a model to predict error rates in temporal pointing tasks with a temporal distance from the beginning and a limited time window for selection. By formulating visual moving targets into the temporal pointing task, the model can predict the selection error rate of visual moving targets that require negligible movements of users when considering cues integration  [ 25 ] and input latency  [ 27 ]. The latest work on this research presented an intermittent click planning (ICP) model that describes the process of planning and executing the click actions, extending the error rate prediction to moving targets with large movements of users  [ 35 ]. Nonetheless, the ICP model needs to track the user trajectory until the last submovement to predict the pointing error rate, which could be difficult to apply in a situation where the trajectory of the user movement is unavailable.

Under the inspiration of the temporal pointing  [ 26 ] and a Dual-Gaussian hypothesis in FFitts law  [ 4 ], Huang et al.  [ 21 ] formulated a 1D Ternary-Gaussian model to quantify endpoint distribution in 1D unidirectional moving target selection. The model was proposed based on the assumption that endpoint distribution is a Gaussian distribution that consists of three independent Gaussian components corresponding to absolute precision, motion and precision tolerance of the target. The model showed high fits with empirical data on endpoint distribution and pointing error rate. Their follow-up study extended the 1D model to 2D moving target selection by introducing a velocity coordinate system  [ 22 ]. The proposed 2D Ternary-Gaussian model can precisely describe endpoint distributions for 2D moving target selection and predict pointing errors. Because the 2D Ternary-Gaussian model was only verified with moving targets of regular shapes (i.e., circles and rectangles), the model may lose its efficacy on targets of arbitrary shapes.

2.2 Effects of Target Shape on Pointing Performance

Target shape is an important determinant of pointing-based target selection tasks in HCI. For 1D target selection, the discussion of target shape mainly lies in the nominal width and effective width of a target  [ 40 , 43 ]. Unlike the nominal width, the effective width is derived from the distribution of endpoint coordinates, which can partially compensate for speed variance from MT prediction  [ 43 ].

In realistic interaction scenarios, targets to be selected usually have bounded sizes in two dimensions. Many studies, therefore, looked into the effect of the 2D target shape on pointing performance. Circular targets are usually adopted in pointing experiment design due to a single parameter of target size (i.e. circle's diameter) and negligible effects of approach angles of pointing on target size  [ 2 ]. Regarding rectangular targets (width W and height H), MacKenzie and Buxton  [ 30 ] extended Fitts’ law to 2D target acquisition tasks with five interpretations of target width concerning different approach angles of pointing to the target. The interpretation of ”smaller of W and H” resulted in higher fitness values than others and this model has been employed in many studies  [ 32 , 39 ] because of its operational simplicity and expansibility. Accot and Zhai  [ 1 ] found an unequal impact of amplitude constraint W and directional constraint H, with the former dominating the latter. Therefore, they proposed a Euclidean model with one free weight η . There are other studies investigating the relationship between W, H, and approach angles of pointing, and proposed new models to account for such a relationship  [ 3 , 42 , 45 ].

Note that the aforementioned studies generally focused on pointing tasks with rectangular targets. Pointing at targets of arbitrary shapes is increasingly common on user interfaces, such as games, video surveillance systems, interfaces with irregular “skins” [ 15 ], and arbitrarily shaped “touchable” objects within interfaces [ 36 ]. Sheikh and Hoffmann  [ 37 ] found a significant effect of target shape on MT in a Fitts’ law study. By using a bivariate-normal distribution along with a “cookie-cutter” method, the estimated target shape could improve the prediction accuracy of Fitts’ law for targets of different shapes. In another noteworthy study, Tovi Grossman et al.  [ 14 , 15 ] proposed a probabilistic Fitts’ law model, which can accurately predict MT across a range of non-rectangular shapes in static target selection. The central idea of the probabilistic Fitts’ law model is to map the probability of hitting a target with an open-loop movement to an index of difficulty value. Although these studies had associated static target shapes and endpoint distribution with MT, they did not predict the endpoint distribution of target shapes.

The Inscribed Circle model[ 46 ] used an inscribed circle algorithm to split a target into smaller circulars to obtain the sub-Gaussian distributions for the GMM. As the Inscribed Circle model, we also leveraged the GMM algorithm in combining sub-Gaussian distributions to get the overall distribution for shape components. However, target shapes are usually composed of different parts with different semantic information, the inscribed circle method breaks the connection between these different parts. In other words, this method may inappropriately divide a shape that has obvious manning to human into scattered parts, for example, the wings of a pigeon in Fig. 1 may be divided into 3-4 small circles. As pointing is a visually-guided target-directed action, visually meaningful parts of targets would affect endpoint distribution, which inspires us to adopt an existing method [ 28 ] to decompose target shape into visually meaningful parts for modeling.

2.3 Human Perception on Objects with Arbitrary Shapes

In the fields of psychology and computer vision, there are many studies on human perception of object shape. We review the studies related to arbitrary shapes to get enlightenment for building our model.

Previous studies on human's ability of object recognition showed that we perceived the shape of an object by its parts and their categorical spatial relations  [ 7 , 23 ]. The results imply that we can decompose object shapes into multiple parts and use these parts and their spatial relations to represent object shapes [ 11 ].

Zhang et al.  [ 44 ] found that the human internal probability density functions, which could account for the distribution of possible error with values associated with different movement outcomes, were typically multi-modal and could be better described as mixtures of multi-distribution in a speeded reaching movement. This indicates that the endpoint distribution of selecting moving targets with arbitrary shapes could be represented by a mixed distribution model.

Trommershauser et al.  [ 31 ] presented a model to predict participants’ aiming positions in the task of hitting a target with the presence of a penalty region around the target. The results showed that participants shifted their aiming positions away from the middle of the penalty region, with larger shifts for closer penalty circles and higher magnitudes of penalty. When the area outside the target is regarded as the penalty area, the humans’ aiming positions will be far away from the edge of the target and concentrated on the polygonal centroid of the target. This result is consistent with the finding of Grossman et al.  [ 15 ], that is, when the participants point to a target with an arbitrary shape, the polygon centroid can be regarded as the center of the endpoints. These works suggest that when users click on a target, the number of endpoints in each area of the target may be related to the distance from the area to the target centroid.

In the field of computer vision, representing shapes with meaningful parts is an important topic in shape analysis and part-based object representation [ 7 , 12 ]. Researchers in this field also attach importance to human perception of shape to mimic human decomposition behavior  [ 9 , 38 ]. An algorithm called Dual-Space Decomposition (DUDE)  [ 28 ] that utilizes a dual-space method to decompose a non-convex polygon into several approximate convex parts has attracted our attention. It is considered that the decomposition created by the DUDE algorithm is very similar to man-made decomposition. We consider that this decomposition, which is close to human perception of shape, could be a better reference for achieving the primitive geometries of shapes.

3 THE TWO-COMPONENT ASSUMPTION

As mentioned earlier, we assumed that the pointing uncertainty for arbitrary shapes is a combination of pointing uncertainty associated with target shape and pointing uncertainty caused by target motion. This assumption was proposed in response to two significant observations in our data. To begin, we discovered that selection endpoints of large or slow-moving targets were more influenced by target shape; the endpoints were scattered in each part of the shape with specific semantics (as shown in the top-left of Fig. 2 ), which usually results in a multi-modal distribution. Second, selection endpoints of small or fast-moving targets were less influenced by target shape but more influenced by target motion; the endpoints were scattered as a uni-modal distribution affected by the overall size and motion of the target (as shown in the bottom-right of Fig. 2 ).

We attribute this phenomenon as follows: on one hand, as the human internal probability density functions estimate movement outcomes according to target shape and try to maximum reward possible (hitting the target) [ 44 ], when the target is static or moving at relatively lower speeds, the user tends to fully utilize each part of the target shape to maximize the chances of hitting the target, resulting in endpoints that vary with the target shape. On the other hand, when the target is moving at a higher speed, to make their vision and movement catch up with the moving target, the user needs to reduce the focus on the target features that are not relevant to the target motion [ 8 , 10 ], so endpoints are less affected by the target shape but more affected by the target motion. In addition, target shape and motion are complementary visual features [ 33 ], users usually rely on target pointing tolerance (i.e., target size) to compensate the effect of target motion, so the effect of target motion on endpoints could be weakened for larger target [ 21 , 22 ].

Based on the above assumption, we proposed the Shape-Adaptive Ternary-Gaussian model to predict endpoint distribution for moving targets of arbitrary shapes.

4 THE SHAPE-ADAPTIVE TERNARY-GAUSSIAN MODEL

According to the Two-Component Assumption, one intuitive way to build the model is to linearly combine two pointing uncertainties (i.e., endpoint distribution) related to target motion g ( x , y ) and target shape f ( x , y ) as follow:

\begin{equation} T(x,y) = \lambda g(x,y) + (1 - \lambda)f(x,y), 0 \leqslant \lambda \leqslant 1 \end{equation}
(1)

where g ( x , y ) is the endpoint distribution model for target motion and f ( x , y ) is the distribution model for target shape. We use the 2D Ternary-Gaussian model to represent g ( x , y ), and build a new model called DUDE-based model for f ( x , y ), both of which will be introduced in the following sections. λ represents the adjustment of the weights that the user considers the shape or the motion of the target. According to the Two-Component Assumption, we denote the adjustment λ by the following formula:

\begin{equation} \lambda = a + b\frac{V}{W_{t}}, \end{equation}
(2)

where V is the speed of the target, W t is the length that tangential to the target's moving direction (as shown in Fig. 3 (a)), a and b are two constants.

4.1 2D Ternary-Gaussian Model

The 2D Ternary-Gaussian model is a model to quantify endpoint distributions for moving targets with regular shapes (such as circles or rectangles)  [ 22 ]. The 2D Ternary-Gaussian model regards the endpoint distribution X as a 2D Gaussian distribution of a 2D random variable:

\begin{equation} X\sim N(\mu,\Sigma) \end{equation}
(3)

The distribution can be determined by the mean μ and covariance Σ associated with the size (diameter for a circle) and speed of the target:

\begin{equation} \mu =\bigl ({\begin{matrix} \mu _{t}\\ \mu _{n} \end{matrix}}\bigr) = \bigl ({\begin{matrix} a_{t}+b_{t}V+c_{t}W_{t}\\ 0 \end{matrix}}\bigr) \end{equation}
(4)
\begin{equation} \Sigma =\bigl ({\begin{matrix} \sigma _{t}^{2}& 0\\ 0 & \sigma _{n}^{2} \end{matrix}}\bigr)=\bigl ({\begin{matrix} d_{t}+e_{t}V^2+f_{t}W_{t}^{2}+g_{t}\frac{V}{W_{t}} & 0\\ 0 & d_{n}+e_{n}V^2+f_{n}W_{n}^{2} \end{matrix}}\bigr) \end{equation}
(5)

Where W and V represent target size and speed respectively, and a t , b t , c t , d t , e t , f t , g t , d n , e n , and f n are free parameters. The size of the target is defined as the distance between the two furthest points inside the target. The subscripts t and n represent the two axes of endpoint coordinates, i.e., t is the axis tangential to target speed and n is the axis normal to it. W t and W n represent sides that tangential and normal to target speed respectively (as shown in Fig. 3 (a)). The probability density function (pdf) of the above 2D Gaussian model for moving target is given as follows:

\begin{equation} g(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi \sigma _{t}\sigma _{n}}exp(-\frac{1}{2}[\frac{(x-\mu _{t})^{2}}{\sigma _{t}^{2}}+\frac{(y-\mu _{n})^{2}}{\sigma _{n}^{2}}]) \end{equation}
(6)

Where x and y represent the position in the speed coordinate system  [ 22 ]. The speed coordinate system is a target's centered coordinate with x-axis tangent to the target's moving direction and y-axis normal to the target's moving direction. As previous work found that using polygon centroid to define the center of arbitrarily shaped targets for the purposes of modeling is more suitable  [ 15 ], we treat the polygon centroid as the target center for a shape.

The 2D Ternary-Gaussian model is consistent with our analogy to the pointing uncertainty caused by target motion in the Two-Component Assumption. Thus overall properties of the target (i.e., bounding box size and speed) are filled in the model to obtain endpoint uncertainty caused by target motion (i.e., g ( x , y )).

4.2 DUDE-Based Model

We consider interpreting the endpoint distribution of pointing at arbitrary target shapes by a mixture distribution, which can be viewed as a combination of endpoint distributions for separated parts. To do this, we 1) decompose an arbitrary shape into multiple parts using DUDE algorithm [ 28 ]; 2) determine a rectangular simplification for each part; 3) create distributions for each part based on the simplified rectangular; 4) combine the distribution of each part into a total endpoint distribution using GMM. We introduce the above 4 steps in the following sections.

4.2.1 Dual-Space Decomposition. Dual-Space Decomposition (DUDE) [ 28 ] is an efficient dual-space method to decompose a non-convex polygon into several approximate convex parts. In [ 28 ], users were asked to draw lines to segment the displayed polygon into meaningful parts. The decomposition created by DUDE is statistically similar to the man-made decomposition results. In this way, we can treat these parts as the minimum semantic units of the shape perceived by the users, implying that these parts should not be subdivided and they are very likely to contain only one distribution centroid. A brief description of DUDE is described as follows.

Given a polygon P with arbitrary shape, the DUDE first computes the convex hull CH ( P ) of P . Then it determines bridges β and pockets ρ from the convex hull CH ( P ) of P . A bridge connects two points from the space exterior, and it can not intersect the boundary of P except at its endpoints. A pocket is completely in $\overrightarrow{P}$ ; it is a subset enclosed by bridge and the boundary of $\overrightarrow{P}$ . Based on the determined bridges and pockets, DUDE calculates the concave features (concavity). DUDE then searches the intolerable concave features within CH ( P ), and finds a valid diagonal that can resolve the curvature feature here less than τ . Finally, by solving linear programming problems, DUDE obtains the final cuts. All the parameters of the DUDE were set the same as in its original work [ 28 ].

4.2.2 Simplify Each Decomposed Part to Rectangle. In the previous step, we decomposed the overall target shape into several smaller parts to facilitate the modeling. However, those smaller parts were still irregular shapes that couldn't be directly estimated by any existing model. We, therefore, need to find reasonable simplification for each decomposed part, which allows us to determine a parametric distribution (2D Gaussian distribution in our case) based on the simplified part. The work in [ 4 , 5 ] presented a model that can accurately predict the 2D Gaussian distribution for rectangular or elliptic objects. Without losing generality, we chose to simplify the decomposition part to a rectangle, as a rectangle and an ellipse are equivalent in the model (an ellipse or a rectangle has the same distribution if the major axis and minor axis of the ellipse are equal to the length and width of the rectangle).

The overall process of simplifying each decomposed part to rectangle is shown in Fig. 3 (b): i. - iv. To make the distribution of the simplified rectangle as close as possible to the distribution of the decomposed part, we use a minimum bounding box algorithm to obtain the bounding box fitted to the decomposed part (Fig. 3 (b): ii.). We further shrink the minimum bounding box by moving each edge of the box toward the center until the length of the edge inside the decomposed part is more than 1/3 of the original length of the edge (Fig. 3 (b): iii.). By eliminating unnecessary details that hardly affect user pointing, we are able to create a better approximation of the decomposed part (Fig. 3 (b): iV.). Further details (including pseudo-code and empirical parameter determination) can be found in Appendix A.1.

4.2.3 Distribution for the Decomposed Part. Previous works [ 4 , 5 ] showed that finger touch points in regular shape follow a 2D Gaussian distribution, and presented a Dual-Gaussian model to predict the endpoint distribution. This model should meet the conditions for modeling the endpoints for the decomposed part after simplification. To apply the Dual-Gaussian model to the decomposed part, we just need to separately fit the parameters of the model using the height and width of the simplified rectangle. As a result, the endpoint distribution for each decomposed part is approximated as a 2D Gaussian distribution X :

\begin{equation} X = X_r\sim N(\mu,\Sigma), \end{equation}
(7)

where the X r is the relative component in the Dual-Gaussian model's original form. By applying the Dual-Gaussian model, we can get the following formulation for μ i and covariance Σ i of the endpoint distribution of the i-th decomposed part:

\begin{equation} \mu _i = \binom{\mu _{i,x}}{\mu _{i,y}} = \binom{c_{i,x}}{c_{i,y}}, \end{equation}
(8)
\begin{equation} \Sigma _i = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma _{i,x}^2 & 0 0 & \sigma _{i,y}^2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha _x + \beta _x w_{i,x}^2& 0 0 & \alpha _y + \beta _y w_{i,y}^2 \end{pmatrix} \end{equation}
(9)

As shown in Fig. 3 (c), ( c i , x , c i , y ) is the centroid of the simplified rectangle of the i-th decomposed part, w i , x and w i , y are the width and height of the simplified rectangle of the i-th decomposed part, respectively. α x , β x , α y and β y are constants and can be measured via a separate experiment. Given the mean μ i and covariance Σ i , the 2D Gaussian probability density function (pdf) for each decomposed part can be determined as:

\begin{equation} P_{i}(x^{\prime },y^{\prime })=\frac{1}{2\pi \sigma _{i,x^{\prime }}\sigma _{i,y}}exp(-\frac{1}{2}[\frac{(x^{\prime }-\mu _{i,x^{\prime }})^{2}}{\sigma _{i,x^{\prime }}^{2}}+\frac{(y^{\prime }-\mu _{i,y^{\prime }})^{2}}{\sigma _{i,y^{\prime }}^{2}}]) \end{equation}
(10)

We use x ′ and y ′ here to indicate that the Equation 10 uses local coordinate of the simplified rectangle with an origin at the center, x-axis parallel to the rectangle's long edge, and y-axis parallel to the rectangle's short edge. Note that, to make the probability density of position ( x ′, y ′) consistent with the global model, we need to convert the position into the global coordinate system: P i ( x , y ) = FromLocalToGlobal ( P i ( x ′, y ′)).

4.2.4 GMM-based Distribution Combination. The last step is to combine the distribution of each decomposed part into a total endpoint distribution of the shape. We use the GMM to generate a combined distribution composed of the speculative (Gaussian) distributions of all the parts with weight w . Thus, we have the formulation of the DUDE-based model as follow:

\begin{equation} f(x,y)=\sum _{i=1}^{K}w_{i}P_{i}(x,y)\; \; \; \begin{pmatrix} \sum _{i=1}^{K}w_{i}=1 \end{pmatrix}, \end{equation}
(11)
where K represents the number of parts.

In Equation 11 , we must determine the weights ( w i ) of the 2D Gaussian distribution for each part. We design a weighting function with two shape factors. One is the area of each part. Larger areas are more likely to have more endpoints, so their weight should be greater. The other factor is the distance between the center of each part’ center and the target centroid. We treat the polygon centroid as the target center for a shape according to  [ 15 ]. Previous work suggested that participants are more likely to point at the centroid of the target than on the edge  [ 31 ].

The weighting function used to determine the w i is shown in Equation 12 . The numerator in Equation 12 consists of two parts: the area of the rectangle, and the distance between the center of the rectangle and the polygon centroid of the target.

\begin{equation} w_i = \frac{c l_i s_i\ + \ d d_i}{C}, \end{equation}
(12)

where l i and s i represent the long and short side of the rectangle respectively, c and d are constants, d i represents the Euclidean distance between the polygon centroid of the target and the center of the rectangle, C is the normalization constant, which can be expressed as $\sum _{k = 1}^K(c l_k s_k\ + \ d d_k)$ .

After obtaining f ( x , y ), we must consider a problem before incorporating it into Equation 1 , which is that the endpoint distribution centers of the two models g ( x , y ) and f ( x , y ) could be inconsistent. Previous research has demonstrated that the endpoints for moving target selection tasks would be “shifted” behind the targets [ 21 , 22 ]. This phenomenon is well modeled in the 2D Ternary-Gaussian model where the distribution center is shifted from the target center to ( μ t , μ n ), whereas the DUDE-based model does not account for such an effect. This leads to inconsistencies in the centres of the two models when combining. We therefore need to move the centre of the DUDE-based model to the centre of the 2D Ternary-Gaussian model. To do so, we simply translate the coordinate system of Equation 11 to the centre of the 2D Ternary-Gaussian model as follows:

\begin{equation} f(x,y)=\sum _{i=1}^{K}w_{i}P_{i}(x+\mu _{t},y+\mu _{n})\; \; \; \begin{pmatrix} \sum _{i=1}^{K}w_{i}=1 \end{pmatrix}, \end{equation}
(13)

Note that for static targets without the motion component, we can use Equation 11 directly. Only when we need to combine the shape component with the motion component, we should use Equation 13 .

5 FITTING PARAMETERS OF THE DUAL-GAUSSIAN MODEL AND THE TERNARY-GAUSSIAN MODEL

As mentioned earlier, the DUDE-based model used the Dual-Gaussian model as its sub-model, while both the Inscribed Circle model [ 46 ] and the Shape-Adaptive Ternary-Gaussian model used the 2D Ternary-Gaussian model as their sub-model. 6 MODEL EVALUATION

We evaluated our model with three studies. The first study evaluated the DUDE-based model on static targets to validate its performance in describing shape, the second study tested the overall Shape-Adaptive Ternary-Gaussian model for moving target shapes, while the third study evaluated our model's performance at extremely high speeds and different orientations. As a comparison, the Dual-Gaussian model and the Inscribed Circle model were included in the first study, while the Ternary-Gaussian model and the Inscribed Circle model were employed in the last two studies.

Ten target shapes were used in the experiments. Eight of them (Shapes 1-8 in Fig. 5 ) were selected from previous works on modeling pointing at targets of arbitrary shapes  [ 6 , 15 , 17 ]; they were either left-right symmetrical or up-down symmetrical. The rest two shapes were newly introduced to include more complex geometric features in the paper; Shape 9 in Fig. 5 is centrally asymmetrical and Shape 10 in Fig. 5 is centrally symmetrical.

The Hellinger Distance [ 18 , 34 ] was used to evaluate the performance of different models in all experiments. The Hellinger Distance is a measure to quantify the similarity between two probability distributions with the range from 0 to 1. The value 0 means the two distributions are entirely consistent with each other, and 1 means that they are completely inconsistent.

6.1 Study 1: Performance of the DUDE-Based Model

Using the estimated parameters of Dual-Gaussian model, we compared the performance of our proposed model with the Dual-Gaussian model and the Inscribed Circle model. For the DUDE-based model, the DUDE algorithm decomposed the ten shapes with default settings in  [ 28 ], and the decomposition results for the ten shapes are shown in Fig. 6 (a). For the Dual-Gaussian model, the width and height of the shape's bounding box were used as target width and height to fill in the model. It was suggested that the polygon centroid, rather than the center of the bounding box, could be a better option for representing the target center for arbitrary shapes [ 15 ]. We tested them both and finally chose the polygon centroid because the model performed better in this case. As the Inscribed Circle model was originally designed for moving targets, we replaced its inside sub-model (Ternary-Gaussian model) with the Dual-Gaussian model to create a distribution for each circle, making it adapt to static targets. Model-2 in its original study [ 46 ] was used. The decomposed parts for the ten shapes are shown in Fig. 6 (b). The patternsearch function provided in MATLAB was used to estimate the values β with a loss function minimizing the sum of Hellinger Distance between predicted distribution and the actual ones in all conditions. The estimated β is 0.4233.

6.1.1 Participants, Design and Procedure. We recruited 15 right-handed participants (7 females and 8 males, with an average age of 24.87) in this experiment. All of them were familiar with computers and styli.

This experiment included 3 target sizes (96, 144, 192 px), each of which was repeated 16 times. We defined the size of the target as the distance between the two furthest points inside the target. The 16 repeats of each size were evenly placed on the boundary of a big circle with the center located at the screen center and 396 px in radius. A start button was placed at the center of the big circle (Fig. 4 (b)). When the participants pressed the start button, one target appeared and the participants were required to point to the target as fast and accurately as possible. This design was to balance the potential impact of the user movement direction on the endpoint distribution. We finally recorded a total of 15 participants × 10 shapes × 3 sizes × 16 repeats = 7200 endpoints. The order of all trials was randomized. An outlier is a point that is three standard deviations away from the mean of all points. 71 outliers were removed in this experiment.

6.2.2 Verifying the Two-Component Assumption.

In this section, we use the collected data in Study 1 and 2 to verify the Two-Component Assumption. We focus on Shapes 3 and 10 as their data are representative to justify the assumption. Fig. 10 presents the endpoint distributions of Shape 3 and Shape 10 in two sizes (Size = 96 px vs. Size = 192 px) and 3 different speeds (Speed = 0 px/sec vs. Speed = 192 px/sec vs. Speed = 768 px/sec). It can be learned from the figure that endpoint distributions in the Speed = 0 px/sec condition (static) are more affected by target shape as the distributions of these targets are typically multi-modal (along the x-axis for Shape 3 , along the y-axis for Shape 10 ). Conversely, endpoint distributions in the Speed = 768 px/sec condition are more affected by target motion, resulting in the distributions closer to uni-modal. These results suggest that the endpoint distribution of pointing moving targets with arbitrary shapes is determined by two major components: target shape and motion. And the weights for these two components vary depending on target speed.

Besides, the endpoint distributions in the Size = 192 px condition are more likely to scatter depending on target shape, indicating higher influences from target shape. The endpoint distributions in the Size = 96 px condition tend to flock together, indicating higher impacts from target motion. These results suggest that increasing target size can mitigate the effect of the motion component on endpoint distributions.

We used Equation 2 to model the adjustment of the weights of the two components. The fmincon function provided in MATLAB was used to estimate the values a and b with a loss function minimizing Hellinger Distance , yielding a = 0.5011 and b = 0.0624. The best-fitted positive parameter a and b confirms our assumption that the weight of motion component increases with speed and decreases with size, and the reverse is true for the weight of shape component.

6.2.4 Cross-Validation. To investigate the robustness of our proposed model across different shapes and participants, as well as to verify whether overfitting occurred, we performed cross-validation tests.

We first performed a training-test split across different shapes. To guarantee that the model can gain valid knowledge from the data, we need to ensure that there are enough samples in the training set, so we adopted the Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation. Specifically, we randomly used nine shapes as the training set, and validated the performance of our model on the dataset of the remaining one shape. The cross-validation was repeated 10 times. The mean values of the parameters in the adjustment of the weights of the two components ( a and b in Equation 2 ) were 0.4803 (SD: 0.0242) and 0.0569 (SD: 0.0028) during the cross-validation process, indicating relatively stable values of the two parameters across target shapes. Our proposed model obtained the lowest average Hellinger Distance of 0.2555 (SD: 0.0472), followed by 0.3040 (SD: 0.0798) for the Ternary-Gaussian model, and 0.3173 (SD: 0.0484) for Inscribed Circle model. Besides, the average Hellinger Distance of our proposed model (0.2555) was very close to the result obtained from the whole data set (0.2534). Therefore, the predicting power of our proposed model is robust across different target shapes.

Then we performed 100 times random-sampling 3-fold cross-validation across different participants. To ensure that each experimental condition had sufficient data to construct the corresponding endpoint distribution, we performed a 3-fold cross-validation. Specifically, we randomly used the data of 10 participants as the training set and the data of the other 5 participants as the test set. The mean values of the parameters a and b were 0.4885 (SD: 0.0113) and 0.0554 (SD: 0.0012) during the cross-validation process. The average Hellinger Distance increased from our proposed model (0.2858, SD: 0.0186), the 2D Ternary-Gaussian model (0.3309, SD: 0.0183) to the Inscribed Circle model (0.3437, SD: 0.0192). Although the performance of all models deteriorated, our proposed model still has the best performance among the three models. Therefore, the predicting power of our proposed model is robust across different participants.

7 DISCUSSION

7.1 Comparison with the Previous Work

Our work is a followed-up to the previous work [ 46 ]. Compared to the previous study, our work makes the following significant improvements.

First, the key difference between our proposed model and the previous work is the different basis assumptions. The previous work proposes a two-part model in the form of Global and Local uncertainty parts. The effects of shape and motion were confounded in the previous work (both are reflected in Global and Local uncertainty), which results in inferior performance (especially for slower targets) compared to our new approach. The model in our work was established according to the Two-Component assumption, a new modeling idea that first described the effects of shape and motion independently and then combined by a weighted linear function.

Second, the free parameters in the previous model are shape-specified, which means that the model needs to be trained specifically for different shapes. We solved this problem by introducing two functions. The first function is used to weigh the two components in basis assumption (Equation 2 ). This function introduces two shape-related factors: the speed of the target and the length that is tangential to the target's moving direction. These factors come from the Two-Component assumption, which was verified by observations on our data. The other function is the weighting function that combines the distribution of each decomposed part (Equation 12 ). This function introduces two shape-related factors: the distances from the target centroid to each decomposed part and the area of each decomposed part. As shown in Section 6.1.2 , these two factors are significantly correlated to the number of endpoints in each decomposed part. These two introduced functions and the corresponding shape-related factors improve the robustness of our model so that it can be applied to different shapes. They also increase the interpretability of our model.

Third, the previous work uses an inscribed circle algorithm to split a target into smaller circulars to obtain the sub-Gaussian distributions for the GMM. However, the inscribed circle algorithm breaks the connection between the different components of the shape and destroys its semantic information. We decompose arbitrary shapes using DUDE algorithm that is more aligned with human perception. We conducted an experiment with static arbitrary shapes (Study 1) and the results show that DUDE-based model performs better than the inscribed circle algorithm in describing shapes.

Finally, our work considers a wider range of shapes, speeds, and sizes compared with the previous work, and discusses user performance and model interpretation based on the new results. The results demonstrate the robustness of our model under the discussed conditions.

7.2 General Model Evaluation

This work presents a model for quantifying the pointing uncertainty of moving targets with arbitrary shapes based on the assumption that the endpoint distribution of pointing is combined by two components: a shape component and a motion component; they correspond to the influences of target shape and target motion on endpoint distribution respectively. As a result, we first proposed the DUDE-based model to describe the shape component for our final model. Results show that the DUDE-based model outperforms both the Dual-Gaussian model  [ 4 , 5 ] and the Inscribed Circle model  [ 46 ], as evidenced by smaller Hellinger Distance [ 18 , 34 ] between the predicted and the actual endpoint distributions (proposed model = 0.2775, Dual-Gaussian model = 0.3157, Inscribed Circle model = 0.3009). By linear combining the DUDE-based model (shape component) and the 2D Ternary-Gaussian model (motion component), we established the final Shape-Adaptive Ternary-Gaussian model for moving targets of arbitrary shapes. Results show that the proposed model outperforms the 2D Ternary-Gaussian model  [ 22 ] and the Inscribed Circle model  [ 46 ], as evidenced by smaller Hellinger Distance [ 18 , 34 ] between the predicted and the actual endpoint distributions (proposed model = 0.2534, 2D Ternary-Gaussian model = 0.3040, Inscribed Circle model = 0.3135). We also conducted two additional experiments (Study 3) to assess our model's performance at extremely high speeds and different orientations. The two additional experiments yielded similar results with Study 2, demonstrating the robustness of our model at different speeds and orientations.

7.3 Insights for Interface Design

According to the superior performance achieved by the proposed model, our work also provides the following insights for interface design involving pointing at moving targets of arbitrary shapes:

1) Our results showed that endpoint distributions in slower and larger targets tend to be more affected by target shape, resulting in multi-modal distributions. Conversely, endpoint distributions in faster and smaller targets tend to be influenced more by target motion, leading to distributions closer to uni-modal. These results suggest that users tend to fully utilize each part of the target shape to maximize the chance of hitting the target for slower and larger targets, while for faster and smaller targets, users could barely consider the target shape but pay more attention to catching up with the moving target. Therefore, for slower or larger targets, designers should pay attention to the influence of target shape on user performance, while for faster and smaller targets, it is optional for designers to ignore target shape and focus more on optimizing other design factors such as target size and speed.

2) Our data showed that the DUDE-based model achieved higher average performance in describing endpoint distribution on static target shapes. Although it could be not true for some shapes (e.g., shapes 1), the endpoint distributions of the majority of shapes are strongly related to human perception of target shape. For static or slower targets, the endpoint distributions are composed of multiple distributions that correspond to multiple meaningful geometric parts of target shape, resulting in an overall multi-modal distribution, as shown on shapes 3, 4, 10, etc.. To describe the endpoint distribution of an arbitrary shape, it is quite feasible to create separate distributions for decomposed parts of the shape and merge them into the final distribution. However, there may be a decomposition method than DUDE that is more consistent with human perception when pointing to the target. We suggest that future research should pay more attention to human semantic understanding of target shapes.

3) When evaluating the weighting function of the GMM in section 5.4.1, we found that the number of endpoints falling in a decomposed part is positively correlated to the area of the part and negatively correlated to the distance from the part to the centroid of the target. This indicates that the larger a part or the closer a part is to the centroid of the target, the more endpoints will fall in the part. To make a target easier to select, designers could consider increasing the area of parts that is close to the target's centroid.

8 LIMITATIONS

There are two major limitations in this study. First, it is reported that the 2D Ternary-Gaussian model is limited in some aspects such as inaccurate estimation for non-normal distribution (as revealed in this study), lower explanatory power in high-speed targets and the large number of free parameters  [ 35 ]. As the proposed model is an extension of the 2D Ternary-Gaussian model, it could be affected by the above constraints as well. More comprehensive and in-depth studies are needed to evaluate the potential effects of the limitations of the 2D Ternary-Gaussian model on the interpretability of the proposed model.

Second, to integrate the 2D Ternary-Gaussian model with the GMM, the proposed model first splits the shaped target into primitive geometries, and then estimates the distributions of these primitive geometries independently. Due to the limitations of the decomposition method, we do not have the means to achieve a polygon decomposition that is fully consistent with people's perception. When the user's perception is consistent with the decomposition method, our proposed model is a good representation of the distribution of the user's endpoints (e.g. Shape 10 ). Otherwise, our proposed model may cause the final distribution synthesized by the GMM to a distribution with multi-modal (e.g. Shape 1 ), which is quite different from the actual distribution. This can be solved by using a decomposition method that is more in line with the user's perception. Besides, it is worth exploring an optimal way to filter the distributions of primitive geometries to make the peeks formed by them smoother.

9 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we propose a model that can precisely describe endpoint distribution for pointing at moving targets with arbitrary shapes. Three studies were carried out to validate the model. We first evaluated the performance of the DUDE-based model for static targets of arbitrary shape, and then we evaluated the performance of the Shape-Adaptive Ternary-Gaussian model for moving targets of arbitrary shapes, finally we further evaluated our model's performance at extremely high speeds and different orientations. Results showed that this model fitted the empirical data well and showed the robustness of the model across experimental conditions. The form and coefficients of the model reveal the adjustment between the user considering the shape of the target or the motion of the target. In the future, we are interested in extending our research into 3D space such as AR and VR, where target shape and size may dynamically vary as the target moves and the user's viewing angle changes. Systematically understanding how users perceive and optimize their actions on dynamical shape-changing targets may be a direction to address such a problem.

A.1 Details Process of Simplify Each Decomposed Part to Rectangle

A.1.1 Algorithm Process.

A more specific algorithmic process is given in Algorithm 1 . For each decomposed parts ( P i ), we have its counterclockwise arranged vertices set V , V = { v 1 , v 2 ... v n }. We first calculate the minimal bounding box 2 for each P i , and then we can obtain the four sides ( s i ,i = 1,2,3,4) of the minimal bounding box. For each side ( s i ), we calculate the intersection set ( T ) of the line segment 3 joined by two adjacent points in the set V with that side. And then, find the longest line segment ( l max ) joined by two points in T , if l max is larger than or equal to η | s i |, we regard this line segment as one side of p i ’s rectangle. While η is a constant parameter, and 0 ≤ η ≤ 1. If there is no satisfied l max , we move the side ( s i ) in the direction of its normal vector to form one new line segment ( s i ′). Repeat the above steps, until new four sides of the rectangle are found. Finally, we obtain the decomposed part which can reflect the main region for each decomposed part.

It should be noted that we only use the algorithm for the target shape's decomposed parts. Because the majority of these parts are small enough, using a decomposed part as an approximation is acceptable. Furthermore, because the decomposed parts generates by the DUDE algorithm are approximate convex hulls, the preceding method of moving the edges to the center of the graph can always find the position that cuts the geometry in the appropriate proportion.

A.1.2 Determination of empirical parameters η . Parameter η in Algorithm 1 determines the size of the decomposed part, it also influences the performance of the distribution model: when η takes a small value, the area of the found decomposed part is large, and it cannot sufficiently omit trivial portion that does not affect user pointing; Conversely, if η takes a large value, the area of the found decomposed part is small, and the main region that affects user pointing may be dropped. In this section, we evaluated the efficiency of Algorithm 1 and determined the final value of η .

Using the model without applying Algorithm 1 as a baseline, we set η to 0.2, 1/4, 0.3, 1/3, 0.4, and 0.5, and we calculated the average Hellinger Distance values obtained by our proposed model with different η . Results show that the average Hellinger Distance is 0.2893, 0.2807, 0.2775, 0.2747, 0.2780, and 0.2907 for η taking 0.2, 1/4, 0.3, 1/3, 0.4, and 0.5 respectively. The average Hellinger Distance for the model without applying Algorithm 1 is 0.2977. The models with Algorithm 1 applied performed better than the model without it. And the best model performance is achieved when η is taken as 1/3.
