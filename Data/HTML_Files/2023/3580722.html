CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

There is an increasing interest in the HCI community in designing for bodily practices. We report on a soma design process for martial arts and the resulting artifact – an interactive wooden dummy. Through a detailed account of the design process, we show how it enriched and revamped the bodily practice, but also how it changed the martial arts expert in the design team. Based on a phenomenological account of his experiences, we argue that the estrangement methods in soma design may allow practitioners engaging as soma designers, to cultivate and create new artistic habits fused with thought and feeling, changing themselves and their practice in directions they seek.

1 INTRODUCTION

Designing for bodily activities is becoming an increasingly popular topic in the field of interaction design. There are interaction design projects for running [ 46 ], dancing [ 2 , 16 , 35 ], and extreme sports [ 56 , 57 ]. In these works, it is clear how technology adds novel experiences [ 57 ], changes existing [ 7 ] or even creates for entirely novel bodily practices [ 43 ]. As with any new technology entering into a pre-existing practice, changes will occur to: the practice itself; those engaged in the practice; the organisations surrounding it; the rules, the objectives and participants  [ 34 ]. For example, the martial arts vest designed by Chi and colleagues  [ 7 , 8 ] for Taekwondo competitions required competitors, judges and spectators to adjust their expectations and strategies. Turmo Vidal describes how yoga experts changed their practice to accommodate new technology she had developed [ 72 ], adapting their teaching methodology, and occasionally the exercises themselves, in order to accommodate the technology and its role in the practice. What we ask here goes to the deeper aspects of the bodily practices – its norms and underlying aims and how those may become discernible, articulated, challenged and even creatively changed in the design process. As argued by Shusterman  [ 63 ], there is a difference between“routine, unintelligent habit” and“intelligent or artistic habit” fused with thought and feeling. What we will argue here, is that soma design methods  [ 26 , 29 ] may help practitioners uncover their own deeply ingrained, tacit, normative habits making them attainable for change. Through a detailed phenomenological account, we will show how the first author, an expert practitioner acting as soma designer, changed through the design process, enabling him to cultivate and create new artistic habits..

The soma design process led to a tool aimed to be used in training sessions – named Koinryō 1 . Martial arts in general and Bujinkan Budo Taijutsu , practiced by the first author, provides an engaging domain for design due to its strong normative stance – prescribing certain movements, principles, strategies, pedagogy, and values. While we might at first assume that this would mean that practitioners would resist change, that the norms are so strong that nothing can be changed, we note instead how creative change and development is encouraged in Bujinkan Budo Taijutsu. It is seen as a path to improving the practice. This said, any changes need to relate to the norms of the practice, harmonising with its overarching philosophy.

In the soma design process of Koinryō, the tension between normative expectations and movements enabled by different versions of the prototype, helped the expert to uncover and articulate some of the tacit norms of the martial art practice itself. As we will show, the design process unearthed norms, re-enforcing some while challenging others. In the end, the soma design process led him to extend his repertoire of solo training exercises not only with the artefact, but also without it, for example, as we will show in detail below, practicing elbow locks with unbendable objects.

Our main contribution lies in the detailed, phenomenological account of a bodily practice expert in a soma design process. Even though bodily practice experts are often engaged with design work as users, facilitators and designers, their engagement is often positioned as contributing to designed artifact(s) to be used by others. Moreover, while expert-led or autobiographical design processes reported in the literature have led to changes in participant behaviour or understandings (such as in [ 2 , 38 , 57 , 65 , 70 , 72 ]), they have, with few notable exceptions [ 35 , 39 , 51 ]) not engaged with changes of movement-based habits or increasing aesthetic appreciation of somatic experience. We add a meticulous, detailed, description of how a soma design process changed the somatic practitioner himself. We argue that such a reflective and creative design process could be employed by bodily practice experts as a path to not only enriching an established bodily practice, challenging and developing its norms and practices, but also changing the experts themselves – no matter whether the design outcomes becomes a standard part of the practice or not. The estrangement methods and (self-) reflection offered by the soma design process is thereby of relevance to those who (1) want to become better experts in their own bodily practice, or (2) want to improve and change their practice.

2 BACKGROUND

2.1 Soma Design

We base our design explorations on soma design [ 26 , 30 ] – a framework that focuses on bringing the felt experience of bodily activities to the foreground of awareness and using those felt experiences as a resource in the design process. Soma design utilises a first-person perspective and brings attention to the creative meaning-making that emerges during the design process. Soma design has proven to be generative to designing for somatic practices, such as dance [ 2 , 15 , 35 ], singing [ 11 ], breathing [ 36 , 65 ] and yoga [ 72 ].

Grounded in the pragmatist philosophy of somaesthetics [ 63 ] and Sheets-Johnstone's work in evolutionary biology [ 60 , 61 ], soma design adopts a non-dualistic stance towards of our lived experience. By introducing the concept soma , Shusterman refers to "our bodily subjectivity", an integrated whole where movement, emotion, thought and intercorporeality are not separable, and where the subject possesses bodily, somatic intentionality and subjective agency. Soma design calls for cultivating awareness and places our somas centrally as the site of meaning-making when interacting with technologies and the world at large. In order to cultivate somatic awareness, soma design uses a variety of techniques, some of them build on engaging with somatic practices such as Feldenkrais, yoga, dance, and in this work, martial arts.

The philosophical grounding of soma design resonates well with recent analysis of martial arts by philosophers and autoethnographers. The identification of somaesthetics appreciation in martial arts was suggested by Mullis [ 48 ], who discussed how such appreciation is cultivated in both practitioners and spectators of Chinese martial arts. Cohen's "somatic codes" [ 10 ] explain the meaning-making process karate practitioners engage in when learning movements. The somatic codes correspond to what Sheets-Johnstone framed as "somatic logos" [ 60 ]. That is, the meaning of a sequence of movements is embedded in the movements themselves – no language is needed to explain them. Lastly, Pettinen [ 55 ] introduces the concept "embodied judgement" to explain how martial artists, and specifically in Bujinkan Budō Taijutsu , learn to move and engage with one another. Through introducing the concept embodied judgement he suggests that martial movement is not acquired as an "automatic" movement, but, through reflexive engagement with other participants and contexts, practitioner will hone meaning-making and somatic decision making processes that lets them judge when and what to do.

2.2 Designing for Established Bodily Practice

Nylander and colleagues [ 53 ] identified four themes in HCI research on sports: design to increase bodily control and awareness; sports motivation and fun; pain and discomfort; and the use of pre-existing technology in sports. More recently, a 2021 literature review by Turmo-Vidal and colleagues [ 75 ], mapped out the design space of wearables in sports and fitness. The review grouped works by topics such as: the practice used; output and input technologies; social aspects of the design; and the length of the interaction. Their survey, despite their primary focus on wearable devices, identified certain aspects of the design space that are relevant to this work: synchronous versus asynchronous interactions, changing practices, prescribed versus non-prescribed interaction, and the role of the person using the technology. In a survey from 2009, Baca and colleagues [ 3 ] reviewed different technological advances and their effect on sports. In their survey, they covered the systems for performance analysis, for assisting coaches, for making judgement in sport events and systems used for entertainment. They set emphasis on the future systems that could facilitate novel strategies and interventions – rhyming with our intentions here.

By instead classifying the works by their contribution towards practitioners the following broad categories emerge: (1) systems that are focused on improving the performance of an athlete/practitioner, usually with the assistance of a coach or other expert; (2) systems that are aimed at assisting in the instruction of a bodily practice; and (3) systems that aim to enrich, transform or reinvent the bodily practice by technological intervention.

In the first category, we find several works exploring performance enhancement in martial arts using technology. Benitez Santiago and Miltenberger [ 4 ] explored the possibilities of using video feedback to improve performance of Capoeira practitioners by recording and replaying their movements for further analysis. Hämäläinen [ 23 ] explored a "video mirrors" – systems that use video recording to mirror the user – and their appropriateness for use by martial artists and acrobats wishing to improve their technique using different interface strategies. Jeong and colleagues introduced sensors to the handle of a shinai 2 with the goal of using measured pressure on the handle to improve top athletes performance. Performance enhancement is in focus for many design works in sports, for example for skiing[ 42 ], running [ 52 ], golf [ 52 ], and strength training [ 54 ].

In the second category, we find works that examine how artefacts can be used to enhance instruction on how to perform a particular bodily practice, often involving an instructor, but sometimes by letting the end-user do it themselves. The BodyLights system, designed by Turmo-Vidal and colleagues [ 72 , 73 , 74 ] could be used by an instructor in such bodily activities as yoga and weight-lifting to help practitioners visualise their posture and correct it according to normative expectations of the practice. Turmo-Vidal further explores the use of different wearable systems for purpose of assisting instructors in teaching bodily practices [ 71 ]. She frames it as designing for intercorporeality and focuses on interactions between teachers and students, and on the use of biofeedback. As another example of using technology to enhance instructions, Chu and colleagues [ 9 ] used Tai-Chi, a form of a Chinese martial art, as a case study for teaching physical tasks using virtual reality. They compared several projections of a prerecorded teacher in the VR space, including frontal, mirrored, surrounding and co-located, and their affect on movement accuracy, eventually coming to the conclusion that none of these techniques improve upon traditional teaching Tai-Chi.

Finally, in the third category we find designs that enhance and sometimes extend on the experience of practitioners of a bodily practice. These includes works that use visualisation to enrich such practices as skateboarding [ 57 ], martial arts [ 24 ], climbing [ 33 ], trampolining [ 21 ] and dance performances [ 16 , 35 ]. Haptic feedback was suggested by Allen d’Ávila Silveira and colleagues [ 2 ] to act as a choreographer, disrupting and guiding dancers’ movement in order to facilitate exploring new dance moves. Drones were also explored as means to enrich and expand established bodily practices such as Tai-Chi [ 37 , 38 ], opera [ 14 ] and running [ 17 , 44 , 46 ]. A sensor vest was designed by Chi and colleagues [ 7 , 8 ] as a mean to improve upon the judgement process in Taekwondo competitions, therefore enriching the experience of competitors, judges and spectators. Within the "exertion games" framework [ 43 ], designers have engaged with physical, exerting game play involving interaction between several players inspired children games such as tug-of-war [ 41 ] and sports such as rugby and boxing [ 45 ]. In these designs, full-body movement capture was combined with a projection in order to encourage players to interact with each other using brute-force, either directly [ 41 ] or through a touch sensitive elastic screen [ 45 ].

2.3 Expert practitioners in design

The core idea of participatory design [ 47 ] is that including those who will be using new technology into the design process, thereby leading to better design as well as empowering those who will be using the tools designed. Sometimes, like in the original UTOPIA project from the 1970’s [ 67 ], this means involving domain experts, thriving off their expertise. Expert-led design processes  [ 20 , 76 ] lets domain experts innovate in their field of expertise through supporting their processes. In the field of designing for somatic practices, Schiphorst and colleagues [ 59 ] propose involving somatic connoisseur – an expert practitioner who helps designers to enhance their somatic appreciation. Similarily, in embodied sketching [ 50 ], an expert bodily practitioner helps sensitise the design team as well as creating in and through the embodied practice. Expert practitioners have played an important role in many soma design projects, for example, in designing musical performances [ 11 , 15 ] or meditative experiences [ 65 ].

In these projects, only a few examined the relationship between the expert practitioner and the designed artefact or considered how the introduction of an artefact would affect the practice or the practitioner. One such examination was done by Karpashevich and colleagues’ [ 35 ] describing the experiences of a ballet dancer attempting to dance with their interactive ballet dress. The dancer reported how the limitations and visualisations in the artefact let her explore new means of expression, moving in previously "unthinkable" ways. In Lie and colleagues’ [ 39 ] participatory design of the SonicHoop , the expert aerial hoop dancers in the study reported that the sonification of their movements encouraged them to explore movements that generate interesting soundscapes, rather then let the music guide their movement. Another example is Hämäläinen and colleagues [ 24 ] who projected exaggerated movements of a martial arts practitioner in a game environment. In their user study, practitioners using the system over a prolonged period voiced concerns that it might encourage martial artists to engage only with the well-depicted movements portrayed in the game, while dismissing other ones; in turn violating the norms in their martial arts style. Such challenges to norms are also addressed by Chi and colleagues [ 7 , 8 ] who designed a wearable vest to be used in Taekwondo competitions, assisting referees in setting scores. The introduction of the vest required changes in tournament rules and as consequence adjustment of competitor-, referee- and spectator expectations. Another example by Turmo-Vidal and colleagues [ 72 ] reported on how yoga instructors changed exercises and structure of classes when wearable pointing devices helped students learn better alignment of different postures.

In summary, expert-led design work is both common and often, not always, successful [ 22 ], in terms of leading to innovative technology and changed practices of relevance to an established bodily practice. Within the framework of soma design, the design approach is shown to be transformative both for designers who engage with building it [ 28 , 64 ], and for the users who engaged with the resulting artefacts [ 64 ]. However, none of the studies reported here, have provided a detailed, phenomenological account of exactly how the soma design process transformed the involved expert practitioners and their bodily practice.

3 DESIGN PROCESS

3.1 Methods

Following the example set by Beuthel and Wilde [ 5 ], the design process presented here is a simplified, linear, depiction what was a non-linear, messy, back and forth process entangled with and determined by the affordances of available technologies and financial limitations.

The design of Koinryō was done in the framework of soma design [ 26 ] in which the designer(s) engage deeply, through all their senses, with the design materials, be they physical, digital, cultural or movement-based, in order to cultivate their aesthetic sensibility and create innovative design. Soma designers often rely on processes of defamiliarising what is habitual and tacit  [ 40 ] through estrangement methods  [ 78 ] thereby becoming sensitive to what is habitual as well as what could be novel, non-habitual practices. Estrangement is a disruption of the habitual way of moving, thinking, or feeling. Conceptual element are taken from or added to the body or the action, causing norms, traditions and other structures to become unstable. This destabilization brings new experiences into awareness, making them accessible for scrutiny and potential change [ 27 , 78 ]. Practically, estrangement practices interferes with the habitual through asking designers to perform an action "the other way around", in the non-habitual manner. This can be applied both to movements, such as folding your arms the other way around, or more generally to norms, ideas, thought patterns, emotional responses. Estrangement calls us to challenge what is , asking designers to respond differently, act, move and feel in novel ways. Exactly how to devise an estrangement experience depends on the design challenge hand. In the design process described here, estrangement centred on disrupting Bujinkan Budō Taijutsu movement patterns, but also engaging with interactive digital and other materials with the habitual Bujinkan Budō Taijutsu movement patterns and observe how those challenged or changed the practice.

Estrangement methods, in fact, already play a significant role in the acquisition of new movement practices, and are common in many established bodily practices including martial arts [ 10 ].

This design work was autobiographical [ 51 ], aimed at designing for the author Yoav's own practice – he is a 3rd Dan black belt in the martial art Bujinkan Budō Taijutsu . This meant that Yoav was required to find ways to defamiliarise his own martial art practice. Resonating with autobiographical design, in this work author Yoav used auto-ethnographical methods, following that of Höök [ 25 ]. This entailed documenting his experience with a new technology or practice, grounding design process in these observations. He meticulously documented his exploration of the design material in short written- or voice-based notes, images and videos. The second author, Pavel Karpashevich, acted as the supervisor of Yoav. Pavel had previously engaged with experiences bordering between comfortable and uncomfortable in soma design processes [ 32 , 35 , 36 , 69 ]. He was keen to explore martial arts as it also borders on the uncomfortable. Pavel had also engaged with boxing in the past. The third author, Kristina Höök, has a long-standing commitment to soma design [ 26 ]. Her approach to soma design is as a design research program as outlined by Redström [ 58 ]. With this project, she was hoping to explore the boundaries of the program— how does the rougher, harder, sometimes uncomfortable experiences lead to somaesthetic engagement and appreciation? Pavel and Kristina followed Yoav's autobiographical journey, meeting several times to discuss choices of materials, lessons learnt on a conceptual level, and ways of engaging with Koinryō.

3.2 Exploring the Design Space for Martial Arts

After discussing different options, author Yoav presented to the team a void between individual martial art practice and training with training partner, amplified by onset of the COVID-19 pandemic which limited group training. The team set as a goal to explore this design space. Author Yoav engaged in a series of dedicated individual training sessions aimed at exploring this design space. In order to defamiliarise and uncover habitual martial movements, he engaged in a variety of estrangement exercises, some of which were already established within Bujinkan Budo Taijutsu , while others were inspired by Wilde and colleagues [ 78 ]. These exercises included: 1) disrupting movement of the legs, shoulders and center of mass by tying the ankles or knees 30-50 centimetres apart; 2) limiting the shoulder movements by affixing an elbow at a certain position to the body with a wooden stick or tying the elbows to the torso; 3) using weights and resistance belts to alter movements such as side-stepping and punching by holding weights or connecting weights to the belt to create an improvised "tail"; 4) manipulating his own body, for example by pulling his own knee using a rope anchored at the wall, in a manner similar to a takedown manoeuvre practiced in some martial arts (see figure 4 ). These exercises helped to deconstruct specific movements, for example, side-stepping was deconstructed by wagging a "tail" made of weights, amplifying the rotational force of the movement. He also highlighted what forms of agency can be given to resulting artefact that would allow Yoav to bring interactivity into his martial art training.

During the exploration phase, different exercises were documented in video, images and diary entries following the example of auto-ethnographical process presented by Höök  [ 25 ]. These materials were used in the later analysis of the design process and outcomes.

These early explorations of martial arts movement explored also how the natural environment can be used as a design resource [ 66 ], as author Yoav trained mostly outdoors. By engaging in martial arts training both indoors and outdoors, Yoav explored the experiential qualities and dynamic gestalt of the materials he engaged with, such as the ruggedness of tree bark, the slipperiness of an icy field and the elastic properties of the rubber bands and young tree branches. The protruding branches of fallen tree invited author Yoav to consider them as indicators of imagined attacks that can be moved around, attacked from below, or against. These explorations included conditioning exercises that are practiced in author Yoav's branch of Bujinkan , such as hitting a dead branch until it breaks, or pulling pieces of bark from decaying logs and stumps. Similar to how brick-breaking or tatami-cutting is practiced in other martial arts, breaking these parishable materials provided reassurance of the practitioner's martial prowess. Such practices cultivated awareness of the impermanence of the materials used, and sparked interest in the opportunities presented by using materials who can be destroyed through practice.

3.3 Early Prototypes

Based on the insights from the defamiliarisation of the habitual martial art routines, two different forms of interaction were selected as the most evocative: restricting and resisting movement in grappling exercises, and attacking and defending in striking technique exercises. These were further researched by constructing and testing low-fi prototypes, shown in Figure 5 .

Restricting and resisting movement in grappling exercises was explored by cardboard, elastic band and rope prototypes attached to hips and arms, blocking or resisting joint movements. This was done in order to simulate a) a standoff with a sparring partner, in which arm locks would be used as an attack technique, b) maintaining balance in the situations when the legs are blocked.

Attacking and defending in striking technique exercises was explored by using tree branches, fallen trees or a rope tied between trees as makers for the possible movements of an imagined opponent. Extending a boxing bag or a tree trunk with "arms" made from wooden sticks, combining them with a dōgi (training jacket), rope or resistance belt, allowed Yoav to explore such movements as pulling, pushing, blocking, deflecting and throwing; all with the added "arms". Yoav experimented with free swinging pendulums, constructed from rope and weights, as well as rigid ones, constructed by attaching a training sword to a stick in a cross-like structure, moving in synchronisation with pendulum, or swinging the pendulum and evading or blocking it on its return movement. Most of these experiments were conducted outdoors utilising the natural environment, embracing a Bujinkan tradition of training in nature[ 18 , 19 ].

Through the engagements with the prototypes representing grappling through movement restriction, this form of interaction was discarded. The prototypes revealed that designing for this particular type of movement restriction required two connected bodies, as well as whole body engagement, and thus, it could not be reduced to limiting movements of a single or a small group of joints in just one body.

Therefore, the further design work was focused on the artifacts that could engage in striking techniques, such as boxing bags and "wooden dummies" 3 . Different ways to allow these prototypes to "attack", "block" or "evade" were considered, for example by attaching different arm- and leg-like appendages capable of different modes of movement, such as three degrees of freedom or rotation around one or more axes, to the swinging part of the early prototypes (see figure 5 , G & H, figure 6 bottom right).

The cases, when the prototype swung its "arms" towards Yoav, were reminiscent of an attack of a training partner and made Yoav want to try out different blocking stances in return. The movement of the whole prototype in space, especially when covering a wide area, invoked a feeling of the prototype evading or engaging.

Engaging with the early prototypes made it clear to Yoav how the actuators could be added to the interactive artifact – specifically, for manipulating the arms’ positions, as well as starting, stopping and adjusting the swinging motion.

3.4 Martial-play

During different stages of designing and construction of the artifact, Yoav engaged in a "martial-play" with it. These martial-play sessions were from 3 to 8 minutes long and were a free form of training involving the application of Taijutsu techniques in a playful and curious manner [ 55 ]. During the final stages of the project, at least one session per day was performed, followed by a short verbal reflection, were video recorded.

In addition, author Kristina, who has no martial arts background, and a colleague J, who had some experience with Taijutsu , were invited for a recorded interaction session with Koinryō, along T, an expert teacher in Wing-Chun 4 and his student D. The visitors who were familiar with the project, consented to being recorded and interviewed. There were additional sessions with the author Pavel and other colleagues, which were not recorded. The work was conducted during the height of the COVID-19 pandemic, therefore only people who were in regular contact with author Yoav were contacted. Video recordings, annotated interactions with the artefact, along with materials from earlier stages of the design process, were transcribed and coded [ 6 ].

4 Koinryō

Based on the experiments with prototypes for striking exercises, Koinryō was built as an interactive opponent for practicing attack and defense techniques. The intent was to bring the experience of outdoor training into indoor setting, giving agency to such equipment as a wooden dummy, boxing bag or tree branch; altering their traditional passive role in the training process, but without modelling its looks based on human morphology (as that would create expectations that Koinryō would not be able to meet).

Koinryō consists of several parts, from top to bottom (see figure 9 , right side): a platform, a wooden shaft, pneumatic muscles, a body and arms. The platform is connected to the ceiling and carries the body via the shaft; pneumatic muscles that can pull the shaft towards the platform and thus create a swinging movement. The main body was made from a wooden log, sawed along its radial axis, and hollowed to house air pumps and FlowIO [ 62 ]– a miniature platform for pneumatic development. The arms were made from a single forked tree branch, and are mounted on a horizontal axis through the lower part of the body; they are capable of rotating along their axis in the vertical frontal plane (see figure 8 ).

Both the main body and arms are constructed from wood, forged in the same area where Yoav was training. Both parts were processed in ways that retains their natural appearance as much as possible. The used natural materials contributed to the experience of training outdoors and opened a dimension of fragility and impermanence of the artifact. Parts of the design such as the bark were deliberately left on, in order to be broken off while training, render a sense of live action similar to Yoav's training experience in the forest.

Koinryō movements are controlled by three pneumatic muscles made from a latex tube and a plastic sleeve [ 1 ]. Pneumatic muscles were chosen for their high power density and fast actuation rate, as well as their conformance to human levels of force, qualities that were informed by exploring elastic bands and green, flexible branches. The muscles are connected to the platform on one side and to the shaft on the other. In order to increase the effective range of the muscles, additional springs were added between the muscles and the shaft resembling the sinews of an organic body in function.

Koinryō relies on the readings from FlowIO's internal acceleration and gyroscopic sensors. The trunk and branches were equipped with an array of capacitive sensors (four on the main body and two on each arm) for sensing touch and distance to the human partner. The system uses a current monitor circuit for detecting resistance applied to the movement of its arms. When exploring the movements of different versions of the prototype, the experience of keeping in touch with the trunk or arms of Koinryō as well as following its movements through touch were evocative, reminiscent of following a partner or trying to feel the intentions of a partner through subtle changes in their posture and balance. The arms allows Koinryō to switch between stances: with either the two branches leveled, or one branch centered at eye level while the other is at midriff height, imitating the position of the hands in some stances used in Taijutsu . In addition, the arms’ movements are capable of generating additional torque adjusting Koinryō’s movement trajectory such as when suddenly switching between the two stances.

The software is designed as a set of state machines mapping sensor data to state transitions in turn driving movements of the arms and pneumatic muscles. The system uses hardware abstraction libraries provided by FlowIO, Arduino, sensors’ manufacturers and the "Embedded C++ Template Library" [ 77 ].

In the final iteration of Koinryō, the software supported several movement profiles for both pneumatic muscles and the arms: movement-averse / movement-maximising behaviour for the body, as well as touch-averse / touch-seeking behaviour for both arms and body. In addition to the set profiles, there was an adaptive profile, switching behaviours at random time intervals.

5 RESULTS

All the verbal data collected during the project was transcribed, coded and then analysed using thematic analysis [ 6 ] in a bottom-up manner with the goal of finding an overview of tensions between the design process and the result. After that, the video data was revisited to explore the non-verbal data, and interactions were coded in top-down approach. As we were interested in phenomenological accounts of how the soma design process changed Yoav and his practice, any mismatches or design decisions that matches with Yoav's expectations of what martial arts training should be, in a sense, uncovering underlying norms, were especially interesting to analyse in detail. From the data three major themes where such revelations of norms came about emerged in the anlaysis: (1) the aesthetic qualities of the physical and digital materials and how those harmonised, or failed to harmonise, with the martial arts norms; (2) the movement qualities that emerged from the interaction with Koinryō in line with or opposed to the norms of the practice; and (3) the changes the expert went through, over time, when engaging with Koinryō. As we will show, these experiences helped Yoav to, in the end, achieve a better somaesthetic appreciation of his own martial art practice. The three are a broad categorisation of where the norms of the Taijutsu practice revealed itself, both in terms of what certain practices entail and their underlying aims or general philosophy of the practice.

5.1 Material Qualities for Martial Arts

To give a feel for the aesthetic affordances of the materials that make up Koinryō, let us provide some accounts of how the authors and the experts from the other bodily practices engaged with it. This reveals some of the properties and norms that the expert-designer had intentionally (or sometimes unintentionally) built into the system.

Fragility and Robustness The martial-arts play sessions Yoav performed with Koinryō put considerable strain on its construction: parts broke or were pushed out of alignment, and different parts of the technical set up at times became disconnected. The fragility was an intentional part of the early design explorations, aiming to mirror training in the woods, breaking dead branches or tearing off pieces of bark. As such fragility continued to be a design resource to the Koinryō project rendering the kind of experiences Yoav wanted the system to exhibit. This should not be interpreted as if all kinds of fragility were desirable. Over time, Yoav made sure to replace broken parts with better ones. For example, the robustness and integrity of the major "trunk" of Koinryō was important to maintain. The main body was initially made from 3D printed plastic, but when redesigned it became more durable when replaced with hollowed out logs.

The fragility and the fact that parts kept falling off made Koinryō come across as a sensitive and precious construction. Guests were sometimes deterred from attempting to hit, push, pull or otherwise engage with it in a rough manner. For example, the guest T, expert teacher in Wing-Chun , tried Chi Sau exercise 5 with Koinryō, but without punching the artifact. When asked about it, he said he was only attempting at soft training, as he was afraid that he would otherwise break it.

Yoav found himself repeatedly encouraging participants to be more affirmative and harsh in their interactions with Koinryō since breakability was, to him, an integral part of outdoor training and what he wanted to see here as well. In a video snippet where Yoav is demonstrating Koinryō to invited guests, he comments: "Yes, it breaks. It should be breaking" , demonstrating how by punching Koinryō as if it was a boxing bag. Author Kristina noted: "When I watched you interact with it I realised that we were doing it wrong." and she then went on to engage in more rough play with it, making pieces of the bark fall off.

Here, we note a norm-related tension between Yoav's Taijutsu practice – where breaking materials are indicative of a powerful technique – and everyday perception of technologies as precious, costly, irreplaceable creations.

Care When actively used, Koinryō required repeated adjustments, such as readjusting the position of the arms, reconnecting springs, tightning the pneumatic tubes to stop leaks, and, in early iterations, redesigning and replacing broken parts. The adjustments themselves took on a form of care, requiring attention from Yoav as a process of continuous improvement and adjustment. Yoav notes at one time "now Koinryō has some balance issues, it kinda leans to the right." This attention and care was, at time, reminiscent of training with a human partner. For example, when Koinryō made a suspicious cracking sound Yoav would immediately stop training and examine whether some damage was made: "this sound reminded me of a training partner tapping loudly 6 ." During the beginning of interaction, Koinryō was programmed to perform a sequence of movements that would show that everything was working correctly. The sequence reminded Yoav of bowing and taking stances which is sometimes practiced before sparring. To his surprise, Yoav. found himself bowing back to Koinryō.

Lack of strength. Both Yoav and guest participants commented on Koinryō’s inability to generate movements that are comparable in strength to that of a human sparring partner. In his notes, Yoav comments on the lower force of the movements exhibited by the artifact "it's not as strong movement as I believe it should be" , but at the same time, the movements were more bodily engaging and less predictable "[Koinryō] gets me to move with it and also sometimes the movements surprise me" . Participant D, the Wing-Sun student, also commented on its unpredictability, demanding her to be pliable and ’feeling’ the performed movements "to be really soft and really feel, because of the randomnness of the reaction. And this is what we are practicing in Chi Sau – to be really soft" . T commented that "Because I'm soft I can follow it. If I had not been training Wing-Chun the way I do, I probably couldn't follow it as I do. [...] Because probably I would have restrained it, but I am used to just follow it." . By that he emphasised the role of keeping your arms soft in Wing-Chun training. When Koinryō presented both excessive force and surprising movements, it was perceived as a positive behaviour: " It feels like I need to respond to it when it has a lot of force. I need to get out of the way, and that's really nice."

These accounts reflect different underlying norms in the Taijutsu and Wing-Chun martial arts practices: the former emphasise footwork with large steps, while the latter emphasise the use of soft arms at close range.

Agency. Koinryō was designed around touch and kinaesthetic sensing and the aim was that it would actively engage, sometimes initiate interactions. This presented practitioners used to train with a human partner or alternatively a wodden dummy, with the challenge of understanding its (limited) agency: “When to move forward? When should I try and push forward and not only push back.” . Koinryō became perceived as a different kind of training altogether, since it was actively engaging, showing some agency, something which a wooden dummy would not: "I was moving to some place and then I realised‘oh no it moves back towards me!’" . At the same time, Koinryō was more predictable than a human counterpart "it is more predictable than training with a human partner, it still feels very passive, it's a very passive partner." Shaping Koinryō’s agency helped Yoav to make tacit understanding of the role of a training partner in exercise explicit.

In summary, the affordance of the physical and digital materials that make up Koinryō, resulted in a multitude of occasionally contradicting experiences: it was sometimes seen as fragile, but still inviting and withstanding physical force; its movements were soft and lacking strength, but still at times surprisingly forceful; Koinryō was far from replacing a living human training partner, but is not a mere passive device like a boxing bag or a wooden dummy; and somewhat unexpectedly it invoked a sense of care 7 similar to a human martial arts training partner. While each of these tensions could be explored furthered, potentially leading to continued redesign of Koinryō, in this context, we are interested in how they revealed some of the norms of the practice to Yoav: the importance of care for your partner; the difference between soft and rigid arms, large and small footwork, and what those bodily engagement can achieve in combat; how breaking branches or making bark fall off in outdoors training lets him know how strong his engagement is; or how the agency of the other alters movement practices.

5.2 Movement Qualities

In Bujinkan , movement norms are embedded in katas 8 – a sequence of defined movements used for training purposes – and wazas 9 – structured confrontation-like movement sequences practiced with a partner. The kata and waza convey knowledge on how to move in prescibed ways to attack or ward off an attack. But in order to perform martial arts, you cannot solely rigidly move in only one way. The ways katas and wazas are constructed also aim at teaching more abstract principles of movements in martial arts, usually through the use of henka 10 which deliberately diverts from the original kata and waza in order to let participants explore and be challenged to, for example, maintain balance or divert the force of an attack.

Movement sequences within the norm. Koinryō was designed to be a smart, interactive boxing bag, tailored specifically to the movement norms as expressed in kata , waza , and their underlying principles of the Bujinkan Budō Taijutsu practice. Koinryō was explicitly designed to afford certain kinds of movements and exercises and prevent others. For example, Koinryō aims to encourage tai-sabaki 11 – a particular form of footwork letting practitioners achieve an advantage by controlling the range, momentum and balance shared with their partner. In one stage of the design process, Yoav noted in his diary: "It felt like I was using my legs more, switching hands and switching positions more than in previous iterations [of Koinryō]. It is less about responding to the arms and more responding [to] the body" . This was a quality that he realised he had been seeking in the design. To comply with the norms of the practice, it was of key importance that Koinryō would encourage footwork and not only moving of the arms.

Yoav continued to struggle with this aspect of the design. The footwork requires both variation of height (by bending knees) and variations of movement direction (to include sideways and diagonal movements as well as moving forwards towards or backwards away from Koinryō). By adjusting the control algorithm to take into account both contact with Koinryō’s arms and its current trajectory, Koinryō started responding with wider movements of its body, encouraging Yoav to engage with the whole body rather then to rely solely on the use of hands when interacting with it.

While the footwork required careful design alterations, Yoav was pleased to see how Koinryō afforded other specific recognisable kata such as hi no kata 12 , as well as a variety of striking techniques such as shutōken 13 , straight punches, hooks, uppercuts, elbow strikes and finger strikes without much redesign work. These movement affordances were revealed to Yoav during his repeated "martial-play" sessions with Koinryō.

Disabled kata and waza . Some movements that were explored with early prototypes as well as in the estrangement exercises Yoav did without technology, such as locks and throws, were not afforded by the later iterations of the system. As for throws, Koinryō’s attachment to the ceiling made the actual sensation of throwing extremely awkward, as Koinryō "resisted" in ways that no person can. This revealed the importance of manipulating both your own and the opponent's balance as a single connected soma when executing a throw. This "connected soma" sensation was disabled by Koinryō’s binding to the ceiling, its limited directions of movement and its light weight.

In some of the exploration exercises a dōgi 14 (or a belt) was tied to a tall boxing bag. The sleeves were used for the pulling and pushing movements in order to initiate a throw, while the bag was kicked in the manner of swiping the opponent's legs, see Figure 11 . Since Koinryō was quite short and attached to ceiling, it only extended as low as Yoav's midriff and had no parts corresponding to legs or hips. Another kind of throw, ganseki nage 15 , involves hooking the opponents arm between the practitioner's neck and raised arm, and then throwing the opponent by lifting and twisting their shoulder, see Figure 12 . This kind of manoeuvre was tried with an earlier prototypes of Koinryō, but again, the later iterations of Koinryō and its anchoring to the ceiling allowed author Yoav. to practice the setup to the throw (hooking the arm) but not the lifting and twisting required to complete the throw.

Arm locks were also explored with the earlier prototypes, specifically two locks in which pressure is applied on the opponents elbow in order to affect their shoulder, back and overall balance. Surprisingly, the fact the Koinryō does not have bendable elbows (or in fact anything resembling and elbow) created an interesting opportunity. It allowed Yoav to practice both: positioning the body correctly for the locks, and to apply a much higher force than with a human training partner. In this respect, Koinryō partially afforded the exercises. These exercises, though, were not attempted with the later, more interactive version of Koinryō.

Novel movement sequences. Additionally, Koinryō allowed for novel movement sequences, which are not normally a part of the Taijutsu practice. One of this movements is the synchronised swinging movement, which involves lightly touching or holding one or both of Koinryō’s arms and trying to follow its swinging movement, augmented by the pneumatic muscles, until the swinging rhythm is not intentionally broken with with a throw or a strike. This movement is reminiscent of some balance exercises performed with a partner that are used as a preparation for throws. The other movement is " wing-chun hand movement" (see figure 13 ), in which Yoav would wrap around one of the Koinryō’s arms with their hand from the inside-out. This movement is not encouraged in Taijutsu , as it uses only the hands of the practitioner, not engaging feet and torso. There is an emphasis in Taijutsu on using the whole body as a unified structure in order to place ourselves in a more protected position while breaking the structure of the opponent, opening them up for attacks, locks or throws.

Movements in disguise. Finally, certain kinds of movements, facilitated by training with Koinryō, appeared, at first glance, to be conforming to the norms of Taijutsu , by being clearly recognisable as an existing kata . On closer inspection, though, they presented challenges hidden in the movements. An example of this kind of movements is depicted in Figure 14 . In this sequence, you can see how Yoav is switching a stance in the end of a punch in order to perform another attack. This might seem as an acceptable movement, but, in this transition, Yoav is exposed to Koinryō. The follow-up attack would be done from the range in which one might be open for a counter-attack – a counter-attack that Koinryō is incapable of doing, but where a human training partner would be. Performing such manoeuvres can lead to developing weaknesses in style and habitual reactions. Another potential challenge lies in the timings and cues offered in the interaction: with Koinryō, it is possible to perform a correct movement but with incorrect timing or in response to an incorrect "cue". For example, a common cue for an upcoming attack is an opponent shifting the weight forward, or turning the hip. Koinryō has no hips to turn or variable weight to shift with, but instead it makes a loud noise from its pumps at the start of a movement. This might sensitise one training with it to respond to the sounds of the opponent rather then to the movement of their bodies.

In summary, the dynamic unfoldings of Koinryō’s movement sequences had to be repeatedly experienced and redesigned in order to comply not only with specific kata and waza , but also to adhere to the underlying philosophy of the whole practice. This is perhaps most clearly seen in the struggles to engage the right kind of footwork. While Bujinkan Budo Taijutsu is allows and even encourages variation in kata and waza , these innovations must adhere to the overarching values and strategies conveyed in the practice. The challenges uncovered through designing Koinryō brought Yoav to inquire whether they unfold when engaging with more traditional training tools such as a boxing bag, as well.

5.3 Changing Koinryō – Changing Myself

In this section, the pronoun "I" will be used to describe Yoav's personal experience. As shown through the material explorations and the new movement practices arising, the soma design framework provided a path to deconstruct martial art habitus, defamiliarisation of the various movement practices, and ways of introducing different technologies and materials into the training. Here, I would like to discuss how engaging in the design work, helped me to develop better somaesthetic appreciation of my own martial art practice. This appreciation contributed not only to improving myself as a practitioner, but also made me articulate (to myself) previously tacitly understood stylistic norms. The design process itself also generated novel training exercises inspired by Koinryō that I continued to engage in my normal training routines, even without the artifact.

Let us start with an account of the novel training exercises, before turning to how I changed my expertise and started to uncover some of the norms of my practice.

New training exercises – inspired by Koinryo. Ideas and novel movements, which were devised during the training with Koinryō, have been added to my everyday practice and also inspired novel forms of exercises. These included the use of positioned branches to practice blocks, tai-sabaki 16 , as well as counter-attacks. Another exercise inspired by the design of Koinryō is the use of branches to practice locks – such as muso-dori , musha-dori and oni-kudaki as well as throws such as ganseki nage , depicted in Figure 15 – all of which involve an elbow lock as part of their fulcrum. By placing branches in a position, where the attacking hand would be, it became possible for me to practice positioning my body for the lock and to apply pressure on the branch until it breaks. This, in turn, allows me to practice these locks against simulated resistance with a vigorousness, that I would not normally use when training with a human opponent. By allowing fragility in my design, I opened up to the idea of exploring it in new settings, such as the elbow locks I described.

Improving Practice. The designed artefact and the design process itself, with its attention on reflective practices, enabled me to identify ways, in which my technique was no longer, in all parts, compatible with the norms of Taijutsu . An example of that is the engagement with "bad" interactions shown in Figure 14 . In these example, movements that had felt correct were later identified through the analytical process as transgressive to Taijutsu norms. It is a principle imparted by many techniques that you either position yourself at such range from the opponent that it would require them to take another step to attack, or that you maintain several points of contact with your opponent in order to discern your opponent's next move by tactile means. In this engagement with Koinryō, I did neither.

Articulating Tacit Norms. Another form of transformation spurred by the design process was a better articulation of what is considered normative in the martial art. This transformation was facilitated by designing prototypes that challenge the norms, and then reflecting on the tension generated in order to articulate what norm was challenge, why it was challenged, and whether I wished to accept the norm, reject it or continue challenging it. This can be illustrated by the " wing-chun " hand movement – prior to the design work and interaction with Koinryō, I would have not recognised this movement as a transgression on Taijutsu norms. Only by performing this movement both against passive and responsive prototypes and reflecting upon these interactions, I came to recognise that it is indeed transgressive , and I came to articulate why it was so, by comparison with similar non-transgressive interactions. The Taijutsu norm that is being transgressed is the one that constitutes that manipulation of the opponents hands and posture should always be driven and supported by footwork and the use of the whole body 17 . When exploring movements against rigid arms, I was forced to use footwork in order to manipulate the relative position of myself against the arm. While using soft and passive arms, it was not obvious to me that I am using only my hand to manipulate them. Only when combining a structure that is both rigid and interactive it became apparent to me that the movement is transgressive – this was due to Koinryō’s resistance to the movement, although not strongly enough to force me to do adjust my footwork. The reflexive practices encouraged in soma design helped me to articulate what exactly is transgressive in the movement.

A second example of revealing norms on several levels, is how Musha-dori was enacted with Koinryō. In musha-dori , the right angle is forced on the opponent by capturing the opponents palm behind one's back, placing one's hand behind the opponents elbow, and through a sudden drop of one's hips while pushing the opponent's elbow forward and upwards, depicted in figure 15 b. When practicing this joint-lock on Koinryō (and on earlier prototypes), the rigidness of Koinryō’s arm turned out to be not an obstacle, but an opportunity. With Koinryō, I could train by applying more force and doing it quicker than I would normally do with a human partner. This disrupted normative movement became a starting point for the new forms of training that support normative movement principles around how to apply a shoulder lock. This revealed norms not only in the form of the performed movements, which were disrupted in this case, but also in the functional intention of these movements. In the case of musha-dori , it is forcing the elbow joint to bend or break in the process. This exposes the social norms of training – an agreement that I cannot hurt my training partner. Koinryō introduced no such delimitation, combined with its apparent fragility, as well as knowing that parts can easily be replaced, it was inviting and even encouraging to explore such interactions.

In summary, the design process of Koinryō did not result in just a training tool – it changed Yoav as an expert practitioner. The estrangement exercises and material exploration allowed Yoav to innovate his training routine with new exercises enacted by, but independent, of Koinryō. The reflective process of designing, engaging and redesigning with Koinryō brought to foreground what was previously tacit – which movements are normative, which are not, and what separates them.

6 Discussion of Results

As we discussed in the background above, previous research show that whenever technology enters into an established bodily practice, the bodily practice will change. The design account above, leading to Koinryō, shows many instances of clashes between what is being designed and the pre-existing norms of the practice. If Koinryō would be more regularly used as part of Bujinkan Budo Taijutsu it would most likely change aspects of the practice.

We might argue that introducing technology into a martial arts practice is not necessary or even desirable as it changes the ’purity’ of the art form that otherwise does not rely on any technology. At the same time, the overarching philosophy of martial arts entertains and encourages change . Martial arts practices are seen as an on-going creative practice as practitioners need to develop new techniques to overcome the established practices in their field, which in turn becomes the new norm which needs to be overcome by new techniques. This transformation happens both in the movement patterns trained, as well as in instruments such as the set of rules (in competitive martial arts), its values and basic assumptions. In fact, new technologies often lead to novel martial arts forms. For example, the martial arts practice named Kendo was a response to introducing shinai , a wooden baton used in manner similar to a sword, together with protective garments, in turn leading to the development of new techniques and rules  [ 13 ]. In martial arts, technologies and novel movement patterns are not seen as breaches but sought after as they may renew the practice, invigorating and improving on it.

But our point here was not to discuss how to arrive at the perfect design adhering to the underlying norms of the practice nor how to make changes that would improves the practice. Instead we aimed to provide a rich phenomenological account of how the design process changed the expert practitioner himself . Through the detailed account of his autobiographical soma design journey above, it is clear how tacit norms of the practice became articulated, challenged, and understood by Yoav. It involved not only understanding the meaning and purpose of some particular kata , but the underlying philosophy behind why that kata is trained the way it is. It allowed him to deconstruct the habits he had learnt, and moreover, let him device novel artistic habits. Let us turn to some of the somaesthetic theories to explain and deepen our understanding of Yoav's process and why it is important.

6.1 Aesthetic appreciation

In his writings on somaesthetics Shusterman repeatedly comes back to the need to disrupt habits and become more aware, discerning the different parts of a movement or emotion, feeling every little change in your body. For example, training your breathing to achieve better focus, or correcting your eating habits from what is“ habitual ” to shape new, more reflective, thoughtful movements. Returning to Dewey, Shusterman [ 63 ] discusses the difference between“ routine, unintelligent habit ” and“ intelligent or artistic habit ” that is fused with thought and feeling. To Shusterman, the art of somatic reflection and conscious control is thus itself a“ refined intelligent habit emerging from and coordinating a background of countless other habits that constitute the developing bundle of complex, unstable, opposing attitudes, habits, impulses we call the self. ”

What is it that we achieve if we disrupt the habitual, if we listen more attentively to our own movements, discerning all the small changes and processes that are involved in moving? Shusterman argues that such engagement may lead to better appreciation, richer bodily ways of being in the world, and deriving more pleasure and beauty. In the phenomenological account above, Yoav has been learning and creatively changing his body practice through the meeting with a design process. He has not only expanded on his repertory of possible movements, but creatively devised new ways of training, new ways of moving, new experiences that improves on his practice. Höök argues that this is the main contribution of soma design [ 26 ]. While soma design is grounded in somaesthetics, it does not rely on pre-made bodily practices, such as yoga or martial arts, as a path to extending on our repertory of experience. Instead, by using creative methods such as estrangement it allows us to create novel movement patterns, arriving at novel experiences, experiences that we may never have had before [ 26 , 36 ], and, as we have shown here, potentially changing the expert designer.

Looking back over the account of Yoav's design journey, through the estrangement methods, the meeting with the digital and physical materials, and designing iterations of the Koinryō design, he came to see the underlying norms and reasons for, e.g. certain kata and waza . He also learnt to distinguish between changes to the established practices that could improve his practice and those that would break with the overarching philosophy of the practice. His practice became " intelligent or artistic habit ", fused with thought and feeling, and his practice changed .

6.2 Transforming designers

As discussed while surveying the prior art above, experts have played a significant role in design work done in HCI before, by being a representative user [ 20 ], by being part of a user study [ 24 ], by contributing their expertise to sensitising the design team [ 50 , 59 ], or by taking a central role in the design process [ 11 , 21 , 33 ]. However, the bodily practice of the expert and how those changes through the process is often omitted, or presented as if they are an unchanging, anchoring element independent of the design. Here, we provided an account of how intimate engagement in design work by a bodily practice expert did in fact transformed the expert himself. It thereby provides another example of the transformative power of soma design, previously shown for designers who are not bodily-practice experts and for users [ 64 ].

6.3 Limitations and Ethics

There are well known tensions between autobiographical design and HCI practices [ 12 ] which have influenced the design and reporting of this paper. One such tension is that autobiographical design cannot provide any claims of generalisability. Throughout we have deliberately avoided any such generalisation. Our claim is not that the design is of relevance to martial artists. Koinryō represents specific needs and values of Yoav at the time: forced to train by himself, and being disconnected from his training group, Koinryō was shaped to fit these particular needs and context. In fact, we are not making any knowledge claims pertaining to the design as such, instead our knowledge claims concern the change in Yoav and why it came about.

This brings us to a second tension between autobiographical design and HCI research: how to verify the authenticity of reporting and authorial voices. What the autobiographical voice offers is qualitative depth, and as discussed by Neustaedter and Sengers, this depth in reporting may reveal big effects [ 51 ]. The big effects we report on here concern how norms are made explicit, changed and challenged by a soma design process.

The tension between norms and designed artifacts present ethical considerations for future designers: can we predict how our artifacts going to shape future users [ 14 ]? Will they change and develop their aesthetic skills in the direction they want to change? Here, we claim that using soma design, and specifically using experts as designers who reflect on how they are transformed by the design process, can identify at least some of the tensions between their practice's norms and the designed artifact. As argued by Eriksson and colleagues, self-cultivation is an ethical act in itself. Articulating the tacit norms of a bodily practice is therefore a crucial step in designing systems that engage with these norms – reinforcing or challenging – in an ethically responsible manner.

7 CONCLUSIONS

Through a soma design process, a novel artefact, Koinryō, able to participate and potentially enrich certain aspects of martial arts training, was devised. It was designed specifically for the Bujinkan Budo Taijutsu martial art practice through an autobiographical soma design process.

From the design of the artefact we have identified and articulated some of the requirements and limitations for future designs targeting sparring in martial arts, such as the material requirements on robustness, fragility, strength, responsiveness or ways of surprising the user. We have also brought forth some conceptual strategies to work around limitations such as emphasis on softness or cultivating a relationship of care with the artefact.

This said, our main contribution lies in uncovering how a soma design process can help expose the tacit norms of a practice and thereby help expert practitioners develop their own practice. The soma design process, involving a bodily practice expert who used an autobiographical design process when designing Koinryō enriched our understanding of what those norms are on a felt level rather than as an abstraction. The design work proved to be generative for the expert's martial arts training even after the artefact was decommissioned. Our analysis revealed how layer after layer of meaning-making and inner-outer stances in the martial art practice were brought to the fore, and made attainable for change. It encourage Yoav to take a more refined look into any tensions between what is practiced and that the novel design affords and recognise that designs can simultaneously conform and challenge a movement culture rather than neatly fall into one category.

Let us end with a quote that captures the richness of Yoav's design journey:

“Think that what is written down here was done just for you, and do not consider simply looking at it or familiarising yourself with it or imitating it. Rather, you should consider these principles as thought they were discovered from your own mind, and continually make great efforts to make them a physical part of you.”

– Miyamoto Musashi. "The Book of Five Rings".[ 49 ]
