MyCareBudget: Co-creating a Healthcare Digital Commons with and for Disabled Citizens and their Unpaid Carers

Peter Glick , Open Lab, Newcastle University, United Kingdom, p.glick2@newcastle.ac.uk
Clara Crivellaro , Open Lab, Newcastle University, United Kingdom, clara.crivellaro@newcastle.ac.uk

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3580934
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Supporting disabled populations and their unpaid carers through designing sustainable healthcare interventions and infrastructures, is an important, yet challenging, area in HCI research. We report on a collaboration with 23 disabled citizens, unpaid carers, and a care organisation, wishing to co-develop digital responses to challenges they face in the management of self-directed care budgets. We describe how leveraging participatory methods, including asynchronous and remote engagements, enabled the co-creation of a sustainable digital common-pool resource, used by over 5,000 people worldwide. This study contributes novel configurations of methods and tools for co-design with ‘seldom heard’ populations. Demonstrating how these enabled the collective articulation of what constitutes trust, governance, and responsibility, in the design of a digital commons, “MyCareBudget”, offering peer-produced care documents for use by disabled citizens and their unpaid carers. We discuss implications for HCI interested in co-designing sustainable socio-technical interventions with underserved and marginalised populations, in healthcare settings.

1 INTRODUCTION

HCI's interest in supporting disabled citizens 1 and their unpaid carers 2 in their everyday lives to successfully access healthcare service provisions, has been growing for the past decade. HCI research has explored the complexities of tasks and challenges characterising home-based unpaid carers, as they manage end-to-end care, alone and without support [ 9 , 16 , 28 , 51 , 74 ], where citizens find themselves forced to assemble personal infrastructures that tie together the multiple, siloed healthcare service providers they depend upon [ 30 ]. Research in this area has focused on developing careful design of technologies that ‘work’ for these ‘seldom heard’ 3 populations, traditionally considered at the margins, with explicit attempts to support their empowerment [ 31 ]. Scholars have also paid significant attention to mitigating potential negative (unintended) consequences ensuing from socio-technical interventions and fostering caring relations [ 44 ]. One important strategy in this area has focused on the inclusion of disabled citizens and their unpaid carers in the design of systems that are to benefit them, and for these to be centred around matters of concerns [ 6 , 7 , 37 , 43 , 73 ].

Yet, research in this area faces key challenges. HCI has highlighted issues of access, equity and power dynamics when working with marginalised communities in design processes [ 2 , 18 , 19 , 37 , 48 ]; it has also problematised the extent to which interventions can be deemed sustainable [ 5 , 70 , 76 , 82 ]. This latter aspect is significant, given that universities’ research cultures are characterised by short-term projects bounded by research funding, that sees researchers dipping in and out of contexts [ 63 , 85 ] and focusing on the design of novel products, rather than questions of sustainability and democratic considerations for the learning and empowerment of participants [ 13 ]. These challenges are foregrounded with marginalised populations, where the researchers, often ‘external’ to the contexts they study or operate in, can struggle to understand complex ecosystems, leading to false assumptions and unhelpful interventions that can have negative consequences, leaving participants feeling powerless [ 75 , 80 , 90 ]. Building on this body of work, we ask how co-creating socio-technical responses with disabled citizens and their unpaid carers can sustainably help address some of their immediate needs, while fostering critical reflections on questions of governance, trust and responsibility in digital innovations and their design?

We report on a study that involved a collaboration with 23 disabled citizens, unpaid carers and a third sector care organisation in the United Kingdom (UK). The study aimed to build safe design spaces to enable a citizens-led co-design of a digital intervention that leveraged the whole life skills of participants [ 37 , 81 , 88 , 90 ], while attending to questions of values, responsibility, and sustainability. In a context characterised by increasing individualisation and fragmentation of healthcare service provision due to neoliberalism [ 30 , 66 ], we describe how our participatory process leveraged the authors’ skills alongside the participants’ situated knowledge arising from, but not limited to, their care practices [ 35 ]. Employing novel configurations of participatory design methods and tools that were devised to support remote and asynchronous participation, given our participants’ unpredictable lives due to their priorities of care — the collaboration co-created a digital commons, “MyCareBudget” 4 , that responded to the challenges they face in managing personalised healthcare infrastructures. We show how our co-design methods and process enabled the collective identification of risks and consequences in a digital intervention's design and outcome, critical for a population with complex healthcare needs [ 15 , 57 , 67 ]. Anticipating ethical implications, complexities, and costs associated with the long-term maintenance of novel computer systems, we describe how the co-creation of a digital commons [ 76 ] facilitated consideration of democratic questions of sustainability, governance, risks and responsibility in digital interventions and community-driven activism [ 5 , 37 , 55 , 69 , 89 ]. This clarifying design, financial and administrative commitments and roles upfront in the co-design process.

Our study responds to Bødker and Kyng's call for “PD that matters” , where participants are positioned as research partners, and researchers work with them as activists to achieve meaningful and sustainable outcomes, and sustained democratic control over technology [ 13 ]. Sitting at the intersection of healthcare and social justice, our case study contributes to critical disability studies [ 14 ], health activism [ 68 , 84 ], and HCI concerned with developing sustainable interventions through digital commons [ 55 , 86 ] in the context of personal healthcare infrastructures. The work shows how participatory methods can configure effective co-creation partnerships and collective action with marginalised populations to seek social change [ 68 ] through the co-design of a sustainable digital commons that delivered mutual aid - used by over 5,000 people worldwide.

We contribute insights from an asynchronous and remote co-design process with a community of disabled citizens and unpaid carers, configured to enable participation and examine issues of trust, ownership, risk, and responsibility in the design of digital interventions. This study was conducted in the UK, however, our insights and methods are relevant to HCI researchers worldwide who study the design of digital infrastructures with and for disabled citizens and their carers.

2 RELATED WORK

Building healthcare infrastructures with and for unpaid carers is a growing area of interest in HCI research. This is partly due to the increasing number of unpaid carers across the global north [ 74 ], who are engaged in the mentally, emotionally and physically demanding work of caregiving, with limited resources for support [ 16 , 17 ]. Indeed, studies over the last decade have examined the work and challenges of providing care in a home setting for those with complex long-term health conditions [ 9 , 16 , 28 , 51 , 74 ]. Across these studies, citizens were found to be operating within a siloed set of healthcare service providers offering little support in managing care, each citizen forced to assemble their individual care infrastructures [ 17 , 30 , 31 , 71 ]. The work required of citizens to connect, configure, and navigate infrastructures and relational practices between the forms of care provided by hospitals, clinicians, paid carers, pharmacies, support agencies, financial organisations, families, and friends, is essential — yet unpaid and invisible [ 17 ]. In response, HCI has offered differing approaches to alleviating and ameliorating the lives of disabled citizens and their unpaid carers as they build personal healthcare infrastructures. For example, Chen et al. [ 16 ] suggested how integrating technology responses could improve carers’ productivity vis-a-vis their invisible and high workloads, and related stresses. Yet, Gui et al. [ 31 ] called to expand the scope of design for this population, identifying the navigation of healthcare infrastructures as a critical part of their care work. Gui et al. further urged the HCI community to consider how the design of online platforms might benefit unpaid carers by supporting knowledge sharing and empowerment. Building on these studies, Schurgin et al. [ 74 ] found that access to resources was critical to their infrastructuring work, asking HCI to consider the complex roles and relationships of the unpaid carer. Echoing this appeal, Kaziunas et al. [ 44 ] problematised tendencies to techno-solutionism when designing for and with this marginalised population, asking HCI to engage with the complexities of social and political contexts of healthcare systems.

Movements such as disability justice [ 14 ] and health activism [ 68 , 84 ] in HCI, have advocated for the need to conduct research that foregrounds participants’ lived experience, repositioning them as active co-creators of research [ 6 , 7 , 37 , 43 , 73 ], best encapsulated by the statement, “Nothing About Us, Without Us” [ 14 , 75 , 90 ]. Challenging narrow understandings of disabled communities as a population with ‘deficiencies’ and needs [ 4 , 56 , 81 , 90 ], this body of research calls for collaborative research endeavours to recognise disabled citizens’ expertise as extending far beyond that of, for example, their care practice [ 8 , 52 , 88 ]. However, collaborative design and collective action with marginalised and underserved populations present challenges for both HCI researchers and citizens. The citizens’ participation in face-to-face workshops and other group activities is inhibited by both social barriers that force their isolation, and the unpredictability of their lives due to care responsibilities [ 28 , 30 , 68 ]. Opening up spaces for participatory engagements between marginalised populations and their authorities, also demands careful consideration, as existing power dynamics between these groups can inhibit input from the citizen who fears reprisal when criticising services [ 2 , 19 , 28 ].

The domain of citizen healthcare brings with it heightened ethical responsibility when considering the “enormous challenge” [ 39 ] of developing interventions that are politically relevant and sustainably beneficial in the long term. Participatory processes and in particular PD are seen to play an important role here, as they promote political engagement, ownership, mutual learning, and development of participants’ skills [ 32 , 53 , 70 , 78 , 85 ]. In particular, the recent shift in PD from a focus on the development of products, to the processes that foster the infrastructuring of publics around matters of concerns [ 21 , 23 ], is seen as an attempt to renew PD's original democratic and political aims of supporting power redistribution and conflict negotiation - these challenging hegemonic forces in society. This work often favouring social innovation over technology innovation [ 6 , 7 , 21 , 87 ]. More recently, Bødker and Kyng [ 13 ] call for a “new PD” , advocating for collaborations between researchers and publics that enact equitable allocation of power and resources, moving PD to a more political stance.

In this regard, emergent research in PD and HCI has looked at the potential of peer-to-peer production such as digital commons as a response to these issues, as well as questions of sustainability [ 32 , 34 , 53 , 76 , 82 , 85 ]. Digital commons - common-pool resources (CPR) that share artefacts for communal appropriation - are seen as a potential response to issues of responsibilities tied to the required skills and resources needed to develop and maintain digital innovations [ 76 ]. This is because processes of co-creating and sustaining digital commons, termed ‘commoning’ [ 50 , 55 ], embed upfront in the design process questions of governance that promote sustainability. Digital commons are therefore understood as a collective endeavour based on democracy, not hierarchies, serving as a hub for the distribution of social capital [ 41 , 86 ]. A critical aspect of commoning is that it is not simply about the co-design of the technology itself but also about responding to PD's original democratic aims, through nurturing processes of considering how technology is to be governed in the short and long run [ 37 ]. This includes the collective identification and ownership of risks and co-responsibilities between researchers and participants [ 40 ], these shifting in scope and ownership as the design progresses from the conceptual to an operating intervention [ 49 ].

While commoning work is arguably emergent in HCI, fewer studies have explored the benefits that such processes can deliver in community healthcare settings. The co-creation of digital commons offers the potential for guiding PD practices and collaborative processes that embrace the need for research partnerships with marginalised populations, while attending to questions of sustainability and responsible innovation that benefit those they are meant to support. In what follows, we report on a study that explored a collaboration with a public of disabled citizens and unpaid carers, leveraging the remote co-creation of a digital commons that supported their workload of accessing the benefits of self-directed care budgets, extending past design and into a sustainable operation.

3 CASE STUDY: PERSONALISED CARE OF LONG-TERM COMPLEX HEALTH NEEDS IN THE UK

Our case study unfolded in the UK, where significant changes in healthcare services for disabled citizens who require 24/7 care for their long-term complex health needs, such as profound learning disability, cerebral palsy, genetic disorders, and multiple sclerosis, have taken place over the last ten years. Until recently, disabled citizens’ care was delivered by care agencies funded and commissioned by local healthcare authorities. This, however, resulted in a transactional ‘one size fits all’ model of service provision, which had significant shortcomings for disabled citizens and their unpaid carers, evidenced by a lack of choice, control and independent living [ 24 , 58 ].

Recognising these limitations and the demand to better respond to the heterogeneous needs of disabled populations, the UK National Health Service (NHS) has reformed its healthcare system by adopting what is known as a ‘personalised care’ model and paradigm [ 42 ]. This model, also adopted in other countries in the global north, seeks to enable citizens’ choice and control in their lives of care [ 24 ], where those with complex health needs can arrange their own care through ‘self-directed care budgets’. These are known as Personal Health Budgets (PHBs) in the UK, where the NHS has forecasted 200,000 PHBs will be active by 2024 [ 60 ]. The budgets can be handed directly to the disabled citizen or their unpaid carer, often a family member, providing them with agency over their care services. For example, allowing them to recruit paid carers that align with the budget holder's values, personal preferences, and life choices [ 24 ].

Yet, while the underlying concept of PHBs is well-meaning and motivated, prior research has shown how PHB holders (disabled citizens or their unpaid carers) may not achieve the autonomy they seek if the state fails to support them with the responsibilities of management and legal liabilities [ 47 , 66 ]. Similar issues have been found in other countries, where in the name of personalisation, disabled citizens and their unpaid carers are left without support to manage their caregiving duties [ 16 , 17 , 26 ].

4 APPROACH

4.1 Background

The study we report on is part of an extended longitudinal research project that began in 2019. The study was aimed at engaging disabled citizens and unpaid carers to explore the challenges that PHB holders were facing and the role of HCI in this space. The initial and primary motivation for conducting the study project stemmed from the lead author's lived experience in assisting with the management of a PHB for a family member, and informal discussions with other PHB holders. We adopted PD as an approach that incorporates Action Research (AR) methodologies [ 1 , p.277] and that seeks to address issues that participants deem significant, with researchers acting as activists on their behalf, while acknowledging the complexity of social issues, conflict, and power relations in any design process, as described by Bødker and Kyng [ 13 ]. AR is characterised by open-ended and iterative ‘Plan-Act-Reflect’ cycles, where researchers and participants co-devise and deliver interventions or actions in response to a particular context [ 38 ]. Then, reflect on the results of the intervention, and based on these, devise a new action within a subsequent cycle [ 45 ], aiming towards sustainable change [ 85 ].

We next provide an overview of our first AR cycle, followed by AR cycle 2 which this paper reports on, where we describe its aims, objectives, and co-design. A third, future, AR cycle, will aim to evaluate the digital commons. See Figure 1 .

4.2 AR Cycle 1

Spanning a period of over 18 months, our first AR cycle involved the lead author planning and then recruiting participants - enlisting disabled citizens, unpaid carers, a third sector care organisation, and state healthcare officers.

The ‘Act’ stage of this first cycle invited this group to take part in research as active participants within a series of online/offline participatory research engagements. These aimed at uncovering the barriers and challenges they faced when accessing and managing PHBs, and fostering discussion and reflection through the collaborative analysis of the data gathered. A set of guiding questions were prepared for one-to-one semi-structured interviews, including, “Can you talk me through the process of gaining and running your PHB”, “What do you need to do to run your PHB?”, “Where did you go to get information or support?” .

The research surfaced complex issues related to power relations at play. Citizens viewed their officers with fear, reporting their care funding being withdrawn without notice; whilst the healthcare officers believed their actions were justified given their responsibilities for public funds. The participatory research also surfaced how PHB holders, while achieving a degree of choice and control over their care, effectively become unpaid ‘managers’ of a residential care home, carrying all the associated burdens and responsibilities, but receiving minimal training or support from their healthcare authorities, impacting on their ability to realise benefits from their PHBs [ 28 ].

4.3 Aims and Objectives of AR Cycle 2

Our AR cycle 1 generated collective understandings and reflection, feeding into AR cycle 2, which is at the centre of this paper. Here we respond to the first AR cycle by continuing to work with the citizens and the care organisation to co-create digital responses to the critical issues they reported.

A key objective was to ensure equity in the engagement process, such that all participants would have opportunities to participate fully in shaping ideas and potential responses. This was challenging as the citizens were time-poor due to their care responsibilities and disabilities, experienced unpredictable lives due to their priorities of care, and were distributed throughout the UK. A second objective was for the process to leverage the personal motivation of participants to co-create a response they deemed worthwhile for themselves and their peers, this requiring their long-term engagement. A final objective was to foster spaces where participants would feel safe to freely express criticisms of their state's services and of each other's views, if they wished to, and to promote their individual opinions, leveraging their implicit and explicit situated knowledge including, but not limited to, their care practice [ 8 , 37 , 88 ].

4.4 Recruitment

Our AR cycle 1 and its participatory research generated significant enthusiasm for those involved, who found meaning in the collective process of sharing and understanding common challenges, finding empowerment and peer support, as much as engaging in the research [ 28 ]. Because of this, participants wanted to continue to be involved as they were motivated to ‘do something’ about the common issues that emerged from the participatory research. Therefore, all the citizen and third sector participants involved in AR cycle 1 remained involved with the research and this study's AR cycle 2.

4.5 Ethics

The authors’ university ethics process was used to gain ethical approval at the outset of this study. No children or other vulnerable groups took part in this study, easing the approval. Microsoft Forms were used as an online Consent Form, with Participant Information Sheets either emailed as PDFs or hosted on the digital commons.

4.6 Positionality

As stated, the lead author's motivation to conduct the research was grounded in direct experience. Hence, his positionality played a role in the recruitment of participants and the building of rapport and understanding in engagements, as he was situated in the context characterising the participants’ lives of care [ 37 , 46 , 80 ]. The lead author engaged in reflexive discussions with the second author throughout the longitudinal study, in order to engage critically with his subjectivity and positionality in the research. This acknowledging the ‘entanglement’ of a researcher with their communities [ 5 , 22 ], subjectivity in this study viewed as a positive knowledge resource that is “partial, situated and contextual” [ 11 ].

4.7 Configuring Co-design

The objectives described above directed the lead author to configure the participatory design process in ways that would be sensitive to the contextual realities of participants, constrained by the priority of their care, this forming the ‘Plan’ stage of this second AR cycle. Participants found it difficult to travel, either hindered by societal hurdles to disability, or the need to schedule specialist carers for their disabled children, and so could not commit to group session start times due to their care obligations. Therefore, in line with ‘respectful design’ as discussed by Rajapakse et al. [ 72 ], we considered ways to maximise inclusivity and respect through non-traditional engagement methods and tools that supported participation.

Our co-design process demanded different tools to enable the differing forms of engagement. The lead author created a set of criteria that allowed for: a range of technical literacy; smartphone-only usage, as some participants lacked laptops and tablets; no large downloads as older smartphones may not have free capacity and to reduce costs of mobile data; and re-use of tools for subsequent research. We noted that disabled participants could already access technology using specialist tools. Open-source tools were sought, but none matched the criteria, hence a fallback of leveraging proprietary tools that were available free of use, either by restricting usage to a free plan or by leaning on their corporate responsibility to provide free access. ‘Collabito’ ( www.collabito.com ) was used for the text-based engagements, ‘ProvenByUsers’ ( www.provenbyusers.com ) for the drag-and-drop sessions, and InVision ( www.invisionapp.com ) for building prototypes.

Early in the process, participants raised concerns over the sustainability of any interventions they may co-create, with participants suggesting the creation of a “wiki” , a digital common-pool resource (CPR), or the use of available, easy-to-use technologies. Therefore, we considered questions of ethics, accountability, and sustainability as part of the co-design process. Specifically, we sought to incorporate in our process Ostrom's design principles for the sustainable governance of a commons [ 64 ], Bettaga et al.’s [ 5 ] tactics that support digital communing, and responses to Taylor et al.’s [ 85 ] challenges to sustainability.

4.8.1 Item 1 – Issues and Responses: Participants were invited to a focus group that extended over 14 days. In the first session of this focus group, participants were presented with ‘scenarios of care’ that the lead author created using data from our AR cycle 1. Over the 14-day period, the lead author used data from the ongoing discussions to create new scenarios of care and in this way fostered further discussions and helped shape ideas. The new scenarios included participants’ quotes from discussions, such as, “I can get advice, what I need is stuff done” and “If I did give this work to someone else, they would have to go through one hell of an education programme before they're able to do it.” Conversations pointed to a common need of PHB holders for what we term ‘care artefacts’ - documents used to manage the PHB and the paid carers, for example, staff management, payroll, health and safety, and duties of care (see Figure 2 ); the majority comprised of one-page documents (see Figure 3 ), but others, including care plans and staff contracts, extended to 20 pages. The challenge here was a lack of provision from their healthcare authorities, the third sector, commercial organisations, or availability on the internet - requiring each PHB holder to determine the need for each care artefact and then create them without support.

An anonymous survey followed this session to capture participants’ reflections on the engagement method.

4.8.2 Item 2 - Requirements Elicitation: This focus group was constructed around a care scenario in which participants were encouraged to reflect on when they first gained their PHB and how an online resource could have most effectively supported them. At this time, a decision was made regarding a name for the digital commons, the lead author suggesting “MyCareBudget” , which was agreed by the participants.

4.8.3 Item 3 – Categorising Care Artefacts: The previous design sessions identified 70 care artefacts with 19 associated categories, necessitating this design session to determine participants’ categorisation preferences. To accomplish this, an online drag-and-drop activity was developed in which participants individually selected each care artefact and dragged it to a category they considered it most closely matched.

4.8.4 Item 4 - Rapid Prototyping: A clickable, low-fidelity, text-based prototype was created and then discussed in a focus group, with the comments received leading to the building of a graphics-based prototype used as further input to the focus group.

4.8.5 Item 5 - Gathering of Care Artefacts: All the participants were requested by email to forward the care artefacts they used, regardless of their form – digital, or photographs of handwritten documents. Assurance was given that they would be anonymised.

4.8.6 Item 6 - Commons Governance: The purpose of this session was to explore governance, with the lead author prompting participants to discuss issues and mechanisms including conflict resolution, monitoring, and end-users’ rights. This being a new and abstract subject to the participants, they were sensitised using two drag-and-drop activities in which participants worked alone. The first activity sought to capture end-user roles, entitled “What can users do?” , where participants were given end-user actions including ‘Delete pages’, ‘Add comments’, ‘Download’, to be dragged and dropped over the types of end-users: guests, registered end-users, and administrators. The second activity purposed to elicit reflection on the graduated sanctions [ 64 ] that should be given to end-users when they use MyCareBudget inappropriately or in error, this entitled “Crime and Punishment” . Participants were presented with possible end-user actions, including “Posting violent or sexual images”, “Continued bad behaviour after a warning”, “Spelling errors” and so on – these to be dragged over the sanctions of “Leave as is”, “No penalty but an administrator is to edit”, “Warn user but no penalty” , and “Block user” . Following the two activities, a focus group discussion took place with the lead author prompting participants to discuss issues and processes of governance, and to consider promotion and maintenance beyond the research process.

4.8.7 Item 7 - Pre-Deployment Checks: A focus group was hosted with a question that asked, “ You have had a look around the website. What do you think? Let me know all your negative points please.” This question specifically asked for their negative comments, as by now there was a sense of community in the group, so there was a need to guard against participants being unwilling to criticise the work of one another.

4.8.8 Item 8 - Promotion: Twitter was selected to promote MyCareBudget as there were hashtags linked to personalised care, unpaid carers, and healthcare authorities. A Twitter account was established for the project, where the lead author posted tweets highlighting the capabilities of MyCareBudget, with participants then promoting MyCareBudget following a single prompt. Currently (January 2023), MyCareBudget has been accessed by over 5,000 people in its first 16 months, now gaining over 200 people per month, 80% from the UK and the remainder worldwide.

4.9 Data Analysis

The 354 textual comments from all the group discussions, outputs from the drag-and-drop activities, the survey, and the care artefacts, were analysed by the lead author using reflexive Thematic Analysis [ 10 ]. This method was selected as the research questions were aimed at actionable outcomes, data was sourced from differing engagement methods, and the sample size was more than ten [ 12 ]. The comments were loaded into NVIVO data analysis software ( www.qsrinternational.com ), and following a first read-through by the lead author, an initial set of codes was created using open coding. This first set was validated by rereading the comments until no more codes were created.

This open coding resulted in a sizable collection of codes that the lead author organised into a multi-level hierarchy - the top-level nodes generating a set of themes. Of all these themes, the ones that best responded to this paper's research question were selected: the issues within the practice of managing a PHB; responses to these issues; unintended consequences; and facets of the co-design process. This initial set of themes was iteratively refined, defined, and validated with the second author until saturation and agreement were reached.

4.10 Overview of MyCareBudget

MyCareBudget is a digital commons of resources that resembles Wikipedia ( www.wikipedia.org ) in presentation, menus, and navigation, as well as compatibility with smartphones, tablets, laptops, and desktops. A difference in use is that Wikipedia's digital articles are typically found by entering search parameters, whereas the digital resources on MyCareBudget are discovered by browsing, as a newcomer to PHBs is unlikely to know the care artefacts they require. The MyCareBudget homepage reflects this by providing two browsing options: lists of care artefacts for more experienced PHB holders; and a guided tour for newcomers. After selecting a care artefact, it can be downloaded as a Microsoft Word document, an Adobe PDF, or a link to Google Docs. 4.11 Technical Architecture

The sustainability of the intervention was the primary concern also in regard to technical choices. One concern pertained to the hosting of servers and domain names. Whilst the authors’ university would have provided these by default, the transference to external service providers at the end of the research would have incurred a loss of reputation gained, due to the forced change in the domain name. Hence the lead author used external organisations from the outset. In terms of software used to build and host the website - open-source was purposedly selected [ 5 , 62 ]. MediaWiki ( www.mediawiki.org ) was chosen due to its use for Wikipedia and thousands of other digital commons, with its large support network and corpus of literature. Virtual servers were selected as they can be readily scaled to match traffic demands, this furthering sustainability.

The development and operational roles for technical, administrative and support functions during the research were owned by the lead author, as although at least one participant had the skillset required, the tasks would have been a significant demand on their time. Ownership of these tasks following the research was discussed within the commons governance engagement (see Item 6 above).

5 INSIGHTS

Our insights describe: (i) Personalised Needs and Mutual Aid exploring how participants navigated the challenges of personalisation in PHBs and deliberated on how they could support their peers; (ii) Delivering Trustworthy Content exploring participants’ considerations of issues of trust for an online resource of care artefacts; (iii) Taking Responsibility describing participants’ concerns on how their innovation could impact their peers, its potential misuse, and sustainability; (iv) Benefits of Participation reports on participants’ perspectives on the benefits of our co-design process. Pseudonyms are used throughout.

5.1 Personalised Needs and Mutual Aid

At the start of our co-design process, all participants felt free to voice how they had been tasked by their healthcare authorities to recruit and manage a care team, but without training, documentation, or advice. To fill this gap, the majority of participants wished they had received support from their peers, ideally through one-to-one conversations with a peer mentor. Orla, a third sector company director who had sustained a spinal injury in her early twenties, lamented the lack of access to a mentor, “It would have made a big difference to me to have had anyone else [to] speak to who had a PHB... My [healthcare authority] didn't offer to put me in touch with anyone.”

Yet, in discussions, they each considered such a possibility as unattainable, so they turned to the internet to seek the ‘care artefacts’ they needed to perform this management. Kayla, a national disability advocate who has a muscle disease, realised that her peers possessed the knowledge on PHBs, the issue was how to access it, “The knowledge is out there, it's just getting it to the right people in the right way.” Several participants explained how they searched for, but failed to find care artefacts, which forced them to create these themselves, Kayla continuing with “... I was kind of hoping that I'd be able to find [care artefacts] on the internet... and that didn't really exist.”

In this way, a few of the participants began to envisage an online resource for sharing their care artefacts, the first design session acting as a creative space for participants to imagine responses to their issues with PHBs. A discussion that began with a vague idea for a technology that could hold their working documents, moved on to describe what this technology would need to contain, how it could be accessed, and its functionalities. Amelia, a healthcare professional and parent of a profoundly disabled young adult, was the first to raise the concept, saying, “For me, there should be a piece of technology or a resource somehow, that has all the bits that you need... when you're setting up [a PHB].” Maria, mother to a child with 24/7 physical care needs, put forward that the care artefacts would need to be downloaded to a local device and any changes broadcast, “...downloaded from a regularly updated website, with update alerts to anyone registered.”

Maria's seemingly unremarkable statement triggered a discussion regarding the meaning of taking collective action to provide care artefacts that needed to be individualised for each person – something they considered to be an apparent contradiction. For example, Amelia considered that, “... [care artefacts are] to be adapted for your own needs.” This discussion took place over several days, the engagement design allowing participants time to reflect and respond. Here, participants envisaged their artefacts could be useful as a starting point for their peers, as something they could customise. They considered how multiple versions of a single type of care artefact could be offered online, showing the variety of ways different people had personalised them. Julie, who has used a wheelchair for over a decade, saw the need for different versions of a care artefact, “I think multiple examples [of each artefact] with the pros and cons of each.” This call for diverse care artefacts was evidenced in the care artefacts sourced from participants, where 13 types of artefacts had up to four versions that differed in style and content. For example, an ‘Accident Log’ varied from documents that asked for a wealth of detail for each accident event, to others that recorded a simple list of events.

Thus, in the process of working out how they could respond to the challenges they and their peers face when running a PHB, considering the practicalities of what it should contain, and how it should be delivered, participants reconsidered the value of sharing multiple versions of care artefacts for people to draw from. Concurrently they reconsidered the value of multiple versions in which care can be performed, and the need to foreground that. Deliberating ideas and options, participants discerned between personalised care and ‘individualised’ need. In the process, catering for multiplicity didn't seem much of a contradiction but rather a value underpinning their potential design response and collective action.

5.2 Delivering Trustworthy Content

In the co-design process, initial conversations included considerations and discussions over the viability of a potential resource, issues of trust related to the hosting platform, and sources of care artefacts, as well as moderation and presentation of advice. Collating and disseminating ‘trustworthy’ content appeared to be a critical aspect of this work, given the potential consequences that ‘harmful’ content could have on the care practices of disabled citizens and their unpaid carers.

In early discussions, social media was dismissed immediately as a platform through which they could disseminate advice. Participants considered that social media would have demanded significant work to validate content for ‘trustworthiness’. It was seen as a poor choice as a hosting platform for care artefacts, viewed as a transient medium where documents appeared and disappeared, could only be shared in read-only form, and overall was not considered ‘objective’. For example, Jasmin, mother of a young adult demanding 24/7 care, stated, “I haven't got enough time to deal with social media... seen as what's posted isn't fact/law”.

Several participants voiced that the artefacts needed to be hosted on an online platform where documents could be stored permanently. This in their views, would provide their peers with opportunities to participate in discussions as well as edit, correct and improve artefacts, and, in this way, add value and trust. Colin, a former project manager and now a disability advocate with multiple sclerosis, suggested, “Allow templates to be changeable or added to” , and Amelia “... it would be nice if [people] could interact, edit or make suggestions.” In this way, participants began to see their work as a kind of ‘living archive’ of common-pool resources.

Participants also discussed where the care artefacts should come from. They considered their peers as a more trustworthy source of information compared to their local health authorities or other bodies. For example, individuals that would be considered ‘experts’ in this area, including state healthcare authorities, were dismissed due to their lack of accurate, situated knowledge on setting up care infrastructures at home with a PHB. This point being an example of where the citizens’ voices would likely have been inhibited if their health authorities had been present in the design engagements. Instead, those with lived experience of running a PHB were valued as trustworthy. Margot, a healthcare professional and parent of a severely disabled child, said, “Families are the best resource. Having and managing a small budget [in an organisation] is very different to employing staff 24/7 [in your home]. There is a vast amount of information that professionals do not know.”

Having settled on who should contribute care artefacts for public benefits, participants moved on to consider questions of how to maintain trust in such a ‘living archive’. This led to considerations on the role of moderation of content and input - the ‘commons governance’ drag-and-drop activity designed to achieve just this. It appeared clear that all participants wanted to protect MyCareBudget from potentially harmful content such as online abuse that they had witnessed on the internet, believing this would demand significant time. Collectively, they decided that those who spammed or posted hate messages were to be permanently blocked as they were viewed as vandals. However, forgiveness was granted to those authoring content that included swearing or venting anger at their healthcare service providers. The participants perhaps recognised this frustration in their own lives or wanted the online space they were creating to also cater for opportunities to discuss broader issues in current healthcare provision. In any case, most participants placed little trust in internet users as a population. For example, Amelia suggested that “If you did nothing, I think the usual internet idiots would wreck it.” Sandra, a third sector director, agreed, seeing moderation as a need, “I do think that having a moderator to monitor content and a way to check anything new before it is published is going to be necessary.”

As to who would perform the moderation, there was agreement that moderators should be their peers, as they would possess the knowledge to verify content. This was also seen as a way of building a community of peer moderators around the care artefacts. Here, Sandra continued with, “[Moderators] need to have good knowledge of Personal Health Budgets, preferably by lived experience of having one.” Erin, mother to a profoundly disabled teenager needing 24/7 care, adding, “Having a democratic moderator system where fellow moderators engage with each other.”

The rapid prototyping design session offered the participants contrasting choices over the look and feel of their website: a simplistic text-based version versus a graphics-based version - the differing styles promoting a discussion around ‘trust’. The graphics-based version was viewed as professional-looking, this denoted commercialisation to participants, implying a financial aim underpinning the website, rather one of care. However, they also discussed that the website should not appear too amateurish, as in the text-based version, as that suggested the website had been built lacking care or knowledge, and hence lose trust. Gabrielle, who parented a disabled adult, expressed a need for a balance between care, professionalism, and commercialism, “It's got to be slick without feeling commercial.”

Thus, during design sessions, participants explored who or what can be considered trustworthy in terms of resources and moderation, including the role that aesthetics can play in delivering the ‘right’ message to their communities of peers.

5.3 Taking Responsibility

Co-creating MyCareBudget offered opportunities for participants to exercise their agency. This in turn generated significant discussions around the responsibilities and risks that come with ‘publicising’ and sharing their care-artefacts online, as well as creating and making visible peer-to-peer communities around PHBs.

Participants perceived MyCareBudget as exposing their invisible work to their healthcare authorities, seen as risky to themselves and their peers. For example, they were concerned that ‘showing’ how they manage their PHBs (for example, how they deal with legal and safeguarding needs or personal choices around staff contracts and safety assessments) through the sharing of their care artefacts, could offer opportunities for their healthcare authorities to judge their work. The fear here was that their healthcare authorities might eventually demand PHB holders chronicle all aspects of their care, not only imposing institutional-like procedures onto their private homes but exposing the citizens’ private lives to surveillance. Penelope, who was a wheelchair user of 19 years, stated, “ There is the right to not have extra paperwork. To not turn your home into an institution... I think the greatest risk is that we create some kind of panopticon in our own homes.”

All participants felt that their PHBs had given them choice and control in managing their care, and they did not want to risk relinquishing this back to their authorities. This was broadly connected to a lack of understanding and dialogue between state healthcare organisations and PHB holders, as well as power asymmetries at play. This is best encapsulated in Sandra's statement “... big organisations suck power back.” However, as discussions unfolded, it appeared that the benefits that MyCareBudget could deliver would offset any potential risk, with participants consciously accepting and embracing the responsibility of creating an online repository. Sandra concluded with, “... it feels like a helpful, informative, useful website is going to outweigh downsides”.

Questions and concerns regarding the sustainability of MyCareBudget were at the forefront of our co-creation process. The commons governance session was designed as a focal point for discussions on this topic and as part of this, the participants believed that active governance would be essential to achieve sustainability. Even prior to this session, the participants voiced concern about how the care artefacts could be kept up to date with changes such as in employment legislation, Colin stated, “... things change all the time. [MyCareBudget] needs to be kept updated. " Looking ahead, they wondered whether the website could be monetised to ensure MyCareBudget longevity, though they questioned whether this would be desirable. Here, Paula, mother to her profoundly disabled son, stated, “I think there should be some kind of money involved, [governance] could easily be a full-time job and it seems unfair that disabled people are expected to educate and support without compensation.” Colin disagreed, “For me it's giving back to the community, so that we all benefit.”

As a response to this discussion around sustainability, the two directors of the third sector organisation who participated in this study offered to both fund and administer MyCareBudget once the research funding ended, seeing the benefits and authority of MyCareBudget arising from it being designed by PHB holders. Orla, one of the directors, stressed its value as a community-led initiative “And we feel that it started off as a user-led project [so it] should stay a user-led project.”

These conversations demonstrated that the participants created MyCareBudget as a political act that would support campaigns arguing for more support from their state healthcare organisation. Also as an expression of their lives of care (and related challenges), extending outwards to their unknown peers in a philanthropic sense, owning the risks that their actions would incur.

5.4 Benefits of Participation

During the commons governance activity, participants reflected on their experiences of co-creating MyCareBudget, unprompted. In these discussions, they considered how the co-design process enabled them to create a community of practice, feeling the process also helped them express and take an activist stance to disseminate knowledge, with a sense of excitement and ownership developing as ideas started to take shape.

Several participants found pleasure in co-creating something that would support those who may consider getting a PHB, Amelia saying, “It just feels like a good thing to do, to help make it easier for people coming after you.” Kayla considered how the co-design process enriched her and the other participants beyond creating something that might benefit society - her social capital had developed in taking part, also seeing this in other participants, saying, “This meets our need to feel productive in society and to expand our knowledge and skills. It has therefore developed us as people.” Several participants placed themselves back to when they started with a PHB, wishing they had access to MyCareBudget, Margot saying, “I love [MyCareBudget]. It would have been so helpful to start with.” Beyond this, several participants stated that reading how other participants managed their PHBs, helped them improve their own management, and articulate and make sense of their own implicit knowledge. Here, Erin reflected on how the process was “... also a way of exercising your brain and knowledge which may otherwise be inaccessible. In the case of this PHB project, I feel I'm learning as I contribute.”

A community of action appeared to form early in the co-design process, surfacing a sense of empowerment as the participants were collectively gaining confidence and developing a socio-digital innovation that could support their peers. Several participants felt strongly about the value of what they were doing as a political act. In this sense, the exclusion of state authorities or officials from the process was considered important, as it enabled their autonomy and reduced dependency on healthcare authorities. In Sandra's words, “... [we are] part of a collective movement, to be more empowered, and less at the mercy of statutory systems.” Many concurred with Sandra's statement. Jez, an international disability advocate who had sustained a spinal injury in his late teens, also expressed how the process helped them seek and create meaning, purpose and belief in something beyond their own selves, “Being a part of something bigger than [our]selves and wanting to help others go through a slightly better/easier experience than [we] may have gone through.”

An anonymous survey took place at the end of the first focus group to elicit responses on the tools and methods, with all participants welcoming the asynchronous and remote co-design, and appreciating the text-only interactions. Respondent#1 saying, “I found it an enjoyable experience and probably easier than a face-to-face or Zoom focus group where technical hitches can occur and where commitments at home can mess up the best-laid plans.” Respondent#2 preferred our non-video method with people they did not know, as it avoided social awkwardness, “With a group that you have never met, I would have been less keen [on video conferencing].”

Our design process and dialogical approach appeared to have enabled the above benefits. In the co-design process, participants felt they could safely criticise both the healthcare services they had received and the comments from their fellow participants, feeling able to share insights about their lived experiences of care and professional careers.

6 DISCUSSION

Drawing from our insights and learning gained from the design and delivery of our participatory process, we discuss implications for HCI. We examine the role of HCI in redistributing access to the resources required to support personal healthcare infrastructuring, how co-creating a digital commons can promote responsible digital citizenship, and recommendations to HCI when configuring co-creation with marginalised communities.

6.1 Redistributing Access to Healthcare through Infrastructuring

During our co-design process, we learnt about the importance of personalised healthcare that can cater for diverse abilities, lifestyles, values, contexts and needs. Given people's challenges and efforts to connect a siloed set of healthcare service providers, incurring costs of time and stress [ 30 , 31 ], the co-design process and resulting digital commons illustrated two forms of infrastructuring. Firstly, the digital commons and its care artefacts supported future PHB holders to realise the benefits of PHBs, these arising from choice and control in their lives of care [ 24 ]. Secondly, the design process enabled the bringing together of a group of citizens to form a ‘public of action’ around common issues – much in line with the infrastructuring discussed by Le Dantec and DiSalvo [ 21 ] and others [ 20 , 89 ]. Yet our co-design process was less about the “discovery of unknown issues” [ 21 ] – rather it served to create spaces where issues which were felt as an individualised struggle could be safely voiced, to enable the surfacing and discovery of common, collective issues, which our participants and lead author wanted to redress as mutual aid. As Spade described [ 78 ], mutual aid exposes gaps and failings in the system, illustrating how personal health budgets within the UK neoliberal healthcare system have materialised into an ‘individualised’ matter, where responsibility for care work is offloaded onto the individual, becoming invisible, with the state providing only minimal support.

Previous work on infrastructuring has shown the benefits and tensions of supporting socio-material relations and collaborative dialogue between civics, public institutions, and authorities [ 61 , 65 ]. In this study, local health authorities were excluded due to the worries voiced by our participants – these insights developed in our previous AR cycle that highlighted unpaid carers’ fears of retaliation for speaking up, with the risk of losing their care funding [ 28 ]. Further, participants sharing their care-artefacts and hence making their work visible to healthcare authorities was a source of worries and anxiety for our participants. These tensions are not uncommon – for example, even when risks are less personal as in highway maintenance, Harding et al. [ 36 ] reported how citizens felt fear and vulnerability when providing information to their civic authorities.

The authorities’ exclusion from this study was both productive and a missed opportunity. Productive, as it enabled us to create safe design spaces for our participants to connect to one another and gain social capital, expertise (evidenced by the way they spoke about the process), and ultimately co-create a digital resource that over 5,000 people around the world currently use. A missed opportunity, as the officers’ presence may have invigorated dialogical spaces – in the way that other research within HCI and beyond have described [ 2 , 61 , 65 ] — where co-creative dialogue between civics and authorities can end “cycles of hostility” through improved communication [ 65 ]; it can also help sensitise frontline civic officers to the needs and desires of citizens [ 61 ]. Yet, opening discursive spaces that can lessen the ‘distance’ between citizens and institutions brings significant risks arising from the uneven power dynamics. Björgvinsson characterised this risk as “research responsibilities” [ 7 ] that includes researchers’ abilities to foster agonistic public spaces but also to protect the people within these spaces.

We believe that creating safe design spaces for our participants ultimately delivered the greater benefit in our context, as our participants were empowered by the possibility of helping their future peers. MyCareBudget enacted a start of a societal reconfiguration – in the manner that Le Dantec and DiSalvo discuss [ 21 ]. However, this benefit was at the expense of broader systemic service transformation, in that MyCareBudget addressed immediate needs, rather than more profound systemic issues within the broader landscape of neoliberal care service provision in the global north. This points to the challenges of fostering broader systemic change, more so where priorities in healthcare are driven by profit rather than public well-being and public value. Paradoxically, by reducing dependence on an absent state, MyCareBudget could be at risk of justifying and reinforcing their absence (the status quo) [ 78 ]. Our hope is that the visibility of what the state is not providing will generate insights and accountability in this regard.

We have located this study in the UK, with its free access to healthcare, but see transferability based on the global healthcare infrastructuring work of HCI authors [ 16 , 17 ]. For example, Gui and Chen [ 30 ] described a similar neoliberalist offloading in the US, where the authors reported on the healthcare industry who “secretly move the burden of labor onto individual health consumers” .

Future work within HCI could explore how community-led innovation can respond to current injustices in healthcare provision and infrastructures, while being mindful of the complex power dynamics at play. Clearly, working towards social justice goals for disabled citizens demands the transformation of the institutions that deliver their services. As a response to this, the lead author is planning a future, fourth, AR cycle to work in collaboration with citizens, third sector organisations, and healthcare authorities. We see technology playing a role here, potentially allowing the citizen to be distant yet involved in conversations. The implications of this approach delivering insights into how HCI can support citizens to engage with their civic and state when adversarial relationships exist.

6.2 Nurturing Responsible Digital Citizenship through Co-creating Sustainable Digital Commons

In our co-design process, responsibilities for our socio-technical intervention (its design, deployment, publicity, and maintenance) were shared between researchers, the participants, and the third sector organisation collaborating with us. Through the collaboration, we came to appreciate the relationship between trust and responsibility — where trust manifested as less a matter of shared responsibility between parties, and more about a process of becoming together. In turn, responsibility emerged as a matter that is co-constructed and defined within a collective of interdependent individuals. Our participants have created a digital commons where they took responsibility for caring for one another and change political conditions by building relationships that were more ‘survivable’ [ 78 ].

In the process, responsibility shifted dynamically between researcher and participants - and on to their unknown peers who form the imagined future beneficiaries and contributors of MyCareBudget. Our participants took responsibility for sharing their multiple care artefacts but also recognised their unknown peers’ responsibilities to personalise them. This view on trust and responsibility extends Jansen et al.’s study [ 40 ] of healthcare responsibilities between patient, clinician and technology, where co-responsibility is discussed as more static and simply assigned to people; and Karasti's and Baker's work [ 43 ] that describes how participants take collective responsibility when their decisions impact on their communities.

Responsibility for the sustainability of the intervention was seen as critical by both researchers and participants from the conception of this study. Participants were concerned about who would update and maintain the resource. Researchers had an ethical imperative that any intervention delivering value to the lives of this marginalised and underserved population, must not be withdrawn as the research funding ends. HCI authors have written on the criticality of sustainability in our interventions, seeing the design methods to achieve this being fundamental to PD, deeming it insufficient to just ‘abandon’ technology in its context, no matter its worth [ 34 ]. Taylor et al. [ 85 ] also explored this issue, adamant that researchers own the responsibility to at least examine the potential for sustainability, the authors recognising challenges of resource, usage, and technology. In response to these challenges, we adopted a low-cost operational model, leveraged a digital commons for its openness to content generation, and used open-source software to promote stability. However, as stated by Manuel and Crivellaro [ 53 ] and Hamm et al. [ 32 ], the use of open-source software cannot imply that non-technical citizens have the ability to update it. The skill set demanded impedes accessibility to perform online edits, delimiting the ownership of such public technology by citizens and their communities. We join the call from Manuel and Crivellaro for HCI to develop open-source software that is more widely accessible to those with low IT skills.

We are conscious that opting for a more sustainable option for our participants came with trade-offs between social innovation against technical innovation, as discussed by Björgvinsson et al., Le Dantec and Carl DiSalvo, and Teli et al. [ 7 , 21 , 87 ]. Others have acknowledged digital commons often entail the same trade-off [ 35 , 44 ]. In HCI, Teli and colleagues [ 86 , 87 ] detail the value of co-creating commons as a collaborative production, or as described by Bødker and Kyng [ 13 ] - “democratic control of IT” . From our study, we see sustainability in social innovation outweighing that of technical innovation.

Further, as a digital commons, the intervention excludes by default those without internet access, the authors seeing value in face-to-face engagements once COVID-19 permits access to these populations who are at high risk of infection.

Our process was conceived to maximise our participants’ agency and sustainability - to avoid potentially harmful consequences that may come from delivering a short-lived digital innovation. We also witnessed the use of a digital commons as a novel way of distributing the resources of our participants and their peers, as a collective response to care for one another. This use of a digital commons allows for scaling out of this mutual aid to a wider population [ 33 , 54 ], whereby the care artefacts are intended to be changed to suit individuals whether they are funded by PHBs in the UK, or their equivalents in other nations - evidenced by the worldwide use of MyCareBudget.

6.3 Configuring Co-creation with Marginalised Communities

HCI has developed important methodological considerations when co-researching with marginalised populations [ 2 , 56 , 68 , 72 , 83 ]. Researchers have foregrounded how PD processes are usually predicated and configured with particular populations in mind, typically eloquent, affluent and from privileged backgrounds [ 37 ]. Thus, researchers have called for the inclusion of those less privileged, and more specifically, for closer collaborations with disabled populations [ 37 , 79 ].

Our co-design process was configured from a disability justice approach, to deliver access, inclusion, equity, and meaningful participation. This was achieved by configuring engagements from the outset, utilising the authors’ skills in research methods to leverage the participants’ whole-life expertise [ 3 , 88 ], whilst promoting their agency to ensure they could participate in developing responses [ 27 ]. Further, we adopted Bettega et al.’s [ 5 ] five tactics that support digital commoning: foster self-reflection in participants; understand and respond to needs; respond to participants as a group; problematise the standard toolset; and offer technologies suited to average capabilities.

A temporal configuration of our engagements meant that sessions spanned between 7 to 14 days and were conducted asynchronously. This allowed participants to participate at their convenience and without time constraints, over several days, allowing them to evaluate their own comments and those from others, carefully. Yet, the delivery of the process required significant efforts from the lead researcher, who felt responsible to attend all the extended sessions throughout the day and evening. Furthermore, the dialogical nature of the design of the sessions – whereby the lead researcher would analyse data from discussions to construct scenarios to promote further discussions in the following sessions, added further pressure. Future work would benefit from considering the advantages of a temporal configuration of engagements, however, this would have to be carefully considered to mitigate against participants losing focus, and stress on the researchers.

We considered how a spatial configuration of our engagements could mitigate barriers to participation including participants’ time-poor lives and their financial and mobility issues. We configured our engagement to be entirely remote, with input as typed or mouse-driven format and no video meetings. This appeared to be received positively by most participants – removing the social awkwardness that can arise in video calls. However, our overriding concerns regarding the participants’ time-poor lives meant we missed opportunities for synchronous, discursive sessions. Future research might consider a mixed approach of remote asynchronous sessions that allow people the time to reflect and consider their own and other comments, interspersed by face-to-face video conferencing that supports small groups’ discussion and feedback. Yet, as reported in this paper, we struggled to find open-source design tools that would suitably cater for our process and that would work for our participants. Hence, future research should also look to develop tools that respond to marginalised populations’ needs and requirements, leveraging open-source software for commoning [ 55 , 86 ] and making them widely available, thus removing financial barriers and aligning with the community-driven ethos of digital commons.

Finding appropriate ways to challenge participants step-by-step. In our study we exposed our participants to unfamiliar designs and technologies such as online bulletin boards and drag-and-drop activities, but also unfamiliar concepts, for example, the governance of a digital commons. In this sense, we have configured our participatory process in ways that gradually challenged our participants ‘step-by-step’ over the course of the engagements. This careful configuration sensitised participants to use the tools as they gradually gained confidence and proficiency. Thus, besides attending to the “ average capability of the group members” as Bettega et al. put it [ 5 ], we recommend future work in this space to consider how exposing participants to gradual, appropriate, and carefully planned technical and conceptual challenges can ultimately be more productive in the course of co-design.

A safe and respectful configuration . Considerations of safety are essential when conducting work with marginalised populations [ 7 ]. As discussed above, excluding healthcare officers promoted participants’ emotional well-being and enabled the voicing of service issues without fear of reprisal. Insights from months of prior work (AR cycle 1) and the positionality of the lead author, meant we were aware of the anxiety and stress that the presence of authorities would have caused. We respected and carefully attended to participants’ unpredictable lives arising from their priorities of care, and their associated barriers to participation in face-to-face workshops and other group activities [ 30 , 68 , 72 ]. While the lead author's positionality may have played a role in accelerating trust building and understanding the complexities of our participants’ lives – we invite other researchers working in this space to ensure they have an in-depth comprehension of the political and power dynamics at play, supported by disabled citizens and unpaid carers as collaborators in the research, before crafting participatory spaces.

Participants’ continued participation in the shaping of the directions of this study, their increased social capital, enthusiasm and rich output generated from the engagements, and that thousands of people have accessed MyCareBudget since its deployment, shows this work is clearly critical and valuable, and is clear evidence of the success of our configuration. More broadly, we believe future work should consider the methods, tools and configurations we developed in this study, further configuring them in ways that cater for diverse populations, abilities and complex lifestyles in order to foster and ensure meaningful participation.

7 CONCLUSION

We collaborated in Participatory Design with disabled citizens, unpaid carers and a care organisation to deliver a sustainable social innovation in the form of a digital commons. This commons currently supports personal healthcare infrastructures to over 5,000 people worldwide, growing by 200 each month. The co-design process delivered insights that exposed issues of trust, ownership and distribution of responsibility, pointing to the benefits and challenges of this approach for value and sustainability, and for the participants’ benefits. These have significance due to the increasing numbers of unpaid carers, the rising popularity of Personal Health Budgets and their self-directed care budget equivalents throughout the global north.

Our case study responded to the call from Bødker and Kyng for a more activist stance for Participatory Design, by involving our participants as partners, addressing “the Big Issues” of importance to our participants, and for sustainable democratic control over technology, whilst ensuring their safe participation. The insights further contribute novel configurations of methods and tools for HCI to research alongside underserved and marginalised populations, offering co-design implications for a sustainable digital commons that provides for democracy in both its design and as an intervention. We add to the corpus of HCI literature that leverages Participatory Design to deliver social justice for disabled communities, working alongside participants in the spirit of “Nothing About Us, Without Us” .
