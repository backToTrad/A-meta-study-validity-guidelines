Designing Anekdota: Investigating Personal Metadata for Legacy

Michael Nørgaard Jørgensen , IT University of Copenhagen, Denmark, mnoj@itu.dk
Tom Jenkins , IT University of Copenhagen, Denmark, tomje@itu.dk

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581495
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

This project uses research through design to explore how metadata could be used as a design material to create inheritable artefacts that make personal legacy experiential. Metadata is produced as traces of life, and by designing artifacts that capture and represent these traces, interaction design offers the possibility to experience the everyday practices of someone else as they are passed on and inherited. We use interviews with people interested in data practices, memorializing, and who have experienced grief to find themes and insights for designing with metadata as well as for legacy. Based on these, we developed the prototype Anekdota, a handheld metadata detector that lets an inheritor experience bequeathed location metadata. From a series of think-aloud walks, Anekdota was tested with participants to reveal sensitizing concepts for designing for metadata, and imaginative leaps for how this metadata may become a part of future legacy practices.

1 INTRODUCTION

As we go about our life, we constantly generate data. This is a fact of contemporary technoculture—our smartphones keep logs of calls and texts, as well as locations we search for and visit; our apps store our music listening history, providing us with years’ trends to review and suggested artists; and online retailers have records of our purchasing habits. When we produce this data, we also produce metadata—data that speaks to the patterns of consumption, of time, sequence, and action. This metadata isn't as useful for predicting what music will you like, or providing restaurant recommendations, but when taken seriously offers a means of reflecting on past experiences [ 36 , 39 , 48 ]. The temporal content of metadata provides reflective insight entirely on its own. Metadata is not the content of an event but can provide intriguing glimpses into a person's daily life and routines. For example, when a photo is taken, an image is created. At the same instant, that image is associated with metadata: it is timestamped, GPS located, and affiliated with color settings, resolution information, device information, lens settings like f-stops and ISO numbers, and shutter speed. Presented with care, metadata such as these can reveal stories of human presence and daily life—ones that can be revealed by designing with them.

One question that can motivate this design is what happens to these data after death? HCI has seen a significant movement towards considering the role of death in interactive systems, and how to be more mindful toward this inevitable end, even as data lives on [ 6 , 7 , 46 ]. ‘Thanatosensitivity’ urges more structured approaches for considering the role of death in interactive systems [ 33 ], questioning how we might design across generations, and consider the inheritability of data (ibid.). As research through design (RtD), this project takes inspiration from these agendas, utilizing a design process to imagine futures where personal metadata is a part of the multitude of possessions a person leaves behind upon death, something that a person's descendants may look upon as part of their commemoration—where metadata becomes a part of a person's legacy. While services exist from technology companies to commemorate and delegate access to data after death, such as Google's Make a Plan for My Account and Facebook's Legacy Contact and memorialization features [ 8 , 19 ], this data in itself is not itself something that we usually imagine as a part of a person's legacy. In other words, what might it look like if our personal metadata were possessions to be inherited by others? How could you look at them, experience them, engage with them? How could people interact with them for the purpose of commemoration and remembrance?

This project explores how people might come to commemorate a person's legacy through the location metadata left behind by digital products like Google Maps. Location records are metadata produced in everyday life, traced into servers as a person goes about their daily routines. This project, like other design research projects in HCI [ 9 , 39 , 40 ], takes these traces and treats them as data in themselves, and uses them to drive a design process. Because metadata is indexical in nature, it refers to the behaviors that produce it. As data to design with, we hope to reveal this behavior, leading to systems that may be able to develop richer connections in heirs to those who produced the data—using this metadata for legacy. However, it's not yet clear what uses or requirements users might have for metadata-driven experiences, especially in such an emotionally weighty area as personal legacy and remembrance. This project studies what personal metadata can be, what it means to commemorate a person's legacy, and investigates the capacity to which personal metadata might be part of that commemoration. This exploration is synthesized into the prototype Anekdota, which presents location metadata as traces to be investigated, followed, and experienced first-hand. This paper makes three contributions to HCI: first, it suggests what people might desire from metadata as a form of personal legacy (section 4). Second, it offers an example of design's capacity to work with metadata as a design material (sections 5 and 6). Finally, it evaluates Anekdota with participants, developing from that evaluation themes for future work with metadata as a kind of legacy practice (sections 7 and 8).

2 BACKGROUND: DATA LEGACY IN HCI AND DESIGN

As mentioned above, legacy, personal data, and how these perpetuate after death is of increasing interest in HCI [ 46 ]. Gulotta et al have defined legacy as “the creation and dissemination of identity over time” [ 24 ], and as “a dynamic and subjective process through which information, values, and memories are passed down to future generations” [ 26 ], emphasizing that these legacies are interpreted by others [ 25 ]. Briggs and Thomas underscore this concern, calling digital legacy “a valued collection of digital information that may be difficult to control or disseminate after death" [ 5 ]. One prominent example of this form of legacy in HCI design are the Technology Heirlooms [ 38 ], which explore the human practice of passing heirlooms on, the advent of digital legacy, and the difficulties that have emerged in between them: that digital pictures, documents, social media profiles, and so on have become cherished material that do not lend themselves easily to inheritance [ 8 ]. Encapsulating digital legacy into a physical form, their project draws from notions of slow technology [ 27 , 34 , 40 ] to design for experiences that evolve over extended periods, taking into consideration both those making a bequeathment and their descendants in multi-lifespan design. The possibility of digital legacy includes strategies for designing such as combining digital information across services, and thinking of digital information as part of a larger perspective on cultural heritage [ 24 , 26 ], operating across cultures [ 35 , 44 , 45 ]. Chen et al have used workbooks to explore how participants might be motivated to bequeath their data after death [ 10 ], and Holt et al have examined how data like these have particular implications for privacy and security [ 28 ]. Wallace has used design to support bereavement practices, developing systems that support ongoing reconfigurations of emotions over time [ 47 ]. In particular, this project is inspired by slow technology [ 27 ], arguing for interaction design artefacts that are not afraid to subvert expectations, provoke reactions, and express something about themselves and their environment. The philosophy has been adopted into and explored by numerous projects that explore personal archives [ 34 , 40 ]. Slowness calls attention towards manifestations of metadata for legacy that do not work in a goal-oriented, rational, and efficient way, but rather encourages pause and reflection as a foundation for discussing the possibilities of metadata as a form of inheritable legacy. This project builds on these agendas, exploring how material artefacts designed to be used over long periods of time could inspire new and richer forms of legacy-related data experiences.

2.1 Data Experiences

Designing for legacy built on “digital footprints” [ 5 ]—the data and metadata created and left across a slew of platforms—has close kinship with designing for other forms of personal data. Elsden et al argue that current design trends using personal data are too focused on solutionism, overly rational analytical insights, and individual goals [ 16 ]. They suggest a shift from data as focused on gaining actionable self-knowledge, to how we can use data to have a better experience of and in the world. Our experience of data might become more reflective than analytical. Their concept of the “quantified past” denotes that data can restructure how we encounter and remember the past, particularly by experimenting with alternative, physical representations of data. By emphasizing ambiguity in that physical design process [ 21 ], we can create “layers of meaning unique to the viewer that productively support more subjective interpretation” [ 16 ]. Data experiences for reflection are illustrated by Odom and Duel as they investigate how a user's music listening history, generated by services like last.fm and Spotify, can be used to design more self-reflective ways of collecting, possessing and listening to music [ 39 ]. Their OLO Radio allows the user to explore their own listening habits by ‘triangulating’ listening habits across years, months, and days. Here, the capacity for metadata to provide long-term reflective experiences, the open-endedness of the design, and most importantly how temporality is highlighted become essential components of designing reflective metadata artefacts. Designing systems that engage with metadata offers an opportunity to gain insights about a person's temporal life, and the ways that this temporality may be manifested. White et al's Memory Compass [ 48 ], for example, uses a smartwatch app and a ‘casting’ metaphor to imagine fishing for location-based data from your own location history to reminisce on memories caught nearby. Investigating metadata for the purpose of slowness and supporting long-term experiences continues in Olly, a music player which utilizes music listening history to play songs from the user's past [ 34 ]. The authors investigate unawareness, or computational objects which act on their own accord ; intersections , or unintentional circumstantial interactions with a system that happen via other activities in a users’ life ; and ensembles, how the collections of material and social elements become unique over time. Building from these, this project imagines experiences for metadata as legacy where the user's relationship to bequeathed data and places might similarly evolve gradually over time, slowly forming new interpretive constellations as an archive is materially explored.

Legacy also consists in part of passing down physical artefacts such as furniture [ 38 ]. Correspondingly, metadata can be produced through the physical objects that surround us. The Long Living Chair [ 41 ] is a rocking chair that logs and displays its patterns of use to encourage owners to keep it longer. A data display in the chair is placed out of sight and accrues data slowly, but changes will be noticed during life changes such as moving. The ListeningCups [ 13 ] provide an example of how audio recordings might become a tactile through 3d-printed ceramics. By translating audio recordings into bumps on a teacup, they can not only be reflected upon directly, but also explored subconsciously through fiddling. The authors emphasize that the design of data representations is inherently subjective, and it is users who give life to the data: “the focus can shift to the ways people might recount these stories, or the ways they might imagine past events (real or not)” (ibid., pp. 12). Desjardins et al. direct their attention toward residents’ perception of IoT data, applying a combination of probes and speculative sketches to produce imaginative scenarios of how data might become a more meaningful part of everyday life, arguing that data has the potential to become more tangible, narrative and visible [ 12 ]. Designers could encourage a more active relationship with data, asking people to make more conscious decisions of how it is produced, where it goes, and what to do with it.

2.2 Virtual Possessions and Reminiscence

Odom, Zimmerman & Forlizzi describe virtual possessions’ experiential qualities in order to understand why they complicate phenomena such as legacy [ 37 ]. They separate virtual possessions into three distinct categories: Material possessions such as photographs or documents that have become virtual; Immaterial possessions that include virtual messages, social media profiles, game avatars; and metadata, which are described as logs that capture people's actions. To them, the general problem with virtual possessions is that they cannot be encompassed by theories of material possession attachment [ 2 , 31 ]. This presents an important implication in designing for metadata as legacy, when making attempts of juxtaposing or equating digital material and physical possessions. The authors use empirical studies to articulate three experiential characteristics that these virtual possessions have in common: Placelessness, which denotes that they can be accessed nearly anywhere and can be present in multiple places at once; spacelessness that makes their size and scale; and formlessness , as they do not have an inherent form but be manifested in different ways and mixed with other digital content. This lack of form may come at a cost. For Van Gennip, Van den Hoven & Markopoulos [ 22 ] the consequences of storing memories digitally puts us at risk of losing everyday moments of reminiscence. When people are asked to indicate valuable items during home visits, digital mementos are often overlooked. The challenge for interaction designers to design for users to have more desirable remembering experiences of data. As a first move to address this issue, they investigate how remembering experiences are cued through everyday life by mementos, public images, and particular spaces. The combination above of digital legacy and data experiences for reminiscence helps articulate a design space for understanding metadata as a form of legacy that can be passed on to others in a material form that supports reflection.

3 METHOD

To explore how metadata could become a part of legacy practices, this project uses research through design (RtD) [ 18 , 20 , 49 ]. By engaging with problems in an open ended and material fashion, RtD uses qualitative methods to inform a design process, allowing designers to reflect on issues and understand the broader possibilities of an issue or agenda [ 14 , 20 ]. There are two general challenges to approaching metadata as a possible vehicle for an individual's legacy from an RtD perspective. First is understanding and articulating personal metadata as a material to be designed with, and second is understanding the practices, values, and meanings of inheriting a person's legacy, as well as people's existing perceptions about personal metadata. To learn how others understand legacy and possibilities for metadata, we used qualitative interviews [ 43 ]. Exploring a design space for metadata as legacy requires not only investigation into metadata, but also legacy as a human phenomenon. What is legacy, how is it created, and how is it passed on? How can it be described as a practice? How is it produced by a person's everyday activities, domestic possessions, and feelings? To answer these questions, we conducted a field study with 4 participants using an open-ended, semi-structured interview [ 1 ], supported by various printed material and exercises for participants [ 11 ]. Inspired by transfer scenarios [ 32 ] and extreme characters [ 15 ], we recruited participants via a public Facebook post in our city, finding individuals that had rich and evocative relationships with generational legacy (P1-P4, Table 1 ). Because ‘legacy’ is an abstract idea, these participants were sent a ‘legacy inventory’ to fill out beforehand, inspired by, augmented home inventories [ 23 ]. It asked participants to consider legacy-related items in their life, list them in a table with descriptions of how they are used or are, and describe the attachment they have to them. The interviews were between 1.5-2 hours long and had 3 parts: the participants' understanding of legacy, how they understand their personal metadata, and finally how they could imagine metadata as a form of legacy that could be passed on. Participants were shown examples of metadata, specifically music listening history and location history. To open up the participants' thinking around metadata and how it can be used in design, we presented some example materials, including OLO Radio [ 39 ], the Memory Compass [ 48 ], and the Long Living Chair [ 41 ]. Finally, we asked the participants to go to their Google account's feature Make a Plan for My Account , where they choose what happens to their data after death. This prompted a conversation around how they imagined their own legacy and what might happen to metadata that they produce. Each of these interviews were recorded, transcribed, and thematically analyzed [ 29 ].

4 METADATA AS A MATERIAL FOR LEGACY PRACTICES

This section presents the results of the interviews with P1-P4. Material forms of legacy was the first topic explored in these interviews. As expected, each of the participants had different understandings of and relationships to legacy practices, especially in things. We describe these themes below, paying particular attention to what legacy is understood to be and how it may be designed.

4.1 Themes of Legacy

Reminiscence. Physical objects tie us to the experiences we have had. For legacy, these memories are of person who is no longer with us, but the thing allows us to feel connected to them again. This is especially strong in P2’s legacy practice: “I identify a lot with my things. I use real things to remember, sort of like anchors to experiences and to people I have known.” For P1, this comes out in photos, and how they support memory: “I think [family portraits] lack something more human. When it becomes just the positive representation there isn't really much human about that.” P1 dismisses curated aspects into a person's life that may not reveal much about them, wanting instead smaller details help know them better or differently: “ their way of phrasing, facial expressions, enunciation, those things. [...] I think that is what you grasp for the most. That is what I do anyways.” As our own memories are fallible, metadata—traces of their actions—could remember people differently, or even more truthfully. P1 reflected on her childhood memories of her father: “My father passed away when I was 6 years old, so that means that the idea I have of him isn't real. I do not know him, and I never have. It is a child's fantasy about their parent. And dad is a hero when you are 6.” Legacy transforms things into reminders that can affect our memories of a person.

Closeness. The evocative nature of physical artifacts and legacy produces a sense of connection. P1: “There is comfort in physical legacy. It is something you have always had, something you can always return to. Something you come from, something you are, no matter what happens.” She explains how these things are particularly powerful for her due to the absence of intimate memories about her lost father: “You grasp for something you cannot reach when it is a person who is no longer here. And especially when it is a person you don't even know. I don't know my father, so there is a kind of closeness in that I can sit and look at something of his I had no idea existed. Suddenly I know something entirely new about him…. That is something.” P2 frequently applies needlework metaphors to legacy, such as tying ribbons, stitching, binding together, threading, and so on, resulting in complicated lacework of connections to and between personal relations: “I think I have made a lot of ties. I am surrounded by things. I want to say that I practice it [legacy], because I can walk around all these things, look at them and say ‘my grandmother had a lot of these plants’. My neighbor loves elk and has given me this [points to elk figurine]. My childminder's daughter, who is like a sister to me because she has known me always, gave me this blue bird [points to bird figurine].” This theme encourages consideration of how artefacts could convey a sense of presence and closeness over time, exploring how a user could gradually uncovers new insight into a person's life through their metadata.

Investigation . Most participants could recall moments of engaged and rigorous speculation on the origins and meanings of physical legacy. P3 grew an interest in heritage in his teens, leading him to search for genealogical information about his ancestors and to create a family tree. When asked why he responded: “It was exciting. I want to know where I come from. I never quite learned where I am from [...] It was simply about curiosity. It is not a need to bring my parents with me, it is just me wanting to know who I am.” Beyond the desire for connection, legacy evokes curiosity and provides a sense of purpose. P2 describes her mother's old armchair, which she discovers has had a long history of changing hands: “When you looked at it, it was tattered. You could see all the times people had reupholstered it. It had been around my uncle and aunt. You could see that my aunt in the 70s wanted it brown and orange [...] I think a lot of old ladies in my family have sat in it. It makes me wonder if my grandmother had also sat in it and been uncomfortable.” These things evoke stories and offer windows into people's lives. P1 describes how she expects a lot of things to acquire meaning as her life develops : “If I go home to my parents and look around, I don't see a lot of things related to legacy, but I probably would in 30 years when they are dead and gone.” Legacy is dormant in materials, acquiring meaning when new information is uncovered, or through life events such as bereavement.

Inspiration. The power of legacy can lead to imitating ancestors’ lifestyles, traditions, and so on, as well as rebelling against them. P2: “My grandfather, uncle, and I have used [a bayonet] as a gardening tool. I have embraced it because I think it is a funny tradition. It is also about doing something with it that makes it so special.” Referring to a bottle from a flea market, “My father found one of these on the beach by Vesterhavet. Then he made a lamp for me [...] They used to wash up on shores because sailors threw them overboard. And finally, I found another one, and I thought I could make one for my husband.” P4’s legacy practice was about his father and the history of Lithuania, his home country. Describing a ring inherited from his father: “I think my father was really in this underground where they were publishing Lithuanian patriotic literature […] On this ring is a Lithuanian grand duke Vytautas, during his rule Lithuania became the biggest throughout our history, he managed to win a battle against German Teutonic knights.” Legacy can also inspire a person to rebel against tradition. For instance, P2 describes how her prominent legacy practice is a reaction to her mother's ambivalent relationship with legacy: “It's also a way of defying my mother […] But I have grown up and can see that things have looked different.” Legacy can function to preserve aspects of a people's lives that we can build upon in our own lives.

Productive reading. Legacy objects can convey rich stories about an object's original meaning and use for the person who owned it. For example, P1 describes an old sketchbook left behind by her father: “I found his old sketchbook a few years ago, in a box that hadn't been thrown out. To find this thing that tells a story about a time before I was born [...] it wasn't really memories, but it is something concrete from a person who is no longer here. It is a tiny insight into that person's life, that I couldn't have gotten otherwise.” P1’s description reveals that objects can connect people to a past they have never experienced [ 30 ]. Although neither P1 nor her mother knew anything about the sketchbook's origin, P1 saw it as a testament to her father's fallibility and cherished it as something that could challenge her tendency to idealize him. As a design consideration, productive reading reveals that the truth of a dataset is not necessarily vital to an experience. What is more interesting is the artifact's ability to prompt interpretation in a way that engages a user's imagination about the underlying events—how it could become a material for design.

4.2 Themes of Metadata as a Design Material

We also asked participants about metadata more generally. Responses concerned their perceptions of personal metadata, as well as the prospect of metadata as a form of legacy. These themes are less about understanding what metadata is, and instead what it could be—imagining metadata as an open-ended design space, beyond its current forms and uses.

Physicality. Participants referred to data as something that exists in an omni-present, all-encompassing form, as floating in the air, as something that spreads, or is quickly evolving. While this imagery is consistent with data gathering taking place during the COVID-19 pandemic, it underscores how metadata is seen as placeless, spaceless and formless [ 37 ]. These qualities lead to confusion: placelessness makes us unsure of data's location, making them feel out of our control; spacelessness makes it hard to grasp size and scale, making them feel impossible to interact with and curate; formlessness denies the sense of history gained through material qualities like patina, and make them feel less real. For a designer, there is an opportunity to investigate these qualities as they lead to design concepts.

Performativity and expressivity. To our participants, there was a sense that producing metadata was also a performance. P3 said “I think you should make the comparison to a person sitting looking over your shoulder. Would you be okay if someone were sitting in your living room and listening to your music? No.” While privacy was a concern, P3 noted that this performance could offer insight in aggregate: “what fascinated me was that on google you can tell when an epidemic is on its way because people start googling certain things [...] in a way we are all little pieces of the bigger puzzle, but through data you can matter as that little piece.” In each of these cases data is something that can be performed , not just produced [ 16 ]. As imagined by our participants, metadata was tied to feelings, personality, and identity. P1: “music could be really relevant, but that is because it feels like a form of metadata that relates tremendously to my personality […] Imagine giving that [metadata] to your child and saying, ‘here's my life, you can examine everything.’” In the same way that our house's decor, garments and other personal expressions come to reflect our identities, metadata might similarly become intentional expressions of our selves. Just as a person can influence and curate their legacy, so could their metadata become a corpus of personal expression.

Playful filtering. P1 and P3 both have professional backgrounds in data science and have developed a rich relationship to data. For P1, her interest in data stems from analyzing it mathematically to see what can be discovered. P3 notes that metadata can convey an abundance of information about a person but is not understandable simply by looking at it. To him, ‘raw’ metadata is nonsensical, as data requires treatment, filtering, removing noise, and aggregating to understand it. OLO Radio works well to make the data it represents legible and interactive. “That one's fun, I like that. You can skip, you can search. But you have to be really lucky and hit the mark to have a fun experience, otherwise it is just noise, or worthless data” (P3). Playfulness here can help users to explore data—by cleaning, sorting, and searching in the data, users become archaeologists digging through datasets in search of discovery.

Respectful distance. Metadata was often treated skeptically, especially by P1 and P3, who distrust companies collecting user data. Despite their concerns, there are factors that might make metadata use more acceptable as part of legacy. For P1, the discussion about metadata as legacy revolved in part around her father's location history metadata: “I know he has travelled a lot, but I do not know where, and I don't know when. I would like to know more about that.” She continues to explain how this desire is morally ambivalent: “I think it would be really creepy [...] I have a curiosity about it, but at the same time there is respect for a person's privacy.” When discussing how it might be possible to mitigate these concerns, P1 thought that if a person had had some form of awareness around these metadata being collected, that logging was intended, and that they were consciously given, it could alleviate her feeling of intruding. “I think that the really detailed logging wouldn't be to my liking. If I can know that he was in this place during this week, that would be okay. ” Keeping a respectful distance means designers should balance abilities and constraints for exploring these data, so that a user can acquire meaningful insight while maintaining respect for the person the data comes from.

5 DESIGNING WITH METADATA FOR LEGACY

Based on the themes in sections 4.1 and 4.2, we moved forward with a design process. This process used these themes as both inspiration and as a guide to when something may or may not be acceptable. They gave some bounds to what might work or be unacceptable in an emotionally charged space. The first activity of this design process consisted of a material exploration of the first author's location history metadata. We understand location history to be metadata in this example as it was produced by Google Maps in the background automatically. Rather than being related directly to the app's usage, it instead was annotating over a decade of living in Copenhagen. This metadata was downloaded from his Google Account as a JSON file that contained approximately 700,000 JSON objects, each representing a moment location was logged (Figure 1 , left). We first mapped these moments using Google Map's Heatmap function (Figure 1 , right). We identified trajectories of travel, as well as four major ‘blobs’ of accumulated data: a childhood home, a current neighborhood, the city center, and our university. The next two subsections describe design experiments that begin to imagine how this data could be made into a vehicle of legacy, drawing on the themes above. These concepts are software sketches, early explorations to how metadata could support legacy.

5.1 Metadata as Legacy Design Concept #1: Synchronous Compass

Our first design exploration began to investigate the possibilities for using this data to explore legacy via synchronicity [ 36 , 39 ]. The Synchronous Compass tries to build on the inspiration and reminiscence in the data by nudging the user out into the world on a path of investigation . This concept filters nearby location history temporally, visualizing it on a map. When the current time and date corresponds with a moment in a particular year's dataset, a circle is drawn on the map. For example, if the author had remained in their apartment on this day in last year, circles are draw there repeatedly. If they were travelling through the city, circles would be placed along their route. Synchronous reanimation of these moments made it seem as if these were contemporary people moving around, resulting in a strong sense of presence. Observing these moments’ time, location, and travel speed drove interpretation and of what the person was doing, and where they were going. Figure 2 illustrates the design concept: a device that reveals nearby synchronous historical location activity.

The Synchronous Compass concept itself seemed too direct in how it presented the location data, conflicting with the theme of respectful distance . By rendering people's pasts so clearly, their activity was obvious, which felt like a violation of privacy. There wasn't enough ambiguity in the system to prompt productive reading —it was just visible. The concept informed the rest of the design process, though. The compass metaphor that placed the metadata in space meant that context could come from real-world surroundings, rather than just a visualization on a map.

5.2 Metadata as Legacy Design Concept #2: Forecast

Previously, we imagined synchronicity as echoes or events from this moment in the past. Instead, this concept uses data from the relative previous and subsequent week to convey a sense of a person's activities over a fortnight . Forecast is a display on a wall that visualizes a heatmap based on the person's recently analogous historical location activity (Figure 3 ), changing as time passes. Active spots could mean that the person was spending a lot of time in an area, while the display being completely empty might represent travel away from the city. In this concept, the city map behind the heatmap visualization has been removed, leaving the user to interpret the hotspots as more abstract representations of activity, attempting to be more respectfully distant to what this metadata might reveal. The concept was further developed to include exploratory qualities. Beneath the circular frame hangs a device that the user can detach and carry with them, using the handheld device to guide them towards metadata in the city.

These design ideas seemed like promising ways to work with location history metadata while placing the data directly in the city. While it was possible to learn about a person's activities through metadata visualizations like heatmaps, experiencing the location data without the physical location itself is comparable to experiencing music listening history without hearing the music. For location history metadata, that means that these metadata are best understood when experiencing their location directly. These design experiments helped us focus in on some themes of metadata and legacy that seemed especially expressive for design: fostering closeness to the data and making the data physical seemed like they would help a user engage in investigation , leading to productive reading in a user about bequeathed location metadata.

6 ANEKDOTA, A METADATA DETECTOR

Based on the simplicity of Forecast's handheld device, this concept is a system that allows the user to physically search for, encounter, and examine a person's residual location history metadata scattered around a city: a metadata detector called Anekdota or “things unpublished”. This concept required prototyping a portable device that could access GPS location, analyze location history metadata, and produce some form of feedback for the user. We chose an android smartwatch as the basis for prototyping. The first iteration was a simple map interface centered on the watch's location displaying a heatmap built from location data. It relied on a smartphone to sense the user's GPS location, store the location history JSON file, process that data based on the user's location, and transmit the results to the smartwatch for rendering, vibration, and interaction. We discovered that designing expressive output based on the presence of metadata at a location requires a new understanding of the dataset's shape and size relative to the types of places and scale of moments. At home, there may be 130,000 accumulated moments within a 20-meter radius, but by walking outside onto the street, this number would fall to around 50. On a rarely used street, this number would decrease to around 5-10 moments. A favorite café would see an increase to around 100-200, and a preferred supermarket 500-1000. Heatmaps began to seem like a problematic way to illustrate location information—they dulled the form, scale, and presence of the data. Data everywhere looked rather alike despite vast differences in scale: the apartment's 130,000 moments looks little different from 50 moments on the street.

6.1 From a Heatmap to a Wave

Inspired by electrocardiogram (EKG) readings, the heatmap was reinterpreted into a simple wave visualization in a second iteration. This wave imagines data as a trace that can be harvested and interpreted, varying according to the shape and size of the metadata. The wave is simple, but at the same time has various characteristics that can be manipulated to make it expressive—amplitude, wavelength, and frequency (Figure 4 ). These attributes gave us ways to characterize the location data by more than just the number of moments. Google estimates a user's activity for most moments, classifying them as standing still, walking, running or in a vehicle. The wave concept uses this information to apply more dimensionality to the metadata. Like the EKG, the wave evoked a sense of something living.

6.2 Conveying Shape and Scale

In the next refinement of Anekdota the number of moments affected the amplitude and wavelength of the wave, while local activity affected frequency. These were tied logarithmically to the number of moments to emphasize smaller changes. This iteration was significantly better at conveying difference. Frequency managed to convey the motion qualities of the metadata by increasing the speed of the wave as movement in the data increased. In testing, we observed how Google's location history accumulates. It puts heavy emphasis on when a person is standing still, as the logged moments quickly pile up, while when travelling the data is spread out more thinly. For example, a single restaurant visit accumulates data that might take several months on a walking route. Places of interest stand out very clearly in the data, even after just a few hours there.

Despite a significant improvement from the first iteration at conveying the differences in metadata traces, we felt that Anekdota still put too little emphasis on changes in the metadata. A user moves around an area while paying attention to the wave. When they notice a fluctuation, they look at their surroundings to find the cause. In the meantime, the wave might change further, but that difference wouldn't be noticeable when looking back at the device. A user would have to pay more attention to the wave than their surroundings. An iteration was needed to ensure that the user can direct their attention towards their surroundings, while being able to naturally sense when they enter a metadata hotspot.

6.3 Sensing Changes

For this iteration, changes in the metadata traces needed to be made more prominent. Earlier, software glitches would sometimes cause a wave's frequency to become zero or negative, meaning that the wave would stop or move backwards. These motions seemed more effective at drawing attention and conveying changes than amplitude and wavelength alone—a happy accident. Fewer detected moments now caused the wave to move backwards, while increases in data detected caused the wave to move forwards. No change at all meant that the wave fell still. Together, these led to stronger visibility for changes in the metadata. Following a walking route, the wave is mostly stable. Entering a hotspot would cause the wave to move forward rapidly. These changes helped to make the metadata seem more physical and present. The watch's haptic feedback helped make changes more prominent via vibration patterns that accelerated when detecting more traces and slowed when detecting fewer. Because activity was no longer conveyed by frequency, we added a second red ‘activity wave’ to the display. This wave's frequency, amplitude and wavelength were all dependent on the motion in the moments. Low motion would see the activity wave calm down and become slow, balanced, and longer. More motion in the data would see it move faster, increase in amplitude, and decrease in wavelength. This activity wave made for an even clearer distinction between places of interest and trajectories of travel, as the red and black waves would switch places. In a place of interest, the black wave is prominent, and while searching for metadata, the red wave grows.

6.4 Temporal Examination

The final iteration of Anekdota considered when a user has encountered a trace at a location and wishes to investigate it further, exploring the temporal qualities of the metadata. Here, the design took inspiration from OLO Radio [ 39 ], which explores music history across different temporal modes: cyclical and linear time. For Anekdota, cyclical time answers questions concerning a person's habits pertaining to a location. A hotspot in a park area might lead one to inquire whether the person went here during summer months, for example. With linear time, the user can determine the part of life the traces are from. For a workplace, one might ask how many years they were employed there. Although linear time could filter the metadata all the way down to specific days, hours, and seconds, we decided that it needed to remain approximate. Anekdota's temporal filtration was limited to looking at the metadata through preset ranges, including months, days of the week, and times of day for cyclical time and years for linear time. Using the buttons on the smartwatch switches between filters, and turning the crown adjusts the current filter. The introduction of temporal filters made for a radical change in exploring with Anekdota. Where before stumbling upon data fluctuations was a part of the experience of encountering data, Anekdota had gained new depth. A place of interest felt like a treasure chest that could be opened and examined. Each spike in the data hid a trove of context. Through playful filtering , Anekdota had gained a richer interaction quality. It challenges the user to figure out how location and filters might be combined in inventive ways to make discoveries in the data.

6.5 Final Iteration: Form

During the design and prototyping of Anekdota, physical form was rarely considered because attention was primarily on the interactive expression, particularly around making the metadata legible and intriguing. In early iterations, the Anekdota prototype was simply a smartwatch with the bands removed (Figure 5 ). The form that Anekdota eventually took was meant to convey the provisional nature of the concept: it is meant to prompt open-ended imagination about the future of metadata, and so a simple white 3D-printed case was designed to carry the smartwatch, creating a convincing but unresolved metadata detector (Figure 6 ).

7 INTERPRETING ANEKDOTA

Prototyping Anekdota offered a way for us to critically reflect on the design goals and process as well as produce a system that could be tested with real people (P1, P5-P7 in Table 1 ). All participants were of approximately the same age and based in our city, which makes it difficult to generalize these findings to a broader population. As with many RtD studies in HCI [ 20 , 21 , 31 , 38 , 45 ], the goal of this study was to gain a richer understanding of the design space with a smaller group of participants to inform what might be meaningful for future research and practice. We asked these participants to test Anekdota as a simple tool to experience metadata and interpret this ability into their own understanding of legacy. In this test, the participants explored metadata on loosely guided walks around our city that lasted between 60 and 90 minutes. The participants were free to explore and use Anekdota as they pleased but were nudged towards notable metadata hotspots if leaving areas with much data present. Participants were asked to think aloud [ 17 ] while using the device to record their reactions, and the walks were followed by an open-ended, semi-structured interview [ 1 ]. As before, these were transcribed and analyzed thematically [ 29 ]. It's important to note a major limitation of this method. In these walks, Anekdota used the same dataset from the first author that formed the basis for prototyping. This dataset included over a decade of location data that offered a richness that was relevant to this area. Knowing the content and context of the data meant that the first author could also help to guide the walks. The provenance of the data was known to the participants, but the lack of a more personal connection to the data could preclude the kinds of emotional connections that might otherwise occur, making implication for real legacy less valid. Despite these limitations, this approach did seem to offer enough context for our participants to understand the device and encourage them to envision new ideas or uses for it.

Experiencing Data Directly . Participants observed that Anekdota's manifestation of a data set differed from traditional ways of seeing data. P1, a data scientist, remarked that the experience differed vastly from her own practice with and relationship to data: “ It is truly a way of integrating with data, in an entirely different way than I would do otherwise. I would just sit down and look at it. This is integrating with data, rather than just looking at it .” Throughout the evaluation she was shocked by the presence she felt through Anekdota, and how its representation of the data was stimulating and refreshing for her: “ I work so much with it [data] that I don't really think about it anymore when I see anyone's data. For me it's just data points, but here I am actually experiencing it. I can sit and work with millions of data points in a day, and it becomes whatever, but I am experiencing it in reality. It makes it more human, the fact that it belongs to somebody. It is someone's data .” In general, explorations with Anekdota became more than just seeking out place hotspots and investigating them using the filters. Participants also engaged with the landscape. P5 made the peculiar and specific discovery at a fountain that the person's data had always walked past it on the right. Participants were driven by the idea that even the tiniest details might reveal behavior, leading to speculation about the personality behind the metadata. By encountering the metadata, users felt a connection to the person who generated it.

Voices from the Data . On several occasions, Anekdota was perceived to talk, react, or be alive in some sense. Although it was not designed with the intention of creating presence, many aspects of the experience come together to create the sense of something living. For P6 this is described as almost otherworldly: “ You feel like you are walking in the shadow or in the footsteps of these people [...] you have a form of connection to the person, and you feel connected to this ghost that you are chasing [...] In a nice way, it's a kind ghost. It feels like walking next to a person who has a presence, who has existed, who doesn't exist anymore. It doesn't have to be scary, on the contrary it is very exciting ” (P6). For P5, Anekdota seems to be telling a story: “I have this feeling that there is a person on the other end trying to tell me something. They want to show me where something is.” P5 later remarked: “ The haptic feedback creates a sort of heart rhythm association. It feels like the person has been particularly alive at this location. It could be a person that you have lost, and, if you have a gravestone you usually talk to, you could talk to Anekdota instead. You could say, do you remember when we went to this place? [...] you could have a conversation with your lost one. It could be a sort of interlocutor for the dead. ” Anekdota's role shifts between being a sensing apparatus and a kind of simulated personality. In some cases, Anekdota even seemed to express feelings about surroundings. P5 understood high movement activity (red wave fluctuations) to signify places the person disliked, imagining it to correspond to crowded areas. When participants began to filter through time, they began to consider what factors might have created these patterns. “ Now you can see the person's behavior in it [...] now it is more about looking at a place and what they used it for, which says a lot about a person . (P1)” The temporal filters transformed these metadata from a residual trace into anecdotes and stories about the person who produced them.

Purposeful Exploration. For both P5 and P6, Anekdota evoked strong associations of solving a mystery: “ I get this adventurous urge to figure it out. It's like this cliché. The man is dead, he left a note that says go to this place [...] what is the person trying to show me? Am I speaking with a person from the beyond? Where were you going? Who were you? ” (P5). Anekdota's cryptic expression of data challenged them to put together pieces of a puzzle. P6 felt that Anekdota prompted the user to manipulate, experiment, and play with it to figure out what is possible. As a device, it demands engagement to be useful: “ there is no overview here. You must go out and move around to get an experience out of it, which makes it fun ” (P1). For her, the fact that the experience was not obvious became a playful invitation to explore. Participants also imagined inventive uses of the Anekdota to explore its possibilities. P1 suggested that one could isolate the person's trajectory between home and work, finding where they lived; P5 suggested that stillness in data at train stations could reveal the person's punctuality. Anekdota encouraged creative interpretation of the metadata. The metadata directed participants’ attention towards spaces they would otherwise have ignored, defamiliarizing well-known areas [ 3 ]. To P1 Anekdota was like a sound walk, where the user walks through an area listening to related audio content: “ I like [sound walks] when it is an environment I know, because it is an entirely new way of looking at the environment […] I never notice this place when I pass by, which I do about 60 times a year, but I start to observe it when I use [Anekdota] to figure out what is happening in this narrative, why there are a lot of data here. ” For P6, the fundamental ability of Anekdota is that it creates space for exploring the data: “ [Anekdota] creates a space around it. […] In this setting, we gain the opportunity to ask questions and be curious about things. It creates a space for conversation in that sense. It is not just a space that we talk about exists, but one that clearly exists when we roam around and try to find answers. ” For P6 the new perspective on this space is something meaningful, motivating exploration.

Data in Relation . Participants’ inquiries into and interpretations of Anekdota's traces were heavily influenced by their personality, identity, and experiences, affecting productive reading drastically. P5 found a hotspot on a street corner outside a café. Because the evaluations were conducted during the COVID-19 lockdown, he did not associate the hotspot with the café, but instead imagined the person waiting on the corner. When asked about this, he remarked: “ It's the lockdown that make you think you can't sit inside […] I think about these data with the current situation as context .” Their interpretation was shaped by their surrounding and perception of the world, suggesting that experiences would vary significantly if participants re-visit locations. P6 suggested that a data hotspot might signify the person having lingered before going to a night concert in a nearby venue, based on their own habits in the area. Similar cases across evaluations suggest that the productive reading of traces and their surroundings depends heavily on personal experiences. If using Anekdota with her parents’ data, P1’s attention would focus on their activities at her own age: “ if it was my mom and dad, I would adjust their age to my own. Just so I could learn something I otherwise could not [...] it would be natural for me to return to it all the time out of curiosity. It's something you are never done with. You would probably use it less over time, but you would keep returning to it in those moments when you need your parents and need to reflect yourself in them .” With time, her curiosity would develop new questions, and a rationale for returning to Anekdota and re-visiting places.

Revisiting Shared Memories . Anekdota was designed with the idea that closeness in legacy could come from learning about unknown parts of an ancestor's life. We imagined that users would primarily highlight the experience of investigation and discovery , like stumbling over a shoebox of pictures from a parent's childhood in the attic. Using Anekdota would be less about the theme reminiscence , and more about exploring unknown aspects of a loved one's life. Instead, participants imagined using Anekdota to re-experience memories that they shared. P1: “ There is this sculpture park in Silkeborg where he loved to go, but I can only remember one specific place. My memories don't go any further than that single sculpture, and I am not sure if there are different or better sculptures in that park that you would rather look at. It would become very explorative in that sense.” This emphasizes how participants used Anekdota to explore the data of a person with whom they had no shared memories in our field test, making this kind of exploration impossible. Artefacts for legacy should of course have deeper and richer context to become meaningful to the people who experience the data.

Interpretive Fragility . During the walks , participants developed interpretations of the traces they encountered. At the beginning, the participant would sense a trace, determine its characteristics (still or moving, high or low), and observe their surroundings to try to make sense of it. There was a tendency for participants to interpret and explain traces through scenarios that relate to the participants’ immediate experience of their surroundings. P6 discovered a trace at a 7-Eleven while staring through the window and assumed that the person had been standing in the same spot looking through the same window, despite the data having a radius of 20 meters. P5 observed stillness on a bike lane and visualized a person stopping to check their phone. In fact, this stillness could have been the result of travelling both on foot and by bike across many separate moments. There was a disconnect between Anekdota's cumulative representation and participants' tendency to interpret the data as specific scenes, meaning traces came to represent significantly more than expected. Patterns at a café came to represent a person's coffee habits, dating life and more. Early theories became pervasive interpretations for the remaining evaluation. Participants recognized this tendency, saying “ I start to interpret things really rapidly ” (P1) and “ you can know a lot, and then there is a lot you cannot know, because you have to make these interpretations. In the process you can learn a lot, but you can also create a lot of non-knowledge ” (P6).

Intentional Bequeathment. Finally, during the evaluations, we asked about participants’ ethical comfort looking at another person's metadata. Most participants expressed concern due to the intimate and exposing nature of location metadata, even though Anekdota's implementation was considered appropriately vague, built on respectful distance : “ I think if it was more precise, it would be scarier…. It's mystical enough that it isn't a crime to follow in your footsteps” (P7). Of course, this test differed from using Anekdota with data from a person who is no longer alive. In the tests, participants were effectively following alongside their ‘ancestor’ in a social experience of exploring metadata. They remarked that they felt comfortable that the first author had willingly shared these data and was present to monitor their exploration, as it created an atmosphere of consent. Some participants concluded that they would wish for these data to not only be given consensually, but also that the bequeathing person was aware of the contents. P1 describes how these conditions would be problematic for her father: “ if it had been my father, it might have felt wrong because he would never have understood how much and how substantial this tracking actually is. [...] Just because you hand it over willingly does not mean you understand what you are giving away .” Experiences built on metadata, even after a person's passing, must consider how to represent the donor's relationship to their metadata thoughtfully and comprehensively.

8 DISCUSSION

Building from the evaluation, the following sections relate to sensitizing concepts (after [ 4 , 34 ]) that can be used in future design to frame inquiry into designing for metadata in general as well as legacy more specifically. Because the user tests were based on location data that was not bequeathed to our participants by somebody close to them, it's difficult to make strong claims about how Anekdota worked in terms of legacy as such. For this reason, we structure the discussion into sensitizing concepts for metadata coupled with associated imaginary leaps [ 12 ] that project how that metadata concept might be extended to legacy, articulating future directions for design to support legacy activities and experiences in future systems. The sensitizing concepts are based on the findings more directly and relate to metadata alone. As imaginary leaps argue that when designing with data, we should not be afraid to reposition the data into more playful, imaginative, and silly narratives or modes of interaction that do not necessarily correlate with the reality or rationality of a situation [ 12 ]. Here, we use these leaps to interpret implications for legacy from our evaluation. Finally, we reflect on design choices made during the process, and how larger-scale datasets like this one can be made more understandable and tractable for designing.

8.1 Metadata as Mysterious and Trackable

A strong finding from the user test was participants' descriptions of how Anekdota evoked a narrative for them. They found the system playful, engaging, and challenging, which created associations of it being like a game, exemplified by ideas of purposeful exploration and voices in the data . One described the sense of being part of some greater noir fantasy, as the protagonist chasing down a missing person. These associations of investigation and detection, puzzle and mystery solving to follow obscure breadcrumbs and traces was something that participants enjoyed. Making the location metadata material and sensed gave strong motivations for participants to follow them and try to figure out what had happened in the world to produce them. Designers can build to emphasize these feelings of purpose, meaning, and importance that come from feeling on the path to a greater discovery.

Imaginary Leap: Legacy Metadata for Slow Acquaintance and Re-Interpretation . Long-term use of an artefact like Anekdota could lead to inquiries and interpretations of the metadata traces co-evolving with a user's identity, personality, interests, and other aspects of themselves. This interpretive leap might be considered as a projection of data in relation over time. Just as inherited objects mostly lie dormant but can be re-visited throughout life for reminiscence and reflection and looked upon with new eyes, so could metadata for legacy. The highly situational circumstances of interpretations above imply that designing legacy metadata must be mindful and active towards supporting perpetual re-interpretation of metadata, diffractively developing new relationships with the metadata as it is lived [ 42 ]. These findings resonate with the Technology Heirlooms project about the temporality of memories: “even if we remember things from the past the same, the way we feel about them can change” [ 38 ]. Taking inspiration from designing for revisitation and reflection [ 40 ], an artefact like Anekdota might encourage re-visitation by offering different constraints, revealing different information or changing how data is presented to make experiences new and rewarding even as people change.

8.2 Metadata as Present and Purposeful

Anekdota provides an example of a physical data manifestation that allows for more sensory and material encounters with metadata. Specifically, Anekdota manages to establish its narrative around an existing material trace by addressing the spaceless, placeless, and formless material qualities of location history metadata [ 37 ]. Anekdota turns location history metadata into an intricate landscape that can be felt in an embodied way. The physicality of Anekdota ultimately negotiates the placelessness, spacelessness and formlessness of location history metadata. The result is a placeful, spaceful and formful manifestation of metadata that offers a way to experience data directly . This shifts the perspective on location metadata as being more than a residual trace, and instead a design material that matters in contextually relevant ways in the places that they were created as being data in relation to the world that it comes from. These attributes resonate clearly with location history metadata but could certainly become relevant for other types of metadata placed in the world.

Imaginary Leap: Legacy Metadata for Presence and Companionship . Our findings suggest an opportunity to explore how metadata experiences like Anekdota can emphasize moments shared in the past between people. Experiences like these might be especially powerful for childhood memories which are often fractured and faded. This interpretive leap might be considered as a projection of voices in the data over time. Metadata could let a user reconnect with memories pertaining to people we have lost. This echoes discussion on home IoT data's capacity to fill in the gaps in residents’ memories, as data defamiliarizes the mundane tasks people quickly forget [ 12 ]. Likewise, metadata can reveal aspects of our behavior that we are unaware of, providing people the opportunity to see shared memories from a new point of view. Participants’ experiences using Anekdota revealed that metadata could provide a way for artefacts to be animated by a personality. Because metadata map to behavior, metadata produce liveliness via reenactment. Metadata offer a way to think through how objects could encapsulate aspects of a person's life, adding a sense of presence to a device, ‘computational companions’ that play a role in commemoration or loss. One participant remarked how Anekdota might be used simply as a therapeutic device for the bereaved, like visiting a graveyard to commune with lost loved ones. While this is complete representation of personality feels ethically dubious to be sure, there is perhaps something appealing about some rhythm of use from a beloved person being stored in a favorite artifact.

8.3 Creating and Transferring Metadata

Designing for metadata as legacy should consider how those who would bequeath metadata might come to develop a relationship to, gain ownership of, and develop material attachments to the metadata they produce throughout their life— intentional bequeathment . The theme of Performative Metadata from the field study takes the data produced about us as something that we do with purpose. Ensuring that those bequeathing their metadata attend and engage with what they pass on is necessary to make experiencing that data ethical [ 28 ]. In this project's design process, the first author explored their own location history and judged which aspects should be emphasized in Anekdota. Desjardins et al argue that the focus of data could shift from objective truth telling representations of data, to telling stories: “the focus can shift to the ways people might recount these stories, or the ways they might imagine past events (real or not) … [this data] invites our imagination to run wild: every person will likely tell a different story with their data set.” [ 12 ]. Understanding metadata as malleable and performative presents trajectories for how it might be attached, transformed, and transferred for future consumption.

Imaginary Leap: Legacy Metadata as Ambiguous and Revealing . Anekdota's approximate expression evoked participants’ curiosity to explore the data, but also uncertainty about the “non-knowledge” it produced. There was no way to know whether their interpretation was correct. This interpretive leap might be described as a projection of interpretive fragility over time. The experience emphasizes uncertainty and counters the myth of objectivity and truth that metadata represents—described by one participant as looking at a silhouette behind frosted glass. Anekdota invited participants into a playful atmosphere around data that was not uncanny or a violation of privacy by not being wholly representative. In this mode, metadata produced over the course of a lifetime from a variety of sources can be made to be less functionalist or factual, echoing Elsden et al's call for narratives in data [ 16 ]. This imaginary leap points towards ways of tempering our problematic relationship to personal data that is too representative or factual, suggesting futures where the approximate and evocative metadata of others become a meaningful part of our lives.

8.4 Design Reflections on Anekdota

As an example of an RtD process, Anekdota offers one possible way that metadata could be designed for legacy, predicated on a set of values from field work. Reminiscence, closeness, investigation, inspiration, and productive reading were taken to heart as driving the design concept in sections 5 and 6. Iterations on the design qualities of Anekdota came from both expressing this design intention, but also the themes of metadata. Being able to tune the data and make sure that the data presented were legible and scaled well helped ensure that the themes of legacy were present in the final form. Finally, the scale and relatively abstract nature of location metadata may also lead to certain kinds of strategies for interacting with it. Serendipity as a design strategy for would be less effective for location metadata than it would be for image or music-based artefacts [ 36 , 40 ]. To understand location data and learn things from it—even if those things aren't strictly true—needs being there, exploring a place and the dataset directly. Making metadata physical and performative and expressive via the fine-tuning of the wave interaction, playfully filtering the data across cyclical and linear time, and particularly respectful distance as a concern throughout led to a concept that seemed to hit a kind of “sweet spot” for interaction—it made a large dataset easy to explore while remaining intriguing, motivating our participants to seek their own stories in the metadata.

9 CONCLUSION

This project set out to explore how people might come to commemorate a person's legacy in the future through the metadata created by their digital products today. It used a process of research through design to explore and suggest opportunities to design with metadata as a material for legacy. This process began with a field study that sought to find evocative themes about how people live with metadata and practice legacy, imagining a future where metadata becomes a component of bequests. These themes informed the design and prototyping of Anekdota, a handheld system that lets the user sense another person's recorded location metadata. As an example of how metadata can become a part of legacy practice, Anekdota builds on over a decade of recorded location data, materializing them into something that can come alive and be present for people in new ways, making tangible and experiential something that might otherwise be residue of technology.

To see how others made sense of Anekdota, participants explored our city using Anekdota, taking part in a discussion around the possibilities of using metadata as a part of legacy practices. These discussions led to sensitizing concepts for designing for metadata in the future. These include metadata as being mysterious and trackable, present and purposeful , and as needing special care in creating and transferring it to future generations. Each of these sensitizing concepts for metadata led to three imaginary leaps for designing future metadata experiences with legacy in mind: metadata for slow acquaintance and re-interpretation , using metadata for presence and companionship as it animates and inhabits devices and systems around us; and inviting for fractured visions on the truth of a dataset, where interpreting ambiguous and revealing aspects of data is as meaningful as representing the data in a representative way. These offer provocative possible directions for future design work that materializes metadata as part of legacy practice, illustrating how metadata could become an enduring part of meaningful relationships—even after death.
