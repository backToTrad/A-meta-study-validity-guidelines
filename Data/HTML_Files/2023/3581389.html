3D Building Plans: Supporting Navigation by People who are Blind or have Low Vision in Multi-Storey Buildings

Independent travel and navigation in new environments, in particular multi-storey buildings, is a major challenge for people who are blind or have low vision (BLV). Using tactile maps as part of orientation and mobility (O&M) training, BLV people can build a cognitive map of an environment before visiting. Tactile maps of multi-level environments, however, have received little attention. We investigated the usefulness of 3D printed models of buildings, through a user study with nine BLV adults. Three designs were evaluated: flat, overlapped-sliding and overlapped-rotating. All three designs were reported to be useful, usable, engaging and allowed participants to build a cognitive map of the building. There was a strong user preference for the overlapped presentations, which were reported to be more effective in supporting cross-floor spatial knowledge. This exploration of the design space of 3D building plans demonstrates their value and we hope will encourage their provision in O&M training.

1 INTRODUCTION

Human navigation relies on building a comprehensive mental representation of the environment known as a cognitive map or mental map  [ 30 , 75 ]. Navigation in multi-storey environments, including shopping centres, museums and hospitals, is particularly challenging and often causes disorientation when travelling between floors  [ 44 , 45 ]. It requires us to build a multi-level cognitive map that is a “mental representation of the multi-level built environment”  [ 44 ]. This task is made more complex by irregular or incongruent floor plans, partially overlapped floors and disorienting staircases  [ 44 , 45 ].

Independent travel and navigation in new environments, in particular multi-level buildings, is a major challenge for people who are blind or have low vision (BLV) [ 59 , 68 ]. This can impact confidence, autonomy and participation in the workplace and community  [ 5 , 68 ]. Independent navigation by BLV people relies on building a cognitive map of the environment [ 41 , 42 ]. While there is a surprising lack of research into multi-level spatial cognition by BLV people, we would expect that, just as for sighted people, building a cognitive map of a multi-storey building is more difficult than building a cognitive map of a single-storey building.

Orientation and mobility (O&M) training is commonly used by BLV people to prepare for travel in new environments [ 6 , 8 , 21 ]. Pre-visit O&M training supports independent travel, by allowing BLV travellers to plan ahead and increase confidence before travel to an unfamiliar environment [ 47 , 73 , 76 ]. Tactile maps are an important part of this training. They serve as an introduction to the layout of an environment and help build a cognitive map prior to visiting  [ 6 , 20 , 70 , 71 ]. Indeed, a tactile map can be more informative of the spatial layout of a new environment than physical exploration of the space  [ 6 , 13 , 50 , 59 ].

Tactile map presentations of multi-storey buildings generally present a tactile floor plan for each storey as illustrated in Figure 2 . This requires the reader to mentally merge the information on each graphic to understand the spatial relationships between each level.

More recently, 3D printed maps (3DPMs) have been suggested as an alternative to traditional tactile raised line maps (RLMs). They offer an advantage over raised line graphics as they allow direct representation of 3D objects including representative icons [ 36 ] and are effective at conveying composition and spatial information [ 26 ]. Almost all prior research, has only considered 3D maps of outdoor environments  [ 14 , 22 , 26 , 34 , 61 ] and single-storey indoor environments  [ 48 ]. A notable exception is [ 72 ] which presents a multi-level, stackable 3DPM of a museum. This work found that the map supported the creation of a mental model but did not investigate the kind of cognitive map engendered or how the representation of the multi-level floors impacted the efficacy of the model.

In this paper we address the overarching research question: Can 3D models of multi-storey buildings help BLV people build spatial understanding of such buildings and construct an effective multi-level cognitive map? Our main contributions are:

  • Two novel designs for 3D models of multi-storey buildings. These designs overlap the individual floors above one another but allow users to independently move the floors using either a sliding or rotating mechanism to facilitate access during tactual exploration.
  • A controlled study with nine BLV participants comparing the effectiveness of these two designs with a more traditional flat presentation of the floors. For each presentation condition–flat, overlapped (rotating) and overlapped (sliding)–we evaluated spatial understanding including route planning, usability, engagement and user-preference. We were surprised to find that all three models were equally effective. However, there was a clear preference for the models with overlapping floors. Indeed, we found that when using the flat model most participants in effect created an overlapped model by manually positioning the floors above one another in order to better understand the connections between them.
  • Insight into the cognitive models BLV people create of multi-storey environments from tactile models and how this is impacted by model design. Our study suggested that it was easier for participants to build an understanding of cross-floor relationships with the overlapped models than with the flat model and that they gave rise to a more integrated multi-level cognitive map than the flat model.
  • Confirmation of the usefulness of providing models like these for O&M training before visiting multi-storey public spaces. Participants in the study confirmed the importance of having a cognitive map of the layout of an unfamiliar building before visiting it, with half indicating that building a cognitive map was more difficult if the building was multi-storey. All participants felt our models would be useful and almost all strongly agreed that pre-journey access would make them feel more comfortable to visit a multi-storey shopping centre.
  • Preliminary advice for the design and production of 3D printed models for O&M training for multi-storey buildings. This exploration of the design space of 3D building plans demonstrates their value and we hope will encourage their provision in O&M training.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Cognitive Maps

Spatial understanding of our environment is fundamental to everyday life. This is built around a mental representation of space called a cognitive map. A cognitive map is a mental representation of the “spatial environment to support memory and guide future action”  [ 19 ]. It is used for route planning and to track position and orientation while travelling [ 53 , 64 ]. Our cognitive map is built from experience, vistas, verbal descriptions and maps [ 62 ]. It is an allocentric representation that encodes approximate distance, relative directions, route connections, landmarks, as well as, emotional associations  [ 49 ]. However, it is not a precise map with a well-defined scale. Instead, it resembles a patchwork collection of sketch maps at different levels of scale and detail [ 53 ].

Cognitive mapping of multi-storey environments, such as shopping centres, is a challenging task due to the added complexity of processing multiple floors  [ 45 ]. Irregular and incongruent floor plans, partially overlapped floors and disorienting staircases make this task even more difficult [ 44 , 45 ]. Understanding level-related building features is imperative to navigating these environments  [ 45 ] and lifts and staircases are known to serve as important orientation features  [ 17 ]. Research suggests that we do not build a true 3D map of three-dimensional environments. Instead we build a bi-coded representation in which horizontal and vertical dimensions are encoded separately [ 39 ]. Li and Giudice [ 44 ] hypothesise that our mental model of built environments comprises a cognitive map for each floor, information about between floor connections including position and orientation on each floor, between floor-alignment information and an encoding of height for each floor. They call this a multi-level cognitive map.

2.2 Orientation & Mobility Training: Raised Line Maps

Navigation in unfamiliar indoor environments can be challenging for BLV people  [ 3 , 46 ]. Orientation and Mobility (O&M) training aims to equip people with the skills, strategies and information they need to understand their surrounds and safely navigate to their destination  [ 6 , 21 ]. Increasingly, HCI researchers are turning their attention to the many accessibility barriers faced by BLV people in navigation. Tools such as Apple Maps [ 4 ], Google Maps [ 24 ], as well as Goodmaps [ 23 ] and NaviLens [ 54 ] which are specifically designed for BLV users, help by providing turn-by-turn directions when navigating in an unfamiliar environment. However, they do not support creation of a cognitive map of the unfamiliar environment before travel. Raised line maps (RLM) play a major role in presenting spatial concepts through touch and support spatial learning  [ 15 , 51 ]. They are an important component of pre-visit O&M training and enable BLV travellers to build a cognitive map of a new environment before visiting it and may be used in route identification and learning [ 20 , 40 , 74 ]. Figure 2 shows a typical raised line map of one floor of a multi-storey building. Several recent surveys with BLV people have found that there is a need for further support for indoor navigation, particularly for shopping centres, including a need for tactile maps [ 16 , 18 , 63 ]. Blind experts have reported that the biggest indoor navigation problem when navigating public spaces includes finding escalators, lifts and stairs  [ 63 ]. The provision of accessible maps providing spatial information such as the location of these features is imperative to independent navigation by BLV people  [ 57 ].

2.3 3D printed tactile maps

More recently researchers have begun to explore the use of 3D printing for the production of tactile graphics as an alternative to raised line graphics. Several studies have explored the use of 3D printing for the production of tactile maps  [ 9 , 28 , 37 , 67 ]. Other work has explored a combination of 3DPMs with audio feedback  [ 12 , 25 , 31 , 57 , 59 , 66 , 69 , 72 ].

3DPMs have the obvious advantage of directly representing the height of map features. They have been reported to be a helpful indoor mapping tool  [ 52 ]. Interactive 3DPMs also improve memorisation of spatial information and user satisfaction [ 22 ]. A controlled user study with BLV adults by Holloway et al. [ 36 ] found 3DPMs improved short term recall and understanding of relative height of map features. Further, there was strong user preference for 3D maps over RLMs.

Importantly, 3DPMs provide an opportunity to improve accessibility in public spaces [ 36 ]. They also favourably promote inclusion and accessibility to the broader sighted community [ 37 ]. A notable example is the cultural institution, Van Gogh Museum[ 1 ]. The museum recently installed multi-level 3DPMs of the building including touchable floor plans to improve accessibility. The importance of providing 3DPMs to increase confidence and to support independent exploration of public spaces, in this case museums, was reinforced in a study by Wang et al. [ 72 ]. In parallel to the research described here, they developed BentoMuseum, a multi-level, stackable 3DPM of a museum. Twelve participants who are blind reported that the model enabled them to create a rough mental map which enhanced their confidence and orientation when navigating in the museum. However, the study did not investigate the structure of the cognitive model or objectively measure its effectiveness. Furthermore, the study did not evaluate different representations for multi-level environments.

Thus, there has been little exploration of the design space and presentation of 3D floor plans of multi-level environments. There has yet to be a more rigorous evaluation into cognitive mapping and cross-floor spatial understanding with multi-level 3D maps. To the best of our knowledge we are the first to offer a comparison of flat and overlapped designs of 3D multi-level maps. We investigate the efficacy of these designs for cross-floor spatial cognition by BLV people.

3 DESIGN EXPLORATION

We developed three distinct approaches using co-design with a blind colleague. The three designs are shown in Figure 3 . We decided to use 3D printing to produce the floor plans as previous research (detailed in Section 2) has found that 3D models have benefits over RLMs. In particular, this allowed us to print realistic 3D icons for the vertical connectors, i.e. the escalators and lifts.

Our first design was a flat model in which each floor is printed separately and placed on a horizontal surface in front of the user. This is the traditional approach used by O&M instructors when creating RLMs of multi-storey buildings.

The other two designs were more innovative. Multi-level cognitive mapping requires the touch reader to build a mental model of the transition points between the floors [ 44 ]. To do so the touch reader must mentally match the top and bottom of the vertical connectors such as escalators on the different floors. This requires mentally aligning the floor plans. We conjectured that this has a high cognitive cost and may be error prone.

The use of 3D models suggests another approach. At its simplest we could provide a 3D model of the building, as a kind of ‘dollhouse’. The great advantage is that the vertical connections and alignment between the different levels is shown explicitly, potentially reducing the cognitive effort required to build a multi-level cognitive map. However, there is a potential problem. Previous work has highlighted that touch reading strategies for 3DPMs involve the use of two hands, multiple fingers [ 50 , 60 ] and vertical positioning of hands in order to read over elements with height [ 36 ]. While the open front of a dollhouse allows for tactual exploration of the rooms, features such as the stairs, wall height and the constrained vertical distance between floors may be obstructive for touch readers. We therefore wished to develop an approach that combined the appropriate features of the dollhouse, that explicitly show the 3D structure and vertical connection, with those of the flat map with easy access by hands. Our starting point was a 3D model of the building similar to a dollhouse. Our first thought was to allow the touch reader to remove a floor from the model so they could freely explore it with two hands. However, the disadvantage is that it may be tricky for touch readers to re-position the floor in the model after exploration. We therefore explored mechanisms in which the floor remained connected to the model frame but allowed the floor to be moved away from the other floors. This lead to two designs:

  • Sliding model: In this approach each floor could be slid to the side of the model using a slider mechanism similar to that used for sliding drawers in cupboards.
  • Rotating model: In this approach the floors were attached to a vertical rod. Each floor was free to rotate around the rod allowing it to be moved away from the other floors.

The two approaches have different advantages: the sliding model maintained the orientation of the floors during exploration while the rotating model allowed for unobstructed access from all sides of the floor. We now detail some of the design considerations and decisions.

Cross floor connections: We ensured that cross-floor connections via the vertical connectors were tactually recognisable by using 3D icons for escalators and lifts that bridged the levels and by printing tactile ground surface indicators (TGSI) at points of connection.

Alignment & orientation of floors: Our co-design partner stressed her preference to be able to freely move each floor but also wanted to be able to quickly align them. All models were designed with features to indicate the correct alignment and orientation of floors including braille labels at the front of each floor, consistent location of vertical connectors across all levels; and clear boundaries for each floor.

Unobstructed touch reading of floors: In the two designs with overlapping floors we separated the floors by 5cm to provide space for touch reading when overlapped. Floors could be completely slid or rotated away from the other floors, allowing unobstructed access by two hands.

Structural design: The support structures in the two designs with overlapping floors needed to support the weight of the 3D printed floor plan and pressure from touch reading interaction by users. They needed to support two-hand exploration, making sure touch readers don't need to hold or support materials while doing so  [ 50 ]. The supportive structures were designed to be unintrusive and unobstructive.

Sliding mechanism: Three prototypes of the sliding mechanism were trialled to determine the best way to achieve structural support of floors and a smooth sliding mechanism. Off-the-shelf products were trialled as well as custom plywood back-frames. We found that heavy duty groove ball bearing drawer runner slides provided the smoothest motion and greatest structural support. A custom back-frame was constructed to facilitate the sliding mechanism. Sheets of plywood were laser cut to 355mm × 185mm pieces. Two or three layers of the plywood pieces were glued together to construct a base and backboard. Grooved ball bearing drawer runner slides were screwed to the backboard. The 3D models of each sliding floor plan was designed with an extension of 10mm along the back. This had 2mm × 8mm holes to guide the placement of screws and attachment of the drawer runner post printing.

Rotation mechanism: A custom base was constructed for the rotating mechanism. Sheets of plywood were laser cut to 215mm × 185mm pieces and glued together to form a base. A threaded stainless steel rod (dia. 10mm) was joined to the plywood base. An external thread on the steel rod and an internal thread in the base facilitated disassembly for storage. A steel shaft collar (bore 10mm) was placed above and below each 3D printed floor. A rubber washer was placed between the printed materials and shaft collar to provide some resistance when floors were rotated. The 3D models for each rotating floor plan had an extension and bore (10.7mm), allowing the floor to slide onto the rod.

4 EVALUATION

We conducted a controlled study with the three designs. The study was designed to address the following interlinked research questions:

  • Would 3D models of multi-level built environments be valuable in O&M training? We asked participants about the difficulty of building cognitive maps of multi-level environments and the usefulness of the models in pre-visit training.
  • What kind of cognitive map do the models engender and is this affected by the model design? We explored this by asking questions about participants’ mental model of the building and evaluating its accuracy .
  • How appealing and effective for spatial understanding are the models and how is this affected by the model design? This question explored participants direct interaction and perception of the models. We compared the touch reading strategies used with the three designs as well as their effectiveness for spatial understanding including route finding, usability , user preference and engagement . We also asked for design feedback .

A more detailed description of the questions and measures used is provided in Section 4.3 . A pilot study was conducted with a touch reader to ensure: all tactile materials were tactually distinguishable; to assess the complexity of the building plans; usability and smoothness of moving mechanisms; and to assess the duration of the study procedure.

4.1 Materials

For the study we used 3D printed floor plans of multi-level shopping centres. Previous work has highlighted BLV end user interest in maps of shopping centre areas [ 69 ]. Three shopping centre building plans were designed on TinkerCAD. Each shopping centre (A-C) was distinct in its layout but map features remained constant across all. All map features with the exception of vertical connectors were low in height for easy tactile exploration  [ 35 ]. Consistent features across building plans included: a base (3-5mm); raised walls (5mm); three floors; an irregular third floor; doorways (20mm), main entrance (40mm); and barriers around voids (height 2.5mm, width 1.5mm).

As detailed in Section 3, each shopping centre could be presented in three ways: flat presentation in which the floor plans were placed side by side; rotate in which the floor plans were arranged vertically and allowed to rotate; and slide in which the floor plans were arranged vertically and allowed to slide.

All materials were printed on an Ultimaker S5. Floor plans for the flat presentation were designed with a base of 3mm, while those for the sliding and rotating presentations had a base thickness of 5mm. In order to further improve the structural integrity and minimise the risk of bowing the infill density was increased to 50% and the infill pattern cubic subdivision was selected on Ultimaker Cura. Each floor plan had the approximate dimension of 185 mm x 170 mm.

Indoor landmarks are an important component of cognitive maps for both sighted and BLV people [ 30 , 38 ]. Our floor plans included 3D icons for common landmarks in shopping centres. As we had recall tasks in the study we limited the number of icons so as not to overwhelm the participants [ 32 , 35 ]. Each floor had icons for two shops or facilities, an escalator, a lift and TGSIs.

Prior research has found that in order for icons to be tactually recognisable they should be simple, have a minimum size of 1.5cm 3 , distinctive shape and associated with items that are usually touched  [ 37 ]. Informed by this we created a variety of icons. We used icons for a food court (bowl with chopsticks) and bathrooms (a toilet) that had previously been found to be easily recognisable by touch readers [ 37 ]. Designs for 3D icons of TGSIs, lifts, escalators and main entrance were created on TinkerCAD. In addition various 3D icons for a bookstore, cafe, juice bar, toilets and supermarket were downloaded from Thingiverse 1 . These were modified to ensure they were clearly recognisable at the required scaling. All designs were evaluated by a touch reader and those found to be most tactually distinguishable were selected for the study. The 3D icons were 20-55mm in height. All icons were printed in black or white to contrast on a black or white or grey floor plan to support participants with low vision. A key of the 3D icons was printed and accompanied by braille labels and large print.

4.2 Participants

Nine adults who are blind or have low vision participated in the study, listed in Table 1 . Participants were recruited through special interest groups on social media and a registered pool of participants. All participants were compensated AU $ 100 for their time. The majority had some exposure to tactile graphics and 3DPMs. Participants were aged between 23 and 56 years old. Participants self-reported their level of vision. This varied from: totally blind, no usable vision; legally blind, no or very limited usable vision; and low vision, some usable vision. The formats participants used to access print was also recorded.

4.3 Procedure

We used a within participant design, with each participant using each of the conditions–flat, rotating and sliding–with a different floor plan–A, B or C–for each condition. Presentation order of the conditions was counterbalanced but the floor plan order was fixed ensuring the choice of floor plan for each condition was counterbalanced. The study had seven parts with two breaks, taken before the second and third models were presented. On average it took participants about 90 minutes. The study procedure, questions, and associated scales, as well as participant responses, were communicated and collected verbally.

Part 1: Initial Questionnaire. RQ1-difficulty : After signing a consent form participants were asked to provide demographic information. They were then asked to answer the following questions using a 5-point Likert scale:

  • How confident are you in visiting new buildings, from (1) very low confidence to (5) very high confidence?
  • It is important to me to have a mental model of the layout of an unfamiliar building before visiting it, from (1) strongly disagree to (5) strongly agree.
  • I find it more difficult to build a mental model of the layout of multi-storey buildings than single-storey buildings, from (1) strongly disagree to (5) strongly agree. They were then asked why?

Part 2: Practice Session. Participants were presented with a sample 3DPM of a single floor and the 3D icons key to familiarise them with the study materials and procedure.

Parts 3 to 6 were repeated in turn with each of the three presentation conditions.

Part 3: Model Exploration. RQ3-touch reading strategy : Each model was placed in front of the participant and they were asked to explore it at their leisure. Their hand movements and manipulation of the model was recorded. For the rotate and slide model the floors were vertically aligned. In the case of the flat model, each floor was handed to the participant one at a time in a counterbalanced order. When participants were handed a the floor they were told its level and each floor was numbered. Participants were asked to arrange the floors however they liked and then to explain their choice.

Part 4: Model Recall. RQ2-accuracy : Once the participant indicated that they had finished exploring the model, they were requested not to touch it and asked six questions assessing the accuracy of the cognitive map engendered by the model. The questions involved points of interest located on different levels of the floor plans in order to assess cross-floor spatial knowledge. The questions and the use of multiple choice were based on previous work  [ 22 , 27 ]. Two multiple choice questions evaluated survey knowledge performance . For example:

  • “You are facing the main entrance, 12 o'clock is directly in front. What is the relative direction of the bookstore?”
  • “You have just stepped out of the L3 lifts. What is the relative direction of the toilets?”

Two multiple choice questions evaluated route knowledge . For example:

  • “Which of the two locations are separated by the shortest route using the lifts?”
  • “Which of the two locations are separated by the longest route using the escalators?”

Two multiple choice questions evaluated corresponding point identification across floors. For example:

  • “What is directly below the cafe on L1?”
  • “What is directly above the toilets on L2?”

RQ2-mental model : After this participants were instructed to describe their mental model of the building in as much detail as possible. An important aspect of multi-level cognitive maps is the extent to which floors are represented as separate entities as opposed to an integrated whole  [ 39 ]. To further investigate their cognitive map participants were asked to identify what best described their mental model of the building ranging from: “(1) I definitely visualise the building as a whole” to “(5) I definitely visualise the floors as separate entities” and why. Participants were also asked to rate the level of agreement with the following statements using a 5-point Likert scale:

  • I have a clear picture of the entire building layout in my mind.
  • The vertical connectors did not help me make cross-floor connections.
  • I found it difficult to understand the spatial relationship between the floors of the building.
  • The vertical connectors (escalators and lifts) enhanced my spatial understanding of the building.

Part 5: Spatial Understanding Tasks with the Model. RQ3-spatial understanding : Participants were then instructed to make use of the model to complete tasks to plan a route and identify points of interest that relied on cross-floor spatial understanding. We recorded whether participants identified an accurate route or corresponding point. Participants were not given a time limit for task completion and were able to make multiple attempts if needed. Immediately after each task participants were asked to rate the level of difficulty on a 5-point Likert scale from ‘very difficult’ to ‘very easy’ and were asked the reason for their answer.

RQ3-touch reading strategies : The participant exploration strategy was recorded and they were encouraged to describe the route aloud as they traced the route by finger, as well as verbalise any points of difficulty or ease. There were two route planning tasks, for example:

  • “Find a route that uses the lifts to get from the food court to the main entrance.”
  • “Find a route that uses the escalators to get from the cafe to the juice bar.”

And one corresponding point identification task, for example:

  • “Please describe what is directly above the cafe on L3.”

Part 6: Building Plan Usability and Engagement Scales. RQ3-usability : Participants were asked eight questions from the System Usability Scale [ 10 ], omitting the two questions relating to integration/consistency of the system as this did not make sense in the context of this study. The term ‘system’ was replaced with ‘building plan.’

RQ3-engagement : A short form of the user engagement scale (UES-SF)  [ 56 ] was used as a measure of engagement for the three presentations. The UES-SF consisted of twelve items related to the subscales: focused attention (FA); perceived usability (PU); aesthetic appeal (AE); and reward (RW). In scoring the UES-SF, PU items 1-3 were reverse coded. The scores were calculated for each of the four subscales as well as an overall engagement score for each presentation condition. The details of the UES-SF are listed in Appendix A.

Part 7: Post-Presentation Questionnaire. RQ3-preference : After seeing all three presentation conditions participants were asked the following to ascertain user preference:

  • “Rank the models in order of preference and the reason for each.”

RQ3-design feedback : Participants were then asked their level of agreement with the following questions using a 5-point Likert scale:

  • “Overlapped floors enhanced my spatial understanding of the building.”
  • “Non-overlapped (side-by side) floors enhanced my spatial understanding of the building.”
  • “The sliding mechanism was useful.”
  • “The rotating mechanism was useful.”

They were then asked if they had any ideas to improve any of the models.

RQ1-usefulness : Finally, participants were asked for potential use cases for the building models, whether they could see a use for such models, and if so, when and where. They were then asked for each of the presentations whether pre-journey access to this kind of model would make them feel more comfortable to visit the shopping centre.

5 RESULTS

5.1 Data and Analysis

Data consisted of participant task results, responses to scale questions, responses to open-ended questions, and observations. While we do report statistical significance the small sample size means that we are unlikely to find significance at p < 0.05 level for small effects. A recent review of quantitative empirical evaluations of technology for BLV people, found that 30% of studies use sighted participants to verify statistical significance  [ 11 ]. We have chosen not to do this because of potential differences between BLV and sighted participants [ 2 , 7 , 33 , 65 ]. Some studies chose to exclude statistical tests because of a small sample size and have turned to qualitative evaluations. Here we present both quantitative and qualitative findings.

Task completion time was recorded but is not reported as no significant difference was found between the models for any of the activities.

5.2 Thematic Analysis

Participants’ hands were filmed during interaction with the models and their answers to questions and comments were audio recorded. Open-ended question responses were transcribed and observational data was also transcribed into observational statements describing the interactions with the maps. A mixed approach of inductive and deductive thematic analysis was used. Specifically, three researchers identified areas of interest and generated initial theory-driven codes based on the study procedure and research questions, such as recall and cognitive load . These initial codes were used by the three researchers to independently code the transcripts of four participants. The codes were then reviewed, cross-checked and revised, with any discrepancies discussed and resolved through mutual agreement. The coding for the remaining transcripts was completed by a single researcher with validation by a second researcher. The coded data was examined, sorted, and collated by all researchers to generate a set of potential themes. These themes were then reviewed and refined. The final themes were: importance of 3D maps; touch reading strategies; cognitive mapping processes; navigation; usability; user engagement and preference; and model design.

5.3 R1: Would 3D models of multi-level built environments be valuable in O&M training?

In Parts 1 and 7 of the study we asked questions to probe whether models like those presented in the study would fulfill a need.

RQ1-Difficulty. Only one participant self-reported a very high confidence in visiting new buildings, while others either had low (4 participants) or neutral (4 participants) confidence. Eight of the nine participants agreed or strongly agreed that it was important to them to have a mental model of the layout of an unfamiliar building before visiting it while one participant reported this to be neutral (Figure 4 ).

About half of the participants agreed with the statement: “I find it more difficult to build a mental model of the layout of multi-storey buildings than single-storey buildings” (Figure 5 ). The additional cognitive load was explained by there being more to remember, the added challenges of floors with different layouts, and understanding the relationship between floors.

“It is often difficult to visualise how different levels relate to one another, and sometimes the correspondence that I expect is not there, so I get caught out.” [P9]

Two of those who did not believe multi-level buildings were more complex spoke confidently of their abilities to develop mental models:

“I can overlay the next levels in my mental map; I can build a 3D mental map.” [P6]

RQ1-Usefulness. After seeing each model eight of the nine participants strongly agreed or agreed that pre-journey access to building presentations such as the model they had just seen would make them feel more comfortable to visit the shopping centre (Figure 6 ).

“I would definitely know what to do based on this map.” [P9]

One participant (P8), however, disagreed with this as they reported that they struggle with complex buildings such as shopping centres, and that they would need to learn the space through O&M training sessions on site.

After seeing each model, participants were asked whether they could see a use for the building plan, and if so, when and where they can see a use. All participants agreed that there was a use for the building models.

“Yes, absolutely, everywhere.. definitely shopping centres, airports, office buildings, libraries, any multi-storey building accessed by the public.” [P2]

Again a Kruskall-Wallis H test did not reveal this difference to be statistically significant at the p = 0.5 threshold, χ 2 (2)=4.409, p=0.110, however, given the small sample size it suggests that there may be a trend. “I somewhat visualise them [floors in the flat presentation] as separate entities because they are not on top of each other.” [P4]

“I'm working out how they line up in my head.” [P4]

In contrast, with the overlapped presentations:

“I definitely visualise them as a whole because that's the way they are presented to me.” [P4]

However, another participant explained that whether the floors were presented as flat or overlapped, they still visualised the floors as separate entities:

“Each are an individual map in my head, I can picture those [escalators and lifts] over three levels but definitely its own separate map... each floor has its own rules and orientation, its own landmarks.” [P2]

Two participants explained that it was easier to visualise the floors as separate entities.

“For a more detailed map, you need to start breaking it down into different levels.” [P6]

The movement of overlapped floors also played a role in how participants visualised the building. One participant explained that in the same way they physically moved the floors for the rotating presentation, they too visualised the movement of floors when building their cognitive map.

“I was actually imagining the model and moving the levels out from above each other because that was a bit easier for me.” [P9]

RQ2-Accuracy. We posed six recall tasks (relative direction; route selection; and corresponding points) to test the accuracy of the participant's cognitive model. Accuracy was measured by the percentage of correct responses. While we expected to see an increased accuracy for the overlapped presentations, there was little difference between the presentation conditions (Figure 11 ). This suggests that despite any differences in the cognitive map engendered by the presentation method, functionally they encode equivalent information.

5.5 RQ3: How appealing and effective for spatial understanding are the models and how is this affected by the model design?

RQ3-Touch Reading Strategies. In Parts 3 and 5 of the study we recorded participant hand movements in order to understand how they moved the floors, how the floors were arranged at key moments during exploration, and their hand movements during tactual exploration.

Floor Movement and Arrangement: Participant movement and arrangement of the floors was thoughtful and directed to building the mental model (Part 3) or answering the spatial understanding tasks (Part 5). Participants constantly slid and rotated the floors to provide easy access with their hands. Participants often offset the floor of focus, while others offset floors above or below the floor of focus. Floors were often returned to their original position using key landmarks, such as escalators or lifts, that would match back up in the correct alignment. See Figure 12 .

The flat presentation offered flexibility as floors were not constrained by supports or frames. This allowed participants to arrange the floors in the way that felt most natural or intuitive. For example, floors were typically arranged from left to right, or front to back (Figure 13 ), in front of the participant, depending on what they thought was most logical.

“It makes sense, floor one is closest to me, then I go up to floor two, then I go up [to floor three]... It's logical for me to have it [the floors incrementally] further away.” [P1]

Interestingly, six of nine participants chose to overlap the floors of the flat presentation during the exploration and during spatial understanding tasks . For instance see Figure 12 (A).

“I think I did this [overlapped floors] a couple of times to check that things were aligning the way I thought they did.” [P9]

One participant clarified that this was to reduce the cognitive load of creating a cognitive map:

“It's really interesting, I'm trying to picture how I would get from one to the other, but without it being on top of each other it's harder. So I'm going to put them on top of each other.” [P4]

Hand Movement: All participants initially explored one floor at a time whether overlapped or flat, meaning that a multi-level cognitive map was built level-by-level. The common touch reading strategy with all participants, regardless of presentation method, was to explore each floor individually using two hands:

“It works better for me to work level-by-level.” [P1]

Once an understanding of each floor was gained, participants often proceeded to go back and forth between the floors to get a comprehensive overview of the building. At points during exploration, participants would seek to understand the connections between floors. This revealed the main distinction between exploration of the flat and overlapped presentations. With the flat presentation, participants would sometimes use separate hands on different floors in order to establish the connections between the two, however this appeared to be difficult and not all participants used this method. With the overlapped designs, however, participants frequently realigned the floors after having moved them for individual exploration, and used their hands to follow the escalators and lifts in order to establish the connection between floors, as demonstrated in Figure 14 . This would at times be hand movements up and down these landmarks, or two-hand cross-floor exploration.

“I really liked it, you can see what is exactly above and below other things and you can feel different floors at the same time because you can fit your hands in.” [P4]

All participants were able to complete the route planning tasks on the first attempt. Furthermore, the perceived difficulty of completing navigation tasks was similar across all three presentations, with each receiving an average rating of ‘easy’ in their SEQ scores (Table 3 ).

In Part 7, after exposure to all three presentation conditions, participants were asked to list the presentation methods in order of preference. Here, there was a clear preference for the overlapped presentations over the flat (Figure 15 ). RQ3-Design Feedback. In Part 7 participants were also asked a series of questions about the designs and what changes or improvements they would suggest. Participant responses indicate that both rotating and sliding mechanisms were useful, and overlapped floors received a higher score for enhancing spatial understanding than the flat floors (Table 5 ). They commented on how overlapped presentations guided the correct alignment, order and orientation of the floors as well as allowing displacement of floors to support touch reading strategies:

“It allows you to move things out of the way quite easily, which is good because trying to explore under the levels would be kind of cumbersome otherwise. They're never going to be out of order or oriented incorrectly, that's a really strong advantage.” [P9]

Ultimately, it appeared one key aspect of the both overlapping presentation methods was the ability to bring floors of focus in and out as needed:

“It's great. You can get rid of the noise. Break it down to levels. Having the option of the building overall is great.” [P6]

Preferences for the sliding and rotation mechanisms were mixed with participants finding pros and cons for both. When speaking about the rotating presentation:

“It's more intuitive to flick it away rather than slide it away.” [P1]

Other participants preferred the sliding mechanism. For some participants, they felt that rotating the floor out meant that they lost their spatial awareness as they explored the model. Some participants chose to not move the floors of the rotating presentation as they disliked that they lost orientation when the floors were displaced:

“That is going to mess with my head, I'd prefer to leave it as a whole [aligned] and try and feel in there than pull it out and lose orientation.” [P7]

Others liked how the floors would neatly slide into place with the sliding presentation. The use of a drawer slide mechanism meant there was a clear position in which the floors aligned in the correct position:

“You can just line them up so beautifully.” [P2]

Lastly, it was clear that the design of the vertical connectors, escalators and lifts, were effective at conveying cross-floor connections. Many participants made use of the escalators, voids at escalator-floor and TGSIs at the ‘landing platforms’ of the escalators as they indicated points of cross-floor connections. Indeed, for one participant, lining up the escalators was the first step in building a mental model:

“The first thing I'm going to do is check that I understand how the escalators line up. Oh I like that.” [P9]

The tactile arrows on the lifts were effective in indicating the direction of travel:

“Looks like a lift because it's got an arrow pointing up.” [P4]

Participants provided some suggestions for improving the models. Responses related to: orientation, specifically, indication of North direction [P5]; the addition of a stopper to the rotating model [P3]; the addition of audio labels at landmarks [P2]; and reduction in overall size for easier portability of the models [P9].

6 DISCUSSION

6.1 RQ1: Would such models of multi-level built environments be valuable in O&M training?

Our results were consistent with previous research [ 29 , 72 , 73 , 76 ]: BLV travellers lack confidence in visiting new buildings and it is important to have a mental model of a building before visiting. This reinforces previous work that has highlighted the urgent need for accessible indoor maps of buildings [ 16 , 17 , 52 ].

About half of the participants reported it was more difficult to build a mental model of the layout of multi-storey buildings than single-storey. The additional cognitive load was explained by there being more to remember, the added challenges of floors with different layouts, and understanding the relationship between floors. This is consistent with previous work highlighting challenges in cross-floor spatial understanding by sighted people [ 43 ].

Perceptions of usefulness of our designs was unanimous. Almost all participants reported that pre-journey access to such 3D models would make them more comfortable to visit a new shopping centre. Participants reported they could see a use for such models in shopping centres, public transport, blindness organisations and services, heritage and cultural centres, airports, and office buildings. When asked when access would be of use, the majority of participants preferred pre-journey access. This is no surprise, given many BLV people prefer to plan their trip to an unfamiliar building several days in advance  [ 16 ].

6.2 RQ2: What kind of cognitive map do the models engender and is this affected by the model design?

We found that the mental map engendered by the models corresponded to the multi-level cognitive map proposed by  [ 44 ]: there was a separate cognitive map for each floor; the floors were “glued together” by the vertical connectors which served as important landmarks with both an orientation and position; and a few participants described their overall understanding of the building structure such as irregularities in floor shape. This suggests that multi-level cognitive maps created by BLV people have similar structure to those created by sighted people.

Questions exploring the accuracy of the cognitive model built from the models did not reveal a difference. The recall accuracy for all three presentations ranged from 33.33% - 83.33% for relative direction, route selection and corresponding point identification. While accuracy is low, a comparison of RLMs with 3DPMs on similar tasks achieved a percentage accuracy of 28.8% and 54.9% respectively [ 22 ].

However, participant comments and their answers to how easily they understood the relationship between the floors and to what extent they visualised the building as a whole suggested that it was easier to build understanding of cross-floor relationships with the overlapping models and that the overlapping models led to a more holistic mental model of the building. Overall this suggests that the cognitive models engendered by the different designs were functionally similar, though it may have taken more cognitive effort to build the model with the flat design.

Prior research has found that BLV people tend to employ egocentric strategies when navigating in the real-world, mapping the spatial environment in relation to one's whole body or part of the body [ 58 ]. It was interesting to observe that a number of participants would often physically rotate their head, turn their whole body or lift their arm in the direction of which they would be facing when recalling a route or when exiting the escalators or lifts. One participant explained that moving their body helped their directional awareness:

“I keep on moving my body to work out, how I would be standing, I'm not very good at visualizing things without doing that.” [P9]

As a consequence of these egocentric strategies, BLV people tend to employ an egocentric reference frame when these cognitive maps are based on direct experience of the environment. However, cognitive maps based on tactile maps are predominantly allocentric representations [ 55 , 58 ]. This accords with our finding that the flat model (which is most similar to a traditional tactile map) predominately led to allocentric descriptions of the cognitive map (Table 2 ). However, with the overlapped presentations, the split between allocentric and egocentric descriptions was equal. More research is required to find out if this is an artifact of the small sample size or whether descriptions of the cognitive map and even the underlying cognitive map are, in fact, more egocentric with the overlapped models than the flat. It might be for instance, that a more realistic model leads to the participant more strongly imagining themselves in the space which leads to a more egocentric reference frame.

6.3 RQ3: How appealing and effective for spatial understanding are the models and how is this affected by the model design?

The results above indicate that all three model designs allowed the user to build an effective multi-level cognitive map. We also found that all three designs supported a similar high level of spatial understanding when the model was present. This included route planning and corresponding point identification.

The SUS and UES-SF scores were also similar for all three designs. However participant comments indicated that the overlapping models were regarded as more “fun.” This was supported by user preference which found that designs with overlapping floors were significantly preferred to the flat presentation.

Analysis of the touch reading strategies revealed that participants could effectively use the rotating and sliding mechanisms to focus on individual floors and then move the floors back to explore the vertical connectors. Design feedback was also very positive about the sliding and rotating mechanisms and how they allowed the user to bring floors in and out of focus.

What surprised us was that when using the flat presentation most participants in effect created an overlapped model by manually positioning the floors above one another in order to better understand the connections between them. This may help to explain the lack of difference between the cognitive maps engendered by the three designs.

In summary, our results suggest that 3D models of multi-storey buildings should facilitate both overlapped presentation of the floors and allow individual floor exploration. Both the sliding and rotating mechanisms worked well. Both supported cross-floor understanding and are preferred by users to a flat presentation. If for practical reasons this is not possible then providing individual “flat” floors will also be effective but it is important that users are free to manipulate the floors in an unconstrained way so that they can place the floors on top of one another or explore a pair of floors side by side. This is consistent with Wang et al. [ 72 ] which found that flat stackable floors could be used to create a mental model of a multi-storey building.

We also note that our study provided additional confirmation of the effectiveness of 3D printed icons in tactile maps [ 36 ]. We found that the design of the 3D icons representing the vertical connections between the floors–escalators and lifts–were readily understood and allowed participants to visualise the connections, including changes of direction.

“I do love the escalators because you can really see where you're going to pop out on the next floor.” [P4]

6.4 Limitations & Future Work

The number of participants in the study was limited as a consequence of repeated COVID-19 pandemic lock downs and restrictions. Furthermore, the participants are likely to be unrepresentative of the general BLV population as they were recruited from groups interested in tactile graphics. Future work would benefit from including a broader range of participants with a range of interest and exposure to tactile graphics. Nonetheless, the combination of qualitative and quantitative data has, we believe, provided insight into the usefulness and design of 3D printed building plans of multi-storey environments.

We created maps with reference to local shopping centres, in terms of size and the number of floors being modelled. However, scaling to buildings of greater size or with more floors and architectural complexity may present design challenges, particularly for the overlapped presentations.

Evaluations of the designs on real-life buildings will be of value. In particular, in-situ evaluation could lead to greater insights, especially with the ability to observe the application of the mental models engendered from the models for navigation in a complex real-world environment. Further, interviews with O&M instructors could lead to greater insights into how such materials could be used to support training.

Lastly, the design of the flat presentation allowed users to move the floors freely. We found that most participants chose to overlap the floors, and hypothesised that this helped participants better understand cross-floor relationships. Future work would be to evaluate this directly, comparing, say a flat presentations in which participants are free to move the floors and one in which the floors are fixed to the same base. We would also like to explore designs in which the floors are flat but can be stacked upon one another, similar to the approach used in [ 72 ]. Participant suggestions for improvement will be used to inform the production of future designs.

7 CONCLUSION

Our research is the first to provide a more rigorous evaluation into cognitive mapping and cross-floor spatial understanding with multi-level 3D maps. Through co-design with a blind colleague, we developed two novel designs that vertically overlapped the floors but allowed floors to be rotated or slid to the side. In a user study with nine BLV adults we compared these two designs with a more traditional flat presentation of floor plans. All three designs were reported to be useful, usable, engaging and allowed participants to build a cognitive map of the building. There was a strong user preference for the overlapped presentations, which were reported to be more effective in supporting cross-floor spatial knowledge. Our study also provides insight into the cognitive models BLV people create of multi-storey environments from tactile models. This exploration of the design space of 3D building plans demonstrates their value and we hope will encourage their provision in O&M training and availability in public spaces.
