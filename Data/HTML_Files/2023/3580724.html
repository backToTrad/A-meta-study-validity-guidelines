Visuo-haptic Crossmodal Shape Perception Model for Shape-Changing Handheld Controllers Bridged by Inertial Tensor

CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

We present a visuo-haptic crossmodal model of shape perception designed for shape-changing handheld controllers. The model uses the inertia tensor of an object to bridge the two senses. The model was constructed from the results of three perceptual experiments. In the first two experiments, we validate that the primary moment and product of inertia (MOI and POI) in the inertia tensor have critical effects on the haptic perception of object length and asymmetry. Then, we estimate a haptic-to-visual shape matching model using MOI and POI as two link variables from the results of the third experiment for crossmodal magnitude production. Finally, we validate in a summative user study that the inverse of the shape matching model is effective for pairing a perceptually-congruent haptic object from a virtual object—the functionality we need for shape-changing handheld interfaces to afford perceptually-fulfilling sensory experiences in virtual reality.

1 INTRODUCTION

A user wearing a head-mounted display (HMD) usually experiences virtual reality (VR) while holding a handheld controller as an input device. Handheld controllers also provide various haptic sensations to improve the immersive experiences. However, typical handheld controllers have constant shapes and sizes. This limitation can undermine the user's immersion into the VE. It can be particularly critical when the visual shape of the object represented by the controller changes abruptly and frequently, e.g., from a short gun to a long sword, then to a heavy imbalanced axe. We need to provide visual-haptic congruent sensory experiences, and researchers have proposed advanced haptic controllers called shape-changing controllers as a solution. Among the three modes of haptic perception (passive, active, and dynamic touch [ 25 ]; see Section 2.1 for details), shape-changing controllers emphasize the sensory experiences of dynamic touch .

In dynamic touch, a person holds an object in a hand and wields it to perceive the object's weight, size, or shape through minimal body motion [ 25 , 71 ]. In cognitive psychology, dynamic touch has been a topic of interest for a few research groups [ 10 , 36 , 37 , 49 , 50 , 51 , 53 , 61 , 62 , 69 , 70 , 71 , 72 ]. In interaction design, a few recent studies applied dynamic touch to designing shape-changing controllers of specific features [ 23 , 47 , 58 , 76 ]. Despite these valuable endeavors, this is still an overlooked research space to some extent. In particular, we lack general computational models that account for the users’ perception of object shape by dynamic touch and are also readily applicable to interaction design.

In this paper, we seek a visual-haptic crossmodal shape perception model for handheld controllers to provide congruent multisensory experiences to users in VR. For now, our investigation is primarily concerned with objects that have mass distributions projected onto a half-plane along the length direction when held in hand (see Figure 2 ), which covers a wide range of shape-changing interfaces. Based on the knowledge available on dynamic touch, we consider the moments of the mass distribution of an object, such as mass, a static moment, and a 3 × 3 inertia tensor (Section 3 ). The inertia tensor has three moments of inertia (MOIs; diagonal terms) and three products of inertia (POIs; off-diagonal terms; symmetric), so a total of six variables. We show that the primary MOI and POI are sufficient to represent the mass distributions of handheld objects under consideration by correlation analysis using a computer simulation (Section 4 ). Then, we demonstrate that the primary MOI, POI, and their interaction provide valuable information for the haptic perception of length and asymmetry of handheld objects in two perceptual experiments (Exp. 1 in Section 5 and Exp. 2 in Section 6 ), respectively. Both experiments measured human perceptual thresholds to validate the relevance.

These findings encourage us to use (the primary) MOI and POI as the variables to bridge the haptic and visual perception of object shape. We estimate a haptic-to-visual shape matching model by conducting another perceptual experiment (Exp. 3 in Section 7 ). Human haptic and visual perception are matched using a crossmodal magnitude production method parameterized with MOI and POI. Finally, we validate in the last experiment (Exp. 4 in Section 8 ) that the inverse of the shape matching model is effective for matching a perceptually-congruent haptic object from a virtual object. Given a virtual object, this inverse regression model predicts the configuration of a shape-changing controller that would afford congruent visual-haptic experiences. This is followed by a general discussion on the implications of our model and experimental results and the current limitations (Section 9 ).

The contribution of this work is the first proposal of a general visuo-haptic shape perception model for congruent multisensory experiences between virtual objects and physical controllers, which is practical for a wide range of physical shapes. Also, the haptic difference thresholds of MOI and POI measured for the length, and asymmetry perception of handheld objects are new scientific data to the literature on dynamic touch.

2 RELATED WORK

This section presents a literature review on handheld haptic controllers, visuo-haptic crossmodal perception, and difference perception of haptic stimuli. Studies about dynamic touch are pertinent to our work, and they are reviewed in Section 3 after a relevant theory of physics is explained.

2.1 Handheld Haptic Controllers

Haptic perception occurs in one or more of the three modes: passive , active , and dynamic touch . In passive touch , the perception occurs from skin deformation without movement of joints or muscles. In active touch , a person makes a voluntary movement and obtains haptic perception through both skin deformation and joint movement. From those two types of touch mode, the object's properties, such as volume, weight, and texture, can be obtained with a set of exploratory procedures (EP) [ 40 ]. Unlike the two previous modes, the last mode, dynamic touch , provides haptic information through minimal body movement, e.g., holding or wielding, without using EPs. For example, humans can obtain the length information of a handheld object by grasping the handle and wielding the object without direct exploratory movements of the entire object [ 71 ].

Existing haptic controllers provide haptic sensations with a specific focus on one or multiple perception modes. For example, controllers that have volume-changing handles [ 27 , 63 ] can render virtual objects of different sizes and shapes. The perception occurs in the passive touch as the handle stimulates the stationary palm holding the controller by inflating or deflating. A reconfigurable VR controller [ 75 ] can present various texture sensations to the fingertip in passive touch.

Controllers that provide force feedback, e.g., to the user's fingers for grasping sensations [ 12 , 41 ], generally work in the mode of active touch. Active touch is also dominant in a multifunctional handheld controller that supports a few haptic exploration interactions, such as touching, triggering, and grasping [ 13 ], and in a wearable haptic device on the hand simulating two-finger grasping with force feedback [ 60 ].

By varying their mass distribution, several haptic controllers leverage dynamic shape perception based on the dynamic touch. Zenner and Krüger [ 76 ] proposed a handheld controller containing a weight-shifting mechanism with 1D mass movement. Shigeyama et al. [ 58 ] improved the idea and designed a dynamic weight-shifting mechanism to render 2D shapes by moving two masses independently. The handheld controller in Tsai et al. [ 68 ] delivers multilevel oscillating forces in 3D using a heavy mass controlled by elastic bands and DC motors. Some other handheld controllers make use of powerful airflow to actuate the user's hand to convey the sensations of moving objects [ 30 ], weight shifting [ 29 ], or impact on a sports racket [ 67 ].

In this research, we assume the use of generic shape-changing controllers and focus on the haptic information perceived by dynamic touch when users hold an object in their hand and wield it freely. Then, we derive a computational method, which harmonizes the haptic information with the visual information displayed in a VE.

2.2 Visuo-Haptic Crossmodal Perception

Congruent multisensory experiences between vision and touch play a pivotal role in realizing highly immersive VR. When haptic experiences cannot be made physically identical to visual experiences, we can utilize sensory illusions. To this end, three visuo-haptic illusions have been mainly used: retargeting, redirection, and scaling [ 1 ]. Shape changing is related to the scaling illusion, and we review the previous studies that used the scaling illusion in what follows.

Ban et al. [ 6 , 7 ] changed the surface curvature of a visual object for the same haptic object to test the feasibility of the scale illusion. Dominjon et al. [ 16 ] presented an illusion about a virtual object's mass by changing the control-display (C/D) ratio in VR. Modulating the size difference between physical and visual objects generated different VR experiences. This work was followed up by Samad et al. [ 56 ], who designed a predictive model of the perceived weight by modulating the C/D ratio. Abtahi and Follmer [ 1 ] applied the scaling illusion to a tabletop shape display and showed its effectiveness for the shape-changing mechanism. Furthermore, controlling the C/D ratio varied the perceived range of 1D translation movements made with a physical slider [ 18 ] and the perceived region of the 2D circular movement of a grasped object [ 19 ].

Such illusion-based methods are convenient without requiring special hardware, but they also have inherent limits in the range of applications and the robustness of their effects. A fundamental issue that virtual objects are inconsistent in their visual and haptic impressions persists. A more permanent solution is to develop shape-changing interfaces and devise computational algorithms for visual-haptic matching. For the latter, we need to understand the general relationship between vision and touch for shape perception.

2.3 Difference Perception of Haptic Stimuli

Just noticeable difference (JND), or difference threshold, is the minimum amount of stimulus intensity change a person can detect between two stimuli. JNDs are used as a measure for the sensitivity of perception along with Weber's law, an empirical rule that JND is proportional to the reference stimulus intensity [ 74 ]. The ratio between two stimuli for difference detection is called a Weber fraction, and Weber fractions represent the sensory sensitivity to changes in external stimuli.

JNDs and Weber fractions for several haptic stimuli have been measured under different conditions. Johnson et al. [ 31 ] reported that the Weber fractions of thermal stimuli are 0.5–2.0%, and people are more sensitive to cold stimuli than warm stimuli. Weber fractions as to vibrotactile stimuli are 3–30% for frequency discrimination and 13–16% for amplitude discrimination [ 14 , 21 , 22 , 46 ]. Also, humans can perceive a change in pressure of 4–16% [ 64 ] and a change in the force of 7% [ 32 , 52 ].

The mass (or weight) requires a 5–15% change for difference perception [ 54 ]. The Weber fraction of MOI is 30% for the heaviness perception of a wielded object [ 39 ]. This type of knowledge is pertinent to the design of shape-changing controllers.

A good understanding of the human perceptual performance of dynamic touch is mandatory to validate the effects of shape changing. However, this requirement has been relatively overlooked while emphasizing the device designs. In this paper, we report two kinds of difference thresholds, parameterized with MOI and POI, for the perception of the length and asymmetry of handheld objects.

3 BACKGROUND ON SHAPE PERCEPTION

This section introduces the physics formulation used for dynamic touch research and then reviews the related literature.

3.1 Physics

Consider an object held in the hand and rotating around one fixed point (the center of rotation; COR), as shown in Figure 3 . The object's shape perceived by rotational motion, such as wielding, depends on three types of moments of mass distribution: the zeroth moment (mass; m ), first moment (static moment; M ), and second moment (moment of inertia; MOI) [ 8 , 9 , 20 , 36 , 49 , 70 , 71 ]. The three moments are expressed by

\begin{align} m = \sum _{i}^N m_i \boldsymbol {r}_i^{0}, \quad \textsf {M} =\sum _{i}^N m_i \boldsymbol {r}_i^{1}, \quad MOI = \sum _{i}^N m_i \boldsymbol {r}_i^{2}. \end{align}
(1)
where r i is the position vector of a point mass m i from the COR of the object ( i = 1, ⋅⋅⋅, N ).

Here, the zeroth moment is the total mass of an object. Mass alone does not have significant effects on length perception [ 37 ], but it may as an interaction term with the other two moments [ 36 ]. As the first moment, the static moment is the product of mass and the distance between the objects’ COR and center of mass (COM). The static moment affects the perceived length [ 9 , 37 ], but its effect weakens as the object's length decreases [ 37 ]. Lastly, the second moment, the moment of inertia, represents an object's resistance against angular acceleration. MOI has crucial effects on the length and shape perception by dynamic touch [ 10 , 11 , 36 , 37 , 49 , 51 , 53 , 61 , 62 , 71 ].

In physics, an object's second moments of mass distribution are generally explained using an inertia tensor. When wielding an object, a person obtains sensory information about its shape from the torque applied to the hand. It can be written as

\begin{equation} \boldsymbol {\tau } = \mathbf {I} \; \frac{d\boldsymbol {\omega }}{dt} + \boldsymbol {\omega } \times \left(\mathbf {I} \; \boldsymbol {\omega }\right), \end{equation}
(2)
where τ is the torque, I is the inertia tensor, and ω is the angular velocity [ 26 , 50 ]. The torque, a time-dependent variable of the object, delivers haptic sensations by inducing the change in the tensile states of the muscles/tendons and the skin deformations during the interaction. In contrast, the inertia tensor is a physical invariant of the object, and it should be related to a few physical properties of the object, such as length, shape, and weight, each perceived by dynamic touch [ 71 ].

As shown in Figure 3 , the inertia tensor is a 3 × 3 matrix, and each element I ij represents rotational inertia that occurs around the j -axis when rotating around the i -axis. The inertia tensor I consists of diagonal terms called the moments of inertia ( I xx , I yy , and I zz ) and off-diagonal terms called the products of inertia ( I xy , I xz , I yz , I yx , I zx , and I zy ). They are defined as:

\begin{equation} I_{xx} = \int _m (y^2+z^2)\ dm \quad \mathrm{and} \quad I_{xy} = I_{yx} = \int _m xy\ dm. \end{equation}
(3)
The other components are defined similarly. The inertia tensor is symmetric by definition.

The moment of inertia (MOI) represents the object's resistance against rotational velocity changes around the axis. The product of inertia (POI) is related to the symmetry (or imbalance) of the object's mass distribution on the axis. The two variables offer intuitive interpretations, as illustrated by Examples in Figure 3 .

3.2 Perception

Dynamic touch enables people to obtain several important properties of an object, such as length [ 10 , 11 , 36 , 37 , 49 , 51 , 53 , 61 , 62 , 71 ], width [ 69 ], heaviness [ 3 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 59 , 72 ], orientation [ 70 ], and volume [ 4 , 8 ]. Pertinent sensory signals result from skin deformation and muscle load, which depend on the object's mass distribution.

The perceived length, width, and heaviness are related to the object's MOIs, often following general Steven's power law [ 24 ]. Several researchers reported that the perceived length of a wielded rod is affected by the largest MOI ( I xx = I yy ) [ 10 , 11 , 49 , 53 , 61 , 71 ], the combination of the largest ( I xx ) and smallest ( I zz ) MOIs [ 36 , 51 ], and the static moment [ 36 , 37 ], regardless of the rod's actual length or density. The perceived heaviness of a long-wielded object depends on the object's I zz [ 3 , 38 ], static moment [ 36 , 37 ], and the asymmetry (perceived based on the product of inertia) [ 59 , 72 ]. The perception of volume is related to the smallest MOI [ 4 ]. In addition, the ratio of inertial indices (RII), a ratio between the largest and the smallest MOI, assists in comparing the volumetric shapes of symmetric objects [ 8 ]. The POI presents perceptual information about the asymmetry of a bent rod [ 70 ], but much less is known about this property.

Most of the above studies evaluated the effects of mass distribution on the perceived shape at the suprathreshold level using magnitude production methods. Contrarily, only a few results were published about the human sensitivity of length perception by dynamic touch [ 15 , 39 ]. Thus, understanding the human sensitivity, especially discriminability, of shape perception can support designing effective shape-changing controllers.

4 SIMULATION

In perceptual experiments, it is inconceivable to include all the physical variables related to the mass distribution of an object. Therefore, we conducted a computer simulation to examine the mutual dependence between the physical variables and identify the most critical one for haptic shape perception.

In this research, we consider the shape of objects that have mass distributions projected onto a half-plane along the length direction, as shown earlier in Figure 2 . Other examples are the three objects in Figure 3 a, b, and d. However, our results are not applicable to large 2D planar symmetric objects (Figure 3 c) or 3D volumetric objects (Figure 3 e). This limitation originates from our problem formulation that uses two variables, as described in this section. Extension to the full range of general 3D objects requires the inclusion of more variables and their interaction terms.

In the simulation, we used two cylindrical rods connected at a rotational joint, as shown in Figure 4 (a), where rod 1 is held in hand. This is the simplest but very effective configuration for changing the moments of mass distribution while preserving the mass itself. This model covers the mass distributions of a wide range of shape-changing controllers. For example, the model is fully applicable to the controllers that have mass moving along a 1D line [ 76 ]. It also accounts for half of the controller configurations that have mass moving on a 2D plane [ 58 , 77 ].

The simulation object has three variables: L 1 , L 2 , and θ . L 1 is the length of rod 1 from the rotating point to the joint. L 2 is the length of rod 2. θ is the joint angle between the two rods. The simulation had the following constraints: 1) a rotation occurs at the center of the bottom of rod 1 (the fixed COR); 2) the two rods have the same diameter (4 cm) and density (0.6 g/cm 3 ); 3) L 1 ≥ 10 cm due to the length for grasping; 4) L 1 + L 2 ≤ 50 cm as handheld controllers longer than 50 cm are not common; and 5) 90° ≤ θ ≤ 180°.

For simulation, we considered the static moment M related to COM and the inertia tensor I related to the rotational inertia of the object. M and I were computed using MATLAB while changing L 1 and L 2 by 1 cm each and θ by 1°. Then, we attended to the correlations between M and I , as shown in Figure 4 (b). Note that I xy = I yz = 0 for our simulation model (Figure 3 d). Three variables, I xx , I yy , and M , showed very high correlation coefficients over 0.96. I zz and I xz were also significantly correlated with a coefficient of 0.85. These two variable groups were rather independent, with low correlation coefficients below 0.42.

We also performed principal component analysis (PCA) on the vector space of the five variables related to moments. According to the scree plot shown in Figure 4 (c), two principal components (PCs) are optimal, with the variance accounting for 99.57%. The first PC represented 93.72% of the variance and the second PC 5.86%. In the PCA space shown in Figure 4 (d), the first PC was closely aligned with I xx and I yy , and the second PC was with I zz and I xz .

The above results allow us to select two variables, I xx and I xz , for studying the shape perception of the object model of two connected rods in Figure 4 (a). I xx is the primary MOI and a representative of the $\lbrace I_{xx}, I_{yy}, \textsf {M} \rbrace$ group. I xz indicates a POI for the asymmetry of object shape, and it represents the { I zz , I xz } group. I zz can also be selected as the second variable, but I xz is a better option for representing the asymmetry in handheld objects. In further studies, it would be feasible to incorporate I zz as a tertiary variable to cover the symmetry in the objects; however, in the current study, we focus on using two variables for simplicity. For simplicity, I xx and I xz will be denoted by MOI and POI in the rest of this paper. Note that MOI ( I xx ) and POI ( I xz ) are not completely independent with a low correlation coefficient (0.21 in Figure 4 a).

Figure 5 shows several controllers’ approximate MOIs and POIs to visualize the effects of physical objects’ shape on the distributions of MOIs and POIs. We made a simplified 3D model for each controller and computed its MOI and POI using SolidWorks with representative material properties. The visuo-haptic shape perception model that we will present in later sections covers the MOI and POI values in the large rectangle represented by violet lines. Four one or two-link cylindrical objects that have extreme MOIs and POIs (lengths and asymmetries) are shown at the rectangle's four corners. The controller of Oculus Quest 2 has very low MOI and POI as it is small and almost symmetric. The HTC VIVE controller has a relatively large POI value for its doughnut-shaped tracker. Shifty [ 76 ] is a shape-changing controller with one mass moving in the length direction. Its MOI varies in a large interval with very low POI (almost zero). Another shape-changing controller, Transcalibur  [ 58 ], has two moving masses, and its MOI and POI can be changed in a wide region. Drag:on [ 77 ] is specialized in modulating its POI by two fan-like modules.

Note that the two shape-changing controllers, Transcalibur and Drag:on, can change their mass distributions on the entire 2D plane, as in the objects depicted in Figure 2 (right). Using only MOI and POI cannot fully cover such shape-changing mechanisms, and it will require additional variables in the formulation.

5 EXP. 1: LENGTH PERCEPTION

The simulation results allowed us to choose MOI and POI as the candidate variables to link the haptic and visual perception of object shape. In this experiment, we aimed to confirm the importance of MOI for the perceived length of a handheld object. As reviewed in Section 3.2 , only a few studies investigated the human sensitivity of length perception using relatively heavy and long objects, which are not suitable for shape-changing controllers. In Exp. 1, we estimated the entire psychometric functions, which present the complete information about length discrimination. We also used considerably lighter and shorter objects representing handheld controllers. These results can be instrumental in determining the effective modulation range of shape-changing controllers.

The institutional review board of the authors’ institution approved all four experiments reported in this paper (PIRB-2021-E029).

5.1 Methods

We designed four experimental sessions. Each session shared one reference MOI and aimed to estimate a psychometric function for the probability of users distinguishing a comparison MOI from the reference MOI. The procedure was designed using the method of constant stimuli  [ 24 , 33 ].

5.1.1 Experimental Conditions. As specified in Table 1 , we selected four reference MOIs ranging from 5 × 10 4 g · cm 2 (representing small commercial VR controllers) to 30 × 10 4 g · cm 2 (large ones like a tennis racket [ 65 ]). This range covers the MOIs of almost all handheld controllers. Each reference MOI was paired with seven comparison MOIs (including the case in which the reference and comparison are the same). The comparison MOIs in each experimental condition were equally spaced on the linear scale. Pilot tests confirmed that the smallest (or largest) comparison MOI resulted in a discrimination probability close to 0 (or 1), as generally recommended [ 66 , 73 ].

We made physical objects that had the MOIs as specified in Table 1 . Each object consisted of a hollow aluminum rod, a 3D-printed handle, and two brass disks (Figure 6 , left). All aluminum rods used in each experimental session had the same length. The lengths were 30, 40, 50, and 50 cm, respectively, for the four sessions. The handles always had the same length (10 cm) and diameter (4 cm), as the handle diameter affects the perceived length [ 10 , 53 ]. We inserted the two brass disks into the rod and adjusted their positions to obtain the target MOI. The disk positions that lead to the target MOI were computed using MATLAB and confirmed by SolidWorks. This method preserves the total mass of the assembly. The physical rods made for session 1 are shown in Figure 6 , right. More details on the physical object design are available in the supplemental materials.

5.1.2 Procedure.

Participants sat in a chair and were blindfolded to remove all visual cues (Figure 7 ). Their task was to hold a physical object in their dominant hand and perceive its length by wielding it freely. For dynamic touch, free wielding provides better or similar perceptual performance than constrained wielding, e.g., pivoted at the wrist or rotating in only one direction [ 49 , 70 ]. Participants were instructed to grasp the object's handle comfortably and maintain the grip during wielding.

In each trial, a participant compared the perceived lengths of two objects, reference and comparison, while wielding them one at a time. The experimenter put each object on the participant's hand when requested. The participant could ask the experimenter to switch the objects without a limit. Finally, the participant verbally indicated which object was perceived longer.

Each session for one reference MOI consisted of six blocks of trials. Every session and block had an identical design. Each pair of reference and comparison objects had two presentation orders: first, the reference and then the comparison, and the opposite. Testing both cases for the seven comparison objects required 14 trials in each block. Their order was randomized per block. Consequently, the total number of trials in each session was 84 (6 blocks × 7 conditions × 2 repetitions; 12 repetitions per condition). The participants had a break every two blocks. This procedure took 50 min on average.

5.1.3 Participants.

The experiment had a between-subjects design. Ten volunteers participated in each of the four sessions. In total, 40 participants (31 males and 9 females; age M = 24.7 years and SD = 3.6 years; 35 right-handed and 5 left-handed) took part in the experiment. None of the participants reported sensorimotor abnormalities. Before the experiment, the participants were informed of the goals and procedures of the experiment based on a written document, and then they signed a consent form. Each participant was paid approximately USD 9 per hour after the experiment.

5.1.4 Data analysis. For each session, we regarded the first block of trials as practice and used the data collected in the rest five blocks for data analysis. We pooled all participants’ data and calculated the proportions of responses for “longer comparison object” for each experimental condition. Then, the obtained data were fit to a psychometric function of the cumulative Gaussian:

\begin{equation} \Phi (x;\mu, \sigma) = \frac{1}{2}\left(1+\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{x-\mu }{\sqrt {2}\sigma }\right)\right), \end{equation}
(4)
where 0 ≤ x < ∞ and erf(·) denotes the Gaussian error function. The two main parameters, mean μ and standard deviation σ , were estimated by the Levenberg-Marquardt nonlinear least squares algorithm [ 43 ].

From the psychometric functions, we obtained two values. One is the point of subjective equality (PSE), at which the reference and comparison objects are perceived to be equally long. PSE was determined from the 50th percentile of the psychometric function [ 33 ]. The other is JND (or difference threshold), defined as the distance between the PSE and the 75th percentile on the psychometric function. Then, we computed the Weber fractions using the standard formula:

\begin{equation} W = \frac{JND}{MOI_r}, \end{equation}
(5)
where W is the Weber fraction, and MOI r is the reference MOI.

5.3 Discussion

We investigated the human haptic discrimination performance of length by estimating psychometric functions for four reference MOIs. From the psychometric functions, we retrieved the JNDs and then computed the Weber fractions. The four Weber fractions were very similar at 7% regardless of the reference MOI.

We can compare our results with the Weber fractions published in related studies. For the perception of heaviness by dynamic touch, the mean Weber fraction for various reference MOIs was 28% [ 39 ], and the Weber fractions varied from 13% to 22% when the hand was subject to external force [ 15 ]. These Weber fractions of MOI are 2–4 times larger than our measurement of 7%. The differences can be attributed to two primary reasons. First, the task used in [ 39 ] and [ 15 ] was heaviness discrimination, while our task was length discrimination. Second, the two previous studies used reference MOIs larger than 30 × 10 4 g · cm 2 (tennis racket), but we used much smaller reference MOIs suitable for handheld controllers.

For further analysis, we can apply a formula of MOI for a simple cylindrical rod to an object's length to compare the relationship between MOI and the actual length. The formula is simple: MOI = ml 2 /3 where m is the mass and l is the length. The MOI Weber fraction of 7% means that people can detect a change of 3.4% in the object's length (1.07 = 1.034 2 ). This high sensitivity is comparable to the Weber fractions of visual length perception between 3.5% and 4.8% [ 48 ]. This finding is crucial as it suggests that the human haptic and visual sensory channels have similar sensitivity levels for the length perception of handheld objects. Therefore, users can notice the length difference between virtual and haptic objects with high sensitivity. The current practice of using the same controller for various VR objects may no longer be tolerable for high user experiences. This conjecture is partially confirmed in our final user study in Section 8 . All in all, we certainly need to use MOI to associate visual and haptic shape perception.

Our findings suggest that a difference of MOI of larger than 7% is necessary to perceive the length changes of handheld objects clearly. It should be noted that there are several factors that can influence the perception of length, and this conclusion is based on the specific conditions of our study. This is a new meaningful scientific finding for the design and control of shape-changing controllers.

6 EXP. 2: ASYMMETRY PERCEPTION

In this experiment, we move one step forward by considering curved (or bent ) physical objects—a component instilling imbalance and asymmetry. This configuration covers a wide range of shape-changing controllers. Using the method of constant stimuli, we estimated the psychometric functions of POI for five reference objects with different MOIs and POIs. We retrieve absolute and difference thresholds from the psychometric functions to understand the sensitivity of haptic asymmetry perception in relation to POI.

6.1 Methods

This experiment had five sessions. Each session had one pair of reference MOI and POI, aiming to estimate a psychometric function of POI for asymmetry perception to the pair of the reference MOI and POI.

6.1.1 Experimental Conditions. Table 3 shows the experimental conditions for the five sessions. Session 3 and 4 paired the same reference POI with the smallest and greatest MOI, respectively. Session 5 was conducted because we decided to test another intermediate reference POI (2.5 × 10 4 g · cm 2 ) after Session 1–4 had been completed. The intervals between the comparison POIs were selected by pilot tests to obtain the discrimination probabilities close to 0.5 for Session 1 and 2 and 0.0 for Session 3–5 when the POIs were low. They were chosen so that the discrimination probabilities are close to 1.0 when the POIs are high. These are required to use the method of constant stimuli.

6.3 Discussion

We quantified the human perception performance of object asymmetry (mass imbalance) by estimating the psychometric functions of POI for different MOIs.

6.3.1 Absolute Thresholds. The absolute threshold of POI was 0.18 × 10 4 g · cm 2 when the reference MOI r =5.0 × 10 4 g · cm 2 (Session 1) and 2.35 × 10 4 g · cm 2 when MOI r =30.0 × 10 4 g · cm 2 (Session 2). These POI thresholds correspond to 3.6% and 7.8% of the reference MOIs. Compared to the reference MOIs, the absolute thresholds of POI were very small. It means that people are very sensitive to an imbalance in mass distribution. Furthermore, the absolute threshold of POI increased as the reference MOI value increased. If the MOI increases, the object is perceived to be longer and heavier, and its center of mass feels located at a farther distance [ 36 , 37 ]. Thus, our results show that people may evaluate the extent of asymmetry by comparing the POI to the MOI of the object, and the sensitivity decreases as the MOI increases.

6.3.2 Difference Thresholds. The difference threshold of POI for the reference POI r =5.0 × 10 4 g · cm 2 was 0.29 × 10 4 g · cm 2 when MOI r = 5.0 × 10 4 g · cm 2 (Session 3) and 0.81 × 10 4 g · cm 2 when MOI r =30.0 × 10 4 g · cm 2 (Session 4). These JNDs correspond to the Weber fractions of W = 5.7% and 16.1%, respectively. Similar to the absolute thresholds, the difference thresholds increased with the reference MOI.

6.3.3 Thresholds at Large MOI. Comparing the thresholds obtained in Session 2 and 4, that is, POI r = 0 and 5.0 × 10 4 g · cm 2 for the same MOI r = 30.0 × 10 4 g · cm 2 , the thresholds decreased from 2.35 × 10 4 g · cm 2 to 0.81 × 10 4 g · cm 2 . It was rather unexpected, and we measured another difference threshold in Session 5 for the same MOI r but POI r = 2.5 × 10 4 g · cm 2 , which is the mean of the two POI r values of Session 2 and 4. The threshold was 1.22 × 10 4 g · cm 2 , and it was made clear that the difference threshold of POI decreased as the reference POI increased, as shown in the top-right panel of Figure 10 . This behavior does not conform to Weber's law. It may be attributed to the fact that the reference POI values were close to the absolute threshold of POI (2.35 × 10 4 g · cm 2 , measured in Session 2). In general, stimulus discrimination becomes more difficult as the reference stimulus intensity decreases to the absolute threshold [ 24 ]. For further verification, we will need to measure difference thresholds for larger POI values. Nonetheless, we decided not to pursue it because the maximum POI value used in the experiments, 5.0 × 10 4 g · cm 2 , is already very large for handheld controllers; see Figure 9 (left).

6.3.4 Summary. In summary, POI has prominent effects on the perception of mass imbalance of handheld objects, along with the strong interaction effect between MOI and POI. The human sensitivity of POI to object asymmetry seems to degrade with the increase of MOI, but it needs further validation with additional data. For instance, an interesting question is what happens to the perceived asymmetry if MOI is changed in a large interval given a POI.

Finally, combining the results of Exp. 1 and 2, the two physical variables of MOI and POI and their interaction account for the haptic perception of the length and asymmetry of handheld objects through dynamic touch to a large extent. Therefore, the two variables are appropriate candidates for the link to associate the haptic and visual perception of object shape.

7 EXP. 3: VISUAL-HAPTIC CROSSMODAL SHAPE PERCEPTION MODEL

This experiment aimed to build a visuohaptic crossmodal shape perception model expressed by

\begin{equation} \mathcal {F}: \boldsymbol {\phi } \mapsto \boldsymbol {\psi }, \end{equation}
(7)
where ϕ is the property of a physical object that determines haptic shape perception, and ψ is the property of a virtual object that governs visual shape perception. The model can suggest the best visual shape of a virtual object that matches the perceived attributes of a physical object, such as a shape-changing controller, by dynamic touch. Based on the results of Exp. 1 and 2 that MOI and POI have critical effects on shape perception, we use MOI and POI to compose ϕ and ψ so that the resulting model applies to general shape-changing controllers. This merit is unique to our model, extended from the previous approaches [ 23 , 58 ].

7.1 Methods

7.1.1 Participants. Eighteen volunteers (10 males and 8 females; age M = 24.5 years and SD = 3.3 years; all right-handed) participated in this experiment. The other aspects were the same as those of Exp. 1 and 2.

7.1.2 Experimental Conditions.

Nine experimental conditions were selected to cover various shapes of handheld controllers. We chose three MOI values of 5.4, 14.5, and 26.0 × 10 4 g · cm 2 and three POI values of 0, 2.35, 4.7 × 10 4 g · cm 2 , and then combined them factorially. The MOI and POI values were chosen to be sufficiently distinguishable at the suprathreshold level based on the findings of Exp. 1 and 2. We designed and fabricated nine physical objects with the nine pairs of MOI and POI, respectively, as shown in Figure 11 (left). All of the physical objects had the same handle diameter (4 cm). Their masses varied in a small range from 268 g to 286 g, which was negligible for the shape perception. More detailed information on the sample fabrication is available in the supplemental materials.

7.1.3 Task and Procedure. The experimental task was designed using the magnitude production procedure [ 79 ] for crossmodal matching [ 45 ]. Here, participants first perceive a haptic stimulus and then produce a visual stimulus of the same sensory magnitude.

Participants sat in a chair and wore an HMD (HTC VIVE Pro). They held and wielded a physical object with their right hand while watching a virtual object, shown in Figure 11 (right), displayed on the HMD. Participants had no visual access to the physical objects. The physical object was tracked using an optical motion capture camera (OptiTrack V120:Trio) and then presented to participants using the HMD as a virtual object that had the synchronized motion. Participants were asked to change the shape of the virtual object so that its perceived shape would match the physical object held in the hand. They could adjust the lengths of the lower and upper cylinders, L 1 and L 2 , and the angle between them, θ , using a VIVE controller held in their left hand. They selected a component to adjust using the trigger button and increased (or decreased) its length or angle by touching the trackpad's upper (or lower) area. The VE was implemented using the Unity game engine.

The experiment consisted of eight sessions, and each session had nine trials. In each trial, participants produced the virtual shape of one of the nine physical objects. The order of presenting the nine physical objects was randomized per participant and session.

We provided reference physical and virtual objects at the beginning of each session and whenever requested by participants. The physical reference objects, shown in Figure 12 (left), were 3D-printed (material ABS-100; 280 g). One object had only an MOI component (26.0 × 10 4 g · cm 2 ) with zero POI. The other had MOI=5.4 × 10 4 g · cm 2 and POI=4.7 × 10 4 g · cm 2 . These two resembled the two most extreme physical objects (top-left and bottom-right in Figure 11 ). The 3D models of the physical reference objects were used to visually render the virtual reference objects, displaying the identical visual shapes through the HMD; see Figure 12 (right). These reference stimuli, which had the same haptic and visual scales, helped participants stabilize their criteria of crossmodal matching.

On each trial, the initial shape of a virtual object was set to one of the two reference virtual objects to cancel out potential response biases. Participants were given a few minutes of rest between sessions. The experiment took 90 min on average.

7.1.4 Data Analysis. The first two sessions were regarded as training. The data collected in the next six sessions were processed and used for data analysis. The responses, L 1 , L 2 , and θ for the best matching shape were converted into the MOI and POI of the virtual object, denoted by MOI V and POI V , using MATLAB. The material properties of the physical reference objects were used for the computation. These MOI V and POI V were regarded as the output of the shape perception model in Eq. ( 7 ). The MOI and POI of the physical object, denoted by MOI P and POI P , were used as the input to Eq. ( 7 ). We averaged the data of each participant. Then, we found the best fitting function $\mathcal {F}$ by linear regression. For that, we tested several designs of the input vector ϕ using various combinations between MOI P , POI P , and their interaction term and various simple transformation functions.

7.2 Results

Figure 13 shows the MOIs and POIs of physical objects (left) and the MOIs and POIs of the corresponding virtual objects (right). We conducted a two-way ANOVA on MOI V using the two independent variables of MOI P and POI P . Both MOI P and POI P had significant effects on MOI V ( F (2, 34) = 60.52, p < 0.001 and F (2, 34) = 17.50, p < 0.001). The interaction between MOI P and POI P was also significant ( F (4, 68) = 3.81, p < 0.01). A similar ANOVA for POI V showed that MOI P and POI P had significant effects on POI V ( F (2, 34) = 7.85, p < 0.01 and F (2, 34) = 43.86, p < 0.001). However, the interaction term showed a marginal significance ( F (4, 68) = 2.22, p = 0.0765). These results provide evidence that MOI P , POI P , and their interaction term have crucial effects on MOI V and POI V , and they must be used for the input variables of regression models.

Our subsequent efforts to find the best regression model for haptic-visual shape perception resulted in:

7.3 Discussion

The haptic-to-visual shape perception model in Eq. ( 8 ) maps the MOI and POI of a physical object perceived by the haptic sense in dynamic touch to the MOI and POI of a virtual object perceived by vision. The use of MOI and POI, the two principal physical invariants for shape perception, as the crossmodal intermediary is the gist of our regression model.

7.3.1 Forward Mapping. As shown in Figure 13 , the three POI P values at MOI p = 5.4 × 10 4 g · cm 2 (the smallest) were aligned on a vertical line in the physical space of ( MOI P , POI P ). These three points were mapped onto an almost vertical line of similar MOI V values in the virtual space of ( MOI V , POI V ). At MOI P = 26.0 × 10 4 g · cm 2 (the greatest), the three POI P values, aligned on another vertical line in the physical space, are mapped onto a skewed line with an angle close to 135° in the virtual space. It means the best matched MOI V decreased with increases in POI P even though MOI P remained the same. This behavior indicates a strong interaction between MOI and POI, which is reflected in the regression model in Eq. ( 8 ) that has large coefficients for the term MOI P POI P .

In addition, in the case of three physical objects at POI P = 0, their virtual matches showed increasing POI V as MOI P increased. Increasing MOI for a physical object increases its perceived length and heaviness [ 36 ]. Our impression is that people may perceive the existence of imbalance in a physical object from its heaviness, although the object is physically symmetric. A similar interaction effect was also observed in Exp. 2. This interaction effect was independent of visual cues, and it requires more research for verification.

In this experiment, we could establish a well-defined regression model from haptic MOI and POI to visual MOI and POI for handheld objects. Despite the significant interaction term between MOI and POI, the model is sufficiently simple to allow for the computation of (local) inverses. We remind here that our shape-perception model is applicable to only the objects that have mass distributions on a half-plane along the length direction after projection.

7.3.2 Inverse Model for Visual-to-Haptic Matching. In applications, our haptic-to-visual shape perception model can be more fruitful in its inverse form. For example, suppose a VR scenario in which a character holds an object in its hand and operates it. The user perceives the object's visual shape. From the object's 3D model and material properties, maybe simplified ones, we can compute its MOI and POI. In our shape perception model, they correspond to MOI V and POI V . From these two variables, we can find MOI P and POI P by solving the inverse of our model in Eq. ( 8 ). Then, we can control the configuration of a shape-changing controller to have the MOI and POI of MOI P and POI P . This functionality of visual-to-haptic shape matching is expected to afford congruent multisensory experiences to the user. Our shape perception model in Eq. ( 8 ) is a second-order polynomial of two variables, and its inverse can be easily computed numerically with common equation solvers. Our inverse model can be applied to various shape-changing objects; see Section 9.2 for detailed discussion. The above procedure is depicted in Figure 14 .

8 EXP. 4: SUMMATIVE EVALUATION

Finally, we conducted a summative user study in VR to assess the validity of the visual-to-haptic perception model described in Section 7.3.2 . This experiment consisted of two phases: 1) visuo-haptic crossmodal matching and 2) evaluating user experiences.

8.1.1 Participants. Fifteen participants (3 females and 12 males; age M = 23.8 years and SD = 1.93 years; 14 right-handed and one left-handed) took part in this experiment. In Phase 1 , participants’ task was shape matching between virtual (visual) and physical (haptic) objects. The five virtual objects in Figure 15 (top panels) were used as visual objects. In each trial, participants held a physical object while looking at a virtual object displayed on the HMD. The five virtual objects were placed on a table (Figure 16 ). Participants selected a virtual object, wielded it (in the VE) by wielding the physical object held in their right hand, compared their visual and haptic sensations, and then evaluated how harmonious the sensory experiences were. Finally, they chose one virtual object perceived as the best match to the physical object in shape perception.

Phase 1 consisted of five sessions. Each session had 10 trials. The total number of trials was 50 (5 physical objects × 10 repetitions). The first session was regarded as training, and we used the next four sessions’ data for analysis.

8.1.4 Phase 2.

Here, we evaluated the user experiences of multimodal (visual and haptic) interactions in VR. We designed five virtual scenes in which participants were 1) hitting a tree with an axe, 2) cutting the bottom of a tree with a saw, 3) wielding a magician's wand, 4) slashing with a large sword, and 5) shooting a gun; see Figure 17 for screenshots and the supplemental materials for videos.

In each trial, participants experienced one of the five scenes while holding and wielding a physical object grasped in their right hand. The handheld object was one of three different physical objects: the best-matched objects BM to the virtual object in the scene, the mismatched one MM , or the HMD's original controller CTR (Figure 15 ). For each pair of the virtual and physical objects, participants scored Harmony , Immersion , and Enjoyment of their sensory experiences on a continuous scale of 0–100 by using slider bars displayed in the VE. The three measures were chosen referring to a haptic experience questionnaire [ 34 ].

Participants experienced the five VR scenes in a fixed order. Each scene consisted of three trials. In each trial, participants interacted with the VR scene with one of the three physical objects. The order of presenting the three physical objects was randomized per participant and VR scene. After completing the user study, participants provided free comments about the experiment.

8.2 Results and Discussion

8.2.1 Phase 1: Visuo-haptic Crossmodal Matching. The confusion matrix computed from the data collected in Phase 1 is shown in Figure 18 . The average percent correct (PC) score was 87.3%, indicating that the visual-to-haptic shape matching enabled by our inverse perception model was very effective.

Participants matched visual and haptic objects with small MOI (saw and wand; see Table 5 ) very well (accuracy 90%–100%). This finding is consistent with Exp. 2 that haptic objects with low MOIs have very large sensitivity (small JNDs) of shape discrimination regardless of POI. The identification performance was also good (80%–90%) for objects with medium or large MOIs but small POIs (sword and gun). However, the axe, which has both large MOI and POI, was confused with the sword (large MOI) or saw (large POI). This observation agrees with Exp. 2 that human haptic discrimination performance for large POI degrades quickly.

The above results were consistent with the participants’ qualitative comments. Three participants had difficulty in distinguishing heavy physical objects (axe and sword) with large actual MOIs from each other. Four participants could easily identify the relatively lighter physical objects (gun and wand) or the large imbalanced objects (saw), all of which had small MOIs. In addition, two participants felt it difficult to match the gun and sword because they had never experienced such real objects in their life. Nevertheless, they could respond to the questionnaire by using the visual shapes of the virtual objects and their associated haptic feels.

We should note that shape-changing controllers are unable to vary their mass itself in a wide range; they vary the distribution of mass. For example, a real axe can be heavier 5 kg, but no controllers are made as heavy. It seems that the participants were aware of this fact, either explicitly or implicitly, and focused on comparing the mass distributions between the visual and haptic objects, which led to good matching performances in Figure 18 .

8.2.2 Phase 2: User Experiences. The results of Phase 2 are summarized in Figure 19 for the five virtual scenes and the three types of physical object ( BM , MM , and CTR ). The average score of all three measures, Harmony , Immersion , and Enjoy , was 78.56 for BM (best match), 28.73 for MM (mismatch), and 32.64 for CTR (controller).

For statistical analysis, we conducted a two-way repeated- measures ANOVA on each measure with the two independent variables of Scene and Physical Object . Physical Object had a significant effect on every measure: Harmony ( F (2, 28) = 30.19, p < 0.001), Immersion ( F (2, 28) = 77.58, p < 0.001), and Enjoyment ( F (2, 28) = 57.61, p < 0.001). Scene also had a significant effect on every measure: Harmony ( F (4, 56) = 3.40, p = 0.015), Immersion ( F (4, 56) = 2.85, p = 0.032), and Enjoyment ( F (4, 56) = 2.98, p = 0.027). The interaction between Scene and Physical Object was also significant for every measure: Harmony ( F (8, 112) = 6.69, p < 0.001), Immersion ( F (8, 112) = 9.37, p < 0.001), and Enjoyment ( F (8, 112) = 4.59, p < 0.001). For further analysis, we performed simple effect tests on the data of each Scene for Physical Object . We also conducted Tukeys’ HSD test for significant cases. These results are represented in Figure 19 by grouping the conditions with no significant differences with the same letters.

The experimental results were clear: the best user experiences were observed for the physical objects designed by our inverse shape perception model, BM . We also examined the results of each VE to gain more insights focusing on Harmony (the scores of the three measures were highly correlated). The Harmony scores of BM were over 80 for all the scenes. 1) Wand: This object had a small MOI and POI. In this case, the commercial VR controller CTR , which had similarly small MOI and POI, provided a moderately high score of Harmony around 50; 2) Gun: Here, MOI and POI had both medium values. Harmony was moderately low around 40 for both CTR and MM ; 3) Saw and Sword: These objects had either small MOI and large POI (saw) or large MOI and small POI (sword). The other two haptic conditions were ineligible to represent the object shapes with very low Harmony ; and 4) Axe: The MOI and POI of this object were both large. Both CTR and MM resulted in moderately low Harmony around 40.

According to the participants’ subjective reports, two participants felt more unnatural disjunction between the visual and haptic experiences during interacting with mismatched physical objects in Phase 2 than Phase 1 (the simple wielding task). Four participants reported that the commercial VR controller was not harmonious with the heavy-looking virtual objects. These comments are another piece of evidence necessitating visual-haptic congruent experiences for dynamic interactive scenarios. Finally, one participant presented an interesting idea that adding auditory effects, such as wielding sound, can further enhance the immersive experience.

In summary, our visual-to-haptic shaping matching method 1) enables the accurate identification of a haptic object from a visual object ( Phase 1 ) and 2) improves the user experience of manipulating handheld virtual objects to a great extent ( Phase 2 ). In fact, it is quite a difficult and lengthy task for VR designers to find the best matching haptic configuration for a given virtual object. To do so, they need to iteratively change both MOI and POI and test it while wielding the corresponding physical object. Our inverse shape perception model can facilitate the design task by offering the best-matching MOI and POI, which has the role of a good starting point.

9 GENERAL DISCUSSION

9.1 Summary of Results

In order to provide highly immersive user experiences in VR, the users must be provided with multisensory stimuli that are congruent in every single aspect. For that, the shapes of virtual objects are of fundamental significance. In this paper, we built and presented a visuo-haptic crossmodal shape perception model for shape-changing handheld controllers. First, we conducted a computer simulation to find the most important physical variables for shape perception by dynamic touch. Second, we evaluated the human haptic discrimination ability of the length and asymmetry of a handheld object through two user studies. Finally, we built the visuo-haptic shape perception model and confirmed its effectiveness in VR through a summative user study.

Important results of our work can be summarized as follows:

  1. Two physical variables, the primary moment of inertia (MOI) and product of inertia (POI) in the inertia tensor, can account for the range of the mass distributions of the several shape-changing controllers (Section 4 ).
  2. The MOI of a physical object has a dominant effect on the perception of the object's length by dynamic touch (Section 5 ). The haptic sensitivity is as low as 7% of Weber fraction and comparable to visual length perception.
  3. The POI of a physical object significantly affects the perception of the object's asymmetry (Section 6 ). Also, the MOI and POI have considerable interactions in the asymmetry perception.
  4. A regression function can be estimated from the MOI and POI of a physical object to the MOI and POI of the virtual object best matched in VR by a crossmodal magnitude estimation procedure (Section 7 ). This function serves as a forward model for haptic-to-visual shape matching.
  5. For visual-to-haptic shape matching, the inverse model of the regression function is very effective (Section 8 ). Providing visual-haptic congruent sensations when manipulating handheld objects in VR improves user experiences to a great extent.

We expect our findings to improve the hardware designs and rendering algorithms of shape-changing handheld controllers to provide seamless and fulfilling VR experiences.

9.2 Applying Our Model to Shape-Changing Controllers

Additionally, we can review the classes of shape-changing controllers to which our visual-haptic matching model can be applied in reference to the existing taxonomy of shape-changing artifacts [ 35 , 55 ]. This taxonomy describes many representative features of shape-changing (or reconfigurable) interfaces obtained by analyzing many everyday objects. In particular, our model is useful for shape-changing objects that have: Stretchability [ 5 , 17 ], Curvature [ 5 , 17 , 28 , 58 , 78 ], and Modularity [ 5 , 42 , 57 ]. In addition, among the three types of Size , our model handles Length (1D) completely [ 23 , 58 , 77 ] and Area (2D) partially [ 44 , 58 , 77 ], but cannot account for Volume (3D) [ 5 ].

9.3 Limitations and Future work

9.3.1 Length Perception for Non-zero POIs. Exp. 1 studied the length discrimination performance for symmetric physical objects with zero POIs. However, Exp. 3 showed that haptic shape perception is significantly affected by an interplay between MOI and POI when the objects have large MOIs. This result may also be true for the length perception, as it is part of shape perception. It will be meaningful to study the human performance of haptic length perception by dynamic touch while considering the interactions between MOI and POI.

9.3.2 2D Planar Symmetric or 3D Volumetric Objects. Our research focused on the shape perception of objects that have mass distributions projected onto a half-plane along the length direction. However, as described earlier a few times, mass distributions of some shape-changing controllers vary over the entire 2D plane (area) [ 44 , 77 ] or 3D space (volume) [ 5 ]. Our present haptic-visual shape perception model does not cover such cases. In order to deal with such cases, our model needs an extension by adopting other physical variables of objects, such as the unused elements in the inertia tensor, static moment, or the center of mass, in a visual-haptic shape perception model.

9.3.3 Gender Balance. An early study [ 39 ] reported the effect of gender on inertia perception. In our work, some experiments had the numbers of participants unbalanced in gender. Gender effects in shape perception and its influence on congruent visual-haptic VR experiences will make important research topics.
