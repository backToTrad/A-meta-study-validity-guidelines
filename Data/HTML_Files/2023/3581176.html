Technology can be valuable for providing social and emotional enrichment for people living in residential aged care, but its use is difficult to sustain because of the complexity of the aged care environment. This paper aims to advance understanding of care environments to inform the sensitive design of technologies for social benefit in those settings. We conducted an ethnographic study in which the researcher assisted a care home's leisure and lifestyle team over four weeks. This included supporting the use of video-calling for social connectedness. A decrease in the use of video-calling and an absence of other technology for enrichment in the setting were observed. This paper identifies barriers to effective use of technology for enrichment and the interplay of cultural factors. For technology to best support residents and staff, we recommend key design considerations that target cultural, psychological and physical support and that ensure technologies, when used appropriately, better fit within the environment.

1 INTRODUCTION

The older adult population (aged 65 and over) is increasing worldwide [ 66 ]. In Australia, older people make up a considerable proportion of the nation-wide population (16%) [ 6 ]. This proportion is similar to those in the US [ 63 ]. Ageing results in physical, psychological, and social changes in the human body over time, particularly in later years of old age, which can include increasing frailty, limited mobility, and sometimes cognitive decline [ 66 ]. In Australia, when older adults can no longer live independently in their own homes, they are assessed to be admitted to residential aged care facilities (RACFs), also known as nursing homes. According to the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare's 2019 – 2020 statistics, over half of older Australians living in RACFs have dementia [ 5 ]. People affected by dementia and other age-related conditions might lose opportunities to take part in meaningful and enjoyable activities. Some people might face increasing loneliness as they get older due to reduced social networks and increased mobility constraints. This has been exacerbated by restrictions introduced during the COVID-19 pandemic which made it difficult for family members to visit RACFs. These difficulties have significant implications for the psychosocial wellbeing of residents, who have high rates of depression and anxiety [ 73 ]. In response to these issues, RACFs deliver several different social programs to promote the residents’ social and emotional wellbeing, including some technology-based activities [ 4 ; 9 ; 11 ; 52 ].

HCI researchers have begun to investigate the design and implementation of technology for enrichment in RACFs [ 12 ; 13 ; 15 ; 24 ; 25 ; 30 ; 45 ; 46 ; 48 ; 57 ; 58 ; 62 ]. In addition, disciplines such as gerontology have conducted similar research, investigating benefits and barriers to implementing technologies in this setting [ 26 ; 40 - 43 ; 64 ; 65 ; 70 ; 71 ; 76 ]. Research from both disciplines shows that when used appropriately, digital technologies can offer different kinds of social and emotional support. For example, facilitated video-calling can work well for connecting aged care residents to their loved ones [ 40 ; 61 ; 64 ; 65 ; 76 ]. However, one study on a video-calling innovation found that although this intervention was beneficial to the participating residents, not many of the participating RACFs were able to implement the innovation. Therefore, there was a call for further research to help improve intervention implementation in this environment [ 76 ].

Another kind of technology for enrichment in RACFs that has attracted growing research interest in the HCI community, as well as other disciplines, is immersive virtual reality (VR). This technology is usually adopted to give older adults with limited mobility, including those living in RACFs, the sense that they are travelling to another place or experiencing new activities [ 10 ; 29 ; 56 ], to support reminiscence [ 7 ; 71 ], or to provide a sense of fun and to help enrich social connectedness within the group in RACFs [ 18 ; 19 ; 51 ]. However, although VR has been found to be valuable for enhancing social and emotional wellbeing of aged care residents, prior HCI research shows that introducing VR to this population might encounter some ethical concerns (e.g. triggering unpleasant experiences [ 1 ; 67 ]), highlighting the need for care when designing and implementing technology-based experiences in this sensitive setting. Also, this technology has been found difficult to sustain in the aged care environment [ 60 ]. There is a need to better understand the challenges involved in using technology such as VR and video-calling, if they are to be used more widely to support social connectedness and enrichment in aged care homes.

In this paper, we report a study observing the daily activities of the residential aged care setting, identifying contextual issues inhibiting the adoption of technologies in residential aged care. We conducted an ethnographic study at a Vietnamese-Australian aged care facility. Since the COVID-19 pandemic happened, the facility has been deploying video-calling for residents to stay connected with their family. A participant-observer approach was chosen for this ethnographic study. One of us (the first author) worked as a volunteer assisting the leisure and lifestyle team, also known as the activity team, helping to run social activities in the home, including in the use of video-calling in the facility. Fieldnotes of observations and informal interviews with staff and the residents in the facility were the main data collection techniques.

This paper makes two contributions to research related to the use of technology for enrichment in residential aged care. Firstly, drawing on the fieldnotes, we provide insights into issues related to activities offered within the aged care home including the current use of technology for social and emotional enrichment. Particularly, although video-calling has been adopted since the pandemic, it was observed and reported with a decrease in use after visiting restrictions were lifted. Furthermore, other technology for enrichment was observed being absent or reported abandoned after a short-term use with limited numbers of residents having used it. Subsequently, we identify barriers to using technology in the aged care home that might contribute to the limited use and unsustainability of technology for enrichment in this setting. We highlight the importance of cultural factors to the life and social and emotional well-being of residents living in the care home. Secondly, drawing on these insights, we discuss key considerations to inform the future design and deployment of technology for enrichment in RACFs to meet the cultural, psychological, and physical needs of residents and to support the work of aged care staff, better fitting within the aged care environment. Below, we review key relevant research, followed by details of our methodological approach. We then present our findings of a thematic analysis of our fieldnotes before discussing key considerations for the design of technology for enrichment in RACFs.

2 RELATED WORK

This study builds on research on the design and implementation of technology for enrichment in residential aged care. In this paper, we use the term technology for enrichment to refer to technology used in activities as part of the social program offered for aged care residents in order to bring joy and open new opportunities for social engagement. This aligns with similar uses of the term in existing research on technology and ageing [ 29 ; 59 ; 68 ]. The foci of previous research on technology for enrichment in aged care are varied, ranging from exploring effects and/or design issues of newly-developed technology for enrichment in RACFs [ 10 ; 24 ; 30 ; 36 ; 40 ; 41 ; 45 ; 46 ; 56 ; 57 ; 64 ; 65 ; 71 ; 75 ], to introducing and evaluating its feasibility for implementation in RACFs [ 8 ; 12 ; 15 ; 25 ; 26 ; 28 ; 43 ; 48 ; 58 ; 76 ], and investigating issues brought about by the deployment of the bought-in technology for enrichment in this setting [ 13 ; 42 ; 55 ; 60 ; 62 ; 70 ]. Below, we review key relevant research and explain how this paper contributes to a growing body of research on this topic.

2.1 Positive effects of technology for enrichment in RACFs

Meaningful engagement and social connection play an important role in maintaining the wellbeing of older adults [ 31 ; 44 ]. However, for people living in residential aged care, it is difficult to maintain social connection and continue participating in meaningful activities. There has been significant growth in innovations to promote social and emotional wellbeing of aged care residents. These innovations have included applications that are photograph-based [ 2 ; 24 ], sound-based [ 30 ], multi-sensory [ 36 ; 41 ; 57 ; 71 ], or focused on social connectedness [ 40 ; 64 ; 65 ; 75 ; 76 ]. These innovations have been found beneficial in enhancing social connection and helping address loneliness, depression and anxiety of aged care residents. For example, Tsai et al. [ 64 ] reported that in comparison with a group of residents who received regular care only, there were significantly higher levels of social connectedness and reduced depressive and loneliness scores reported in the group with the use of video-calling (e.g., Skype) to interact with their family five minutes per week over a period of three months. The benefits of using video-based communication tools are especially apparent in cases when older adults’ families are living overseas or not living in the same town with them [ 61 ; 65 ; 76 ].

Houben et al. [ 30 ] studied a sound-based innovation in aged care facilities for four weeks. The innovation, called Vita, is a pillow embedded with a speaker that plays different general everyday sounds (e.g., a dog barking, a bike ride, raindrops on a window, etc.) and personalized sounds in line with residents’ preferences. Their interviews with caregivers who were involved in using Vita found that everyday sounds provided by the innovation helped to stimulate meaningful conversation, playfulness, and connection between aged care residents and caregivers [ 30 ]. Similarly, Gaver et al. [ 24 ], built the Photostroller, which is “a waist-high, portable unit whose most noticeable features are a large screen, a removable control unit and rounded handles and wheeled legs”, for supporting residents in engaging with the world. In their research, they found that the innovation was successful in providing resources for playful discussion, curiosity, sharing anecdotes, and reminiscence among aged care residents [ 24 ].

There has been increasing research designing and evaluating technology that enables aged care residents to virtually explore the world outside of the aged care home, with technologies integrated with multisensory modalities rather than limited to sound and/or photographs only. An example of this kind of innovation is VR. Moyle et al. [ 41 ] introduced a non-immersive VR, known as Virtual Reality Forest, which uses video game technology, involving vivid graphics with background sounds, and motion sensors, to create interactive and virtual environment on a large screen. The study found that this innovation had positive effects on aged care residents with cognitive impairments as it reduced their apathy and increased their pleasure and level of alertness. The effect in increasing older adults’ joy was also found in the study on immersive VR by Hodge et al. [ 29 ]. Similarly, the effect of immersive VR in reducing apathy of aged care residents was demonstrated in studies by Brimelow et al. [ 10 ] and Saredakis et al. [ 56 ]. However, although technology for enrichment shows benefits, it can be challenging and difficult to sustain in the long term [ 60 ]. Below, we review some issues raised by the implementation of these technologies in RACFs.

2.2 Implementation of technology for enrichment in RACFs

Research has identified several facilitators and inhibitors to the adoption and use of technology for enrichment in RACFs. These include individual issues, such as age-related changes and impairments in cognitive and physical abilities of the residents [ 25 ; 48 ; 76 ], cultural influences and digital literacy [ 48 ]. In addition, organizational issues can have an impact, including normalization of the innovation as part of regular on-going activities provided in the home [ 28 ; 76 ], and staff concerns about the safety of the technological device [ 76 ]. Notably, for almost all the kinds of technology deployed in RACFs, staff support is needed during the activities [ 8 ; 12 ; 25 ; 26 ; 28 ; 76 ]. Furthermore, there are limitations with the technology itself, including sound and visual quality, and usability of the device [ 76 ].

Research by Zamir et al. [ 76 ] on a video-calling featured technology, called Skype on Wheels, shows that although this innovation was beneficial to the residents who participated in the study, only four out of the eight care homes, which had accepted to participate in the study, were able to implement the innovation [ 76 ]. Among those who participated, there was considerable lack of engagement from families due to busy schedules, time zone differences if living abroad, or technical issues with their own devices [ 76 ]. Similar difficulties in engaging family members in the video-based communication programs and their digital literacy issues were found in research by Neves et al. [ 48 ]. Family commitment to video-calling has significant impacts on its implementation and sustainability in aged care settings [ 48 ; 76 ]. The family commitment issues were also reported as one of the causes of residents’ disinterest in the idea of using the innovation [ 76 ].

The challenges of implementing technology in RACFs are highlighted in research evaluating its real-world use outside the context of research trials. Sacco et al. [ 55 ] found that aged care residents were more satisfied with the use of video calls to communicate with their relative compared to the use of telephone calls. In contrast, Moyle et al. [ 42 ] found that video-based communication (e.g., Skype) was uncommon and the telephone was the primary form of communication used by residents to contact their loved ones. Moyle et al. [ 42 ] found that one key issue that contributes to the limited uptake of web-based communication technologies within RACFs is insufficient internet access. This challenge is also highlighted in research on VR for enrichment [ 62 ]. Prior research identified varied barriers to the implementation of such technologies including resource constraints within the aged care settings [ 13 ; 60 ; 70 ], the need to select devices and apps that are more appropriate to aged care residents [ 60 ; 62 ; 70 ], and limited staff [ 70 ] especially those who are experienced with the technology [ 60 ]. These suggest that significant changes should be made within the organizations to accommodate the technology [ 70 ]. However, in some cases, changes might not be practical within this environment. In order to accommodate the technology, other approaches should be considered, for example, adapting the technology to better fit with the residents and the residential aged care environment [ 60 ; 62 ].

Building on the growing body of research concerning the adoption and implementation of technology for enrichment in the residential aged care setting, in this paper, we draw on an ethnographic participant-observer study to advance understanding of the complexities associated with the use of technology in residential aged care. In this ethnographic study, the first author participated in the care home environment on a daily basis for four weeks. The home had recently adopted video-based communication technology to help residents stay connected with their family during the pandemic. We aimed to seek better insights into the natural setting of the care environment in order to inform the sensitive design of technologies for social benefit that are better aligned with those settings. In particular we were interested in understanding issues brought about by the use of technology for enrichment in general, and video-calling in particular, in the care environment. However, we found that the care home was not making extensive use of technology for enrichment. Our research therefore focused on identifying the social-technical barriers that might contribute to the challenges in the implementation of technology for enrichment in RACFs. Below, we detail how ethnography contributes to research in this field and how it is utilized as a methodological approach in this study.

3 RESEARCH DESIGN

We conducted a four-week ethnographic study in a residential aged care home, aiming to develop deep and insightful understandings of the natural settings of RACFs and to identify contextual issues to support the design and implementation of technology for enrichment in RACFs. The study was approved by the university's human research ethics committee. The methodological approach of this research was informed by methods described by Lazar et al. [ 33 ] and Neuman [ 47 ]. Ethnography is recognized for gaining insights into the complex social issues that shape human-technology interactions and it is considered as the preferred approach for studying technology introduced into complex social systems [ 27 ]. For instance, Foley and colleagues involved ethnography in their research to gain an understanding of the lived experience and inform the design for and with people living with advanced dementia [ 20 - 22 ]. There are important steps in an ethnographic study including selecting an appropriate research site, choosing a role to play when immersed in the site, and collecting data for research purposes [ 33 ; 47 ]. We describe this methodological approach below.

3.1 Selecting the research site

The research site was chosen on the ground that the organization committed to supporting the goals of the study and the site was accessible by the first author. Importantly, we chose to study a group of people within the cultural community of the first author: Vietnamese-Australian. Cultural or socio-economic differences between researchers and research participants can create a divide and make it difficult for ethnographic researchers to become fully immersed in the research setting [ 33 ]. Furthermore, given Australia's population includes many people who were born overseas or had parent(s) who were born overseas and speak a variety of languages (known as culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) populations), studying groups of people with CALD backgrounds also contributed to this selection. Below is a brief description of the selected research site – Daiviet Home (pseudonym).

Daiviet Home is an over 60-bed RACF established by a not-for-profit and statewide organization, offering a full range of residential care services located in Victoria, Australia. The facility was purposely established for the Vietnamese-Australian older adult community. At the time of this study being conducted, almost all the aged care residents living in the facility were Vietnamese. Some residents had arrived in Australia as refugee immigrants after the end of the former government in South Vietnam (known as the Republic of Vietnam) since 1975 and some arrived later with their children's sponsorship. A few of the residents are from other countries (e.g., Laos and Timor-Leste) or other ethnicities (e.g., Chinese Vietnamese).

Organizationally, the ground floor of the facility consists of three house blocks, namely House A with 21 rooms, House B with 11 rooms, and House C with 11 rooms. The other 22 rooms are located on the upper floor of House A (namely House U) accessed by a lift. Each House has its own dining area. The dining areas of House A and House U are usually used for organizing group activities or occasional events (e.g., Mother's Day celebration). The facility has gardens including for spiritual contemplation located in the middle area of the facility and accessible from all three blocks.

Most of the residents living in Daiviet Home have a variety of health issues and need high care. Besides dementia, residents have movement difficulties, and vision and/or hearing impairments. Almost all residents need mobility aids, in either the form of a walking stick, a walking frame, a rollator walker, a wheelchair, or a chair bed. Some of the residents need hearing or visual aids. They have different interests and preferences in participating in social activities. Some of the residents prefer group activities, some prefer individual sessions such as singing, walking around the garden, etc. Some of the residents living with dementia have agitation and aggression. In some cases, this is managed with diversional therapy. For example, dolls are sometimes used to help residents stay calm. In nursing practice, dolls are found to be helpful for people with dementia by increasing levels of engagement with others and reducing challenging behavior [ 38 ; 39 ]. Notably, almost all the residents need a care staff member to support them whenever they move around the home.

3.2 Participating role

A participant-observer approach was chosen for this ethnographic study. The researcher contacted the facility and obtained permission from the management to work as a volunteer, assisting the leisure and lifestyle team (the activity team) in the home. The volunteer work took place every afternoon from Monday to Friday, over a continuous period of four weeks from mid-April to mid-May 2022. Each session was 3 to 3.5 hours long (61 hours in total). In order to work on the site, the researcher met all requirements established by the government and the facility, working only the days when there was a management team member present, and performing all required daily procedures (e.g., getting all required vaccinations, testing for COVID-19 every day, wearing a facemask and a face shield during work, etc.).

The activity team ran a variety of group as well as individual activities including arts and crafts, playing bingo and card games, garden watering, reading books, Vietnamese roll dice, cooking, baking, singing, doing Tai Chi on wheelchairs (often called Chair-Chi), outings, shopping, celebrating residents’ birthdays, talking about residents’ achievements, playing volley balls, quizzes, practicing Buddhist praying, practicing Catholic praying, etc. The activities were planned monthly, and the residents were informed about the activities at the start of the month. Each month there were special occasions to inform the activities, for example, Easter Days, Vesak Day, Anzac Day, Mother's Day, etc. The activity plans were translated into Vietnamese for Vietnamese residents. The group activities typically took place in the communal dining area of House A and House U. The researcher assisted the activity team during his volunteering period where appropriate. Furthermore, as talking to individual residents was part of the social support programs, when group activities were finished, the researcher engaged with residents by individually talking with them and hearing them reminisce about their life.

3.3 Data collection

The study involved observations and informal interviews with the carers and the residents, with the hope of triangulating results from multiple data collection activities. The informal interviews were conversations or contextual inquiries, which were part of the ongoing relationship between the researcher and the participants. That is, while working as a volunteer in the home, the researcher might ask people questions to clarify things that were not clear from the observation and might discuss issues of concern where appropriate. We also used “probes” to provoke reaction and spark conversation about the adoption and use of technology in the facility. These included smart phones, iPads, and VR headsets. For example, if there were situations where residents needed to see photos during a conversation, the researcher used either his smartphone or his iPad to show them to the residents and then shifted the conversational topic to the use of technology. Similarly, when talking about social activities with activity staff members, the researcher mentioned technology-based activities such as the VR program and later demonstrated a VR headset, asked them to try it on, and then had conversations about the experiences. The goal of these informal open and unstructured conversations was generally to get people talking in a natural setting in order to increase the breadth and depth of our understanding of related issues within the site.

The researcher recorded daily fieldnotes to capture key insights from the observations and conversations with aged care staff and residents. These notes were taken immediately after each event and after leaving the setting (where applicable) and were reviewed after completion of each volunteering day. Additions were made to the fieldnotes when the researcher remembered something afterwards. In addition, photographs and videos of some social activities organized in the home were recorded with verbal consent provided by the facility management representative and the people being filmed for data analysis purposes.

In this paper, we use pseudonyms with prefixes to refer to the participants: R-[pseudonym] refers to the resident, A-[pseudonym] refers to the activity staff member, C-[pseudonym] refers to the care staff member, F-[pseudonym] refers to the family member, P-[pseudonym] refers to the physiotherapist. 3.4 Data analysis

Data included fieldnotes, photographs, and videos recorded during the volunteering period. The fieldnotes were originally taken in Vietnamese and they were translated into English by the first author for data analysis. We conducted inductive thematic analysis. Following this approach, we did not try to fit the data into a preexisting coding frame or our analytic preconceptions [ 50 ]. Our analysis focused on identifying findings within two overarching categories: understanding current activities within the home and understanding complex issues of the care environment that are barriers inhibiting the effective use of technology for enrichment in the aged care home. Codes were refined into themes specific enough to be discrete and broad enough to capture a set of ideas contained in the notes. The first author conducted the data analysis. Initial themes were discussed among the research team until a consensus was reached about the final themes to be included in this paper.

4 FINDINGS

The data analysis focused on two main categories of findings: understanding current activities within the care home and understanding barriers that might prevent effective use of technology for enrichment in the home. However, through our analysis, we found that culture especially plays an important role in the life and social and emotional wellbeing of Daiviet Home residents. Thus, the interplay of cultural factors is presented as a separate subsection. Figure 1 illustrates the relationship between the categories and the themes that emerged from the analysis.

4.1 Current activities within the home

We identified two key themes relating to this category: (1) Limited use of technology for enrichment within the home; and (2) Importance of varied, but low-cost, social activities.

4.1.1 Limited use of technology for enrichment within the home. At the time of our study, the only digital technology used was video-calling to connect residents with their family. However, video-calling was not often used as the residents’ family would prefer in-person visits. Over the four-week period, only eight residents’ contacts were connected to the facility's iPads, looking to communicate with the residents. Prior to the study, however, video-calling had been in much wider use due to restrictions on family visits during COVID-19 lockdowns. An activity staff member, A-Thuan, said:

“In the past, there were only iPads for the administration office. After the pandemic, the IT office just bought more devices, I don't know if they were new devices or old ones, but at that time, their family called a lot. Now, after the restrictions were lifted, most of their relatives come directly to visit, if they test for 1 line [negative result], they are able to come in, so the calls are less often now compared to several months ago” (Fieldnotes, 26/04/2022).

Similarly, another activity staff member, A-Thanh, mentioned:

“Before you come here, it was very busy at this time (3:00 PM – 5:00 PM). The calls were continuously coming. We run from House A to House B, House C, and then went upstairs. There were a lot of calls. If you were here at that time, you could help a lot. Currently, it's fine. Just receiving a few calls. Sometimes there are no calls all day” (Fieldnotes, 28/04/2022).

These reflections highlight the decrease in use of video-calling and emphasize the influences of the government's restriction regulations on the increasing adoption of video-calling technology in RACFs during the pandemic. As keeping residents safe from the COVID-19 disease is important, the video-calling is an alternative medium for family to “visit” their relatives when the restrictions are in place and as staff members reflected, it was not often used once the restrictions were lifted.

To ensure residents’ general safety, staff were always present to support residents in any activities. This need also applied to technology-based activities in residential aged care in general [ 8 ; 12 ; 25 ; 26 ; 28 ; 76 ] and video-calling sessions in this care home in particular. However, privacy was a special concern during video-calling sessions in the home. Though the residents needed a facilitator for assisting and facilitating video-calling, this challenged the privacy of their calls. In order to ensure privacy of conversations between the residents and their family, aged care staff might choose not to be there with the residents. Activity staff A-Linh said:

“Usually when receiving a call, we carry the iPad to the resident's room, we place it on a table or a chair where the resident can see it, and we leave it with the resident, then go out. We also have to ensure privacy, so it's not appropriate to stay in the room when the resident is talking. We might return in about 15 minutes to get the iPad, or the care staff might help us to take it and put it in the nurse station” (Fieldnotes, 02/05/2022).

The researcher had a similar experience during his volunteering period. Upon receiving a call from a family member, F-Luong, looking for resident R-Thuong, the researcher carried the iPad to the resident's room. When seeing the researcher entering the room and telling her about the call, the resident asked for help from the researcher: “May you help lift me upright?”. “Sure”, the researcher responded, then asked the resident: “Where can I place this device?”. The resident pointed to her rollator walker surface next to her bed and said: “Please put it there” (Fieldnotes, 19/04/2022).

The researcher placed the iPad on the rollator walker and stayed in the room to see if the resident needed any more help. However, the resident was staring at the researcher to give a signal for him to leave. The researcher then excused himself, left the iPad with the resident, and left the room, giving her a private space to talk. After about 20 minutes, care staff C-Hue, who had just helped another resident living next to resident R-Thuong took the iPad out of R-Thuong's room and gave it to the researcher.

During the study, the researcher brought along a VR headset and asked activity staff A-Thuan if he would like to try it on with the researcher's support. A-Thuan agreed and put the headset on, watching a 360-degree video recording of the Opera House in Sydney. After removing the headset, they had a conversation about the experience. When asked about the suitability of VR technology for the residents, A-Thuan said:

“This technology is good at letting the residents travel virtually to destinations where they couldn't travel to. It is good with the COVID-19 situation. However, I think this might not be suitable for some residents with vision impairment and those with motion sickness. They might not feel well when wearing it. I have just experienced dizziness immediately take it off. That is a good technology, but I think it might not be suitable for many of the aged care residents…Relating to technology, previously, there was a device, the Wii, which let people exercises and virtually interact through a screen, but there was only one resident, R-Thien, engaged in that activity. Though he engaged, but only in a short-term, a couple of weeks, he was then bored; so, we have not used it anymore” (Fieldnotes, 11/05/2022).

This quote indicates that the home previously used another technology for enrichment (the Wii), but it was not adopted by many residents and its use was not sustained in the home. Furthermore, the activity staff member seemed hesitant about the technology. He compared VR with the previous technology that had been introduced but had not been used for long, suggesting that any previous innovations could be used as a baseline to compare with suggested new technologies. Furthermore, the role of the activity team as “gatekeepers” in selecting social activities to be introduced in the home was emphasized. The activity staff also seemed to presume that the residents would experience the technology in the same way that he had experienced it. As a “gatekeeper”, he would choose not to introduce the technology that he believed would incur harm to the residents. This decision was repeated in a farewell session on the last day of the researcher's voluntary work in the home, suggesting that first-hand experiences of the activity staff member are critical to the decision to adopt VR in the care home.

4.1.2 Importance of varied, but low-cost, social activities. The care home offered several different activities. Many of these were low-cost social activities such as singing, playing cards, and crafting. It was important to have a variety of activities on offer because residents have different interests and preferences. Some of the residents preferred singing and rarely participated in other activities. For example, when resident R-Tran was asked about playing cards, he said: “I am not interested in those group activities, I am only keen on singing” (Fieldnotes, 11/05/2022). For this reason, every week, the activity team would organize singing sessions. Similarly, resident R-An was also interested in singing. She used a handheld reading magnifier to read and sing the songs in the music album when the home was celebrating special events run by the activity team.

Some of the residents (e.g., R-Duong) preferred composing poems. For every special event, such as birthday parties or Mother's Day celebrations, he composed and read his own poems to the audience of other residents and staff (see Figure 2 ). Other residents preferred crafting. For example, resident R-Dinh was observed singing and smiling while cutting paper, although sometimes the paper he cut fell to the floor and he needed help from staff to pick it up. While instructing resident R-Dinh to do crafting (see Figure 3 ), activity staff A-Linh said:

“Resident R-Dinh really likes this activity. I usually organize this activity for him. He likes cutting the paper cards and I will fold them and stick them to make, for example birthday cards. He uses scissors well. However, we need to explain to him what we are doing and for which purposes, for example for celebrating the Mother's Day; we need to explain clearly. If we do not, they won't really engage. Some other residents also preferred to participate in this activity, for example resident R-Nga or resident R-Mi. While they are doing this, we need to provide them with guidance” (Fieldnotes, 04/05/2022).

Although social activities offered in the home needed to be varied, they also needed to be affordable. Due to limited funding, the activities needed to be selected based on the monthly budget. When the researcher mentioned about several residential aged care homes having adopted VR for enriching their residents’ experiences, activity staff member A-Thuan said:

“We do not have much fundings for social activities. Every month, we have been funded $ 110 [AUD] for organizing the activities within the month. Meanwhile, we bought a birthday cake to celebrate residents’ birthdays which costed around $ 50. For the remaining funding, we used it to purchase flowers serving religious practicing, snacks and drinks as small awards for giving residents after playing social games. That's all. If we need more, we must get it from other activities, such as opening the groceries shop every Thursday and earned from those. We can get a few more money to buy some other occasional stuff. So, we have to think about activities, which need to be not only varied to serve different interests and preferences of the residents and help residents less feeling bored, but they also need to be low-cost” (Fieldnotes, 22/04/2022).

Similarly, when the researcher asked about organizing social activities and funding, activity staff member A-Thanh mentioned:

“Working as the activity team, we must be creative. We have to think about a variety of activities that can serve different residents. Some residents do not like group activities, for example, resident R-Tran, he likes singing, so we had to buy an Electronic Piano Keyboard to serve some residents like him. Resident R-An also like singing. To buy it, we took money that we earned from the groceries shop. The activity team does not have much funding, so we need to think about the activities that make residents happy but don't cost a lot of money” (Fieldnotes, 26/04/2022).

4.2 Barriers to using technology effectively in the home

Key barriers to using technology effectively in the home have been categorized into three themes: 1) Residents' enthusiasm for technology did not align with their capacity to use it; 2) Medical conditions and personal preferences prevented technology use; and 3) Residents’ traumatic experiences were often recalled, even with simple triggers.

4.2.1 Residents' enthusiasm for technology did not align with their capacity to use it. Some residents were enthusiastic about using technology such as smartphones or tablets. For example, resident R-Somphone, usually took his smartphone out of his rollator walker and looked at it while he was sitting in the common lounge. He was a Lao-Australian resident and rarely communicated with other residents or staff, possibly due to the language barrier. The smartphone appeared to give him some comfort. However, for many other residents there was a mismatch between their enthusiasm and their ability to use the technology. Despite enthusiasm, other residents in the home had limited knowledge of and capacity to use the smartphones and tablets. For instance, when seeing the researcher carrying an iPad and swiping its screen, resident R-Tran suddenly went to his room to take his phone and showed it to the researcher. Although it was a non-touchable keyboard flip phone, he tried to use his finger to swipe the screen in the same way. On another day, he took his mobile phone out of his rollator walker storage and pressed the phone keypad while having dinner. However, the phone did not work due to incorrect pressing. Similarly, while having a conversation with the researcher, the resident's phone was ringing. He immediately picked up the phone. However, he pressed the incorrect key, so the ringtone was terminated.

Likewise, resident R-Duong owned a smartphone provided by his family. During dinner time, his phone was ringing. Embarrassed, he took the phone out of his rollator walker storage. However, because he took a long time to take the phone out, the ringtone had stopped by the time he retrieved the phone. He said to care staff C-Minh that his phone was having problems and did not work. C-Minh examined the phone and explained to him that his phone was working okay.

Residents were not aware of what an iPad is and what it is used for, but some were interested in trying it. When the researcher asked resident R-Dang whether he used to use iPads, the resident was confused “What is that?”. The researcher then showed the resident an iPad. The resident said: “I have never used this device, I used to have a mobile phone, but I left it for my wife at home” (Fieldnotes, 27/04/2022). Interestingly, however, the iPad provided an opportunity to reconnect with past interests and activities. During a reminiscing conversation with the researcher, the resident mentioned “Đồng chiêm” which is a kind of rice fields in Vietnam and the resident asked the researcher whether he knew it. The researcher then used his iPad to google for “Đồng chiêm” in front of the resident and showed a photo of “Đồng chiêm” to him. The resident looked interested in the iPad and suddenly used his finger to swipe the iPad's screen, which the researcher was holding. However, he swiped it in the wrong way, which made the photo disappear.

After finishing a group Chair-Chi activity, activity staff member A-Thuan and the researcher had conversations in front of the residents about the use of technology in the home. A-Thuan looked at resident R-Hang and said:

“Previously, there was a tablet provided by NDIS to resident R-Hang who is the youngest resident in Daiviet Home. However, from my observation, she did not use it”.

The activity staff looked at resident R-Hang and asked: “Do you still keep the tablet?”.

The resident responded with confusion: “What is that?”.

The activity staff explained. “The tablet, the one that looks like what K [the researcher] is holding”,

“What? I think Lucy [pseudonym of her daughter] is keeping it”, the resident responded.

“Oh, it is in my room, I used it sometimes to watch ‘Paris by Night’”, the resident said, about 30 seconds later (Fieldnotes, 13/05/2022).

Notably, another resident (R-Tuyen) believed an iPad to be a mirror. After she finished a video call with her family, she looked around outside of her room and asked for help when seeing the researcher nearby. She asked, “Hi there, please get this mirror out. Thank you” (Fieldnotes, 04/05/2022).

On another day, when receiving a call from a family member, F-Bao, who wanted to speak with resident R-Huong in House C where there had recently been a COVID-19 outbreak, the researcher carried the iPad to the resident. A care staff member C-Lieu working there then took the iPad and helped set up it on the resident's table and explained the calls to the resident. The iPad was not facing the resident, which meant the family could not properly see her. However, the resident was not aware of this problem until the family asked the staff to adjust the iPad's camera so that they could see the resident.

4.2.2 Medical conditions and personal preferences prevented technology use. Some residents in Daiviet Home do not use technologies due to their complex medical conditions. One resident (R-Huyen) had been provided with a smartphone and a tablet by her family, but she told the researcher that she cannot use the devices for longer than half an hour because of her health conditions. She said:

“I can't use electronic appliances; I'm dizzy at a glance. I can't look at the screen of smartphones and tablets, I can't look at it for a long time, let say maybe 30 minutes. I have an ear infection near my brain, so the doctor said I can't do surgery” (Fieldnotes, 03/05/2022).

Residents preferred the devices to be lightweight. During the group activity, when the resident (R-Huyen) saw the researcher hold an iPad with a slim cover, she said “This is light. Mine is big and heavy. This one is lighter so it's more convenient to use” (Fieldnotes, 05/05/2022). Notably, to resident R-Huyen, creating tangible objects (see Figure 4 ), rather than using technology, would help her feel happier and reduce her boredom. During the conversation, she told the researcher:

“I do not like using technology such as smartphones or tablets. I had them here [in the rollator walker storage]. My medical conditions are just part of the reasons. I preferred doing some things physically touchable, like painting, and cutting orange skins or straws to create artwork. I also like gardening... Those things make me feel happier and less bored while I am living here” (Fieldnotes, 22/04/2022).

This quote indicates that while some residents are enthusiastic about technology, others are not. Although she owned different kinds of technological devices, we did not observe R-Huyen using them or even taking them out of her rollator walker storage until the researcher talked about technology. Interestingly, although personal preferences are varied, during our study, we observed that male residents were more likely to be enthusiastic with the technology than female residents. For example, R-Tran, R-Duong, R-Dang, and R-Somphone, who were enthusiastic with smartphones or iPads and R-Thien who was engaged in the Wii game mentioned by the activity staff, are all male residents. Meanwhile, R-Huyen, R-Hang, R-Nga, and R-Mi, who were observed to be less enthusiastic with the technology and/or reported preferred doing crafting and artwork are all female residents. However, due to a limited number of participants and exceptions (e.g., resident R-Dinh), further research is needed.

4.2.3 Residents’ traumatic experiences were often recalled, even with simple triggers. Many residents had experienced trauma in their lives and these traumatic experiences were often recalled with simple triggers. Notably, a resident (R-Phuong) often told the researcher about her traumatic experience which led her to go into early labor and deliver a son who was born with disabilities. On one occasion, the researcher encountered the resident who stored a lot of snacks, fruits, and soft drinks in her rollator walker storage. The researcher asked about those things, and the resident said:

“Those things are for my son; he is living with disabilities. Because I have early labor during the pregnancy. I climbed a chair to turn on the light and accidentally fell. That made me have early labor and delivered him. Poor him... Thinking about him makes my eyes fill with tears. I keep these for him. I do not want to let him in because I am afraid that he won't go back home. He wants to stay here” (Fieldnotes, 02/05/2022).

On another occasion, after finishing a group activity playing Vietnamese roll dice, the activity team gave the prizes to residents who won the game. However, when the researcher gave resident R-Phuong a bag of snacks, she said with slight tears in her eyes: “He [her son] won't eat snacks, could you please give me a coke. Poor him. He like drinking. He won't eat this. Could you give me a coke?” (Fieldnotes, 28/04/2022). After being given a can of coke, she put it in her rollator walker storage and came back to her room with care staff. Although her son's disability might not have been caused by the accident that induced early labor, the resident appeared to believe this is what caused her son's condition. The story about her early labor and her disabled son was repetitively mentioned every time she talked with the researcher.

Resident R-Huyen told the researcher that she had a traumatic experience related to the death of her ex-husband with the Communist Party of Vietnam during the Vietnam War. Every time she heard, or someone mentioned words related to the Vietnamese Communist Party, she was usually angry. In particular, in a conversation with the researcher, activity staff member A-Thuan said that resident R-Huyen is sensitive to words which are normally used by the Vietnamese Communist Party. He demonstrated by an example:

“One time, as a care staff member does not know about resident R-Huyen's sensitivity to some special words, the care staff asked the resident R-Huyen to borrow a pen but using the word “Cán bộ 1 ” to refer to herself, the resident was then angry and shouted to the care staff that “Cán bộ” are those who killed her ex-husband” (Fieldnotes, 13/05/2022).

These examples reveal that residents’ traumatic experiences were recalled, even with simple triggers related to the traumas. Meanwhile, research shows that some technology (e.g., VR) can trigger negative emotional responses and fears and they could elicit users’ trauma [ 16 ]. Likewise, in aged care settings, care staff have been found to be concerned about virtual experiences offered as part of social programs that could provoke their residents’ traumas. For example, virtual beach and underwater experiences might be frightening for some residents who had a particular fear of water. These concerns might make care staff especially cautious about introducing the technology for this population [ 67 ].

4.3 The interplay of cultural factors

Our study shows that cultural factors played a vital role in the life and social wellbeing of Daiviet Home residents. Culture has been defined as “the customary beliefs, social forms, and material traits of a racial, religious, or social group” [ 37 ].

The social activities organized in the home were integrated with and influenced by the Vietnamese culture. For instance, during the Chair-Chi activities, the activity staff member, A-Thuan, used Vietnamese-friendly music. He said that made the residents more interested in the activity. He elaborated:

“The social activities organized here were very much influenced by the Vietnamese culture, for example, we organize the Vietnamese roll dice game, playing card, bingo, etc. because the residents are very familiar with those things. Some residents who do not participate in the games might watch television shows, we opened some music shows such as ‘Paris by Night 2 ’ for them to watch. The residents pretty much like watching them. Understanding Vietnamese culture is very important in organizing social activities. I think with the young generation, despite Vietnamese, who was born here [in Australia] might not work well in this environment” (Fieldnotes, 11/05/2022).

Similarly, activity staff member A-Thanh said:

“Social activities need to be Vietnamese friendly. Besides playing Vietnamese roll dice, bingo, and cards, we often organize some other activities such as cake baking, opening coffee shops, groceries shops, and street hawker activities. Because in Vietnam, those are very popular and residents here are very familiar with them. Previously, there was one non-Vietnamese guy who come to work as an activity staff with us, he organized a lot of activities that have Western styles similar to other Australian nursing homes, but not many residents were engaged. After a short time, he left the facility. There were also several young guys who came to do trial work here, but then they left” (Fieldnotes, 28/04/2022).

On another occasion, a resident shared a belief that came from her Vietnamese culture. When seeing the researcher walking past her room, the resident (R-An) asked the researcher for a favor. She told the researcher to come to her room, looked for a device on her bed, and asked:

“Could you tell me what is this device? It is not good in the Vietnamese culture to put some devices like this on the bed. Could you please take it out?” (Fieldnotes, 04/05/2022).

In fact, many Vietnamese people believe that placing electronic devices, including smartphones and tablets, on their bed head is not good as it would impact their sleep and brain functions. The researcher looked and found that the device was simply a handheld controller that was used to adjust the bed positions. He then explained to the resident and reassured her. She was not worried anymore. The resident then happily had conversations reminiscing about her life stories with the researcher.

Food was an important part of the culture [ 32 ]. Care staff member C-Hoa said that food influenced residents’ choices in this care home. Despite different racial, religious, and social groups, sharing culinary culture made some residents who were not Vietnamese choose Daiviet Home. She said:

“Previously, we used to have some Cambodian residents and currently, we also have a couple of Timor-Leste residents who are both over 100-year-old and an over 80-year-old Lao resident. They decided to live here because of our food culture. It is Asian style food, which is familiar with them” (Fieldnotes, 20/04/2022).

During conversation with the researcher, the resident R-Huyen often talked excitingly and interestingly about the cuisine that had been common in the southwest region of Vietnam where the resident and the researcher both came from. The observation revealed that every time she was offered meals with food that have ingredients familiar with what was available in her hometown, she was happily eating. Otherwise, she was not happy about the food being offered. Moreover, she often did some kinds of pickles made from vegetables such as gai choy or bitter melon and kept them in small jars for her meals. These kinds of pickles are, in fact, popular in the southwest region of Vietnam and her hometown.

Through the conversations, we found that some other cultural factors could also help with residents’ happiness and influence the kinds of activities people engage in. In Vietnamese culture, older adults normally live with their children's families until the end of their life. Resident R-Huyen said:

“Living with family with children around is happier. In Vietnam, people live with their children until their final breath, but it is the culture here, that older adults living here must go to nursing homes. Our children are busy with their jobs, and we gave our house to the government, so we must stay in nursing homes” (Fieldnotes, 29/04/2022).

She also mentioned:

"I really don't want to come here. I want to live with my children and grandchildren for the rest of my life. It's sad and boring here." (Fieldnotes, 02/05/2022).

Furthermore, many behaviors and interests of the residents with dementia living in the home were explained by cultural influences. For example, care staff member C-Thu said:

“The residents who hold dolls are all following the doctor's recommendation. The doctor said they need doll therapy, for example. The reasons are that those residents used to take care of their grandchildren in the past. In our Vietnamese culture, every time their children have babies, they invited their mother to take care of the babies and also when they are back to work. Then, when they get older, they keep in mind about taking care of their grandchildren, and they think that the dolls are their grandchildren. They always look for them when they were lost” (Fieldnotes, 20/04/2022).

Activity staff member A-Thuan also said:

“Many of the residents living in this nursing home used to... [play] cards when they were young and healthy. They keep remembering them… That is also the reason make us organize playing cards as weekly social group activity” (Fieldnotes, 22/04/2022).

However, this background information about residents' interests was not always captured. Our observations and interviews revealed that the home primarily captured information about residents' physical and dietary caring requirements, rather than information about their life histories. Some of the aged care staff revealed that staff working in the home are aware of the complex conditions of residents, but they might not know exactly what had happened to the residents during their life courses. Care staff member C-Thu said:

“There are care plans, every day we will base on the plan to give care. In the care plan, there will be indicating what issues of each resident and how care should be provided. Everything is there” (Fieldnotes, 20/04/2022).

Meanwhile, care staff member C-Hoa shared:

“At the time of handover, if there are any issues with the resident, staff will be informed by the previous shift staff. Normally, all staff are aware of regular issues of the residents. We know about the health conditions of the residents, for example, their hearing impairment, vision impairment, their interests, their preferences, etc. When we work from time to time, we know about these things; however, in terms of their background or what happened to their previous life, we do not know much. We do not actually know why they like doing these things” (Fieldnotes, 19/04/2022).

According to Brown [ 64 ], “a language is a part of a culture, and a culture is a part of a language”. Staff members who were able to speak Vietnamese would therefore be preferred by residents living in Daiviet Home. For instance, during a conversation between resident R-Huyen and the researcher, a Vietnamese physiotherapist, P-Phuc, came to the resident to do some physiotherapy sessions. When they were finished, the resident told the researcher:

“There are three physiotherapists who often visit us here for physiotherapy sessions that last about 15 or 20 minutes. However, the other two physiotherapists are not Vietnamese; one probably is Indian; they cannot speak Vietnamese and they do not know much about Vietnamese culture, so residents living here only prefer Ms. P-Phuc” (Fieldnotes, 05/05/2022).

5 DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS

This ethnographic study provided insights into activities organized for enhancing the social and emotional wellbeing of residents as well as the current situation of use of technology for enrichment in an Australia-based residential care home. We found that varied, but low-cost, social activities were important. Video-calling was deployed temporarily to deal with the visiting restriction issues during the pandemic. Other technologies for enrichment have not been adopted or were reported abandoned after short-term use with limited numbers of residents having used them. This finding is consistent with prior research, which indicated that technology-based activities, particularly VR [ 60 ] or video-calling [ 76 ], are challenging to be sustained for long-term use within RACFs. The unsustainable adoption and use of technological innovations for enrichment in this setting mean that many technologies that could provide benefit end up unused in a cupboard. On the one hand, it may be that the benefit the technologies offer does not outweigh the barriers to longer-term use. On the other hand, if there are significant benefits, then researchers need to understand how the barriers can be overcome and how technologies can be best designed and deployed for longer-term sustained use in this setting.

Through this study, we identified the complexity of the aged care environment that might contribute to the limited uptake of technology for enrichment in this setting. We found that cultural factors played a vital role in supporting the psychosocial wellbeing of residents living in the care home. Many aged care residents are likely to have experienced trauma in their lifetimes, especially if they come from countries that experienced war. Some residents are sensitive to trauma triggering events, and their traumas were often recalled even with simple related triggers. This is important, as some kinds of technology-based experiences can provoke residents’ traumas (e.g., VR [ 67 ]), resulting in concerns about residents’ psychological safety. Some residents were enthusiastic about the technology, but they had limited knowledge of and capacity to use them. In addition, residents in the home might choose not to use technology due to their medical conditions. In this section, we discuss how the design of technology-based activities can be adapted to this setting so that technology can be used for enrichment more successfully, in a way that culturally, psychologically, and physically best supports aged care residents.

In addition, technologies used for enrichment in RACFs have been developed focusing mainly on residents. Despite the critical role of care staff, they are not generally considered the primary users when designing technologies for social enrichment in aged care. Based on our study, which showed that staff are integral in all activities conducted in the home, we argue that technology for aged care needs to incorporate the needs of staff as well as residents. Thus, we also discuss how technology-based activities can be introduced within the residential aged care environment, in a way that can assist the work of aged care staff.

5.1 Culturally supporting residents

The considerable diversity among the residents is one of the sources of the complex social context of an aged care environment [ 24 ]. Technology-based activities can be designed and used to respond to this diversity. For instance, prior HCI research on the use of VR in RACFs found that VR activities that were personalized to individual interests and preferences provided positive experiences for residents [ 67 ]. Similarly, our research shows that in Daiviet Home, residents have varied interests and preferences; some are willing to join groups activities, some others prefer individual activities (e.g., R-Tran); some residents are enthusiastic about using technology, some are not (e.g., R-Huyen). Therefore, when designing a technology-based innovation, it is important to design a variety of options for individuals, so that the activities are aligned with their interests and preferences.

However, the need for personalized activities raises a tension for aged care homes. While the personalized approach can provide a more tailored experience for residents and is especially valuable for those living with dementia [ 29 ; 46 ], it might not be practical in the care environment where staff have heavy workloads and limited time to spend with individual residents [ 17 ]. Therefore, there should be alternative social activities designed for the residents when individual personalized approach is not practically introduced in this context.

Meanwhile, our ethnographic study highlights the importance of culture to the life and well-being of aged care residents who are from a similar cultural background. We found that social activities need to be organized in a way that fits into the culture that residents are familiar with. Many cultural traditions influence residents’ interest and well-being. These range from material traits, customary beliefs, social norms, cuisine, to languages used daily. Activities that are oriented towards cultural traditions are likely to be more engaging to residents because they are familiar and provide a connection with residents' life histories. The activities that do not fit in with their culture might not be sustained. For example, an activity staff member described experiences with short-term staff who had introduced Western style social activities in Daiviet Home, which had not engaged residents. Interestingly, our study shows that when talking about the topics that are relevant to the residents’ culture, residents were excited and interested, suggesting that they are likely to be more engaged by culturally-relevant activities.

In gerontology, Park et al. [ 53 ] also found the important role of traditional values and beliefs in caring for people with dementia, especially among Vietnamese people living overseas, whose cultural background is similar to that of our research participants. The culturally adapted or community-based interventions were found successfully applied to support this population [ 49 ]. Furthermore, Liu et al. [ 34 ] suggested that a cultural approach should be applied in research into delivering social supports for people with dementia. In addition, Xiao et al. [ 74 ] suggested the support provided to this population needed to be culturally and linguistically appropriate.

Similarly, HCI research on the design of a communication technology for aged care residents by Neves et al. [ 48 ] found that cultural idiosyncrasies can affect the adoption of technology among residents. The authors suggested that the interface should be designed with strong attachment to specific culture of the residents [ 48 ]. Thus, understanding culture of the residents who are going to adopt the technology is essential. When an idea is designed for use in one culture but then spreads to a different culture, with different cultural values, it might be incompatible with the culture that it spreads to. An innovation that is incompatible with culture may not be adopted [ 54 ].

Lesson 1: Technology-based experiences should be designed and used to provide personalized experiences to aged care residents. Where individual personalized activities are not practical, technological innovation should be able to provide a variety of group experiences that are oriented to the cultural backgrounds of residents (e.g., integrating culture-friendly content, avoiding things that have negative connotations according to residents’ cultural beliefs, etc.) .

5.2 Psychologically supporting residents

When introducing technology in residential aged care settings, it is crucial that residents are well-supported, and that the technology does not inadvertently cause harm. Due to residents’ frailty, much effort is required from staff members to support residents. This includes making sure residents feel comfortable using the technology, and ensuring there are no unintended consequences, including inadvertently triggering trauma when introducing technology.

Our study shows that in some circumstances, residents might experience discomfort associated with their health conditions when using technology. For example, resident R-Huyen reported not being able to use electronic appliances, including tablets or smart phones for longer than half an hour. Prior HCI research has also shown that discomfort was a common concern among older adults and their families while using VR [ 29 ; 59 ]. Similarly, a previous HCI review study [ 14 ] shows that the duration of exposure to VR can be associated with users’ discomfort (e.g., symptoms of VR motion sickness). Particularly, the longer the exposure time, the greater the degree of VR motion sickness [ 14 ]. The authors suggested that facilitators set an appropriate experience time when implementing VR. Understanding the individual situation of residents before introducing the activities is needed to ensure their comfort level with the technology and to select an appropriate duration time for the experience.

In addition, our study shows that some residents are sensitive to trauma-triggering events. Technology for enrichment (e.g., VR [ 67 ]) might provoke traumas of older adults. Thus, it is helpful for staff to not only have knowledge about resident's culture but also to be mindful of unexpected recalls of residents’ traumas so that they can be prepared to respond to the situation sensitively and safely (e.g., stopping the experience, providing support, etc. if someone encounters discomfort or trauma). Prior HCI research indicated that checking with their families might help care staff obtain more understanding of aspects of residents’ life histories and therefore would help with this psychological concern [ 67 ]. Technologies have also been utilized to help care staff get to know the residents better (e.g. Webster and Hanson [ 72 ], Foong et al. [ 23 ]). The more knowledge about the residents that care staff obtain, the more enriching experience for both care staff and residents might become.

Lesson 2: When technology-based experiences add value, they should still be implemented cautiously, with residents offered a limited duration to experience the activities. In addition, contextual factors that possibly provoke the residents’ traumas need to be carefully considered in the design and implementation of some kinds of technology (e.g., VR) for enrichment in this population. In some cases, it may not be appropriate to introduce and use technology-based experiences.

5.3 Physically supporting residents

Due to the frailty of aged care residents, who may experience mobility impairments and issues with manual dexterity, physical support is required to ensure residents are able to use technology effectively. However, although staff are needed to assist residents while using technology, this might raise privacy concerns, especially during the use of communication technology.

Our study shows that residents often needed assistance from staff to ensure they were able to use the technology effectively and safely. This aligns with previous HCI research showing that staff support is needed during almost all kinds of technology-based activities [ 8 ; 12 ; 25 ; 26 ; 28 ; 76 ]. For example, residents might need support to lift or secure tablets when using them [ 48 ]. Although new technologies are beginning to emerge that can be used for enriching residents’ experience with minimal support from care staff, they have yielded limited success as the technology-based activities often require further prompting from the care staff to stimulate more meaningful activities (e.g. stimulating storytelling or directing the attention of the residents) [ 57 ]. Extending this work, our study found that the presence of staff might challenge residents’ privacy (e.g., during conversations with family during video-calling). Thus, it is important for the video-calling featured technology to be designed in a way that can address both general safety and privacy at the same time. The design of technology for enrichment that is able to ensure this demand requires further investigation.

When it comes to the design and implementation of communication technology in RACFs to be compatible to the residents’ needs, we show that some residents might not have clear mental models about what the technology (e.g., iPad) is for and how to use it, which can have an impact on how they physically interact with the device. Prior HCI research [ 48 ; 69 ] has similarly shown that aged care participants did not know when to use swiping and tapping motion to interact with touchscreen devices. In addition, our study shows that although some residents, are keen to use personal communication tools, they do not always have the capacity to use universally designed devices. In the encounters we observed, residents pressed the screen incorrectly and took a long time to answer the calls, which meant the calls ended before they could answer (e.g., R-Tran, R-Duong, and R-Dang). Furthermore, some residents have vision impairments, which requires a handheld reading magnifier to see the text (e.g., R-An).

Lesson 3 : When introduced in aged care homes, it is essential that technologies are adapted to be used by residents in aged care homes. For example, input methods should be designed to be accessible for people with tremors and limited manual dexterity, the display size should be increased so that items are visible for people with vision impairments, and the length of time of phone rings should be extended to enable people time to response to video calls.

5.4 Supporting the work of aged care staff

Our study shows that ease of use, saving staff time and effort, and cost efficiency in use could contribute to more frequent use of video-based communication technology. As found in our study, the deployment of video-calling did not appear to require much staff time and effort. The set-up process was quick, as it only required the iPad to be placed on the rollator walker or on the table facing the residents. Although staff facilitation is required, aged care staff are largely familiar with iPads, and any staff members can assist, including personal care assistants, nurses, or volunteers.

Meanwhile, residential aged care is an environment where staff are usually required to provide care and/or support for a large number of residents at a time [ 17 ]. For instance, in the care home observed in this study, there were three activity staff working with over sixty residents living with complex health conditions. Although social activities were led by the activity team, other care staff members are usually needed to give support. Research has found that by providing a supportive interaction environment during the technology-based activities, relationships between care staff and residents can be supported [ 3 ; 30 ; 57 ].

In addition, previous research shows that the (unmet) need to undertake more training in how to use the technology might contribute to negative staff attitudes to the technology implementation in aged care homes. This is one of the major barriers to the sustainable adoption of a video-calling featured technology in the residential aged care environment [ 76 ]. Prior research has shown that lack of access to training in technology use created challenges for staff participating in any technology-based activities [ 13 ; 35 ]. Furthermore, relative ease of use for care staff to implement technology was one of the factors found to support the implementation of VR programs in residential aged care [ 28 ].

In a study working with care staff as a user group to develop a software tool to support care, Webster and Hanson [ 72 ] found that although care staff do not typically use a computer as part of their job, they are open and accepting of new technology. Furthermore, there is a wide variation in age, literacy level, experience, etc. among the aged care staff. Webster and Hanson [ 72 ] suggested that the use of any technology by this user group must take different requirements of care staff into consideration. Particularly, “technology for care staff should require little or no training, so that it does not add any barriers to acceptance. Technology that is intuitive, simple, and easy to use is required to suit the varied abilities of a large range of users” [ 72 ].

Lesson 4: Technology for enrichment needs to be efficient to set up, and easy to use for care staff members, not only the activity team, so that they can conveniently assist the activities. We suggest that further research is required on the design of technology for enrichment that takes into consideration the needs of both residents and staff members across different roles within care homes.

Furthermore, in our study, the activity staff members often told the researcher that social activities in the home are usually organized on the basis of activities that are similar to the resident's past experiences and aligned with their culture (e.g., playing bingo, playing card, Vietnamese roll dice, groceries, etc.). Furthermore, when mentioning technology-based activities, our activity staff participants recalled the story of another technological innovation that had been deployed but failed to sustain in the home (e.g., the Wii game) as a baseline to compare and assume that any technology-based innovation might not be of interest to residents. In fact, Rogers [ 54 ] pointed out that “old ideas are the main mental tools that individuals utilize to assess new ideas and give them meaning… Previous practice provides a familiar standard against which an innovation can be interpreted, thus decreasing its uncertainty” [ 54 ]. In addition, the activity staff participant also showed hesitation to adopting VR due to his first-hand unpleasant experience with the technology (e.g., dizziness).

Lesson 5: When introducing technology for enrichment in RACFs, the differences of the innovation against previous ones implemented, and compatibility of the innovation with aged care residents’ interests, should be identified and made visible to the relevant adopters, especially the activity staff members. In addition, if a prospective technology is to be accepted, a collaboration with the activity staff in identifying its benefits, potential incurred harms, and the ways to prevent those potential harms, is needed. The activity team is responsible for residents’ wellbeing and staff will act as “gatekeepers” in choosing which technology-based social activities to be or not to be adopted in the home. They also act as designers of the technology-based activities [ 67 ]. A clear understanding of the potential impact of the technology would help them make a better decision in supporting or resisting the adoption of technology in the home after their first-hand experience with it.

6 CONCLUSION

Over the four-week period of our ethnographic study, the first author assisted the activity team, including in the use of video-calling for social connectedness, in an aged care facility established for the Vietnamese-Australian older adult community. During this time, we witnessed an absence of technology for enrichment in the setting, other than the use of video-calling for social connectedness to address the visiting restrictions during the pandemic. Some kinds of technology for enrichment were reported as previously introduced but abandoned after short-term use with few residents having used it. Regarding the current implementation of video-calling, a decrease in its use was reported after visitor restrictions due to the COVID-19 pandemic had been lifted. Based on these observations, we argue that technology for enrichment is not likely to be sustained for long-term use in RACFs –although the pandemic gave rise to additional factors, both motivating and demotivating use, that may require further research.

Through our study, we identified key themes relevant to current activities that are important to both aged care residents and activity staff when introducing the activities as part of the social program offered for residents to enhance their social and emotional wellbeing. We found that to meet the different interests and preferences of residents and accommodate the resource constraints of the aged care home, social activities that are varied and low-cost have been most highly considered for adoption. We identified barriers to using technology in the home that might contribute to the limited use and unsustainability of technology for enrichment in this setting. We found that the use of technology is not of interest to some residents. Meanwhile, although some residents were enthusiastic about technologies, they had limited knowledge of and capacity to use them. Some others had medical conditions or were sensitive to trauma-triggering events, which may create challenges for technology use. Interestingly, culture-oriented activities played a vital role in supporting the psychosocial wellbeing of residents living in the aged care home. In addition, culture might inhibit residents’ perception of the technological devices. Some cultural traditions (e.g., cuisine, social norms, languages, etc.) are important to residents’ lived experiences within the home. Thus, we argue that the introduction of technology for enrichment in aged care settings needs to incorporate cultural, psychological, and physical factors influencing residents and contextual issues relevant to staff's work, whereas previous work has not identified the importance of all these factors together in this context.

The results from this study prompt us to suggest key considerations for the design of technology for enrichment that best supports residents culturally, psychologically, and physically, and assists the work of aged care staff, better fitting within the aged care environment. However, as there was limited use of technology for enrichment in our participating research site, further research is needed to investigate the use of different kinds of technology in different RACFs. In particular, future research needs to focus on settings where more technology-based activities have been used in order to better inform the sensitive design of technologies for social benefit in those settings.
