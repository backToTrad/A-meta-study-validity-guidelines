Recommender systems (RS) have become increasingly vital for guiding health actions. While traditional systems filter content based on either demographics, personal history of activities, or preferences of other users, newer systems use social media information to personalize recommendations, based either on the users’ own activities and/or those of their friends on social media platforms. However, we do not know if these approaches differ in their persuasiveness. To find out, we conducted a user study of a fitness plan recommender system (N = 341), wherein participants were randomly assigned to one of six personalization approaches, with half of them given a choice to switch to a different approach. Data revealed that social media-based personalization threatens users’ identity and increases privacy concerns. Users prefer personalized health recommendations based on their own preferences. Choice enhances trust by providing users with a greater sense of agency and lowering their privacy concerns. These findings provide design implications for RS, especially in the preventive health domain.

1 INTRODUCTION

From selecting movies to recommending healthy diets, recommender systems (RS) have become a pervasive part of our digital lives. Most existing RS discover user preferences by observing user activity within an application or on a website, as exemplified by Amazon's “Inspired by your browsing history” widget. However, RS often struggle when there is limited user data–known as the “cold start” problem–and infer user preferences based on implicit user relationships rather than actual activity. In these cases, social media networks can provide additional information about the explicit connections among users, thereby leading to more reliable and accurate recommendations. The prevalence of commerce sites that allow users to register or log in via social media networks has accelerated the process of integrating social media information into RS. This social influence can be especially beneficial in the health domain, as personalized health recommendations have been found to be effective in promoting physical activity [ 26 ], healthy diets [ 1 ], and quality sleep [ 21 ]. Furthermore, health content posted by a user's “strong ties” on social media has been found to be more effective in promoting physical activity than content posted by strangers [ 24 , 25 ]. In recent years, online RS have leveraged the power of social influence in generating product recommendations by exploiting users’ social media data [ 94 ]. Popular health and wellness apps, including the diet and exercise tracker MyFitnessPal , have allowed users to link the app to their personal Facebook and/or Twitter accounts. Social media posts liked by many others can also trigger the bandwagon perception, or the perception that the message is trustworthy [ 80 ]. As these studies suggest, harnessing the power of social media has become an important strategy for promoting health-related behavioral changes [ 47 , 48 ].

While employing social media data in RS has many potential benefits, there is limited empirical evidence supporting the effectiveness of such a strategy, especially in the preventive health domain. In fact, data from several studies [ 17 , 66 ] suggest that users desire individuation when receiving health advice; consequently, providing a user with personalized health recommendations based on other users’ or friends’ activities may backfire. Existing studies have also shown that recommendations may trigger negative outcomes if users perceive that a recommendation algorithm filters out or suppresses certain social identities [ 5 , 43 ]. Enabling user control over recommendations to mitigate potential negative effects has been explored as the remedy [ 34 , 39 ], yet there still remains a lack of understanding as to how different personalization algorithms may elicit negative reactions from users, which could potentially trigger resistance to behavior change [ 7 , 76 ]. Thus, a more thorough investigation into the mechanisms underlying the potential negative psychological outcomes of personalized health recommendations may inform the design of RS that counteract undesirable user experiences. The present study aims to contribute to this growing area of research by exploring how the technological features of a RS may mitigate potential negativite outcomes, which can be critical for increasing users’ compliance with health recommendations [ 53 , 56 ].

To fill these research gaps, we conducted a 6 (Personalization approach) x 2 (User choice: Presence vs. Absence) between-subjects experiment (N = 341) to identify how factors of different personalization approaches may affect users’ perception and how user choice can help to resolve the potential negative psychological effects of personalization approaches for preventive health recommendations. The contributions of the study are threefold:

  • It identifies threat to identity and privacy concerns as two significant user perceptions that may undermine trust and acceptance of health recommendations, thus underscoring how current algorithm-driven technologies may affect users’ self-concepts and enriches our understanding of user trust in health RS;
  • By proposing and empirically testing the effects of providing user choice in mitigating the potential negativity brought by health RS, these findings underscore the importance of affording user agency in the process of health personalization;
  • These results provide guidance for designers who are interested in creating RS that effectively promotes healthy behavior.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Health Recommender Systems

The use of scalable digital health interventions has the potential to significantly enhance the efficiency, accessibility, and personalization of healthcare. However, the vast amounts of user data scattered across different sites present critical challenges to delivering highly relevant information to users [ 93 ]. This issue is particularly important in an ecosystem where appropriate health content is the foundation for successful lifestyle interventions [ 4 ]. Powered by machine learning techniques, RS are increasingly playing a key role in aiding and motivating people to adopt beneficial behaviors through individually tailored messages and personalized health advice [ 30 ]. Daskalova et al. [ 21 ], for example, designed a sleep RS that provides recommendations based on data from similar users, and discovered that such “cohort-based” health recommendations significantly enhanced users’ motivations for behavior change due to social comparison [ 21 ]. The study by [ 26 ] designed a RS building upon users’ answers to a lifestyle questionnaire and found such an approach led to greater intentions to engage in the recommended physical activities. Another example is an mHealth RS providing motivational information that is tailored based on electronic health records [ 37 ], using such demographic factors as age, gender, and employment status.

The aforementioned examples illustrate the three most commonly used personalization approaches among popular RS: content-based filtering, collaborative filtering, and demographic filtering [ 72 ]. While content-based filtering primarily matches items with the users’ own preferred product characteristics [ 68 ], demographic filtering and collaborative filtering involve other users in the recommendation process. Specifically, demographic filtering recommends items that are liked or recommended by users who share certain demographic characteristics such as age, gender, or ethnicity [ 3 ]. Collaborative filtering recommends items that are liked or used by other users who share similar preferences in products [ 74 ].

While traditionally all three types of approaches have relied on obtaining users’ data from their activities on a website, researchers are increasingly investigating other data sources that could help RS build more accurate user profiles and recommend items accordingly. One popular source is users’ social media data, as previous research has demonstrated the effect of social influence in promoting physical activities [ 24 , 62 ]. The prevalence of social login (SL) has accelerated the process of user profile building by granting web application providers access to users’ social network credentials [ 28 ]. Social network-based recommendations [ 57 ] can leverage users’ information from their social connections (e.g., “friending” connections), social actions (e.g., viewing and sharing activities), and social media content analysis (e.g., similarities between content items) [ 92 ]. However, in the human-computer interaction (HCI) literature, the efficacy of leveraging social media for preventive health recommendations is an emerging area that lacks sufficient empirical examination.

2.2 Effects of Personalization Approaches on User Trust – A HAII-TIME Model Perspective

In addition to the computational differences across these recommendation approaches, the ways in which these personalization approaches are presented to the user could differentially affect users’ perceptions of and trust in the system. Users usually learn about how a system works by interacting with its interface. For example, while interacting with a system using collaborative filtering users may see an explanation on the interface saying “People with similar interests also like…”, whereas a content-based filtering system (like Netflix) might provide explanations such as, “Because you watched x, you might also like this...", and a demographic filtering system might say, “Users around your age also enjoyed …”.

How do these explanations affect users’ perceptions and trust in health recommendations? They can nudge users into adopting the recommendations by framing the choice architecture for users. They can trigger mental shortcuts or simple rules of thumb for decision-making in place of thoroughly evaluating the underlying costs and benefits. A tendency to conserve cognitive effort leads to decisions being made through quick, intuitive thinking (System 1 thinking), rather than slow, deliberate thinking (System 2 thinking; [ 41 ]). This kind of dichotomy in information processing has long been recognized by social psychologists who have proposed a number of dual process models such as the "Elaboration Likelihood Model" [ 69 ] and the "Heuristic Systematic Model" [ 15 ]. These models posit that when the message recipient has little or no interest in the subject and/or has a limited ability to process the message, they will not elaborate much on message details and make their decisions somewhat superficially, often by relying on cognitive heuristics (or simple rules of thumb). It is only when the recipient is highly involved and able will they process the information effortfully, by systematically elaborating on the information in the message. However, as Sundar notes, in the current age of online information overload, users tend to be "cognitive misers" [ 27 ] who make judgments based on considerations of efficiency rather than thoroughness [ 80 ]. This means they will likely be swayed by external suggestions and cognitive biases. Users may be unduly influenced by interface cues, such as the explanations of RS, to form attitudes and make quick decisions. Research on health behavior has sought to leverage such decision biases by nudging users toward making better choices. Interventions that harness users’ unconscious or heuristic processing have been grouped under the umbrella term of “nudging” after Thaler and Sunstein first coined the term [ 87 ]. Nudge-like interventions such as interface design for promoting use of default settings [ 16 ], framing effects [ 51 ], and cuing social norms [ 16 ] have been shown to be effective in leading users toward making optimal health decisions [ 52 ].

A dual-process model that proposes both nudge-like and deeper cognitive effects of interface cues and algorithmic attributes of recommender systems is the Human-AI Interaction Model based on the Theory of Interactive Media Effects (HAII-TIME model) [ 81 ]. It proposes two theoretical routes through which AI systems could affect users’ trust and user experience with the system, the cue route and the action route . The cue route specifies how specific interface manifestations of an AI system or its algorithmic attributes could have heuristic value to users, triggering quick judgments about the quality and credibility of the system [ 80 ]. For example, an RS interface indicating that the recommendation provided based on popular choices of other users will trigger the “bandwagon heuristic” [ 28 ] (i.e., "if others think something is good, I should think so, too"), which leads to positive user perception of the quality of the system. Another example is a study  [ 54 ] which suggests that the interface explanations of content-based filtering can trigger the identity heuristic (i.e., the notion that the system treats users as unique individuals) and thereby enhance user trust in the system [ 54 ].

The action route, on the other hand, affects user judgments by affording users the ability to engage with the system [ 81 ]. Here, when the user performs actions on an AI system they may experience a greater sense of interactivity, feel more agentic, facilitate social exchanges between the user and the system, and lead to mutual augmentation. For example, an RS that seeks user input or feedback can enable more individualized recommendations, thus adding value to users and shaping their perceptions of the system [ 83 ].

We discuss both routes of HAII-TIME model, as applied to the current study context, in the sections that follow.

2.3 Effects of Personalization Approaches on User Perceptions (Cue Route)

2.3.1 Threat to identity. The implementation of users’ demographic information has been shown to be effective in enhancing the system's objective performance [ 67 ]. However, psychologically, demographic-based personalization categorization may trigger negative self-perceptions. One potential concern is that demographic filtering might pose a threat to users’ perceptions of identity, especially in the context of health-related recommendations.

Threat to one's identity occurs when one's self-esteem, continuity, or uniqueness is threatened, which can result in resistance to behavioral change [ 10 ]. Since personalization can also be seen as a process of creating social labels for users or communicating users’ social membership to different groups, classifying users based on a set of criteria [ 73 ] may trigger a sense of being judged by the system. For example, providing gender-based health recommendations has been implemented to predict an individual's health-related habits [ 32 ], but it can potentially induce perceived bias that threatens users’ sense of identity [ 31 ]. Furthermore, individuals may be uncomfortable being singled out based on these demographic factors for sensitive or stigmatized health issues. For instance, previous studies have indicated that users generally react negatively towards tailoring primarily based on one's demographic information, such as race [ 13 ] or basic information like weight [ 75 ] since these factors do not accurately reflect who the users perceive themselves to be. Therefore, recommending items based on demographic factors may lead users to feel as if they are being misunderstood by the system based on superficial group categorizations [ 73 ]. In comparison, collaborative filtering groups users with others who share similar preference patterns or similar characteristics [ 74 ] instead of creating salient social labels for each user, which may create less threat to an individual user's identity. In sum, based on the previous literature, we propose:

H1 : Demographic filtering will result in a higher perceived threat to identity than other personalization approaches (H1a), and be more negatively associated with user trust (H1b).

Moreover, health recommendations based on content-based filtering might be perceived as a more accurate reflection of one's personal values and characteristics, since this method of personalization treats each user as a unique individual, building a unique user profile based on one's personal ratings [ 88 ]. Literature in the field of social psychology suggests that such a manifestation of one's personal identity can be quite beneficial in improving well-being [ 19 ]. This is evident in the case of [ 56 ], which demonstrates that personalized medical recommendations generated based on one's unique characteristics (e.g., unique symptoms and needs) may enhance perceived uniqueness, which in turn can increase users’ acceptance of medical advice. Therefore, we would expect to see that content-based filtering that generates health recommendations based on users’ own health preferences (i.e., content-based filtering based purely on information collected within the app) is less likely to elicit a threat to identity. Therefore, we propose:

H2 : In-app content-based filtering will lead to a lower perceived threat to identity compared to other personalization approaches (H2a), which in turn would lead to higher user trust (H2b).

2.3.2 Bandwagon perception. One mechanism that may explain the persuasiveness of social recommendations is the activation of the bandwagon heuristic [ 80 ]. A study examining mobile fitness apps found that social context cues, including the number of followers and the number of profiles followed by the user, were positively associated with fitness goal achievement [ 62 ]. In addition to one's social media network, bandwagon effects can also arise from other users within an app. For example, one study [ 44 ] found that users who received strong bandwagon cues (i.e., a high number of responses and a high helpfulness rating) from the online buddy they connected with (i.e., in-app user) perceived significantly higher levels of social presence and helpfulness than those who received weak bandwagon cues, which in turn led to greater intentions to participate in online health communities [ 44 ].

The present study seeks to determine which source generates stronger bandwagon effects to promote user trust in health RS: collaborative filtering based on information from one's social media network or in-app users. On the one hand, health recommendations from users’ social networks may generate a more substantial bandwagon effect due to the abundance of bandwagon cues on social media. On the other hand, users may find active fitness app users to be more credible and relevant in providing health-related suggestions and thus perceive stronger bandwagon effects. Knowing which source elicits a stronger bandwagon effect will help expand our understanding of how individuals process source cues in the context of health recommendations. Therefore, we propose the following non-directional hypothesis:

H3 : There will be a significant difference between social media-based collaborative filtering and in app-based collaborative filtering upon bandwagon perception.

2.3.3 Perceived privacy risk. Although one's personal information can help generate relevant recommendations, collecting and disseminating personally identifiable information online may raise privacy concerns [ 96 ]. Such concerns are especially salient in the e-health domain, where individuals often share sensitive personal information in pursuit of medical advice [ 29 ]. Prior literature suggests that users have less trust in social media websites to protect their personal information from privacy breaches. For example, the study of [ 46 ] found that the potential reasons why users lack trust in social media websites include the belief that their data will be used for secondary purposes, the belief that their data is not protected from unauthorized access, and the idea that they are unaware of privacy policies and practices. Furthermore, online privacy concerns have been associated with psychological and behavioral outcomes, including decreased user trust and willingness to disclose personal information online [ 36 , 46 , 63 ]. Therefore, we pose the following hypothesis:

H4 : Personalization approaches based on social media data will lead to greater perceived privacy risk (H4a) and lower user trust than those based on users’ in-app data (H4b).

2.4 Effects of User Control (Action Route)

Current RS leverage the strengths of various personalization approaches in search of the optimal approach for each user [ 22 ]. However, recent literature has demonstrated that users may have their own preferences regarding how their personalized recommendations should be generated [ 23 ]. Therefore, how to provide users with a sense of control in the personalization process is increasingly being emphasized in recent RS literature [ 34 , 39 ]. One way of enhancing user control is providing affordances that invite user input, as per the “action route” in the HAII-TIME model [ 81 ]. Specifically, the action route highlights the enabling aspects of AI affordances (as opposed to the symbolic effects of cues shaping perceptions by triggering cognitive heuristics), addressing the human need for control during technology use as a way to balance human agency and machine agency [ 81 ].

2.4.1 How user control affects identity threat and sense of agency. Emerging research on interactive RS has started to explore the effects of user control. For example, one study [ 39 ] found that by allowing users to control contextual characteristics (e.g., location, time) to receive music recommendations, the perceived quality of the music recommendations was significantly increased. Another study [ 34 ] showed that users evaluated the RS more positively if it allowed users to configure the importance of different recommendation parameters. In a similar vein, the study of [ 9 ] discovered that user control over configuring personalization features is positively associated with user satisfaction. However, these systems only extend user control through the freedom to fine-tune the data input within a specific recommendation algorithm. User agency, however, cannot be realized if users are only be able to manage pre-configured algorithms [ 33 ]. One exceptional study [ 23 ] empirically tested a feature of a movie RS that allows users to explore and switch to other algorithms. In analyzing users’ switching behaviors, the study revealed that users chose a matrix factorization algorithm and item-item collaborative filtering more than other algorithms [ 23 ]. Although this study provided important insights into users’ algorithm preferences, the psychological mechanisms driving users’ choices remain unclear.

In our study, we propose that the affordances that enable users to take action, as per the action route in the HAII-TIME model, will help address the lack of user agency that is endemic to personalization [ 81 ]. In the context of customizable interfaces, it has been found that when a site allows users to add different widgets and customize the site interface, it significantly enhances their sense of agency and control compared to content browsing alone [ 60 ]. Similarly, other studies have demonstrated the positive effects of user choice on enhancing sense of control [ 17 , 18 , 57 ]. Based on this body of empirical evidence, we expect similar effects when a RS affords users the choice to make decisions relevant to the personalization process. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H5 : Providing user choice will enhance sense of agency (H5a) and mitigate threat to identity (H5b), leading to improved user trust.

2.4.2 Evaluating usability of providing user choices of personalization approaches. Despite the proposed benefits of user choice, one issue that could arise when allowing users to choose between several personalization approaches is that it may interfere with the user experience. In fact, increasing users’ choices may require greater cognitive effort in decision-making [ 8 , 77 ]. In addition, presenting users with multiple personalization methods may also increase perceived task complexity, especially if users do not know much about personalization algorithms [ 32 ]. The process of making choices also requires more time to finish the interaction [ 1 ] as well as leads to the depletion of self-control resources, a phenomenon called “ego depletion” [ 6 , 42 ]. Another study showed that asking users’ explicit requirements for information in the privacy settings of an RS compromised the user experience [ 95 ]. These findings suggest that when designing user choice for different personalization approaches, there may be potential usability issues. Thus, we propose our finalhypothesis:

H6 : Providing user choice will affect the perceived usability of the RS.

3 METHOD

3.1 Study Design

To examine if different personalization approaches have the proposed effects on user perceptions and trust in the RS, half of the participants were randomly assigned to the personalization condition with no user choice, while the other half of the participants were provided the affordance of user choice and were given the option to select from five additional personalization approaches or stick to the system-assigned approach. This led us to adopt a 6 (Personalization approach) x 2 (User choice: presence vs. absence) between-subjects experimental design, where participants were randomly assigned to one of the 12 conditions. The six different conditions of personalization approach are listed in Figure 1 .

3.2 Measurements

All the measurements were administered on 7-point Likert scales (1 = strongly disagree; 7 = strongly agree) unless otherwise mentioned.

3.2.1 Manipulation check. A multiple-choice question was asked to check the manipulation of the personalization approach: “ Please indicate how your personalized workout plan was generated .” Choices included, “ Based on users who are similar to me in terms of age, gender, and ethnicity ” and “ Based on users who have similar preferences for type, intensity, and frequency of exercise ." To check the manipulation of user choice, we asked the following question: “ When the system chose the approach to generate your personalized fitness plan, were you able to change it to another approach? " with the choice of “ Yes/No/I don't remember ."

3.2.2 Mediators. Bandwagon perception was adapted from [ 84 ] with four items of “ Many users have chosen the fitness plan that was recommended to me ,” “ Many users recommended the fitness plan that I just received ,” “ Many users have rated highly on the fitness plan that was recommended to me ,” and “ Many users have liked the activities in my personalized fitness plan ” ( α = 0.91, M = 4.42, SD = 1.26). Threat to identity was measured by four items adapted from [ 91 ]: “ The website undermines my sense of self-worth ,” “ The website makes me feel less competent ,” “ I would have to change who I am after I interacted with the website ,” and “ The website makes me feel less unique as a person ” ( α = 0.86, M = 2.52, SD = 1.41). Sense of agency was measured by four items “ I had control over my actions ,” “ I am confident in asserting myself ,” “ I had a distinct voice ,” and “ I was self-directed ” adapted from [ 2 , 85 ] ( α = 0.92, M = 4.66, SD = 1.56). Perceived privacy risk was assessed with four items adapted from [ 38 , 59 ]: “ There is too much uncertainty associated with giving my personal information to the website ,” “ Providing the website with my personal information may involve many unexpected problems ,” “ It would be risky to disclose my personal information to the website ,” “ There could be a high potential for loss when disclosing my personal information to the website ” ( α = 0.95, M = 3.93, SD = 1.63).

3.2.3 Dependent variables. Two dimensions of trust (cognitive and behavioral trust) were measured in the study [ 40 ]. Cognitive trust was measured by five items adapted from [ 58 ]: “ The website performs reliably ,” “ I can rely on the website to function properly ,” “ The website uses appropriate methods to reach decisions ,” “ The website has sound knowledge about how to recommend fitness plans ,” and “ The website correctly uses the information I enter ” ( α = 0.92, M = 4.90, SD = 1.32). Behavioral trust was accessed by three items adapted from [ 71 ]: “ I will use this website again ,” “ I will use this website frequently ,” and “ I will tell my friends about this website ” ( α = 0.93, M = 3.59, SD = 1.90). Intentions to follow fitness recommendations were measured by five questions adapted from [ 49 ]: “ Doing well on this fitness plan is important to me ,” “ I will put in a good effort throughout following this fitness plan ,” and “ I will put forward my best effort in carrying out this fitness plan ” ( α = 0.96, M = 4.75, SD = 1.61). Lastly, usability was measured by five items adapted from [ 64 ]: “ I felt frustrated while visiting this website ,” “ I felt discouraged while using this website ,” “ Using this website was mentally taxing ,” and “ This experience of finding my personalized fitness plan was demanding ” ( α = 0.94, M = 2.19, SD = 1.26).

3.2.4 Control variables. Previous studies have shown that individual differences may affect how users appreciate the ability to choose and modify interfaces. For example, users who are proficient in using advanced features of technologies (i.e., power users) appreciate the ability to indicate their news preferences more than non-power users [ 83 ]. Moreover, scholars have suggested that some users have a stronger belief in the “machine heuristic,” leading them to trust machines more than humans because they believe machines are more accurate and objective [ 82 ]. With a focus on the effects of personalization approaches on user perceptions instead of individual differences, we controlled for trait variability in power usage and machine heuristic in our data analyses. Twelve items were adapted from [ 83 ] to measure power usage, which is how tech-savvy the user was (e.g., " I make good use of most of the features available in any technological device ," " I love exploring all the features that any technology gadget has to offer ," α = 0.92, M = 5.44, SD = 0.89). Belief in machine heuristic was measured via five questionnaire items used in [ 82 ]. For example, “ When machines perform a task, the results are more objective than when humans perform the same task ,” “ Machines can handle information in a secure manner, therefore it is okay to disclose my private information to them ” ( α = 0.92, M = 5.19, SD = 0.83).

3.3 Stimuli

3.3.1 Website design. We designed a prototype website for recommending personalized fitness plans, which we named “MyFitness”.

3.3.2 Manipulation of personalization approach. We embedded the explanations that the respective systems presented to users to manipulate different personalization types. Specifically, the website mentioned the personalization approaches to users on the home page, loading page, and result page. Table 1 presents the wording used for the manipulation of each of the personalization approaches on the three pages.

3.3.3 User choice manipulation. To manipulate user choice, we randomly assigned half of the participants to the websites that allowed users to choose from other personalization approaches. In contrast, the other half did not have this option. We designed a choice button that lists different types of personalization approaches that users can either switch to or stick to the current approach. The personalization approaches were presented randomly, while “Stick to the current approach” was always the last option (Figure 2 ).

3.3.4 Personalized fitness plan. Participants received the same fitness plan at the end of interacting with the site, to avoid the confounding effects of recommendation results. We designed the fitness plan recommendation following information from the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services ( https://health.gov/moveyourway ) and chose the physical activities suitable for indoor and outdoor environments (Figure 3 ).

3.4 Procedure

Participants were first asked to indicate their consent to participate in the study, followed by answering a pre-stimuli survey on Qualtrics capturing the control variables (power usage, machine heuristic). Afterwards, participants were randomly assigned to one of the six experimental conditions. To avoid the confounding effects of different fitness plans, the RS provided a fitness plan that was identical across conditions. The only difference was in the way the system ostensibly generated personalized fitness plans. Therefore, any resulting differences between conditions on outcome variables can be attributed to the personalization method rather than the personalized content because the content remained constant across conditions. This methodological strategy has been applied in previous RS research in an effort to ensure the internal validity of experiments documenting differential effects of personalization mechanisms [ 54 ].

Half of the participants were given the option embedded in the website interface to choose from an additional five approaches to generate their personalized fitness plan, while the other half were not. On the homepage, the website "MyFitness" presented the manipulation of personalization approaches to users for the first time (See Figure 4 , which shows a screenshot for the content-based filtering using in-app data conditions).

After this initial introduction, participants who were assigned to the with-choice condition had an opportunity to choose another approach or stay with the assigned approach. Participants were then asked to build user profiles based on the personalized approach that was either assigned to them (i.e., no-choice condition), or the approach they chose (i.e., with-choice condition). In the conditions of content-based filtering and collaborative filtering using in-app data, participants were asked to answer three questions about their fitness preferences, including their preferred intensity level (e.g., challenging, balanced, casual), activity type (e.g., indoor, outdoor), and the desired outcome from the activities (e.g., have fun, be healthier). Participants in the demographic filtering using in-app data conditions answered three questions about their age, gender, and ethnicity. To build user profiles based on social media data, the site asked participants to sign up with their social media accounts. Following current practices of social media log-in, the site asked users to sign up either through their Facebook or Google account and were provided fitness plans based on demographic information in their social media profiles (i.e., demographic-based filtering), what they have viewed and liked on social media (i.e., content-based filtering), or what their friends have viewed and liked on social media (i.e., collaborative filtering). After that, user profiles were successfully built, and participants viewed the manipulation of the personalization method the second time on the loading page. For example, “We are searching for your personalized fitness plan based on your own preferences for the type, intensity, and frequency of exercise...” for content-based filtering using in-app data.

The website then presented its personalized fitness plan. Participants were then given a 4-digit code and redirected to Qualtrics to complete the questionnaire, capturing mediators and dependent variables as well as demographic information. After completing the questionnaire, participants in the social media data conditions were debriefed that "the social media login was false and neither the website nor the researchers have access to their social media information." 3.5 Participants

Upon obtaining Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval, we began collecting data via CloudResearch, which offers access to high-quality participants on Amazon's MTurk platform [ 55 ]. A total of 350 MTurkers participated in our study to receive monetary compensation of $ 1 USD. After excluding participants who did not complete the questionnaire (n = 5) and those who failed the attention check question (n = 4), the final sample consisted of 341 participants, with 59.2% females (n = 202), 39.5% males (n = 135), and 4 who declined to disclose their gender. The majority (76.5%) of our participants self-identified as Caucasian/White (n = 261) followed by 7.9% Asian (n = 27), and 6.5% Black/African-American (n = 22). In addition, participants in this study were highly educated, with 60.2% (n = 206) having a bachelor's degree or above. The average age was 43.06 ( SD = 13.59), ranging from 22 to 65. The average completion time of the study was 5.34 minutes (ranging from 2.23 minutes to 10.56 minutes). No significant effects of demographic information were found on the mediators ( p s >.17) and the dependent variables ( p s >.85).

3.6 Data Analysis Plan

To examine the cue effects (i.e., personalization descriptions displayed on the RS) on user perceptions (H1-H4), a series of one-way analyses of covariance (ANCOVAs) was conducted with data from participants who received their personalized fitness plan from system-assigned condition (N = 166). For the analysis involving user choice (action effects) (H5-H6), however, we conducted 6 (Personalization approaches) x 2 (User choice: presence vs. absence) ANCOVAs among all participants (N = 341). SPSS Macro Model 4 [ 35 ] was used to examine the indirect effects of personalization approaches on trust outcomes via perceived privacy risk (H4), and the effects of user choice on trust outcomes via perceived threat to identity and sense of agency (H5).

The data showed that among the 175 users who were assigned to the with-choice condition, 67.5% (n = 118) switched to another personalization option, and 32.5% (n = 57) chose to stick to the current approach (i.e., the system-assigned condition). Meanwhile, using social media information to generate a personalized fitness plan was found to be the least favorable approach. Figure 6 presents the percentage of participants’ switching behaviors. For example, the percentage of 64.7% means for participants who were assigned to the condition of content filtering using in-app data, 64.7% chose to stick to the current system-assigned approach.

4.2 Manipulation Check Results

The manipulation check results shows that the majority of the participants successfully identified the experimental condition. The percentages of participants who successfully chose the personalization approach are reported as follows: 86.2% from demographic filtering using in-app data, 89.5% from content-based filtering using in-app data, 67.5% from collaborative filtering using in-app data, 72.2% from demographic filtering using social media data, 82.3% from collaborative filtering using social media data, and 78.1% from collaborative filtering using social media successfully identified the approaches the system used, the differences were significant, χ 2 (25, 341) = 964.3, p < .001. In addition, 74.29% of the participants assigned to the with-choice condition and 75.9% of those assigned to the no-choice condition successfully answered whether they had the choice to choose a different approach or not. The differences were also significant, χ 2 (2, 341) = 108.81, p < .001. Overall, our manipulations of personalization approaches and user choice were both successful.

4.3 Effects of Personalization Approaches on User Perceptions (Cue Effects)

Since H1-H4 pertain to the effects of the system-driven personalization approaches on user perception, we only included participants from the system-assigned personalization conditions (N = 166) and excluded participants from the user choice condition who could switch to their preferred approach.

An one-way ANCOVA comparing the six personalization approaches showed that the main effect of the personalization approach on threat to identity was significant, F (5, 158) = 2.45, p = .036, partial η 2 = .07. However, contrary to what H1 hypothesized, collaborative filtering using social media data led to the highest level of a perceived threat to identity ( M = 3.25, SE = 0.29) than other approaches (Table 2 ). Thus, H1 was not supported.

We also conducted structural equation modeling (SEM) analysis to examine H4. SEM is a collection of analytical tools that enables researchers to investigate the connection between variables in an omnibus fashion by examining the total, direct and indirect effects via all mediators at the same time, as opposed assessing each indirect effect separately [ 45 ]. The model achieved a good fit (Figure 7 ), χ 2 (95) = 517.14, p < .001; CFI = .97; RMSEA = .05 (90% CI Lo = .04, 90% CI Hi = .06), although that is not the focus of our investigation. The focus instead is on testing the aforementioned indirect effects in the presence of other mediators in the same model. The indirect effects found in our mediation analyses by using PROCESS macro were significant even when tested in the presence of each other using SEM analysis. Specifically, personalization approach (0 = In-app collaborative filtering, 1 = Social media-based collaborative filtering) had a significant indirect effect through perceived privacy risk on cognitive trust ( b = −.18, p = .02, 95%CI: [-.13, -.01]), intentions to follow advice ( b = −.05, p = .001, 95%CI: [-.11, -.02]), and intentions to follow advice ( b = −.03, p = .01, 95%CI: [-.11, -.01]).

4.4 Effects of User Choice on User Trust (Action Effects)

H5 proposed that providing user choice will enhance users’ sense of agency (H5a) and mitigate threat to identity (H5b), leading to improved user trust. A 6 (Personalization approaches) x 2 (User choice: presence vs. absence) ANCOVA showed a significant 2-way interaction effect on perceived sense of agency, F (5, 326) = 6.91, p < .001. The effects of providing user choice on enhancing sense of agency were more profound for users who were assigned to the social media-collaborative filtering condition, but such a positive effect of providing user choice on sense of agency was not significant for other approaches (Figure 8 ). In addition, the mediation analyses (Model 4) indicated significant positive indirect effects of user choice on cognitive trust ( b = .41, SE = .09, 95%CI[.24, .59], behavioral trust ( b = .55, SE = .12, 95%CI[.33, .79], and intentions to follow recommendations ( b = .41, SE = .09, 95%CI[.25, .59]) through enhanced sense of agency. Thus, H5a was supported.

A 6 x 2 ANCOVA analysis also showed a significant two-way interaction effect on perceived threat to identity, F (5, 326) = 3.00, p = .01, partial η 2 = .04. Specifically, user choice significantly reduced the perceived threat to identity for users who were assigned to the collaborative filtering using social media data condition ( M = 2.60, SE = 0.31) compared to users assigned to this condition but did not have a choice to select a different personalization approach ( M = 3.80, SE = 0.27). For participants assigned to other personalization approaches, no significant difference was found between providing user choice or not (Figure 9 ). In addition, the mediation analysis found significant indirect effects of user choice on cognitive trust ( b = .11, SE = .05, 95% CI[.02, .23], behavioral trust ( b = .12, SE = .06, 95% CI[.02, .27], and intentions to follow recommendations ( b = .11, SE = .05, 95% CI[.02, .22]) through threat to identity, supporting H5b. Specifically, providing user choice reduced perceived threat to identity, which in turn led to more positive trust outcomes. Overall, H5 was supported. The model achieved a reasonable fit, χ 2 (77) = 422.83, p < .001; CFI = .96; RMSEA = .057 (90% CI Lo = .05, 90% CI Hi = .07), but the overall model is not the focus of our study. The indirect effects in the model, which were previously assessed individually, remained significant even when tested together with other indirect effects present in the SEM model in an omnibus fashion (Figure 10 ).

Finally, to examine H6, a 6 x 2 ANCOVA analysis showed that the main effect of user choice on usability was not significant, F (1, 327) = .05, p = .82, partial η 2 = .00. We also explored whether participants spent more time when they went through the choice options compared to those who were not provided with user choice. Inconsistent with H6, we did not find significant differences in the duration of interaction, F (1, 327) = .10, p = .75, indicating that user choice does not adversely affect usability.

4.5 User Feedback about Social Media-based Personalization

In addition to the quantitative measures, participants were asked to provide feedback regarding their thoughts on the website's use of social media data to generate recommendations in an open-ended response form. Three main topics emerged from participants’ feedback: accuracy concerns, privacy concerns, and a desire for personalization.

Regarding accuracy concerns, multiple participants indicated that data derived from their social media friends’ activities would not accurately reflect their unique health needs. One participant wrote, “ What my social media friends do have no relation to what I do individually. I do not want a plan based on someone else's activities but on my own ” (Female, 43 years old). Another participant said, “ I have a wide range of friends and acquaintances. I don't trust that I would get a reliable plan that is suited for me ” (Female, 48 years old). In addition to data from friends being an inaccurate representation of one's own needs, some participants indicated that even data from their own social media activities may not be useful in generating health recommendations. For example, one participant said, “ I don't feel using social media data is appropriate. We all hit "Like" on things we don't care about, and that could skew the effectiveness of the algorithm ” (Female, 33 years old).

In relation to perceived privacy risk, several participants voiced concerns regarding social media data use. One participant wrote, “ I wouldn't use a website that requires my social media information. There is a high chance of data vulnerability and if any breaches occur, it will negatively impact my friends as well ” (Male, 50 years old). Another participant said, “ I am not a fan of using any personal information or specific account data in the use of anything associated with me, my health or my fitness ” (Male, 43 years old). Some participants conveyed anxiety in trusting an unfamiliar website, with one participant writing, “ I was nervous about giving this unknown website access to my Facebook data ” (Female, 58 years old). Although most sentiments regarding social media data and privacy were from individuals concerned about their data being compromised, a few participants appreciated the simplicity of using social media data. “ It was effortless to do so. I felt it was easy to use and I would use it again ,” said one participant (Male, 37 years old).

Thirdly, several participants noted a desire for personalization in the recommendations they received. Some participants expressed that the use of social media data was a simple way to provide unique and useful recommendations. One participant said, “ I think it is helpful because it uses data from people I follow on social media. This means my plan will be tailored to those I associate with. These are my peers. Older people are likely to have older peers and their plan will be different than mine. This is a unique approach ” (Male, 29 years old). Conversely, some participants felt that social media data may not be personalized enough, as noted by one participant: “ The only problem that I can see with using social media data is if I don't have enough fitness information from my searches or viewing of social media to generate a proper fitness plan personalized to me ” (Female, 43 years old).

In sum, feedback from the open-ended question indicates that users may feel that data from their social media profiles would not provide accurate health recommendations given the lack of health information on their profiles and the diversity of their social media networks. Although some participants appreciated the ease of using the social login feature, multiple participants expressed privacy concerns related to the sensitivity of health information and uncertainty about the website. Lastly, participants indicated a desire for personalized health recommendations that they suspect will not be fully satisfied through the use of social media data.

5 DISCUSSION

First and foremost, consistent with the cue effects proposed by HAII-TIME [ 81 ], this study demonstrates that interface cues suggesting the mechanisms used by a health RS can affect user perceptions, with some approaches more likely than others to elicit perceptions of threat to one's identity. While emerging studies have highlighted the increased accuracy of integrating social network information into RS [ 78 ], our study demonstrates that this strategy can backfire in the health context by posing a threat to the user's sense of identity. As reflected in the qualitative results, participants mentioned that their social media profiles would not accurately reflect their unique health needs. Such findings also corroborate previous research indicating that social comparison may harm one's self-esteem [ 90 ]. Therefore, current practices that are embedded in social media feeds, such as “your friends … recently liked this post,” may not be an effective strategy for encouraging healthy outcomes. Our study calls for a careful evaluation of user perceptions when implementing social network information into personalization algorithms. Given the different influences of social media “strong ties” and “weak ties” on intentions to engage in physical activities [ 24 ], it is possible that cues emphasizing close friends’ social media activities can trigger more positive perceptions than general cues from a user's entire social media network. Future studies could examine the effects of tie strength on users’ self-concepts and trust when designing effective health RS by incorporating social media data.

Moreover, we found that personalization based on users’ own preferences that are explicitly disclosed to the RS led to the lowest threat to identity compared to other approaches. This finding extends previous literature by demonstrating the importance of data source in enhancing users’ sense of uniqueness that can increase acceptance of AI-powered health recommendations [ 56 ]. Specifically, content-based filtering based on users’ own fitness preferences led to the lowest level of perceived threat to identity, which in turn enhanced user trust. Content-based filtering based on users’ social media activities (e.g., “based on what you have liked”) does not help to attenuate threat to identity, at least in the context of health recommendations. This may be attributed to the fact that the affordances of “Likes” may not be an accurate indicator of users’ cognitive effort or personal values, as indicated by the comment that “We all hit Like on things we don't care about.” According to previous literature, the affordance of a one-click “Like” button requires little delibration [ 12 ], while other activities such as commenting may be a better indicator of commitment [ 65 ]. Given that users exert greater effort when answering questions to explicitly express their fitness preferences in the in-app content-based filtering condition, a health RS based on one's personal preferences may generate feelings of being carefully listened to and appreciated. Our study echoes the recent call in the health literature that successful behavior change should be initiated through empowering identity [ 14 ]. Potential design implications include incorporating interface cues such as “based on your unique preferences” to boost users’ sense of identity and improve their user experience of the RS.

As predicted, we found that participants receiving recommendations based on social media data expressed significantly greater privacy concerns, which subsequently led to decreased user trust. This finding aligns with prior research that suggests a negative association between social media data use and perceived privacy risk and trust [ 36 , 46 ], especially when social login is enabled [ 18 ]. Our qualitative data suggest a general distrust towards giving websites access to social media data, with participants citing the potential for breaches of privacy that could impact not only their personal data but also that of their friends. Moreover, participants’ feedback suggests that they are especially protective of their health data, corroborating previous studies on data sensitivity in healthcare RS [ 89 ]. Practically, these findings indicate that healthcare RS should consider alternatives to using social media data for generating recommendations in order to avoid privacy concerns.

This study also revealed the positive effects of affording user choice in enhancing users’ sense of agency, which in turn enhanced their trust in the RS. This finding supports the proposition of the action route in HAII-TIME [ 81 ], such that AI systems that invite users to engage in the decision-making process can satisfy human needs for control during their interactions with AI systems, and therefore help balance human agency and machine agency. In addition to corroborating previous studies’ findings that providing user choice can enhance positive evaluations of RS [ 39 , 79 ], our study identifies user choice as a factor that may mitigate perceived threat to identity that may enhance acceptance of RS, which is a new contribution to the literature. Different from cue effects, actions taken by users can encourage users to engage in processing information systematically [ 81 ], thus affording a greater sense of control over the personalization process. These results can be particularly informative for future research on healthcare RS, as sense of agency is a key aspect of motivating prosocial behaviors [ 61 , 62 ]. In addition, our data show that incorporating user choice did not adversely interfere with the usability of the RS, which demonstrates the practical value of providing user choice among different personalization approaches when interacting with RS. It could be that the cost of consumers’ extra effort was compensated by the perceived benefit of personalized recommendations [ 50 ]. Future research could be undertaken to investigate users’ cost-benefit evaluation process during interaction with RS. When designing RS, it is important to consider the design of user choice to ensure that the choice architecture is easily processed by users without undermining usability.

Lastly, the current study did not find a significant effect of social media-based personalization approaches in generating stronger bandwagon perceptions than other approaches. However, such null effects may be due to the lack of statistical power for detecting a relationship, owing to the small size of our sample. Another possible explanation is that social media collaborative-based filtering may not have produced a strong bandwagon effect for participants who do not have many friends in their social network or share similar interests in health and wellness online, or due to the lack of salient interface cues such as the numbers of "likes" or "views" which showed strong effects on bandwagon perceptions in previous studies [ 84 ]. This finding underscores the need for future work to examine the effects of social network size and interface cues when incorporating social media as the source for improving RS.

6 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

This study has a few limitations that could inform future research. First, to ensure internal validity, we presented all participants with the same set of recommendations for a fitness plan to avoid content confounds. Although none of our participants questioned the legitimacy of the RS in their responses to open-ended questions, future studies should consider controlling for perceived recommendation quality or incorporating RS that employ real algorithms to replicate our findings in a field study. Moreover, our study randomly assigned participants to one of the six recommendation approaches to pinpoint the differences among those approaches, while hybrid recommendation systems are increasingly being used in practice. Future studies could examine whether a certain recommendation approach can compensate for the negative perceptions triggered by another when deployed together. In addition, considering that the goal of the present study is to examine the effects of different personalization algorithms on users’ perceptions and trust in the AI system rather than tracking the changes of readiness to change within each individual, our study only examined the initial trust in the recommendations with a one-time interaction, while adherence to health recommendations may hinge upon users’ intrinsic motivation, such as readiness to change [ 70 ]. For example, individuals who report being in advanced stages of readiness to change (i.e., action and maintenance stages) may be more likely to adopt and maintain physical activity behaviors than those who are contemplating exercise (i.e., in the preparation stage) [ 20 ]. Although such individual differences were eliminated by randomization in our experiment [ 86 ], future studies should investigate how individual differences of readiness to change may affect trust in the health recommendations from RS [ 11 ]. Furthermore, the borderline power 1 afforded by the small sample size limits our ability to detect a real relationship, so the null effects should be interpreted with caution. Lastly, our study only investigated user perceptions in the context of preventive health recommendations, which may be associated with greater concerns regarding sense of identity and data privacy, thus limiting the generalizability of the results. Additional research in other contexts, such as movie recommendations or e-commerce, could serve to test the external validity of our findings.

7 CONCLUSION

Leveraging social media networks in algorithm-driven RS has become increasingly important in the promotion of health outcomes. However, such systems often face resistance from users because they tend to trigger negative reactions that can undermine user trust. Therefore, understanding user perceptions is critical for designing effective RS for health interventions. Our study extends prior research by assessing six different personalization approaches and empirically tests how users perceive and trust the RS when they are provided with the choice to select from different personalization approaches. We found that users trust recommendations the least when they are generated based on their social media friends’ activities, due in part to perceived threat to self-identity and privacy concerns. Through the interface feature of user choice, our study demonstrates that providing users with a choice between different personalization approaches can enhance their sense of agency and attenuate perceived threat to identity. These findings advance our understanding of how algorithm-driven personalization may affect users’ self-concepts, and provide design implications that can protect the perceived integrity of the self while avoiding features that may lead to defensive responses that undermine the core objectives of RS for preventive health.
