How Space is Told
Linking Trajectory, Narrative, and Intent in Augmented Reality Storytelling for Cultural Heritage Sites

Jae-Eun Shin , KI-ITC ARRC, KAIST, Korea, Republic of, jaeeunshin@kaist.ac.kr
Woontack Woo , KI-ITC ARRC, KAIST, Korea, Republic of and UVR Lab, KAIST, Korea, Republic of, wwoo@kaist.ac.kr

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581414
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

We report on a qualitative study in which 22 participants created Augmented Reality (AR) stories for outdoor cultural heritage sites. As storytelling is a crucial strategy for AR content aimed at providing meaningful experiences, the emphasis has been on what storytelling does, rather than how it is done, the end user's needs prioritized over the author's. To address this imbalance, we identify how recurring patterns in the spatial trajectories and narrative compositions of AR stories for cultural heritage sites are linked to the author's intent and creative process: While authors tend to bind story arcs tightly to confined trajectories for narrative delivery, the need for spatial exploration results in thematic content mapped loosely onto encompassing trajectories. Based on our analysis, we present design recommendations for site-specific AR storytelling tools that can support authors in delivering their intent while leveraging the placeness of cultural heritage sites as a creative resource.

1 INTRODUCTION

In recent years, persistent efforts have been made to utilize Augmented Reality (AR) technology in producing informative and entertaining content for real-world locations, especially cultural heritage sites [ 25 , 31 , 44 ]. For this purpose, the principles of storytelling have been frequently employed to maximize the benefits that AR can provide, particularly in enriching the visiting experience of such locations [ 40 , 59 ]. Stories provide a continuous thread in which various bits of augmented information anchored to cultural heritage sites can be connected in meaningful ways and form user trajectories that reinforce the site's unique placeness [ 4 , 6 , 58 ]. In doing so, they enable the spatial navigation of outdoor AR environments to become more immersive, thereby enhancing visitor engagement [ 45 ].

Consequently, research concerning AR narratives for cultural heritage sites has mostly focused on what the effects of applying storytelling techniques are for the end users, i.e. visitors [ 50 ]. How the process of creating a relatively underestablished form of narrative is approached and executed by the author was not of primary concern. To put it differently, the resulting content has been regarded more as a form of designed experience [ 38 ] rather than as a product of authorial intent. Such a biased perspective has undermined the potential of AR storytelling as a creative medium, employed by authors taking inspiration from specific sites in seeking novel ways to express their ideas. This led to authoring tools lacking in consideration of spatial and narrative parameters that cater directly to the author's needs and interests in their creative process.

For this reason, we present a multiple case study geared towards developing an authoring tool for AR stories targeting cultural heritage sites that places the creator's intent at the fore. Using our own web-based authoring tool prototype, we observed how 22 participants with varying levels of experience and expertise in storytelling each created linear AR narratives, which we defined as Spacetelling content, for one of three outdoor cultural heritage sites imbued with strong placeness. Applying grounded theory, we collected the authored content for a thematic analysis to uncover common patterns in their spatial trajectory and narrative composition. Combining these data with open coding on in-depth interviews, we identified corresponding links between the content's authorial intent, trajectory, narrative, and the function they serve.

As a result, two distinct types of Spacetelling content— story-centric and space-centric —emerged from our study. When authors focused on delivering their stories via space, they tended to create narrative arcs bound tightly to trajectories within a confined area of the cultural heritage sites they chose, which served to emphasize the site's historical context or transform the space with a completely different meaning. On the other hand, the intent to explore spatial features of a specific site through stories led most authors to map thematic content loosely onto trajectories encompassing the entire space, resulting in AR narratives that reinforce the placeness inherent in the targeted sites.

The main contribution of our study is to provide the grounds on which conventions and principles of site-specific AR storytelling can be identified, established, and reflected in developing creator-supportive authoring tools for cultural heritage sites. Rooted in an extensive understanding of how authors actually work with cultural heritage sites as both a creative resource and target, AR being the technological medium, we assert that: 1) Providing sufficient spatio-narrative templates and AR assets based on intent; 2) Contextualizing spatiotemporal assistance for narrative structure; and 3) Putting the author in the AR user's shoes with Virtual Reality (VR) simulation atop a digital twin are critical features for a successful site-specific AR storytelling tool.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Authorship, Spatiality, and Narrativity in Site-Specific AR Storytelling

In the context of this study, authorship is defined as the right to channel authority into the creation of narrative content at a fundamental level, which allows its owner to execute “individual intention and agency  [ 27 ].” For digital storytelling in general, much of this authorship has been perceived to have transferred from a singular, omniscient creator to the audience-as-user, leading to another instantiation of the “death of the author [ 37 ]” after Roland Barthes's postmodern manifesto  [ 5 ]. This owes to the hypertextual and interactive capacities of digital media technology that bestow the power to determine the course and outcome of a given narrative upon the audience  [ 11 ]. Site-specific AR storytelling takes one step further in elevating the status of the audience-as-user to audience-as-author, as it requires active physical and mental engagement of the end user—site visitors as target users of relevant applications—to implement an augmented narrative in the surrounding real space and ultimately transform it into an embodied experience [ 12 , 21 ].

Subsequently, the notion of narratives as embodied experiences in site-specific AR has contributed to the prevailing current of related studies that focus on storytelling as a strategy for experience design, especially in the digital heritage domain. Here, the main goal is to make visits to certain places as enticing as possible by augmenting their historic and aesthetic features within narrative threads  [ 51 ]. This approach has led to the development of numerous site-specific AR storytelling applications that rely on end user evaluation to assert their merit, delivered at the expense of examining how the creators of those narratives can still preserve and exercise their authorship. In the few cases where the author's intent and needs were brought into focus, they were identified for conditions too specific to be considered in the general context of creating site-specific narratives [ 16 ], lacking in technological considerations of the AR environment [ 57 ], or discussed in terms of how they can ultimately serve the audience better [ 33 ]. This in turn has undercut the importance of site-specific AR for storytelling as an end unto itself, a creative medium engaged with unique attributes of outdoor spaces such as cultural heritage sites.

Shifting the focus from the end user's experience to the creator's authorship in site-specific AR storytelling is imperative for mainly two reasons. For digital storytelling, it provides the basis on which site-specific AR as a novel medium can be construed from the author's perspective to further develop into an innovative and viable means of creative expression  [ 39 ]. For digital heritage, it can contribute to the diversification and enrichment of digital content for cultural heritage sites by identifying and supporting authors’ motivations to engage with the sites as a creative resource, thereby enhancing their sociocultural significance  [ 24 ].

To reinstate the creator as the primary holder of authorship in site-specific AR storytelling, uncovering the ways in which the essentially spatial nature of the medium is understood is a crucial first step. Spatiality has been widely accepted as a key characteristic of computer-mediated communication and digital storytelling on various levels [ 29 , 47 ]. However, it was after the introduction of mobile technology and site-specific AR that the mainly metaphorical use of the term took a turn for the literal [ 60 ]. Narrative space, once an abstract entity that materialized only in the virtual realm, took on a physical form by merging with the real world [ 41 , 43 ]. For this reason, both the geographic and semantic attributes of physical spaces that narrative spaces are connected to and subsumed by are central to the process of authoring site-specific AR narratives.

Regarding how the geographic aspects of spatiality in the real world configure site-specific AR storytelling, Barba et al. [ 3 ] applied the concept of spatial scale in analyzing Pokémon Go. However, the taxonomy they borrowed was based on the spatial cognition of the end user, not on the comprehension of the author in making creative decisions to produce the content. On the other hand, Sharma et al. [ 62 ] proposed a framework for MR game designers in selecting outdoor spaces suited to their purpose and medium. In this study, spatial types were defined by continuity (boundary and trajectory) and specificity (the uniqueness of the landscape). Although it serves as a useful reference point for our inquiry, it did not consider how authors reflect their creative intent in the spatial form of their craft.

Another concept central to spatiality in site-specific AR storytelling is the trajectory as journey, as proposed by Benford et al. [ 6 ]. Acknowledging that journeys provided through spatial trajectories in cultural applications are steered by the end users, they emphasized the role that authors play in structuring the spatial, temporal, and performative elements of the narrative that determines the trajectories in the first place. Despite this importance, research on how authors reach decisions on the implementation of these elements, especially in constructing physical trajectories for site-specific AR storytelling, has been lacking. While a recent work by Park and Woo investigated how the continuity of spatial trajectories during a site-specific AR narrative experience can be maintained, it based its guidelines solely on end user evaluation rather than author requirements [ 52 ].

Turning to the semantic aspects of site-specific AR storytelling, the majority of research has emphasized how placeness, chiefly defined as the authentic quality of a certain site [ 58 , 61 ], is the foundation of a content's narrativity; that is, spatiality and narrativity are connected at an intrinsic level [ 20 ]. In making a distinction between the terms space and place as applied to media environments, Harrison and Dourish argued that whereas space is the structural foundation of a world, place is a space that is culturally invested with meaning and serves as a distinctive frame for spatial interaction [ 29 ]. Revisiting the terms a decade later, Dourish further contended that the two concepts are by no means antithetical and warns against a dualism that separates them, as both are products of social practices that jointly shape computer-mediated spatial experiences [ 18 ].

Azuma presented three key concepts that offer insight into how places in spaces with particularly strong placeness may be narrativized in site-specific AR environments, which are: Reinforcing, Remembering, and Reskinning  [ 1 , 2 ]. Reinforcing complements the power inherent in reality through augmentations, and Remembering draws upon past events and memories associated with a place to augment them as stories. On the other hand, Reskinning uses augmentations in recreating reality to fit the purpose of the story the author wishes to tell. In our study, how these concepts as functions of narrativity in site-specific AR storytelling for outdoor heritage sites are linked to the authoring process and outcome will be examined.

Another way of achieving narrativity in site-specific AR storytelling from the author's standpoint is through remediation. Coined by Bolter and Grusin [ 8 ], it refers to how new media utilize and refashion the old, broken down to hypermediacy and immediacy. Whereas hypermediacy is concerned with the multiplication of mediations that makes content more diverse and multifaceted in a new medium, immediacy refers to the manner in which that medium desires to provide a coherent structure to the point of erasing its existence [ 7 ]. In this regard, remediation offers the basis on which the terms of authorship in constructing narrativity for site-specific AR stories can be determined: deciding where and how to augment different types of preexisting multimedia content within a newly created, highly immersive narrative sequence that is spatially mapped on to the designated site.

Remediation further establishes the connection between authorship, spatiality, and narrativity in site-specific AR storytelling by shifting the focus from end user interactivity to author-controlled intermediality, which concerns phenomena related to crossings between various types of remediated content [ 56 ]. While there have been many attempts to implement site-specific AR narratives through remediation, systematic approaches to their intermediality have been scarce. Various multimedia resources were gathered, remediated, and spatially distributed without much attention to their effect on the spatial and narrative form of stories  [ 63 ]. Therefore, our study will identify how intermedial strategies for content remediation in authoring site-specific AR narratives can best be put into practice.

2.2 Authoring Tools for Site-Specific AR Storytelling

Earlier efforts to develop authoring tools for site-specific AR storytelling can be found in works on systems such as M-views [ 15 ], DART [ 42 ], and InStory [ 13 ], which commonly facilitated authoring for site-specific mobile AR narratives outdoors. Some focused specifically on cultural heritage sites as the target domain. Among those, Archeoguides [ 67 ] proposed an authoring system that allows the user to upload multimedia content and 3D reconstructions to create site-specific mobile AR tours for outdoor cultural heritage sites. Demeris et al. [ 17 ] presented a configuration of infrastructures required to author mobile AR content for cultural heritage sites and deploy them. However, they did not approach site-specific AR storytelling as a means of channeling the author's creative intent through designated places, nor consider the relationship between narrativity and spatiality as key components in their system design.

Research on utilizing advances in mobile and AR technologies to author site-specific narratives, especially for outdoor cultural heritage sites, have been continuously increasing. In 2015, Fidas et al. [ 23 ] conducted a comprehensive survey on six authoring tools-Hoppala 1 , ARIS Editor 2 , TaggingCreaditor [ 65 ], LoCloud [ 22 ], CHEF [ 26 ], and LMAC [ 54 ]—for mobile digital heritage applications, most of which used AR technologies in a site-specific setting. They commonly established connections between the semantic properties of multimedia data and real-world locations through geolocation tags: At this point, authoring tools had begun to take spatiality and narrativity jointly into account for site-specific AR storytelling in outdoor environments. The survey identified a pressing need to investigate patterns in author requirements that could increase the usability and efficiency of such authoring tools, which remains a gap in research that we aim to address through this study.

More recent works have highlighted the process of authoring site-specific AR narratives in both general-use tools and heritage-specific tools. Nam [ 48 ] proposed design factors based on narrative theory for an in-situ AR storytelling system. Kampa and Speirling [ 34 ] also identified and applied author requirements in designing multiple site-specific AR storytelling tools. Although both studies engaged with the authoring process, their focus was not on aiding authors in dealing with spatiality and narrativity to the best of their intent but on enhancing user interactivity with the authored content. In addition, Wang et al. [ 68 ] proposed a workflow for the remote authoring of site-specific AR content but targeted indoor spaces, their focus on providing a better understanding of the physical features of those spaces to the authors as opposed to semantic information that consists placeness.

On the other hand, Shin and Woo [ 64 ] conducted a focus group study with local storytelling experts to understand the author's motivations, goals, and needs in creating AR narratives for historic outdoor locations, categorizing them as either space-driven or narrative-driven based on the author's priority and deriving design guidelines for the two modes of storytelling. Zhang et al. [ 69 ] developed a mobile AR application featuring content that links and represents its narrative flow through a sequence of geo-locations. However, neither of the studies went as far as analyzing the relationship between space and narrative in the stories they presented, along with the role of the author in connecting the two components. Therefore, we seek to investigate how authors attempt to leverage spatiality and narrativity together in creating site-specific AR narratives that reflect their authorial intent.

3 METHODOLOGY

3.1 Research Questions

The goal of our study is twofold: First, we wished to observe how authors approach site-specific AR storytelling for outdoor cultural heritage sites with strong placeness and determine whether common traits can be found and patternized with regard to the spatiality and narrativity of the content they produced. Second, we sought to establish connections between authorial intent, spatiality, narrativity, and function as foundations of creative principles that could be reflected in designing an author-supportive tool for site-specific AR storytelling geared at cultural heritage sites. In order to achieve these goals, we set our research questions as follows:

  1. What types of patterns can be identified in the authorial intent, spatial trajectory, and narrative composition of site-specific AR stories for outdoor cultural heritage sites?
  2. How does authorial intent impact the spatial trajectory, narrative composition, and function of site-specific AR stories for outdoor cultural heritage sites?

To answer these questions, we took the following steps: 1) Defining a format of site-specific AR storytelling for outdoor environments that links spatiality and narrativity; 2) Designing and developing an authoring tool prototype based on the defined format; 3) Conducting a qualitative user study where participants created site-specific AR stories for given outdoor cultural heritage sites with the prototype.

3.2 Spacetelling: Defining the Content Format

We determined the concept, terminologies, and structure of site-specific AR narrative content to be produced through our authoring tool. First, we adopted and appropriated the term Spacetelling , which was first proposed by Ha et al. [ 28 ] in the context of AR and developed further in other branching works [ 36 , 53 ]. We then incorporated key concepts of spatiality and narrativity, which were delineated and contextualized for this study in our survey or related work, into its definition. Here, Spacetelling refers to the act of mapping a linear narrative to a corresponding spatial trajectory through the remediation and connection of multimedia content in AR at specific GPS coordinates. Grounded in this definition, units of spatiality and narrativity were organized into hierarchies that together comprise the format of Spacetelling content.

Terminologies pertaining to the spatial hierarchy of the content are: Space, Place of Interest (PoI), and Geotagged Content. Their definitions were based on concepts of spatiality discussed in the cultural heritage domain [ 30 , 32 , 66 ] and modified for the purpose of the authoring tool. In parallel, Spacetelling content, PoI Content, and Authoring Point were defined for narrative hierarchy. Additionally, we conceptualized the terms Spacescape and Storyscape to refer to the spatial and narrative attributes of a space in their entirety. Figure 1 illustrates how units belonging to a spatial hierarchy are selectively used to map corresponding units of a narrative hierarchy in creating a connection between trajectory and narrative for Spacetelling.

Definitions for each concept in both hierarchies are listed as follows:

Units of Spatiality .

  • Space: A real-world location occupying an area defined by socially agreed demarcations, representing a single, unique, and distinctive identity (e.g. cultural heritage sites)
  • Place of Interest (PoI): A subunit of Space that refers to a visible and meaningful landmark and the area it occupies (e.g. buildings, gates, monuments, pagodas, ponds, lakes, etc.)
  • Geotagged Content: Various types of multimedia content (e.g. text, image, video, etc.) anchored to specific GPS coordinates for a certain PoI
  • Spacescape: The spatial features of Space as a whole, consisting of the geographical placement of PoIs and geotagged content

Units of Narrativity .

  • Spacetelling Content: A sequence of one or more PoI content authored within the spatial boundary of a Space (e.g. a cultural heritage site) in which a linear narrative is mapped onto a specific spatial trajectory in site-specific AR
  • PoI Content: A sequence of one or more authoring points created within the spatial boundary of a PoI that functions as a subunit of Spacetelling content
  • Authoring Point: A geotagged content selected and authored as a subunit of PoI content
  • Storyscape: The semantic features of a Space as a whole, comprised of the subject matter of Spacetelling content and geotagged content within a Space

3.3 System Design and Development

Once the content format was established, we developed a high-fidelity digital prototype for the Spacetelling content authoring tool. The system as a whole was implemented with Eclipse Oxygen.3 IDE 3 and consists of three main components: the client, the server, and the database. The client is composed of a web-based desktop authoring tool and a mobile AR viewer. The authoring tool enables the creation of geotagged content and Spacetelling content, which are shown on site through the mobile AR viewer.

Along with handling authoring and viewing operations requested by the client, the web server facilitates a two-way communication between the client and the database where the authored content and their metadata are stored. The client was built with HTML5, Javascript, and CSS, the web server with the Play Framework 4 , and the database with MariaDB 5 using RDB. As the author's need for systematically managed multimedia resources related to the target space was identified by Shin and Woo [ 64 ], we adopted the metadata schema for visualizing and sharing AR content by Kim et al. [ 35 ] for the database.

In our system, this schema defines the spatial and narrative properties of geotagged content and Spacetelling content, along with other metadata such as author ID, authored time, media type, etc. In addition, we included an anchor table to link the spatiality and narrativity of the content at the system level. This enables the author to create and search for multimedia content uploaded to specific GPS coordinates as geotagged content and remediate them in a spatial and narrative sequence as Spacetelling content.

With the system structure in place, we focused on designing the web-based desktop authoring tool interface. We employed a 3D map interface with CesiumJS 6 for an intuitive means to understand the spatiality of outdoor cultural heritage sites for Spacetelling and author remediated augmentations onto them. Open Street map 7 was used in demarcating the spatial boundaries of the target spaces and their PoIs to visualize them on the 3D map. Next, we separated functions for geotagged content authoring and Spacetelling content authoring into different tabs.

The geotagged content authoring tab allows authors to search for specific cultural heritage sites on the map and pinpoint the exact location on which they wish to upload one or more of four types of multimedia data (image, text, video, and audio) as part of the space's storyscape. Authors are required to fill in spatial and narrative descriptions on the uploaded data according to our metadata schema so that the geotagged content can be systematically filtered and remediated for Spacetelling content authoring.

For the Spacetelling content authoring tab, a five-step process based on the space-driven design guidelines put forth by Shin and Woo [ 64 ] was implemented. We deemed these guidelines to be appropriate for site-specific AR storytelling focusing on the interconnection of spatial and narrative components for a given cultural heritage site. This is because they prioritize the selection of a target space and asserts an authoring tool should be able to provide rich information on its semantic and physical features in order to support authoring that leverages them. Each step reflected the requirements stated in the guidelines, along with actual authoring routines observed in Shin and Woo's study. The details of each authoring step and the corresponding interface are described below and illustrated in Figure 2 .

The Five-Step Authoring Process for Spacetelling .

  1. Select Space : Searching for and selecting the target space by its address or name on the map. Spatial properties (location, surrounding environment, boundaries, size of the area, PoIs, etc.) of the selected space are retrieved on the map accordingly.
  2. Survey Storyscape : Grasping the distribution of geotagged content (visualized as green dots on the map) included within the spatial range of each PoI (blue dots), which can be defined and adjusted by the user. The right side table lists each geotagged content by file name, data type, description, related event, and character. Clicking on a green dot on the map or selecting a row from the table retrieves a detailed preview shown below the table.
  3. Outline Story : Planning the title, a brief plot of the story, related events, related characters, number of PoIs to be used, and the expected amount of time it would take to experience the authored content in a site-specific AR environment. The data gathered in this step function as search and filter terms for Spacetelling content on the mobile AR viewer.
  4. Create Story : Generating and connecting authoring points for each PoI by selecting pre-authored geotagged content from the storyscape or creating new ones with additional text descriptions to form one or more PoI content. Connecting one or more PoI content or PoIs in a linear sequence as a completed form of Spacetelling content. The order in which these tasks are performed is flexible.
  5. Preview Story : Reviewing the authored content as a spatial trajectory visualized over the map, along with the detailed sequence of authoring points at every PoI. Previous steps can be revisited to modify the content before saving and exporting it to the mobile AR viewer.

3.4 Study Design and Setup

Table 1: A comparison of three cultural heritage sites selected as target spaces for our main user study

We conducted a qualitative multiple case study where participants each created a Spacetelling content for an outdoor cultural heritage site with our authoring tool prototype. The study was approved by an Institutional Review Board, and participants signed consent forms regarding the study procedures and data they provided after being informed of them in detail.

We designated three Korean cultural heritage sites for the participants to choose from in performing the task: Hapcheon Haeinsa Temple, Jeju Mokgwana Office, and Gyeongju Daereungwon Tomb Complex. These sites were selected on account of their differences in the number of PoIs, area size, and characteristics of placeness, as is shown in Table 1 . This was to ensure that what patterns may be established from the authored content were not affected by certain features of a single heritage site or multiple similar sites but commonly found across different spaces with varying spatial and narrative attributes.

Through our system prototype, we created geotagged content for each of the three sites and their PoIs to construct sufficient storyscapes prior to the study. We collected four types of multimedia data (image, text, video, audio) related to the target space from various resources, including official websites of the target places, Korean cultural heritage databases, and various social media services. The number of geotagged content we created was similar for all three sites (Haeinsa-78; Mokgwana-82; Daereungwon-77).

First, we outlined the goals and conditions of the task to the participants and gave them a walkthrough of the tool: In creating an original Spacetelling content for one of the three heritage sites they selected, they were required to select at least two PoIs within the target space to map their narratives and make at least one authoring point for each PoI content. For a clearer understanding of how the authored outcome will be seen on site, we showed them images of the mobile AR viewer interface to which a sample Spacetelling content was applied, as depicted in Figure 3 .

Second, the participants performed the task by using the authoring tool prototype on a desktop computer set up for the study. We were present during the entire task to assist them with any trouble or errors that may occur and to observe the participants’ behaviors in the authoring process, but made sure not to make any interventions while they conducted the task. We imposed a 40-minute minimum on the participants in performing the task so that they would have sufficient time to make the needed decisions in the authoring process. Each participant session was screen-recorded and kept note of. After the participants completed the task, we conducted semi-structured in-depth interviews centered on the decisions they made in creating their intended content through the authoring tool and the issues they dealt with in the process. The average time it took for a single session was approximately 2 hours.

3.5 Participants

A total of 22 participants were recruited for our study, with a 1:1 gender ratio between females and males. Their ages ranged from 23 to 55 years ( M =34.95, SD =10.49), and they had varying levels of self-assessed expertise and experience in authoring narrative content of any form (High-6; Mid-9; Low-7).

Participants who ranked themselves at the higher end of the spectrum had received or were undergoing professional training for storytelling, working in directly related fields in some cases. Five of them were graduate students majoring in storytelling, commonly focusing on developing narrative content rooted in the local history. Another five were tour guides employed by local municipal offices and assigned to cultural heritage sites in the region. They had three to 10 years of experience in giving walking tours to visitors at various posts, delivering relevant information and narrating stories to them.

On the other hand, the 22 other less-experienced participants were mainly graduate students in interdisciplinary studies with a passing knowledge and interest in storytelling. In addition, they had not been engaged with cultural heritage sites at a professional level in any regard. Our study included both experts and novices of storytelling for cultural heritage sites primarily to investigate whether the author's intent and needs, and subsequently the type of content they created, were associated with their position and level of expertise. Based on the data we acquired from this demographic, we wished to derive design implications for an accessible and easy-to-use authoring tool that can support authors regardless of prior knowledge or expertise, which not only is a common goal for AR authoring [ 49 ] but would also help establish site-specific AR storytelling as a viable creative medium.

The participants’ evaluations of their familiarity with AR technology were fairly evenly distributed, with more people answering they were acquainted with the term well enough to know or envision what authoring narrative content in site-specific AR is like than those who didn't (High-10; Mid-6; Low-6). Their knowledge and interest in cultural heritage sites were mid-to-low level on the whole (High-6; Mid-11; Low-5).

4 RESULTS

In this section, we present patterns found for and between authorial intent, spatial trajectory, and narrative composition across 22 different Spacetelling content authored through our prototype and listed in Table 2 . Our analysis was rooted in grounded theory, a qualitative approach that collects data without a prior theoretical framework in exploring and conceptualizing their commonalities to further establish connections among those features [ 19 , 46 ]. The data we gathered consisted chiefly of 1) Spacetelling content and their metadata uploaded to our system database; 2) video recordings of the web browser screen and observation logs for each participant's authoring process; and 3) post-task participant interview transcripts. The six-phase thematic analysis of Braun and Clark [ 10 ] was applied to the data in deriving the patterns. This entailed the following: 1) familiarizing ourselves with the data with multiple readings; 2) generating initial codes with open coding; 3) searching for patterns; 4) cross-reviewing potential patterns; 5) defining and naming the finalized patterns; and 6) reporting the patterns in regards to our research questions.

4.1 Authorial Intent

4.1.1 Narrative Delivery. The first type of pattern we identified for authorial intent was found in nine of the 22 cases of our study. In these cases, the authors consistently displayed a tendency to prioritize the narrativity of the Spacetelling content they were developing in the authoring process (P1: “ I wanted my story to be a good match for the place, but I didn't want to change my story around for that purpose. ”). Their focus was on utilizing the spatiality of the sites they chose in ways that could best express the narrative they wanted to deliver. For this reason, their intent was most prominently observed in the first two steps of the authoring process: five out of the nine authors replied that they had initially based their decisions to select and survey a cultural heritage site on how familiar they were with the meanings of the space, as this would make it easier for them to think of a suitable story (P2: “ When I saw the list of heritage sites, I chose the place I already knew a story about. I tell this story often ”; P8: “ It was a natural choice because this place is very special to me.” ).

Six authors also considered the wealth of PoIs within the sites as a crucial factor, for they felt this implied that more narrative elements could be found through the storyscape (P4: “ I didn't really know about any of the three sites, so I chose a site that looked most complicated on the map because I felt there would be a lot to talk about. ”). Consequently, all of these authors chose Hapcheon Haeinsa, which contained the most PoIs of the three sites, as their target space. When asked what drove them in finalizing their decisions, the common factor was strong narrativity: They chose sites that ultimately sparked their interest on a semantic level and motivated them to create stories, which in many cases were different from their initial choice after surveying different storyscapes at length (P3: “ The heritage site I chose was where I could find the right materials for the story I wanted to tell ”; P5: “ I felt drawn to the historical artifacts I saw in this heritage site, and I wanted to make a story about it. ”).

4.1.2 Spatial Exploration. The propensity to create Spacetelling content as a means to emphasize the spatiality of target spaces was common among the 13 other authors in our study. The narrativity of cultural heritage sites that authors chose was utilized to best represent the spatial features they wished to explore for themselves, and for the end users as well (P13: “ I wandered around the place in my mind, then thought of other people who might do the same. ”). In these cases, the author's choice of target space was mostly grounded in their familiarity or attraction to the geographic aspects of the sites, as opposed to the semantic: Eight out of 13 authors initially chose Jeju Mokgwana Office among the three candidates mainly because they had visited the site most often, and three of the remaining five chose Gyeongju Daereungwon on account of its unique spacescape (P11: “ The scenery of the place seemed beautiful and interesting. ”).

Surveying the storyscape was deemed less important for authors inclined towards spatial exploration than for those focused on narrative delivery. They spent relatively less time on this step during the authoring process, browsing the map for each site more for information on the spacescape rather than the storyscape (P15: “ On the map, I changed my vantage point to get a better idea of the space, but that didn't make it any more realistic. ”). As a result, their final selection of target sites tended to remain the same as their initial choices even after this step. In these cases, subjective evaluations on strong spatiality, based primarily on their prior knowledge and experience of the sites, provided them with a purpose to create Spacetelling content for it (P21: “ I pass by here almost every day and I've been inside as well, but don't really know about the heritage. I saw this as a way to take myself around the space and learn about it too ”; P16: “ I know this place very well, and I wanted to create the best walking route for it. ”).

For both groups, the authors’ primary concern in selecting the PoIs was how they aligned with the storyline they had devised. Sequences of PoIs and authoring points were determined mostly by the logical progression of their narratives, especially for non-experts. In the experts’ case, the physical distance between the PoIs was also important. While non-experts relied almost exclusively on the pre-uploaded geotagged content, many experts took time to upload their own multimedia files in the location they wanted to express their intent in detail. Images were almost unanimously the data type of choice in creating authoring points for non-experts, while some of the experts showed a more diverse preference towards text and video in their construction of PoI content.

4.2 Spatial Trajectory

4.2.1 Confined. In eight of the authored Spacetelling content, spatial trajectories were created on partial, limited regions of the target sites across less than half of the total number of PoIs within them. We defined these cases as confined trajectories, the examples of which are given in Figure 4 (A). During the authoring process, decisions on the type of spatial trajectory were mostly made in the third (Outline Story) and fourth (Create Story) steps. In outlining the story, authors for this pattern tended to put in small values for the number of PoIs they would use, a plan they mostly adhered to in the next step (P1: “ It was not my goal to make people visit many spots in the heritage sites ”; P5: “ This was enough for my story. ”).

In the Create Story process, authors of confined trajectories displayed a strong tendency to first make specific authoring points for PoIs they chose before they linked those PoIs to construct a trajectory. When asked what they considered foremost in their selection and connection of PoIs, almost all of them answered that relevance to the premise and narrative flow of their stories was the most important factor (P4: “ I concentrated on dividing my story into smaller parts first, and then connecting them in the right order ”; P7: “ I didn't know which direction to go, but the path was set as I went along with my story. ”). The physical distance between each PoI in their trajectories varied, but in almost all eight cases, they formed a straight line that would induce the user to cut through the target site from one end to the other.

4.2.2 Encompassing. Spatial trajectories of the other 14 Spacetelling content were labeled as encompassing. They consisted of more than half of the total number of PoIs that were connected to cover most of the regions within the target sites, as shown in Figure 4 (B). Authors who created encompassing trajectories tended to select more PoIs in the Create Story process than they had initially planned on in the previous step. In direct contrast to authors of confined trajectories, the majority of these authors first selected all the PoIs they wished to use and aligned them sequentially before they worked at each PoI to create a corresponding PoI content (P12: “ I wanted to come back to where I started my story, so I ended up circling the space and included more PoIs in the story for that” ; P21: “ I found myself adding more PoIs during Create Story so that the entire space could be covered with my story. ”).

When asked what drove their selection of PoIs, they too had mostly answered that the PoIs’ connections to the narrative aspects of the content they had in mind were central to their decisions (P8: “ I wanted people to remember what came before was related to what they were going to learn about next in the story” ; P22: “ The point was to make it seem natural to be following the path. ”). However, a notable difference between the authors of these two patterns was that the latter considered the physical proximity between neighboring PoIs within their spatial trajectory to be most crucial in determining the sequence. As a result, encompassing trajectories mostly formed zigzagging lines that connected PoIs close to one another across the entire area of the target sites.

4.3 Narrative Composition

4.3.1 Progressive. We defined 10 of the 22 Spacetelling Content from our study as having a progressive narrative structure. Progressive narratives contain a clear story arc in which characters are employed to develop and resolve a conflict in the plot through a series of related events in a fixed, linear order. Most of the work regarding the composition of such narratives was conducted during Outline Story, where authors spent much time listing and detailing the elements of their story arcs. These plans were applied to the creation of authoring points as subunits of PoI content, and again to the connection between each PoI content for narrative progression. In these cases, the sequential order of links made both at the authoring point level and PoI content level was essential for the authors in delivering their intended story (P4: “ If people mix up the order and don't visit the PoIs in the way I planned, my story won't make any sense ”; P7: “ In my story, certain things are supposed to happen only after another thing has. ”).

For this pattern, six authors created fictional tales based on the storyscape of the target sites in novel contexts. Stories on Hapcheon Haeinsa led to plots involving a Buddhist statue becoming human (P1), the theft of a national treasure exhibited there (P5), and mishaps of two brothers visiting the site (P7). For Jeju Mokgwana, an adventure story of a man in search of a lost king (P3), a romantic account in which the site's famous tangerine trees play an important role (P8), and a story of friendship between Jeju's famous historical figure and a frog in the pond (P9) were authored. On the other hand, four others chose to retell or revise historical facts related to the target heritage sites, such as a story on the Jeju 4.3 incident (P2) and the site's brief history (P10) at Jeju Mokgwana, or the origin and making of the Tripitaka Koreana(P5, P6) at Hapcheon Haeinsa.

4.3.2 Thematic. In the 12 other cases, authors composed narratives defined as thematic. Instead of a clear story arc, these narratives presented segments of standalone content together for a single binding theme. Authors of thematic narratives tended not to plan their stories with as much detail as those of progressive narratives had. Furthermore, the order in which they connected their authoring points and PoI content was flexible to a large extent for most of them: During Create Story, they often made impromptu changes to their narrative sequence, more so at the PoI content level (P15: “ For me, it didn't really matter whether what I showed at one PoI was given before or after another one as long as the path wasn't too confusing ”; P11: “ I tried many different ways to connect my PoIs, and when I found a more interesting connection I changed the authoring points I used as well. ”).

Most of the stories created in this pattern were suited to tour guides of the selected cultural heritage sites, either giving broad introductions to the space or focusing on a specific related topic: Three authors chose to provide comprehensive information on Jeju Mokgwana (P10, P16, P19), while four others highlighted the its main attractions—buildings within the site matched with famous paintings of them (P20), tangerines (P22), and a historic document on Jeju's history (P17, P18). For Hapcheon Haeinsa, authors targeted specific groups of audiences such as college students (P13) and children (P15) in creating thematic tours. The remaining authors of thematic narratives set Gyeongju Daereungwon as a site for trivia on Kings buried in its tombs (P11, P12) or the various treasures buried there (P14).

4.4 Linking Intent, Trajectory, Narrative, and Function

After patterns of authorial intent, spatial trajectory, and narrative composition for 22 Spacetelling content emerged, we charted each content according to their classification for all factors. This was to determine whether meaningful connections can be established across them, starting from authorial intent. Additionally, we included a fourth factor to identify which of the three functions of site-specific AR storytelling—Reinforcing, Remembering, and Reskinning, as defined by Azuma [ 1 ]—these connections may be associated with. By visualizing the coded data in an alluvial diagram, we were able to derive two dominant branches of connections between the four factors, as illustrated in Figure 5 .

Story-Centric Spacetelling . The first type of connection was found in six of the authored Spacetelling content, which we termed story-centric. This connection occupies most of the upper green section of the alluvial diagram. Of the nine authors who wished to focus more on emphasizing and delivering the narrativity of their content, seven chose confined trajectories that correspond to progressive narratives with clear story arcs of either fictional accounts (P1, P3, P4, P7) or historical events and legends related to their target heritage sites (P2, P5, P6). As a result, these content functioned to transform the given placeness of the target heritage sites (Reskinning) or to evoke collective memories of the past related to the spaces (Remembering).

Space-Centric Spacetelling . The second, space-centric connection we identified was common to 10 cases of Spacetelling content authored in our study. This prevailing connection is visible in the bottom orange section of the alluvial diagram. 12 out of 13 authors who expressed their intent to explore the spatiality of the cultural heritage sites they chose created encompassing trajectories that spanned across the entire target space. 11 of them chose thematic narratives that presented various information directly related to the target sites and their PoIs to be paired with the trajectories. Ultimately, these 10 cases served to complement the placeness inherent in their target heritage sites (Reinforcing).

5 DISCUSSION

5.1 Analysis of Identified Patterns in Spacetelling

Here, we analyze how contrasting behaviors observed in the authoring process of the two types of Spacetelling content can provide the basis on which causal relationships between their intent, trajectory, narrative, and function could be inferred. These differences were mainly found in: 1) the authors’ use of geotagged content; 2) the authors’ consideration of the site-specific AR environment; and 3) the authors’ intermedial strategy.

5.1.1 Analysis of Story-Centric Spactelling. Authors who engaged in story-centric Spacetelling perceived geotagged content in the storyscapes as collective material for narrative development. They relied heavily on the subject matter of geotagged content in determining the story they wished to tell. These authors proceeded to focus on selecting and grouping them as authoring points to compose separated segments of a continuous plot that became PoI content; Only then were they connected in a logical order to construct a narrative flow.

This implies that their intent for narrative delivery was primarily associated with the desire to exercise authorship for storytelling in the traditional sense–being in full control of a narrative world where characters and events progress on a linear story arc. In fact, all nine authors who intended narrative delivery created progressive narratives. That these narratives mainly served to reskin the target heritage site owes to the fact that their authors preferred to provide their own geotagged content. They differed from those provided through the authoring tool in that they contained material for stories diverging from the historical context of the target heritage sites. Adding new geotagged content thereby enabled more creative freedom and authorial control.

Consequently, most of these authors were primarily concerned with how they could maintain their control to achieve a consistently high level of narrative engagement for the end user. As the Spacetelling format requires the user to be on the move to follow a linear story augmented over multiple PoIs in real space, where stimulants extrinsic to the experience coexist, most authors considered this inherent condition as a complicating factor. They thought that when users move from one PoI to the next, a break in narrative continuity will inevitably occur, leading not only to a decline in engagement but also a critical disruption to the reception of the story as a whole.

Therefore, we speculate that the reason seven out of nine progressive narratives were linked to confined trajectories was that authors wanted to minimize the impact of these spatial and temporal breaks in the content: They chose few PoIs in mapping their story arcs for few breaks in between or concentrating the PoIs within a limited region of the target space so that the breaks would be reduced, both in distance and time traveled.

The intermedial strategy of these authors, which was to create many authoring points per PoI content, can be analyzed with the theory of hypermediacy and immediacy in remediation practices [ 8 ]. As they wished to transport the end user to a virtual realm, authoring points had to function as immersive gateways into narrative space. For this goal, the authors allowed their stories to progress to a considerable extent at one PoI (before a break from narrative space occurs) by placing and connecting various types of multimedia content in the story—up to 11 at most—as authoring points bound tightly together: In organizing a multitude of remediations into a structured plot segment, authors pursued both hypermediacy and immediacy to enhance their effectiveness in engaging the user with the augmented narrative space.

We postulate that this further established the dominant connection between progressive narratives and confined trajectories on account of the temporal aspect of Spacetelling, a factor which also complicated their authoring process along with spatial considerations: As the strategy entails that the user would have to spend an extensive amount of time at a single PoI, authors for this pattern may have chosen relatively few PoIs to account for the total time duration.

5.1.2 Analysis of Space-Centric Spacetelling. Authors of space-centric Spacetelling regarded geotagged content as potential guideposts for a spatial journey. Whether geotagged content for certain locations was available to use was considered first in devising a trajectory they wished to create for their target spaces. Contrary to authors of story-centric Spacetelling, these authors tended to first choose PoIs and connect them sequentially, determining the spatial structure of their content before they made decisions on the number and types of geotagged content to use in authoring PoI content.

As a result, the linearity inherent to Spacetelling content was stronger for space than it was for narrative, which explains the main association between spatial exploration and thematic narratives. As each geotagged content was selected as authoring points for the PoIs they were a part of, neither these authoring points nor the PoI content necessarily formed a logical narrative sequence. Instead, they were loosely bound together as a series of augmentations that provided additional information on their specific locations within the trajectory. We infer that this is why space-centric spacetelling content, which mostly took on the form of on-site tour guides, ultimately served to reinforce characteristics already present in the target heritage space.

In the same vein, one of the authors’ main objectives was to enhance the end user's engagement with the target heritage space through the aid of augmented narrative space. In contrast to story-centric Spacetelling, where placing the users in the augmented narrative space was central to its experience, orienting the users in real space to ignite and maintain their interest was crucial. Therefore, the users’ movement along PoIs in the site-specific AR environment was regarded as a driving component in executing their intent for spatial exploration, not as an impediment: The spaces in between PoIs were also thought of as part of their authored content, where extrinsic stimulants could further engage the user with the real space.

We surmise that this idea supported authors in associating their intent to explore space with encompassing trajectories that contain many PoIs covering most regions within the target site. However, the challenge for these authors was determining how they could induce the end user to stay on the encompassing trajectories they devised, as the thematic narratives they mapped onto them were not as continuous as progressive narratives.

The intermedial strategy of these authors was to disperse remediated multimedia data sporadically throughout the PoIs. Because these authors sought user engagement with real space through their content, they aimed to create augmentations that were neither overpowering nor obtrusive to the users’ experience of the real space, but at the same time noticeable and interesting enough to keep them on the trajectory. This led them to create PoI content with generally few authoring points that mostly contained information highlighting the PoI.

As a result, the narrative continuity between each PoI content was inevitably weaker than it was for story-centric Spacetelling: Instead of a clear story arc, loosely binding themes were used to provide sufficient logic for the content's sequence. Authors downplayed both hypermediacy and immediacy to address their priority for spatiality over narrativity in the target heritage sites. As this strategy corresponds to a relatively short amount of time spent per PoI, we infer that this enabled authors to proceed with their initial decision to include many PoIs that span the entire target heritage site without too much concern about prolonging the total duration of the experience.

5.2 Design Recommendations

Based on our analysis, we derive design recommendations for authoring tools that can assist creators in effectively realizing their intent for site-specific AR storytelling. Notwithstanding the fact that the strictly defined format of Spacetelling and its predesignated authoring process can limit the type of narratives to be authored, we believe that these recommendations can be applied to various domains employing site-specific AR storytelling, namely digital heritage. This is because the elements of Spacetelling do not deviate far from the majority of existing approaches to site-specific AR authoring. As reviewed in our related work, they commonly involve the remediation of multimedia content at specific real-world locations to generate spatial trajectories as narrative journeys [ 6 , 23 ].

1) Providing templates and AR assets based on intent : Our study shows that according to the author's priority between spatiality and narrativity in telling a certain heritage site, the content structure conformed to one of two dominant patterns. Reflecting the identified authoring patterns in standardized templates and providing them based on the authors’ intent—whether they aim to deliver a narrative through space or explore space through a narrative—will increase the efficiency of site-specific AR storytelling tools. We propose that these templates be designed as combinations of spatial and narrative models, the former based on levels of scale or regional divisions oriented within the target heritage space and the latter on degrees of linearity.

To apply an example to the Spacetelling tool, authors with an identified intent for narrative delivery should first be presented with the option to author on a story-arc structure mapped over a confined region of the site, selected on account of the relevance between the devised content's subject matter and the metadata of geotagged content therein. On the other hand, authors should also be given the freedom to reject the suggested template and choose from other models of spatial scale, region, and narrative linearity in case their content does not correspond to predefined types.

Likewise, authors should also be able to utilize different types of AR assets according to the type of Spacetelling they aim for. We suggest that assets provided in site-specific AR storytelling tools be categorized for spatial and narrative usage as well: Whereas spatial assets should consist of remediations that can reinforce the spatial attributes of cultural heritage sites (e.g. backdrops or signs), narrative assets should be centered on ones that can expand the impact of narrative space (e.g. 3D character models). For this, metadata properties that can distinguish these features and enable search and filtering functions based on the author's queries are also required.

2) Contextualizing spatiotemporal assistance : For story-centric Spacetelling, the challenge in incorporating the spatiotemporal influence of the site-specific AR environment was to overcome interruptions to narrative continuity caused by spatial and temporal breaks between PoIs. In space-centric Spacetelling, authors invited these breaks as a way to further orient the end user in the target heritage site, but struggled to compensate for a lack of narrative continuity in maintaining the sequence of their trajectories. Additionally, all the authors were conscious of the amount of time it would take for their content to be experienced, as they predicted that user engagement with the content would decrease sharply after a certain point. However, they had no means but to work under self-imposed limits without any validation of their assumptions.

Spatiotemporal assistance provided in accordance with the authoring contexts is a much needed function for site-specific AR storytelling tools. We suggest: 1) Enabling the placement of augmentations external or internal to the narrative in between PoIs based on the type of space authors wish to emphasize; 2) Assigning degrees of importance for authoring points and PoIs in terms of narrative to adaptively control the length of the content's on-site experience; 3) Calculating the estimated duration of the experience based on relevant factors (e.g. distance between every target PoI, the runtime of remediations at authoring points, etc.); and 4) Recommending the optimal amount of time for an AR experience according to the target heritage space.

3) Putting the author in the AR user's shoes : Our study demonstrates that an author-oriented approach to site-specific AR storytelling ultimately concerns the receiving end to a large extent. Authors often considered the end users and their experience during various stages of the authoring process, as they were intrinsically aware of the fact that the authored content can only be completed through its execution and embodiment in real space by the end-user-as-visitor. Therefore, providing sufficient means to understand the on-site AR experience is crucial. However, as Spacetelling is a complex, iterative process involving careful research, planning, and decision making, it can be better conducted in a static, web-based environment where the interface allows for more detailed inputs than in a dynamic, mobile AR environment in situ.

We thus assert that under such authoring conditions, it is imperative for AR storytelling tools to provide remote VR simulations of their content at the authoring stage as an alternative to the real experience. This will enable authors to register augmentations more accurately and make substantiated decisions on needed adjustments without the cost of having to be on site[ 55 ] to see what their intended outcome actually looks and feels like. In addition, we suggest implementing digital twins of target heritage sites with a high level of detail and data systems embedded in the VR environment. This will not only increase the VR environment's resemblance to the actual target site but also allow dynamic information in real space and time to be incorporated into the authoring process [ 9 ].

For the two types of Spacetelling identified, authors sought user engagement with either the augmented narrative space (story-centric) or the target heritage site (space-centric). However, making related decisions was difficult for the authors in the given remote desktop environment, in which the actual setting of their content—real space—is substituted by a purely virtual and abstracted version of it in the authoring process. In fact, many authors directly addressed the need for realistic renditions of the site-specific AR environment in the authoring tool so that they can perceive and navigate their AR narrative trajectories in a similar manner to that of the user.

6 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

In this work, we examined the ways in which authors create linear, site-specific AR narratives for outdoor cultural heritage sites and how these practices could inform the design of tools that support the process. The authoring tool prototype used in our study was designed with a space-driven approach where the target space is determined first and the terms of the authored content are dictated by spatial and narrative attributes specific to that space. We defined and applied Spacetelling as a content format that links units of a spatial hierarchy to a corresponding narrative hierarchy. Our results showed that the author's intent to either tell a story through space or explore space through a story determined the structure and characteristics of Spacetelling content, as was identified in the story-centric and space-centric links between intent, trajectory, narrative, and function.

Analyzing two dominant patterns through contrasts in the authors’ use of resources, consideration of the AR environment, and intermedial strategy, we conclude that these associations call for adaptive guidance on leveraging various aspects of placeness to support authorial intent and pursue stronger end user engagement for the author's benefit. Therefore, we assert that spationarrative templates and AR assets fitted to the author's needs, contextual spatiotemporal assistance, and realistic VR simulation for the content are key functions for site-specific AR storytelling tools to support authors.

Our study contributes to site-specific AR storytelling as a novel creative medium by presenting empirical details on the process and outcome of authoring in multiple cases and analyzing them through theoretical means. However, several limitations need to be addressed in our subsequent research. The first limitation concerns the size of our study. While 22 samples are well within the conventional range of in-depth, case-oriented analyses using a grounded theory approach [ 14 ], increasing the scale of the study may have resulted in other patterns and connections to emerge. Likewise, adding more variety to the cultural heritage sites we designated in our study as potential targets may have yielded different results as well. Although our selection was carefully considered to incorporate a wide range of spatial and narrative attributes, including other sites with even more differences may have motivated authors in ways that were not observed in our study.

Last of all, allowing the Spacetelling format to be more flexible in terms of its spatial and narrative linearity may have presented more meaningful implications for site-specific AR storytelling in general, as the medium is attuned to accommodating hypertextual and interactive content. Therefore, we will focus on developing a full-fledged authoring tool based on our design suggestions in the future, deploying it to a larger number of authors in creating site-specific AR narratives with varying degrees of linearity at a diverse range of cultural heritage sites and also other types of spaces. In doing so, we aim to conduct studies that can add to a more comprehensive understanding of site-specific AR storytelling from the author's point of view.
