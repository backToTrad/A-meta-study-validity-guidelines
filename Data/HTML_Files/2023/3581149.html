Information communication and visualization practices reflect two centuries of developments of conventions and best practices which may not be reflective of global audiences’ methods for conveying information. Contrasting between rural traditional visual culture and contemporary HCI and data-visualization, we argue that an understanding of traditional practices for information visualization is required for building rich data-narratives and making data-driven systems more accessible and culturally situated. Our ten-month ethnographic study investigates how rural Bangladeshi communities construct narratives through visual media. 1 Our observation, interviews, and FGDs (n=54) expose how participants convey risk management, decision-making, and monetary management practices to their peers. We find that villagers used a rich network of polysemic symbols and abstractions to manifest subjectivity, factuality, consequence, situatedness, and uncertainty; varied visual attributes for constructing narratives; and emphasized material relations among components in visuals. These findings inform the design of future systems for decision support in a culturally situated manner.

1 INTRODUCTION

Today's modern information communication and visualization practices reflect two centuries of developments of conventions and best practices. Such practices include adopting a minimalist approach when visually representing information to save resources and avoid the risk of subjectivity in the narratives [ 2 , 43 , 65 , 97 ]. Modern visualization designers in human-computer interaction (HCI), data science, and computer science frequently interpret the “subjectivity" in information and its representation as “untruthfulness" and “bias". As a result “abstraction", where visualization designers discard seemingly unnecessary details from the scene, is prized [ 4 , 5 , 61 , 89 ]. Inherent in this perspective, long rooted in the history of information visualization, is the notion of monosemic approaches towards visualization that construct visuals such that elements and entities have a bijective, one-to-one mapping with their meanings, and the meanings of the elements are known prior to observation of the collection of elements and entities [ 11 , 83 ]. Many visualization designers argue that this helps the visualization avoid harmful, ambiguous polysemic attributes where individual elements in visuals follow and are deduced from a consideration of a collection of element-meaning mapping which is not one-on-one [ 11 , 83 ]. The ideas of abstraction and monosemy in visual representation have developed in the past two centuries and become core practices in modern information visualization.

However, this practice of abstraction and monosemy may not always reflect global audiences’ methods for conveying information. Many communities visually represent their information and narratives using visual grammars which may not cohere with modern information communication and visualization practices. For example, in their work with traditional visual quilt makers, Hindu Idol makers, and witchcraft practitioners in rural Bangladesh, Sultana et al. have shown that such thousand-year-old yet still existing local traditional practices developed their own set of visual grammars over generations and used them for storytelling, record-keeping, moral education, and traditional healing [ 91 ]. This and similar other works showed that the local people's limited familiarity and unfamiliarity with modern visual grammars did not stop them from constructing, following, and decoding the messages from the visuals in quilts, Hindu Idols, and witchcraft amulets and similar other local traditional practices [ 90 , 91 ]. Furthermore, this literature has shown that avoiding subjectivity, discarding culturally situated details, and limiting the visual elements from having a network of possible meanings to be mapped with may limit the usability of visuals for the communities in question. In particular, when the West-centric, abstract, and geometry-based representations of information in visualizations are pushed to cross-cultural communities, they often fall short in connecting with the people when conveying narrative and rhetoric. However, it is unclear how to integrate such local traditional visualization into modern visual practices so that visuals could be more useful in combating daily-life challenges for the communities with specific ideals, education, literacy, and ethics that differ from that of communication and visualization designers.

Our ten-month-long ethnographic work addressed this agenda as a broader goal of our research. We conducted observations, interviews, and focus groups with 54 participants in three villages in rural Bangladesh. Initially, we observed and interviewed them about their daily-life activities and followed how they constructed and used visuals in day-to-day risk management, decision-making, and monetary management for contextual inquiry. In many sessions, we asked them to draw a case associated with their daily life and convey a message to their communities that would curate information to include causation, consequence, and suggestions. We then used those pieces in other sessions to understand how those visual narratives and rhetoric communicated meaning in the community . Our fieldwork broadly focused on the following research questions:

RQ1: How do rural visualization designers encode their cases and messages into visualization? How do they pick the elements and entities to associate with the meaning they want to convey?

RQ2: How are subjectivity and situatedness inhered and communicated in rural visuals? How are the attributes chosen by the designers and related by the villagers?

RQ3: How are visuo-spatial and temporal information, likelihood, and uncertainty encoded and communicated in rural visuals? How do the designers play with abstraction and meaning mapping while their messages in the visuals remain accessible to the villagers?

We found that villagers used a rich network of polysemic symbols and abstractions to manifest subjectivity, factuality, consequence, situatedness, and uncertainty; varied visual attributes for constructing narratives; and emphasized material relations among the visual components. Our findings show that the visual designers integrated subjectivity into the visuals when they wanted to put themselves in the design and wanted their voices to be heard. In working with situatedness, the visual designers reverse-engineered the possible interpretations of elements and their meanings as combinations so that even if the visuals are interpreted in multiple ways by the villagers, the end of the discussions may still be tied back to the message the designers wanted to convey. Therefore, the rural visual designers chose visual elements’ forms, colors, and shapes in a way that villagers could flexibly communicate with the visuals through their existing understanding of local cultural practices, myths, recreations, and moral values.

Our work makes four key contributions to information visualization, narrative visualization, and HCI literature. First, we present a deeper understanding of how rural Bangladeshi communities construct rich narratives in their visuals to convey day-to-day risk management, decision-making, and monetary management practices to their peers. Second, we show how visuo-spatial and temporal information, likelihood, and uncertainty are communicated in rural visuals by the elements and their attributes with culturally situated meanings and existing material values. Third, we show that along with the careful use of polysemic symbols and abstractions for meaning-making, cultural conventions of visual elements and their attributes were also crucial in constructing rural visual narratives. We further discuss how the lessons of polysemy and abstraction in rural situated visual narratives contribute to existing information visualization literature. Finally, we demonstrate how different data visualization standards and best practices, including encoding principles, design frameworks, and choice of colors and shapes, many of which we consider to be understood universally, may not apply universally across cultures and might fail to communicate with non-western audiences and we argue that designs that follow these standards may completely miss the mark because of a failure to incorporate local culture and context.

2 RELATED WORK

The core theme of our work is based on abstraction, monosemy, and polysemy in visualization. Abstract art constructs the visual language using shape, form, color, and line and creates a composition that may exist with a degree of independence from visual references in the world [ 4 , 5 ]. Thus, abstraction indicates a departure from reality while depicting imagery in art, allowing this departure to be slight, partial, or complete from accurate representation. From the Renaissance to the middle of the 19th century, Western art idealized the logic of perspective and attempted to reproduce an illusion of visible reality. By the end of the 19th century, many artists felt a need to create a new kind of art that would encompass the fundamental changes in technology, science, and philosophy. At the same time, modern visualization practices developed in parallel through the work of figures such as William Playfair and Charles Minard. The “abstraction" within information visualization and HCI-viz refers to the process in which visualization designers discard seemingly unnecessary details from the scene to construct a schematic representation [ 61 , 89 ]. Our work will also address the concept of “Monosemy" — the modern way of constructing visualizations where the elements and entities have only one set of one-on-one mapping with their meanings, and the meanings of the elements are known before the observation of the collection of elements and entities [ 11 , 83 ]. Many visualization designers argue that this helps the visualization avoid the “Polysemic attributes" of visualization where individual elements follow and are deduced from a consideration of a collection of element-meaning mapping which is not one-on-one [ 11 , 83 ].

2.1 Subjectivity in Visual Narratives

Much of data science, information visualization , and visualization research in HCI follow the positivist approach, considering subjectivity synonymous with “untruthfulness" or a “lie factor", and intend to minimize this factor in information visualization to sustain objectivity as an ideal practice [ 2 , 43 , 65 , 97 ]. This scholarship often theorizes that an objective visual representation capable of producing repeatable insights is the ideal and rarely discusses the positive aspects of subjectivity. Subjectivity in information visualization is rarely prized [ 2 , 24 ]. However, another literature has argued that eliminating subjectivity is quite impossible [ 48 , 99 , 100 ]. While handling data with personal experiences and having personal intentions, subjectivity in visual encoding is unavoidable [ 100 ]. Therefore, encoding data with subjective influence and working within the constraints of subjectivity has remained a challenge to the visualization community.

In this regard, researchers have attempted different alternative solutions connected to broader discussions concerning data provenance and statistical guarantees. For example, Wang et al. designed a multiple-view system that used two or more distinct views to support the investigation of a single conceptual entity in visuals [ 104 ]. Bonneau et al. associated subjectivity with uncertainties and emphasized investigating visualizing errors and uncertainties in the data [ 12 ]. Ragan et al. argued that provenance information that describes records and histories related to visualization is important for objectivity and developed an organizational framework of the different types of provenance information [ 76 ].

Literature on subjective data in psychology, sociology, literary folklore, and history acknowledges that subjective narratives can be a means of self-evaluation, sharing one's value system, self-exploration, one's practicing agency, and shaping identity and can potentially evoke sympathy, approval, or amusement, and create intimacy, trust, and social connections [ 38 , 52 , 53 , 56 , 109 ]. In parallel, data art literature also considers subjectivity and “point of view" a defining quality that helps the creator communicate their intent [ 62 , 78 , 102 ]. Critical visualization researchers cohere and discuss potential ways for subjective visualization to help the creator's voice and agency [ 30 ]. Researchers have also discussed the potential of subjective choices to communicate personal perspectives, feelings, and experiences in narrative visualizations [ 24 ]. While such author-driven approaches of narrative visualization allow the visuals to have linear ordering and heavy messaging capacities, Segel and Heer suggested that better interactivity and usability could be achieved by not following prescribed ordering and integrating subjectivity in a reader-driven approach [ 86 ]. This information visualization and HCI literature are still expanding by developing more appropriate methodologies, and rigorous analytical processes [ 22 , 55 , 65 ]. We reflect on this literature by addressing subjectivity in encoding principles, design frameworks, and communication through rural Bangladeshi visual narratives, which may tread the boundary between author- and reader-driven narratives.

2.2 Designing Situated Visualization

Situated visualization represents information displayed in proximity to the physical referents (such as people, objects, and locations) to which the data is related [ 17 , 107 , 108 ]. Visualization researchers have argued that putting such in-situ visual signs proximate to the referents benefits the users of public visualization and wearables [ 27 , 63 , 69 , 98 ]. Thus situated visualization is popular among visual designers and researchers. For example, Morais et al. have proposed “situatedness" as a crucial factor in their conceptual anthropographic framework [ 68 ]. While theorizing a humanist approach to graphical displays, Drucker pointed out the need for understanding the co-dependent relation between observer and experience [ 31 ]. Research shows how people collect and draw personal data using situated visuals and their materialities [ 57 ]. Creating and combining meaningful visualizations of personal habits, moods, and symptoms also help users in mindful self-tracking [ 6 ]. Thudt et al. have found the effectiveness in the physicalization of visuals constructed and used for self-reflection of individuals [ 94 ]. Some researchers extended this literature to public visualizations. For example, Moere and Hill discussed situated visualization in urban social and community aspects [ 67 ]. Researchers have worked on understanding the environment and the context where the visualizations will be deployed and identifying other practical requirements, including appropriate form factors, placements, representations, and levels of detail [ 17 , 74 ]. Recently, Bressa et al. have discussed the limited framing of situatedness in situated visualization and urged for field studies and participatory approaches of including the community [ 16 ]. Researchers have also worked on design exercises to develop methodologies for constructing better-situated visuals [ 64 , 81 , 108 ]. This literature informs about the importance of proximity and contextual understanding for the effectiveness of situated visuals.

A group of scholars also had a critical look at the participatory design of situated visuals [ 39 , 66 , 71 , 75 ]. In their extensive fieldwork on co-designing visuals with a homeless participant, Packard pointed out how power imbalance challenged their democratic participation in the process [ 71 ]. Additionally, Grubrium and Harper discussed the complex entanglement among the process of building rapport, trust, and reciprocity and the recognition of power imbalances inherent to matters of representation in such participatory visual research and urged to have more cautious with researchers’ positionality [ 39 ]. Furthermore, Racadio et al. have urged to consider ways of reflecting with participants in participatory activities to probe and reconfigure the power dynamics within the community for its benefit [ 75 ]. This literature informs our method and urges to be careful about power dynamics among the visual researchers, participants, and other stakeholders.

However, most works in the design of situated visualization literature build on the common idea of modern scientific knowledge as universal so that it has the same content no matter who possesses it— discarding the subjectivity in contents. Therefore, “situated" in the visualization scholarship mentioned above is one singular “situated" felt by visual designers and researchers. Consequently, there is limited scope for users with different experiences and different or no formal visualization training to interpret these visuals through their own “situated knowledge" that is subjective to their experience and community. This fact influenced us to borrow relativist scholar Donna Haraway's conceptualization of “situated knowledge" where she argues that facts can differ in their content from one time, place, and knower to another and thus capturing ‘reality as it really is’ may be dependent on institutional, technical, and cultural norms — a blurring of the distinction between context and content of knowledge [ 40 ]. Correll builds on Haraway and extends this concept to the existing data collection and analysis processes practices by data science and computing professionals [ 28 ]. We build on Haraway's definition of situated knowledge and also revisit Benedict Anderson's definition of community: imagined as sovereign, imagined as limited, and imagined as community [ 3 ]. We expand the understanding of “situated" in visualization literature to a more subjective and relativist sphere and re-conceptualize it as “situated knowledge that is shared by the community members who share similar interest and sensibilities of communionship, limitedness, and sovereignty." Therefore we build on existing literature on situated visualization.

2.3 Visualization of Order, Likelihood, and Uncertainty

Visualization research has a long history of presenting a series of observations arranged in chronological order. To represent periodic data, spiral diagrams are popular among visual designers where they place the data loop on a circle that represents a cycle [ 23 , 95 , 96 ]. Designers also use a hybrid form of spiral view to compare among multiple sets of categorical data [ 34 ]. The Gregorian calendar view is also popular among the designers since it allows more granularity in the time domain and therefore helps people identify periodic patterns and outliers [ 20 , 58 , 101 ]. For dynamic visualization, researchers use animated expressions where a visual chart plays each time point frame by frame and continuously shows the changing trend of the data [ 82 ]. To allow multiple dimensions of data to be presented in a dynamic view across time, visualization designers use Trendalyzer [ 21 , 103 ]. A themeriver view and a parallel coordinate chart with a time axis are notable for visualizing high-dimensional time series [ 41 ]. This literature motivated us to investigate how rural Bangladeshi people encode their visuals with time and order-sensitive data.

While tables, histograms, bar graphs, and pie charts have remained the most common visual representations of probability distributions [ 25 ], visualizing uncertainty has been addressed as a challenge [ 18 , 37 , 72 ]. Many researchers have defined it as incompleteness, inconsistency, error, and unreliability in data [ 36 , 47 , 59 , 60 , 80 , 87 ]. Several works have classified it as error, range, and statistical [ 72 ] and measured it using the probability of errors [ 14 , 33 ]. To visualize uncertainty researchers have experimented with statistical techniques like probabilistic graph layout techniques, Bayesian cognitive model, decision tree, and even people's beliefs [ 26 , 46 , 49 , 50 , 54 , 79 , 85 , 106 ]. This literature motivated us to investigate how rural Bangladeshi people design their visuals to deal with uncertainty, likelihood, and consequences.

3 METHODS

We draw on a ten-month-long ethnographic study in three villages, namely Shangkorpur, Rudropur, and Baulia, in Jessore, Bangladesh. These communities are renowned for crafting designer quilts (locally known as Nakshi-Katha) making, pottery design, and Hindu Idol crafting, where they frequently employ many forms of traditional visuals. Therefore, we frequently term our participants as “rural visual designers". We engaged with the villagers and studied local arts and handicraft makers in the villages through participant observations, semi-structured interviews, and focus groups.

3.1 Access to the Participants

To access our participants, the first author sought help from Rural Reconstruction Foundation (RRF), a non-profit global development organization that offers microfinance, education, health, and agricultural programs. She worked with their officials to reach out to the villagers. In this regard, their officials from the head office communicated with the branch offices and introduced us to their front-line microcredit workers [ 35 ]. The microcredit workers visited their clients’ houses to perform the formalities of processing loans and collecting weekly installments from the clients. After arriving in the village, the first author held a public community meeting with the microcredit clients and explained the purpose of the research. We specifically requested that the villager who were familiar with designing and crafting rural visual arts and crafts should voluntarily participate in our study. Sometimes, they asked about the research and possible benefits and concerns. After responding them, the first author recruited participants from the meetings based on their availability. Further recruitment happened through snowball sampling, and they chose among interviews, FGDs, and/or both.

The first author and ethnographer was born, raised, and lived in Jessore for more than 23 years. She is very familiar with many areas in the district and has also worked and conducted research in these areas for several years. Also, she is a native Bengali speaker with a local accent. Visual design research methods encourage this sort of positionality in ethnography [ 71 ], which helped her communicate with the NGO, access the villagers, and build rapport with participants. All interactions with participants were conducted in local Jessorian Bengali, which all the participants and the ethnographer speak fluently. We obtained oral consent from the participants since many villagers were low-literate and would have trouble reading and understanding written informed consent.

3.2 Observation

We started engaging with the participants through observation. We closely follow their lifestyle by observing their day-to-day activities, hangouts, meetings with friends and neighbors, and regular and fun activities. We investigated how they used different types of visuals in their day-to-day lives to communicate mundane and moral messages in groups and professional settings. The observation was participatory. We sought their permission before taking part in these activities with them, observed their actions and responses, and recorded them in our notes. We also often asked about their activities if they were confusing or required domain expertise. We conducted hundreds of hours of observation with more than 100 people (8 hrs/day for ten months).

3.3 One-on-one Interviews

We conducted one-on-one semi-structured interviews with 46 participants who were familiar with rural visual art and craft design. Among them, only five participants were also familiar with basic graphs and charts in modern information visualization they learned at school. The participants chose the venue of the interview. Generally, we sat at their home. Participants were sought based on rapport with the ethnographer in earlier sessions and further through snowball sampling. We asked them about their everyday interaction with mundane quantitative information, including money, time, days in a month, age, etc., along with the social, religious, and spiritual information they often encounter. In interviews, we asked participants to think of visual designs that would help them communicate a narrative or a message to their community. The participants discussed several scenarios based on their daily life experiences and orally discussed hypothetical visuals with us. Six participants decided to sketch their examples and drew images in researchers’ notebook, imagining the target audiences of the visuals would be their community members. We collected these sketches and drawing as part of our fieldnotes and later used them for further discussion with other participants. We also asked them about the mechanisms they use to manage and keep records of such information. We asked further related questions to understand the participant's responses and go deeper into topics. Each interview lasted around 30-40 minutes. We took detailed notes of the interviews and audio-recorded 22 (avg. 25 mins) with their permission.

3.4 Focus Group Discussion (FGD)

We also conducted ten focus group discussions with 43 participants who were familiar with rural visual art and craft design. Among them, only seven participants were also familiar with basic graphs and charts in modern information visualization that they learned at school. Each group consisted of four to eight participants. The RRF microcredit fieldworkers helped us recruit the participants. Before the session, we informed the participants that we would discuss about how different types of visuals they built and used in their day-to-day life. The participants formed groups and found a convenient time for the session. Our previous observations and interviews helped us with contextual inquiry, and based on those sessions, we formulated a more focused set of themes of investigation in the group discussion sessions. In sessions, we discussed their daily lives, how different forms of visual symbols and signs connected with their social, religious, and economic issues, and their significance. We also investigated how such visual information and representations were associated with their professional and daily life and further shaped their morals and ethics. In these sessions, we asked participants to think of visual designs that would help them communicate a narrative or a message to their community. The participants discussed several ideas among themselves, then discussed further to pick one for sketching. They discussed more and collaboratively designed and sketched and drew images in researchers’ notebook, imagining the target audiences of the visuals would be their community members. In the process of sketching and drawing the images, they explained their choice of size, shape, and colors and their materiality to us. We collected these sketches and drawing as part of our fieldnotes and later used them for further discussion with other participants. The FGDs were semi-structured – the researcher led the discussion and asked related questions to engage the participants, clarified their responses, and went deeper into the topic. The questions asked by the ethnographer during sessions spurred further discussions on related topics. The sessions were generally 35-45 minutes long. We took detailed notes and audio-recorded discussions if participants permitted.

3.5 Data Collection and Analysis

We collected approximately 12 hours of audio recordings, 200 pages of fieldnotes, and a total of 21 drawings from the interviews and FGDs. The audios and the fieldnotes were in Bengali. We transcribed the audio files and translated all the data into English. We then performed a thematic analysis on our transcription [ 13 , 15 ]. We read through the transcripts carefully and paid specific attention to patterns that occurred. Then we allowed the codes to develop naturally. Initially, 37 codes spontaneously developed. The most frequent codes were religion, art, color, subjective, perspective, situated, time, pattern, political, voices, suppression, folklore, etc. We clustered related codes into themes after a few iterations. We also discussed our themes with the participants to establish credibility. The following section presents seven drawings as representatives (Fig-1 to Fig-7) and discusses our significant findings.

3.6 Ethical Concerns and Approval

We also studied how the villagers used traditional qualitative and quantitative data and how they managed them in conveying moral lessons and social awareness messages. Therefore, we discussed how they made them understandable and communicative with the villagers who shared the same moral values and different moral ethics. Many of their practices, especially social and spiritual ones, were not based on scientific rationality. We were convinced that they relied on some alternative rationale; hence, we made sure not to impose our morals and values on them, not to demean their values, and not to push our judgment on them. Therefore, we did not intervene to respect the existing cultural values of the villagers. Furthermore, we made sure that our fieldwork did not downplay these local practices and also did not encourage villagers to seek help from such services and resources. When prompted, we also clarified our point of view on this matter and explained our moral standings and preferences. We discussed these concerns with the ethical review board of the researchers’ institutions. They examined and approved them in a formal review. We followed COVID-19-related instructions provided by our institutions and the local government and employed all the protective measures and protocols the local government and NGO suggested. Such measures include wearing masks, avoiding mass gatherings, maintaining social distance, budgeting meeting times with extreme caution, and hiring a paid cleaner to disinfect meeting spaces before and after in-person meetings.

5 FINDINGS

Through the discussions with the participants on the visuals that communicated risk management, decision-making, and monetary management messages, we found five major themes associated with rural visualization practices. We discuss them below:

5.1 Subjectivity

Modern information visualization research often sees subjectivity in data and visualization as a “lack of truthfulness" and, therefore, wants to avoid subjectivity in data, narratives, and encoded visuals. However, rural Bangladeshi visualization designers were aware that the audience of their visual might receive multiple subjective perspectives, and they cautiously reverse-engineered those possibilities in their visual encoding. In this regard, they prioritized having their voice heard. For example, one of the designers of Fig-3 was a local singer, and he explained to us how he received the budget cut negatively even though he understood the local economic situation,

“The budget cut is a case, but what hurts and matters here is the compromise with the stage and instruments and the reduced number of events organized. People thought they might not enjoy this low-budget event this year, so they did not come. This devalues local artistry. The empty chairs showed that neither the authority nor many people worry about my livelihood, my crisis. I blame the people. The program was free. If they had come and watched it, future event organizers would have been hinted that organizing festivals is safe now that people are coming to enjoy them." (P7, Male, 57 yrs)

Thus, the designer conveyed his emotion and affect through the number of empty chairs. He continued that the poor stage and the empty chairs in this piece could potentially convey four other subjective yet very important takes: (a) viewers may think there was budget-cut, (b) viewers may think the expected number of people did not show up, (c) authorities might think it was legitimate to cut the budget as many people did not come and enjoy, (d) local commercial organizers may think it is risky to organize more such festivals given people do not come and enjoy. On top of all these four takes, he wanted to convey that local artists’ emotional damage and economic crisis should also be a big concern. Therefore, he portrayed the Baul [village musician who walks around the village and sings and collects villagers’ donations] in the second image bigger than the people in both of the images and put his attire in the same color as the chair-covers to indicate his extreme poverty (see Fig-8(a)&(b)). Thus, the visual designers worked on both visualization creators’ and audiences’ subjectivity.

In another discussion on Fig-7 with its designers, they informed us that this piece was based-on their lived experiences. They wanted to convey their own problems as well, as they understood the troubles of other stakeholders here, too, and conveyed them in the drawing, as they explained,

“This is a problem for my kids and other parents whose children go outside in the afternoon and play. It is also problematic for the woman who is yelling, right? Because I know she works the whole day, and that is when she rests. Poor space management in this overcrowded house is to blame. I also understand the mosque people's disapproval because the children have destroyed the grass and plants by playing cricket on their lawn (pointing at the space highlighted in Fig-8(d))."(P13, Female, 33 yrs)

Thus the designers constructed the visualization in a way that when the other members of the household and the mosque people take a look at this image, even if they all only see the problem that is only subjective to them, they might all agree to one common problem statement that children's playground is needed. When we discussed this piece in three other FGDs in the absence of the creators, the participants argued that villainizing the woman would be unfair that after her day-long work, children hampered her rest, and she kicked them out. However, a participant who worked at the local mosque defended the mosque's action and pointed to the men sitting on the back wall and smoking (in Fig-8(c)) and commented,

“Children should stay away from places where people can show the audacity to disrespect the religious rules and morals by smoking within the mosque's territory. Maybe this is why he is kicking the children out because he can not kick out those smokers. Obviously, children need a better and safer playground."(P31, Male, 31 yrs)

However, some participants had a different take on the image. They pointed out rejections and emotions in the piece and discussed their effect on the children's upbringing and affect. Fifteen participants also connected the possible consequent rebel behavior of children, as the quote an interviewee,

“The woman kicks the children out, and the mosque guy also kicks them out. They must be very sad every day, so much rejection would psychologically damage them. I do not think this is going to be helpful for their upbringing. I would not be surprised if some of them consume marijuana on the walls like those guys (pointing at Fig-8(c)) and challenge the mosque guys like, "now come and kick me out, if you can", in a few years."(P52, Female, 39 yrs)

Thus, rural visual designers intentionally left scopes for audiences to have subjective takes and audiences saw multiple facets of the problems and discussed numerous concerns and suggestions. Since the visual designers intended this, they performed the encoding in a way that all the possible subjective perspectives could be tied back to the original message the creators wanted to convey and, therefore, enrich the visual narratives.

5.2 Situatedness

Traditionally situated visualization in info-vis represents data that is displayed in proximity to the physical referents, including humans, objects, and locations. Here, the proximity to the physical referents is important. For example, the situated visual in Fig-9(a) showed Carbon Monoxide sensor data on a screen; the red dots on the image indicated the presence of the gas in a specific place, while gas did not create such red bubbles in the physical world (image borrowed from [ 107 ]). However, once the sensor and the screen are moved to a different physical space, the image will not remain the same.

However, rural Bangladeshi visual designers perceived situatedness differently where physical proximity is not necessary. They built on “situated knowledge" that is supposed to be shared by the community members. Hence, cues in rural situated visuals are expected to communicate with the community members through their shared situated knowledge. When appropriate, the rural visualization designers left culturally situated cues next to the elements. With a change in viewing medium, time, or viewer's physical position, such visuals will remain the same. For example, following Islamic culture, half-moon and horn-speakers on top of the building indicate it is a mosque (Fig-9(b)); based on the history of the Bengali calendar, the stage under the Banyan tree means Pohela Boishakh festival (Fig-9(c)); and based on Bengali sign and semiotics, Kolka and floral patterns with new buds mean rural wedding (Fig-9(d)), as our participants explained. Thus, the rural Bangladeshi visual designers extended the understanding of situated visualization from “showing signs in proximity to the physical reference" to “putting signs that can be understood by the members who consider themselves a part of an imagined community". Here, the imagined community refers to a group of people who share similar beliefs and interests and therefore build the communionship, set the boundary of their community, and conceptualize their sovereignty [ 3 ].

An FGD with seven participants discussed Fig-4 in the absence of its creators, and we found how they read the culturally situated hints. They identified this as a wedding event and discussed if the police were there because the girl looked small and underage or because it was a dowry case. Then one of the participants said she believed it was about the bride being underage, and we asked her to explain why she thought so. We quote her,

“if this were about dowry, the marriage would have been canceled already, and the police would have been using their handcuffs. Later they might just set them free, but with the bribe. Because police want bribes and people want to be set free without any complaint on paper, dowry is a severe case, and people are often jailed. Since the police are standing keeping the handcuffs hanging from their waists, nobody is arrested, and it is not dowry."(P42, Male, 47 yrs)

Thus, the participants from different villages could still decode the situated meanings from the child-marriage piece as the visual designers and the discussants shared a similar understanding of Bengali wedding culture and Bangladeshi legal systems. Similarly, while discussing Fig-7 with 18 participants from some other villages than the visual designs’, the participants pointed at the lady's outfit, decoded the meaning of the color, and concluded that she was yelling because she had the voice in the household,

“Pink saree means her husband is alive. Traditionally, Bengali widows wear all white. They are powerless, and they do not talk or protest. Given that she is yelling at these many children, she is probably the elder bride in the household whom everybody has to obey. Nevertheless, it makes more sense that her husband is still alive. That is why she is allowed to yell."(P39, Female, 31 yrs)

Thus, the villagers extended the notion of “situated visualization" conventionally used in information visualization . We found that rurally situated visualization followed cultural, religious, and historical conventions and used community members’ shared understanding of meaning-making, while the idea of community itself was constructed based on shared beliefs, culture, and knowledge. Therefore, such situated visual hints were only understandable to specific communities, and they remained “abstract " to those unfamiliar with the conventions and culture.

5.3 Factuality and Iconography

In modern visualization practices, factuality is understood by the set of knowledge about the visuo-spatial factors that do not change over time, it is not dependent on the way they are conveyed, and it should equally well be extracted from static and dynamic visualizations [ 19 ]. Therefore, the factuality of modern information visualization does not depend on the representative color, form, shape, or other visual attributes. In rural Bangladeshi visualizations, the designers put the image of the exact entities and elements associated with the stories in the picture. For example, while encoding Fig-6, designers put an airplane where the Kutkut game starts and a mobile phone to indicate mobile money. They also name-tagged the hospital, local hotel, cafe, school, college, park, marketplaces, ambulance, etc., to indicate the elements in Fig-1 and Fig-5.

However, while the participants were drawing Fig-1, we discussed whether drawing dead bodies and blood on the street would look like violent content and if they still want to put it this raw. They clarified their position,

“I have drawn eight people in the picture. If I used some other symbol, like green circles to present alive people then dead people should have been put like no circle or some circle of another color. However, people do not care if there are eight green circles vs. four green circles and four yellow circles, or one bar with another little smaller bar, right? They will be shaken seeing the blood and the bodies. It is for their attention. Let's represent humans using human forms and not using something random."(P21, Female, 23 yrs)

Thus, the rural visual designers used the forms of damaged human bodies for human death and blood to convey how severe the consequence of an accident might be. Besides this piece, the participants factually represented the elements and events in Fig-2, Fig-3, Fig-4, and Fig-7.

The participants also talked about how they conveyed emptiness and zeroes through their drawings. While drawing Fig-3, they differentiated that being zero and being void should have two completely distinguishable representations, as they explained from their experience of the Pohela Boishakh event incident,

“If I do not draw anything on the white page and leave it empty, it does not mean the place in the real life was empty. Because there is nothing that can indicate there is nothing. However, there were empty chairs because many people did not show up. If you expect fewer people and fewer people come then that is not empty. If you expect more people and they do not come, then the chairs are empty, and it feels empty." (P7, Male, 57 yrs)

Thus this visual designer used zero and void to represent the value of empty and the feel of empty, which many of today's modern information visualization would miss.

While in the examples above, the participants used one-on-one mapping of fact-vs-elements, in some cases, they presented a fact using a combination of elements that has a well-established meaning by tradition. Note that combining several graphical parameters to an icon representing multidimensional data is one of the popular modern visual methods frequently used in info-viz [ 29 ]. In Fig-4, the participants based on the local cultural theme of the wedding and used the locally available and widely used elements wedding. The material values of those elements were widely known to the broader rural community that when we discussed this piece with 36 participants in the absence of the creators, they immediately interpreted the whole combination and the collective meaning of the elements, as we quote one from FGD-3,

“there is a man with big Topor (traditional groom-hat used in local wedding ceremonies) sitting inside the left side pandal. On the right side, a man is cooking, likely a caterer, because Bengali kitchens do not have such stoves where women cook and women do not cook in public either. Moreover, right next to the male chef, there is the girl's pandal; there is a banner that says, "Shuvo Bibaho". This is a marriage event." (P17, Female, 41 yrs)

While this group of participants could decode this as a marriage, FGD-5 further dissected the piece and collectively extracted more stories from it. They pointed to the police and discussed what it would mean. We quote two of them,

“: Police is here, which means something wrong. However, they are not doing much, which means the reason they were here could not be resolved. Two groups of people are fighting, and their heads are horizontal (pointing to Fig-11). (P27, Male, 45 yrs)

: Does this mean they are spinning in anger in the middle of a quarrel, like the demons’ and Jinns’ heads when they are outraged? So the number of people in each group is quite equal, meaning the tension is very high and sustained. (P28, Female, 39 yrs)

: Could it be that the men are bad, like demons, and trying to cancel the wedding? (P26, Female, 22 yrs)

Note that according to Bengali myths and folktales, demons and Jinns often confront people, rotate heads like wheels, and spin fire-splinters when they are raging. The participants built on this knowledge to decode the subsection of the piece. However, they also were suspicious and discussed if they were child predators, and the designer encoded them with the attributes of demons. Thus, rural Bangladeshi visual designers frequently used factualities and iconographies while constructing their visuals. However, one must note that here the material connections of the elements to the broader socio-culture, local myths, and value systems are significant, as well as the forms, shape, color, and size used in constructing the visuals.

5.4 Frequency, Sequence, and Pattern

Modern information visualization designers frequently use bar plots, polar plots, and line plots in time-series to handle time, chronology, and frequency. In such plots, the designers portray how frequently some event occurs using elements’ height, width, radius, and distance from baseline, leaving the interpretation for the readers of the visuals. However, in rural Bangladeshi visual design, the meaning from the visual representing the frequency is also tied in the visual when it is encoded, and the audience of the visuals is habituated to read it that way. For example, we discussed Fig-7 with an interview participant who was not present when the piece was drawn. She pointed at the yelling woman and explained how she could find this was the woman's frequent action,

“If you look carefully, she is yelling at these many children. And some children are actually leaving through the exit of the household. It seems like she frequently yells at them to kick them out and children know they have to leave." (P34, Female, 27 yrs)

The participants also used recurrent behavior of patterns in visuals, especially in Fig-5. While drawing it, the designers explained to us that their understanding of frequency and pattern was connected to the event that often happens to them or they take part in their daily lives. Therefore, they explained their understanding of recurrence using the Ludo game where events would happen again and again independent of the actors (or the player of the game in this context). We quote one of the participants from FGD-5,

“The road does not change, it is closed-looped. This is the same as women's journey in society; they would go through the same problems every day. Also, the patterns of the problems are the same; women walking past the tea stalls would be harassed. They will be cat-called on their way to school, college, or the market. This pattern is the same for every woman." (P25, Female, 23yrs)

While discussing Fig-2 in absence of its creators in multiple FGDs, the discussants pointed out that the fight at the mosque was portrayed sequentially. Thirty participants took their time in interpreting the piece and interpreted the sequence and consequence of the piece, as we quote one of them,

“Let's read from left to right (pointing to Fig-12, following the sequence in (b)). (i) It is a mosque. (ii) People from far places come to the mosque (pointing finger to the moon and the speakers indicate) and parked their vehicles. Some are holding animals, so they are here for sacrifice. (iii) When the sacrifice was over, and the meat was being cooked, (iv) people were being served the meat and (v) poor people were about to eat, (vi) some were fighting because they wanted to eat more? Now, if we read from right to left, people are fighting because some people are already eating the meat of the sacrificed animals, and they are unhappy. Maybe they are goons and not poor people. They are here to snatch mosque's meat." (P46, Male, 38 yrs)

Thus, the participants told us they were habituated to reading the images to find more perspective from the visual pieces and construct multiple narratives, as they do with local traditional arts. However, they also informed us that such discussions involved more people's views and would generally confirm with their friends and fellows in constructing such narratives.

While all the images we discussed with the participants were based on some message important to society, the participants found patterns and sequences in many of them. They explained that they believed such patterns and sequences could also be broken. In this regard, they pointed to pre-existing visual clues in the images. For example, in absence of its creator, we discussed Fig-6 with 18 participants. The participants pointed to quadrilaterals 3 and 4 in the hop-scotch game-looking table and discussed the most influential spots in an immigrant worker's life. One of them said,

“You can actually make sure to skip both 2, 3, and 4 and directly land on 5. Like you do not get married and just come back someday and enjoy whatever the house offers you. Then not worry too much about the ownership or settled married life. But that would be absurd, like in the game-high likely nobody can jump from quadrilateral 1 to 5." (P35, Female, 49 yrs)

Thus, the rural visualization designers and the audiences used examples and meanings of people's behavior and local games to communicate frequency, sequence, and pattern in local visuals.

5.5 Likelihood and Uncertainty

Our participants also shared how they handled likelihoods in information visualization , especially when they encoded Fig-5 and Fig-6, where they discussed opportunities, possibilities, hope, and disappointments. We noted that the participants divided the subthemes of their thoughts, put them under segments (Fig-5 and Fig-6), and let them branch out, like the modern ways of handling likelihood through a decision tree (Fig-13(a)). While all end-node pairs sum up to 100% in the decision tree, our participants did not worry about any such summation. While drawing Fig-5, the designers explained that factors related to women's harassment on the street remained the same daily, and random bystanders and the police rarely intervened. So the likelihood of women getting less harassed was a low possibility, as they said,

“...(B)ut the next day they might likely face the same problems again because they are going through the same ways, right? Because people are not changing, the harassers are in the same corner as yesterday. Random people will stay on the street doing things but not helping girls. This is how the problem is long-existing, circular, and permanent. So, it made more sense to make it like a Ludo court - you end where you start because it is unlikely your journey will be something different." (P22, Female, 26 yrs)

Thirteen other participants also pointed out that even though the market indicated the neighborhood's growing economy and suggested more social development, it had no physical connection to the main circuit and had a zero impact in diminishing the problems. The participants explained to us that the growing economy did not change the local women's safety situation and therefore they connected their experiences and perceptions to the piece to depict the probable effectiveness of such economic interventions in society, as we quote one of them,

“The modern-looking marketplace has no lane by it, this means even with progress in the market and local economy, the problem stayed the same. These markets got glass walls and doors, but they are filled with nasty people. Previously they used to stand on the street under the sun to tease and cat-call girls and women; now, they stand inside the air-conditioned market to harass women." (P41, Female, 20 yrs)

As the conversation on an effective solution to women's harassment progressed, seven participants showed us the two lanes on the top right and bottom right of the piece and discussed how they interpreted the lanes’ impact (Fig-13). In this discussion, The participants told us that often different NGOs and government initiatives regarding women's safety state that formal education will help resolve it and ensure women's safety as educated women will have economic freedom and power. However, 28 participants’ experiences suggested that the same uncertainty remained even in new workplaces or educational institutes they entered after finishing school and college. Their experiences cohered with designers’ thoughts,

“We have also put the schools and the college and put an outbound lane with it. See, it is the same color as the main circuit. See, we have sent our children to school and educated them. They go to other cities and towns for further study and work. However, are those places free of the problem in question? No, our children travel from the local circuit of women harassment to a new one. While the path is outbound, their experience is pretty much the same in the relocated society. It is uncertain, but based on our experiences, we are not much hopeful regarding our girls getting less harassed after being relocated in a new same circuit." (P48, Female, 25 yrs)

We discussed Fig-5 with 25 other participants in the absence of its creators. The participants agreed with the lanes’ concept and the never-ending loop of women's harassment. The participants picked the mud road on the top-right in Fig-5 and Fig-13, and explained that mud lane and tree as these elements are related to the concepts of change and regeneration in local myth and folktales and therefore they thought the creator of the images used this textbfsigns to suggest a high-probable and sustainable social reformation, as they explained,

“ : I think the creator of this visual wants to put more emphasis on the top-right. All the labels in the photo are in English, except for the park, which is in Bengali. Also, a mud lane and trees are there. Mud and trees are indicators of big changes in our myths, so maybe the creator is trying to say the change should come from the soil. (P16, Female, 31 yrs)

: The children are playing there. These days in the parents-teachers meeting, they say we must carefully notice our sons and teach them to be non-vindictive to the girls. Maybe the artists wanted to convey the same message? (P19, Female, 36 yrs)

: That makes sense because if the change initiative does not involve understanding the culture and environment, they would not sustain, no matter how much money or good lesson you give them. See, there is a tree on top of the house, too. Maybe they want to mean that changes require teaching at home. (P18, Female, 38 yrs) "

Thus, using this particular piece, the participants discussed how they indicated and understood this generation-long issue of gender harassment, portrayed limitations and ineffectiveness of existing solutions, related their perceived influential factors to uncertainties, and suggested possible and sustainable interventions based on their experiences and the visual clues in rural visualization.

6 DISCUSSION

This paper presents our findings from a ten-month-long ethnographic study that investigated how rural Bangladeshi communities construct narratives through visual media. Our fieldwork exposes how participants convey risk management, decision-making, and monetary management practices to their peers. We found that villagers used a rich network of polysemic symbols and abstractions to manifest subjectivity, factuality, consequence, situatedness, and uncertainty; varied visual attributes for constructing narratives; and emphasized material relations among visual components. Our findings inform the design of future visualization systems for decision support in a culturally situated manner.

6.1 Local Conventions of Using Visual Attributes

Our findings show that rural visualization designers relied on existing rural conventions of using color, line, shapes, and forms. For example, while visually encoding outraged people in Fig-4, the designers put the heads of the people horizontally to convey that the heads were spinning in anger which is analogous to demons’ and Jinns’ sign of anger in rural folklore. To convey that the woman who was yelling at the children actually has a voice in the household that she can frequently yell, the designers put a pink saree on her to convey that her husband is alive, which is crucial for women to have a voice in patriarchal societies. To indicate the pandals related to the wedding, the designers put Kolka and flowers with new buds, which are conventionally used in Bengali weddings to signify fertility. Also, the designers used half-moon and horn speakers on top of the building to convey that those buildings are mosques. These conventions are culturally situated in this community. And, they are understandable and relatable to the community members as long as they imagine communionship through shared understanding in common interests, sensibilities of sovereignty, and collective limitations [ 3 ]. A vast body of literature in information visualization works on case-based appropriate and effective visual attributes, including color, line, shapes, and forms [ 10 , 32 , 42 , 42 , 92 , 93 , 105 ]. Our work joins this literature and urges that understanding such culturally situated convention is essential for visual designers, especially those who work in the space of iconography and design technologies for specific communities; otherwise, they might risk designing visuals misleading or futile to the communities.

6.2 Subjectivity, Semantics, and Meaning Mapping

While the majority of today's visualization literature discards the subjectivity in data and visualization, considers that as lie-factor, and wants to remain objective by using only one-on-one mapping of element and their meaning, aka monosemy, rural Bangladeshi visual designers allowed designers subjectivity in their data and visuals, and they left the opportunities for the audiences to have multiple perspectives based on their subjective standing [ 4 , 5 , 61 , 89 ]. For example, in two different discussion sessions on the visualization of the scarcity of children's playgrounds, the female participants and the mosque worker interpreted the image from two different perspectives. The female participants thought it was legitimate that the lady yelled and kicked out the children from the yard. In contrast, the mosque worker thought the children should not stay in the presence of marijuana smokers whom even the mosque could not discipline. However, the piece eventually conveyed to both groups that the scarcity of children's playgrounds is a “community" problem that should be resolved, even though they interpreted the piece from two completely different perspectives. The way rural Bangladeshi visualization allows such polyvocality in design and yet is relatable to people with different perspectives could be a lesson for the visual designers working on developing technology for conflicting voices.

The rural visual designers also used polysemic approaches in their designs where individual elements in visuals followed and could be deduced from a consideration of a collection of element-meaning mapping, which is not one-on-one. For example, in the piece about child marriage, the designer portrayed the police. In many discussions, the meanings of their presence were mapped as “they are doing nothing", “they are helpless here", “they are here because the marriage has dowry issues involved", “they are here because the bride is underage," etc. Even when the one relevant but unrelated interpretation (about dowry) did surface in the discussions with the other participants than the designers, those discussants investigated the meanings of other elements present in the image. They, therefore, switched their interpretation of the police's presence. The way rural Bangladeshi visual designers did not limit the scope of polysemy for the sake of abstraction and getting rid of subjectivity, and yet they were making sense to the general people through the visuals, could be a lesson for visual designers working in cultural semiotics and meaning mapping. Already a body of literature argues for integrating polysemic approaches in mainstream information visualization design [ 83 ]. We build on this literature and urge that more in-depth ethnographic and participatory visual design research should be done to integrate such practices in the visual design of daily life technologies.

6.3 Engaging with Missing History

Our study also found that rural Bangladeshi visual designers often borrowed forms from local games. For example, Fig-5 and Fig-6 borrowed from Ludo and Kutkut courts. Furthermore, they improvised (e.g., they added two lanes heading out of the women-harassment circuits and signs next to immigrant workers’ life circles). While our research and previous work in HCI and information visualization have urged HCI and visualization researchers to engage with local communities through their history, culture, and norms [ 73 , 84 , 91 ], we also argue that understanding habits related to people's recreational practices is also essential. However, this might be particularly challenging for Bangladesh because Bangladesh has a long history of being colonized, and therefore most of the century-old recorded history is mostly about political and financial events important to the colonizers. However, many communities kept their record informally through folk music, dramas, and games that are no longer in practice [ 8 , 9 ]. For example, poems, ballads, and songs about the Santal rebellion of 1855 in Bengal captured the history from their perspective, but these are not well-acknowledged as recorded history [ 7 ]. Moreover, many poems and songs have gone missing over time creating a gap in history. Therefore, such missing history is associated with the respective community's identity and losing that history deprives the associated community of benefiting from data-driven systems, including visualization, and hence, further marginalizes them. We found that rural people often constructed their visualizations using culturally situated and historically significant elements, including mythological and local game components, while the significance of the elements has only been passed down through the generations and was never a part of recorded history. In this regard, we argue that visual designers would benefit by directly engaging with the communities through ethnographic and historiographic studies, and design workshops. Visualization researchers frequently co-design with communities [ 1 , 17 , 73 , 91 , 110 ]. We argue for more such research activism with rural Bangladeshi marginalized populations and similar other communities in the Global South.

6.4 Narratives and Rhetoric in Information Visualization

Our work also joins the existing body of literature on visual narratives and visual rhetoric [ 44 , 45 , 51 ]. This body of visual design literature often considers visual rhetoric and narrative-making as a collaboration where designers look to improve visual techniques by integrating information on designers’ provenance, presence, and positionality, and the interplay of sequencing the frames and forms so that visuals are more accessible and fair to the audience. However, such approaches often mitigate the role of audiences of visualization as passive participants in this collaboration of rhetoric and narrative making. Segel and Heer termed this as “author-driven visual" approach as visual designers are in power to decide the order and message [ 86 ], while a “reader-driven" approach allows the readers to enjoy the free interactivity. Though this work acknowledges that this is not a binary, visualization design pedagogy and standards often refer to it as such. We find that this division is much more nuanced in our fieldwork. Rural visual designers, completely unaware of modern information visualization theories, employed author-driven, reader-driven, balanced visual approaches based on their intended narrative and rhetoric and intention to let the readers use their own judgment for a polyvocal interpretation of the visuals. In effect, the designers attempt to foresee and curate the ways readers will participate in their work, especially with regards to their voice, identity, and values. They also carefully analyzed how their representation would be recognized by the other members of the community. Subaltern scholars often point out the risks of marginalized communities further facing injustice through a misrecognition of them in information and record-keeping [ 88 ]. Therefore, our work extends the existing narrative visualization framework by arguing for the political recognition of both visual designers and readers for more polyvocal and useful visual design for marginalized communities.

6.5 Implications to Visualization

Finally, our study offers important lessons for information visualization researchers and practitioners. Our core take-away for the broader information visualization and design communities is to emphasize that standards and best practices which are often believed to be universally applicable may not be such. Instead, our study shows that rural visualization techniques are closely connected to the history and culture of the rural population, and it is difficult to produce and understand such visualization without the knowledge of the community. Such entanglement between visualization and community is rarely seen in the modern information visualization s practices, which operate largely agnostically from any specific design context or situation. Moreover, in most of the cases, the visualization designers are outsiders of the community whose data are visualized. Hence, ’visualization’ has become a practice that is alienated from the cultural sensitivity of data and representation. We argue that such alienation is not capable of capturing the essence of data and reasoning with information in a rural community. Visualization should be rooted in the history, culture, and the context of a community whose data is being represented. While in many common use cases in information visualization the intended audience is well adapted to standards and conventions, this may not be the case on a global scale. This argument essentially cancels the justification of a ‘universal’ visualization scheme, which is prevalent today. Instead, our paper argues for a situated visualization practice that is more closely tied to the data and its owners.

Next, we turn to today's visualization literacy trend. Most visualization design education and training involve learning maths, statistics, geometry, modern scientific rationales, and the operation of software. This learning process does not allow the visualization designers to learn the traditional visual practice of a community, and hence the scope of their visualization becomes limited. Consequently, they risk missing out on understanding the contextual and situated information inherent and related to the data while designing visuals that might be less useful and accessible to the intended community than expected [ 73 , 84 , 91 ]. On the other hand, the onus of learning ‘scientific’ ways of information visualization is often imposed on the people living outside the ‘modern’ and ‘western’ bubble . This causes an unfair treatment of the marginalized populations by ignoring their traditions and imposing on them the modern scientific visual literacy . To this end, we propose that information visualization literacy should go beyond the learning of statistics, formula, math, programming, and iconography, and should incorporate methods like ethnography, histography, participatory design, and co-design. That will allow the visualization designers to close the huge gap between different modes of visualization, and to make visualization more accessible, meaningful, and effective for a wide range of populations.

7 LIMITATIONS

Our work has several limitations which might influence how our implications and broader considerations ought to be interpreted. First, our work is not free from participation bias and selection bias. We engaged with the villagers through a snowball sampling process. While we can assume that opinions and arguments represent the collective view of the residents of the whole geographic area, this may not actually be the case. Second, our interaction with the participants could also have suffered from experimental and methodological flaws. Question-order bias, self-presentation maintenance, and power imbalances all might influence the things we observed.
