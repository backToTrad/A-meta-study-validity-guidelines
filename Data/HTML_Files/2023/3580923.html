Access to computer-aided fabrication tools, such as 3D printing, empowers various craft techniques to democratize the creation of artifacts. To afford new blow molding techniques in the field of Human-Computer Interaction, we make efforts to simplify this challenging handy fabrication and enrich the design space of blow molding by taking advantage of the thermoformability and heat deformability of 3D printed thermoplastics. We propose a novel and democratized blow molding technique, PneuFab, enabled by FDM 3D-printed custom structures and temporal triggering methods. Then we implement and evaluate a design tool that allows users to play with parameters and preview the resulting forms until achieving their desired shapes. Showcasing design spaces including artifacts with complex geometries and tunable stiffness, we hope to expand access and dive into what more the digital blow molding fabrication can be.

1 INTRODUCTION

Fused deposition modeling (FDM) 3D printing, as accessible and additive fabrication technology, has gained a central place in today's making trends, from rapid prototyping [ 28 , 30 ] to creative expression [ 35 , 49 ], even building interactive devices [ 11 , 12 , 16 , 40 , 50 ]. Taking advantage of automatic and semi-automatic custom design in controlling, 3D printing can also serve as an augmented-craft tool to achieve complex aesthetics by lowering the threshold of the craftsmanship [ 6 , 26 ], which empowers a wide range of possibilities for crafting artifacts. Therefore, we see an opportunity to introduce blow molding techniques that have existed for a long history but not fully been explored in the field of Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) community.

Blow molding can produce hollow structures with large volume, light weight, and complex curvature surfaces, which is widely used in the mass manufacture of plastic products [ 66 ]. Essentially, blow molding is a practice of inflating malleable hollow material known as parison into a target form, which can be controlled by manually shaping or pre-designed molds. This principle is applicable to various materials, including thermoplastic, glass, and sugar. In historical craftsmanship, artisans can blow sugar or glass for aesthetics or utility [ 19 , 33 ]. However, forming the molten material into a parison requires special machinery (e.g., furnaces for molten glass and extruders for plastic resin). Moreover, the expanding process is exceptionally fast and requires operators to be skillful and experienced in shaping the desired form unless a mold, which lacks personalized expression, is applied. Recently, artists have experimented with customizing blow-molded products using digital tools [ 2 , 38 ]. However, the limited exploration of controllability and predictability of blowing shape-changing restricts its personalization and democratization.

Previously, by combining the blowing method with shape memory polymers for 3D printing, material scientists have proposed a new 4D printing paradigm, in which the controllable transformation is achieved by designing composite structures with spatially varied stretchability [ 64 ]. However, this approach poses considerable challenges in relation to democratization in terms of material accessibility and equipment affordability: the method requires specially synthesized resin and a specific photopolymerization process.

Inspired by the shape-changing principle of prior work [ 64 ], this paper presents PneuFab, a novel and low-cost hybrid fabrication workflow that allows normal users to design and create hollow volumetric shape changes through blowing simple FDM-printed thermoplastic structures. To enhance the controllability of the expansion mechanism of thermoplastic inflatables, we investigate the material mechanism of expansion and shrinkage and elaborate its effects on designing composite structures (e.g., thickness) and trigger techniques (e.g., blowing duration and multiple stages). To better leverage PneuFab for novice users, we propose a design tool that allows users to edit and real-time preview the volume-changing results from a simple design with a small set of input. When the design is ready, step-by-step fabrication instructions are generated to assist users in completing the target design.

This paper presents the following contributions:

  • A democratized novel fabrication workflow that enables the multi-stage volumetric transformation of 3D printed hollow structures to fulfill organic shapes and delicate textures with easily accessible FDM printers.
  • A tailored design tool with simulation and fabrication guidance that helps novice users understand and execute the PneuFab workflow.
  • Exploration of the design space for hollow structures with visual aesthetics and interactive function via several novel applications (Figure 1 ).

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Hybrid Fabrication with 3D Printing

3D printing is becoming democratized and ubiquitous, playing an important role in HCI for rapid prototyping [ 5 , 23 , 28 , 46 ] and building tangible devices [ 3 , 16 , 40 , 50 ]. Prior works have explored advanced techniques to enrich the functionality of 3D prints with biomaterials [ 54 , 60 ], articulated structures [ 12 , 62 ], optical displays [ 36 , 63 ], and embedded circuits [ 51 ]. To further extend the boundary of 3D printing, researchers have proposed hybrid fabrication by merging 3D printing with other crafting techniques.

As an accurate, efficient, and automated digital fabrication technology, 3D printing can support the hands-on crafting process with alternative structures and assistive tools to create 3D-printed lithophanes [ 55 ], digital joints for the carpentry [ 26 ], and computational hand weaving [ 6 ]. Moreover, the introduction and combination of other crafting processes empower 3D prints with more unique properties [ 37 ] and functionalities [ 11 , 13 ]. For example, ProtoSpray [ 11 ] uses spray coating to build displays with 3D-printed arbitrary shapes. Hong et al. [ 13 ] leverage copper electroplating to enhance the conductivity of 3D-printed circuit boards. Meanwhile, some works invite human participation and intervention during or after the 3D printing process towards interactive fabrication, which is more personalized, expressive, and artistic with varied outcomes [ 7 , 18 , 48 ].

In our work, PneuFab reveals the interplay between 3D printing and blow molding, highlighting how this emerging fabrication technique could support and supplement each other for a wider design space in aesthetics and functionalities.

2.2 Thermoplastic for Shape-Changing Interfaces

Beyond solely for finalized production, thermoplastic has been widely explored in digital fabrication to achieve shape-changing behaviors. When heated above its glass transition temperature, thermoplastic can be transited from a glass state to an elastic state [ 22 ]. The fabricated thermoplastic objects allow hands-on customization and passive shape-changing with enriched thermoformability from embedded heating elements [ 9 ], metamaterial structure [ 20 ], and heating susceptors [ 61 ]. Several researchers have proposed a wide range of automated tools to facilitate the shape-changing process with interactive fabrication systems [ 29 , 31 , 41 , 56 ]. For example, FormFab [ 31 ] incorporates the assistance of a robotic arm for continuous formative fabrication with thermoplastic sheets.

Another common approach is to leverage the shape memory nature of thermoplastic for 4D printing technology, which enables spontaneous transformation through self-bending [ 45 , 52 , 53 ], self-folding [ 1 ], and self-rising [ 10 , 45 ] structures in response to heat triggering. In addition, researchers leverage the heat-actuated foam material for self-expanding structures [ 15 ]. Recently in the field of material science, Zhang et al. [ 64 ] proposed a novel 4D printing paradigm, which shares a common design vision with PneuFab as both utilize thermo-pneumatic actuation and composite structures with locally varied stretchability. Nevertheless, there are significant differences in material and fabrication methods between the two projects. Firstly, the material mechanisms which determine local stretchability differ. The structures in Zhang et al.’s work [ 64 ] are printed with multi-material of different glass transition temperatures; In PneuFab, we employ only a single material but leverage the difference in heat transference resulting from varied thicknesses. Zhang et al. [ 64 ] specify the light dose during digital light processing (DLP) printing to manipulate the cross-linking density of resin, which determines the glass transition temperature, but the meticulously prepared resin mixture and controlled DLP instrument hinder the democratization. In contrast, PneuFab structures can be printed with off-the-shelf filament and desktop FDM printers. Besides programming the target shapes with different structures, PneuFab further designs a set of temporal trigger techniques for various configurations and wide design space.

2.3 Fabricating Programmable Inflatables

Air is light, ubiquitous, and versatile, which takes up space with indefinite shape and volume. When manipulated, it can drive the deployment of structures and materials through expansion for shape-changing interfaces [ 17 , 32 , 59 ], pneumatic devices [ 65 ], and large-scale furniture [ 39 , 58 ]. Along this line of effort, many prior works in HCI focus on the fabrication of inflatables to achieve the target shape after inflation. Some researchers have proposed designing multi-material constraints for programmable inflation [ 8 , 21 , 25 ]. For example, Luo et al. [ 24 ] utilize knitted fabric with programmed extensibility to control the pneumatic actuators. Belding et al. [ 4 ] combine elastomeric balloons with slit tubes to achieve various motions. Others design the rest shape of inflatables with locally varied stiffness [ 14 , 42 , 43 ]. Powered by inverse computational tools, researchers in the graphics community fabricate the inflatables in flattened [ 34 , 44 ] or compressed states [ 27 ]. Although the above fabrication techniques are well developed, they are still with a high threshold for novices and solely aim for dynamic shape changes that are temporary without a continuous air source. A few studies have investigated inflating rigid material for permanent shapes [ 31 , 57 ]. However, focusing on rigid sheet material, these works require additional material and equipment to control inflation, and thus the fabricated shapes are limited to 2.5D and lack details.

Built upon the prior works on the shape-controlling method, PneuFab offers an easier way of fabricating inflatables using rigid materials by 3D printing and allows for complex inflatable geometries with programmable transformations.

3 PNEUFAB DESIGN SPACE

PneuFab aims to expand the boundary of FDM 3D printing technology by enabling blow molding (Figure 2 ). PneuFab programs and prints thermoplastic composite structures as parisons, which can morph into target shapes with thermo-pneumatic triggering. PneuFab artifacts are transformative with volumetric expansion, inflating small pieces into larger-scale objects. With meticulously designed composite structures, we can fabricate objects with orientational morphing (from basic geometries to amorphous shapes) and transformative texture (from plain surfaces to intricate patterns). Beyond the form factors, PneuFab imparts 3D-printed objects with tunable stiffness, ranging from rigid objects to dynamic structures.

We hope users can benefit from the fabrication and design pipeline of PneuFab for the following potentials:

  • Complementing conventional FDM printing for creative expression.
  • Designing aesthetically intriguing bulbous shapes and delicate textures.
  • Prototyping tangible interactive devices by leveraging the material property.

4 PNEUFAB MECHANISM

PneuFab leverages the know properties of thermoplastics: when heated above the glass transition temperatures, thermoplastic becomes elastic and ductile. And with internal air pressure loaded, the heated object with an enclosed cavity expands like a balloon. Once the object cools down, it becomes rigid and obtains the inflated shape. We use this expansion for creating blow molding artifacts from the 3D-printed parison. Inspired by the previous work by Zhang et al. [ 64 ], which proposed a pneumatic multi-material 4D printing diagram, we design a homogenous material approach for PneuFab and choose polylactic acid (PLA) as the candidate material for accessibility. Although printing multi-material objects is possible with FDM 3D printers, it is hardly feasible for hollow structures since the components of composite structures are spatially distributed. Firstly, printing dual-material requires a wipe tower for FDM printers, and the printing time has been extended several times. In addition, poor layer adhesion causes printing failure when two materials are printed in alternating layers. Therefore, in the case of PneuFab, another more suitable strategy for controlling thermo-pneumatic expansion is needed.

In this section, we describe the investigation of the expansion mechanism of FDM-printed PLA inflatables. Besides the basic expansion pattern, which is also discussed in [ 64 ], we want to discuss three observed behaviors of FDM-printed PLA parison during the heating and inflating process: thermal lag, anisotropic expansion, and shrinkage. These behaviors are associated with the geometries of the printed objects as well as the print settings. We conducted experiments to investigate these correlations, and the results provided us with references for developing assistive design software and for formulating a shape-controlling approach during fabrication. We use commercial PLA filaments (PolyMaker) and FDM 3D printers (Raised3D E2) with a path width of 0.4 mm to fabricate the experiment samples. Since all the digital models of the sample are hollow with thin walls, we set the extrusion rate at 95% as default and the infill at 100% to avoid potential gaps and air leakage.

4.1 Volumetric Expansion

The most basic characterization of PneuFab is the volumetric expansion coefficient $( {{\alpha }_E} )$ , defined as ${\alpha }_E = {{( {{V}_1 - {V}_0} )} \mathord{/ {\vphantom {{( {{V}_1 - {V}_0} )} {{V}_0}}} } {{V}_0}}$ , where ${V}_1$ denotes the volume of the inflated shape, and ${V}_0$ denotes the volume of the initially printed shape. To study the correlation between expansion ratio and thickness, we conducted tests on the PLA hollow cubic samples of the same size with an edge length of 20mm. Since it is difficult to measure the real-time volume during the inflating process, we approximate the volume of objects by measuring the buoyancy derived from Archimedes' principle. The experimental setup employed a fixed pulley system and an electronic tension meter to measure the real-time buoyancy, assuming the pull of the rope equals the buoyancy minus the gravity of the sample. The water temperature was maintained above the glass transition temperature at 80 °C during the experiments, and the inflation flow rate was kept constant at 10 mL/s, which resembled our fabrication workflow.

Figure 3 shows that expansion ratios change over time and the maximum expansion ratios during the experiments were greater than 16 times. We conducted a regression analysis on the experiment data, and the results helped us tune the software parameters to match the physical variables, including thickness, heating time, and inflating time for simulation.

4.2 Thickness Change upon Expansion

We further examined the change in thickness after expansion. We cut through samples printed with different thicknesses and measured the thicknesses after expansion. We chose three points for the thickness measurement and take the mean average. As shown in Figure 4 , we can conclude that the thickness is negatively correlated with the expansion ratio, and as the thickness gets thinner, the tendency for the thickness to get thinner becomes smoother. We also take this into consideration for our software implementation for multiple-phase heating and inflation, as discussed in Section 9.2.

4.3 Thermal Lag

Thermal Lag is the time delay for heat to propagate through the wall of an object. In the case of PneuFab, the increased thickness of the printed pieces leads to the delay of starting points for expansion. We conducted experiments to quantify the lag in heat transference by heating the samples with the same internal air pressure and recording the time when expansion began. We printed PLA hollow cubic samples of the same size with an edge length of 20 mm and varied the thicknesses of hollow cubes in 0.4 mm intervals, from 1.2 to 2.4 mm, considering the printer nozzle width of 0.4 mm. As shown in Figure 5 , while a higher heating temperature accelerates the heat transference, the thicker objects exhibit a significantly delayed expansion behavior. This finding offers an opportunity for us to leverage the expansion lag between the components of a composite structure with different thicknesses and thus design temporal triggering methods, which are further demonstrated in Section 6.

4.4 Shrinkage

Shrinkage, a known mechanism of thermoplastic caused by residual stress, has been intensively explored for 4D printing. In prior works, the shrinkage behavior is manipulated with the printing parameter [ 1 , 10 , 47 , 52 ]. PneuFab does not intend for shrinkage of the originally printed shape but instead for the shrinkage of thermoplastic after expansion. During the inflating process, thermoplastic in an elastic state is stretched with an applied external force, and after rapid cooling residual stress can be built. Upon reheating, the residual stress is released and thus triggers shrinkage. Zhang et al. [ 61 ] have shown the reversibility of shape memory polymers balloon after inflation. However, plastic deformations may occur with excessive external force applied, which makes the shape irreversible [ 22 ]. Therefore, the shape memory effect is no longer available for the PneuFab artifacts with significant deformations, which means that the reheated objects are transformed into a new state with intermediate volume instead of the original size (Figure 6c ). To investigate the reversibility of this shrinking process after expansion, we printed a set of samples with identical geometries, with an edge length of 20 mm, and inflated them into shapes with different volumes. Although the inflated shape cannot be recovered to the original state, we can still leverage the shrinkage behavior for various shape-changing configurations, as we will demonstrate in Section 6.

4.5 Anisotropic Expansion

Another significant expansion pattern discovered in this study is the anisotropic expansion, which is associated with the 3D printing orientation of the parisons. Thus, we examine how printing orientation (with the position of the connector as a reference) and print layer thickness can affect the final inflated result. As displayed in the top half of Figure 7 , the direction of anisotropic expansion is determined by the printing direction. Those printed vertically have the tendency to expand more in the vertical direction, while the horizontally printed sample expands more in the horizontal direction. Meanwhile, higher layer thickness tends to inhibit the tendency as mentioned above, i.e., the height and width bias of the inflation. As shown in the bottom half of Figure 7 , we conduct experiments on the height-to-width ratio by changing the layer thickness for 3D printing. This knowledge helps us to design the 3D-printed inflatable with desired elongation behavior by adjusting the slicing direction and layer thickness.

5 PNEUFAB FABRICATION WORKFLOW

The proposed fabrication method, PneuFab, allows the users to post-process FDM-printed hollow objects into various unconventional shapes through blow molding. In general, the fabrication process starts with making a thermoplastic preform with a cavity for blow molding, called a parison. We design PneuFab composite structure that comprises two types of components in terms of significantly different thicknesses: the membrane and the frame. While the membrane (i.e., the thinner component) tends to be spherical like a balloon with air pressure loaded, the frame is meant to constrain the expansion. Thus, the nonuniform expansion can be leveraged to generate controlled expandable shapes. The fabrication process of PneuFab artifacts occurs in 3 stages: 1) designing a parison with our simulation tool, 2) 3D printing the inflatable structure, and 3) blow molding artifacts with thermo-pneumatic triggering (Figure 8 ).

5.1 Parison Designing

The digital designing process of the parison involves drawing the basic membrane and frame geometries with the CAD software, Rhinoceros. While professional designers can make arbitrary models, digital modeling is challenging for normal users. Besides, imagining the inflated outcome is difficult for those who do not have any previous empirical experience with blow molding. Therefore, we impart a custom design tool that only requires inputs of simple geometries and provides visualized simulation output, allowing users to iterate their design before physical fabrication. Once the designing process is finished, our design tool generates printable 3D models with air connectors in a barbed shape to introduce pneumatic actuation for blowing the parison. The standard connector is easy to print and applicable for silicon rubber tube fitting with a tight seal.

5.2 3D Printing

In this work, we use commercially available PLA filaments (PolyMaker PLA) and FDM 3D printers (e.g., Raised3D, Ultimaker) with 0.4 mm nozzle to fabricate the designed thermoplastic parison. We generate printing files from common slicer software (e.g., Cura, ideaMaker). To ensure the airtightness of printed pieces, we set the extrusion rate at 95% and the infill at 100%, while leaving other printing parameters as default. Note that we set the infill parameter to its maximum to avoid any potential gap, particularly when printing larger-size objects that require infill.

5.3 Triggering

While the parison is being printed, we can set up the water bath at the temperature of 80°C as the heating condition and prepare for the blowing hardware (Figure 9 ). We use hot water to soften the 3D-printed parison, which provides uniform heating and high controllability. A cycle of triggering process includes heating, inflating, and cooling, which can be conducted accumulatively and alternatively. The fabricated parisons are submerged or dipped in hot water for different triggering techniques. After the thermoplastic is heated thoroughly and reaches the glass transition temperature, we inflate the parison using an electric vacuum pump for inflating and a 3-way solenoid valve which is controlled by an Arduino board. When target shapes are achieved, we can simply leave them at room temperature with constant internal pressure to cool down or submerge them into cold water for quicker cooling.

6 TRIGGERING TECHNIQUES

Leveraging the heat transference lag between thermoplastics of various thicknesses, we further design a set of thermo-pneumatic triggering methods. We have observed the thermal lag behavior in the aforementioned experiments that the start points for 3D printed inflatables to begin expanding were delayed with the increase of the thickness. Therefore, due to the significant difference in thickness between the membrane and the frame, the starting points for each component to be softened are varied. We can thus control the inflated shape by adjusting heating times and alternating the heating, inflating, and cooling processes. Since the inflating time and amount of air is subject to personal preference and specific needs, we only discuss the general workflow and thermal parameters. As shown in Figure 10 , we demonstrate how multi-phase shape-changings with significantly different results are achieved with the step-by-step workflow. To clearly demonstrate the workflow of PneuFab triggering method, we assume a significant thermal lag between the frame and the membrane and denote their expansion starting time as ${t}_m$ and ${t}_f$ in a water bath environment.

Phase I: Localized Expansion. We submerge and heat the printed piece in hot water for ${t}_m$ and then inflate it to the desired size. Only the membrane is heated thoroughly above the glass transition temperature and softened, while the frame is merely heated on the surface and remains rigid overall. When we inflate the object at this point, the membrane can be extruded but strictly constrained by the frame.

Phase II: Global Expansion . When we submerge the object for more than ${t}_f$ , both the membrane and the frame are thoroughly heated and in the glass transition phase. Therefore, when air pressure is loaded, the membrane and the frame are both pushed outwards and enlarged. Although they are both compliant and elastic at this moment, the frame with a larger thickness constrains the expanded profile, which will otherwise be an unvarying sphere.

Phase III: Accumulative Expansion . We can view the inflated thermoplastic structure in Phase II as the new basic parison to apply triggering methods. By heating the new shape with an enlarged frame structure for no more than ${t}_m$ , the membrane components can bulge out of the frame, forming an exaggerated shape. The accumulated expansion enables us to sculpt spatially distributed prominent geometries.

Phase IV: Shrinkage. We shortly dip the expanded structure into the hot water in this phase. The bulged membrane can shrink and thus form a novel shape that the conventional blow molding method cannot fabricate. The thermoplastic material is stretched during the inflating process, and residual stress can be built in after the cooling process. Once the inflated object is reheated, the residual stress can be released, which leads to the shrinkage of the thermoplastic, as we have discussed in Section 4.4. Since the frame is thicker, it takes longer to be heated throughout and releases residual stress for shrinkage. In this phase, we leverage the thermal lag for shrinkage to obtain a new shape. Although the membrane cannot restore the original shape, it can therefore shrink into smooth minimal surfaces constrained by the frame after reheating.

7 MORPHING STRUCTURES

To make the full of PneuFab techniques, designing the geometry of 3D printed parison is fundamental. We now describe the composite structure design of PneuFab with distinguished transformations after different triggering methods are applied. The morphing behaviors include linear curvature, planer protrusion, and spatial expansion. All the primitives are designed and generated with our design tool and FDM 3D-printed with PLA filament.

7.1 Linear Curvature

Inspired by phototropism, we leverage the anisotropic expansion behavior of the 3D printed parison and design a series of pipes with linear constraining frames to program the transformation. Therefore, after inflating, the 3D-printed pipes grow like plants with bending, twisting, and coiling behaviors (Figure 11 ). We showcase linear primitives in a slender tubular shape with a length of 50mm, an outer diameter of 5mm, and a wall thickness of 0.8mm. To enable the linear curvature structure, we set the elongation direction perpendicular to the heat bed with a layer thickness of 0.1 mm for 3D printing slicing. The elongation perpendicular to the layers results in bending motion with one-sided frames as constraints, which are designed with a width of 2mm and a thickness of 1.2mm. A longer primitive, with a length of 100mm, can achieve the coiling behavior. When the constraint is designed in a spiral fashion, the tube can exhibit twisting motion. Inspired by A-line [ 52 ], we print samples with a length of 50mm, a cross-section of 5x5mm, and a constraint thickness of 1mm to illustrate how the bending direction is determined by the position of the constraint. We briefly tested how the bending angle is correlated with the constraint thicknesses of 1mm, 2mm, and 3mm. Since these structures are not applicable to the temporal triggering techniques described above, we only submerge and heat them in hot water for ${t}_f$ to achieve the linear curvature morphing.

7.2 Planar Protrusion

PneuFab method allows us to design inflatables that are originally in planar shapes and then transformed into 3D objects. We categorized three strategies for designing frames as constraints on the planar surface (Figure 12 ). We design the samples in a plate shape with a diameter of 25mm and a height of 5mm.

Grid. The intersecting frames are connected with boundaries or each other, dividing the membrane surface into several subcomponents. After inflation, the extruded subcomponents make the planar surface textured. When the grid frames are also softened and expanded, the planar pieces are transformed into 3D objects with bulged membranes. When reheated, the membrane components shrink and form smooth minimal surfaces constrained by the frames.

Contour. The independent frames are deployable after inflation, and the target shape is controlled by the predefined contours. For instance, when only the membrane components are inflated, the concentric circumferential frames restrict the radial expansion and result in an upright, sharp summit profile. By eccentrically distributing the frames, we can create a skewed summit, the orientation of which is determined by the displacement of inner circles. After reheating, the shrunk membranes retract all the frames into stacks. Since the stretched and then shrunk membrane is more flexible and thinner than its original state, it can serve as a hinge for connecting frame components. Thus, the overall structure is elastic and dynamic with tunable stiffness for tangible interaction, which is further described in Section 8.

Kirigami. We borrow the design from the auxetic kirigami structure, which can be deployed after inflation. Kirigami structures exhibit more controllable deformation due to the predesigned slit shapes. The transformations are mostly driven by the expanded membranes, which drive the deployment of kirigami structures.

7.3 Spatial Expansion

We designed several spatially distributed frames which control the stereoscopic expansion (Figure 13 ). The samples are in a cylinder shape with a diameter of 10mm and a height of 20mm.

Pillar. The eight frames, which are polarly arrayed with the axis of the cylinder as the center, constrain the elongation along the axis and make its cross-sectional expansion more pronounced.

Loop. The frames are discontinuously distributed along the axis of the cylinder, which is pushed away from each other after inflation. Similar to the disjointed planar contour frames, when reheated, the shrinkage of the membrane pulls these loop frames back into a pile.

Helix . Although the frame is in a continuous helix shape, the compliance of thermoplastic material makes the frame flexible. Therefore, it can be stretched like a spring by the expanded membrane, and after the shrinkage process, the helix frame is then compressed.

Mesh . Combing loops and pillars, the mesh constrains the overall expansion by dividing the membrane into several small pieces and thus enriches the surface texture.

8 TUNABLE STIFFNESS

Beyond creating static shapes with aesthetic and expressiveness, PneuFab endows the artifacts with tunable stiffness, which come from the flexibility of the material itself, the designed composite structure, and the internal air pressure.

8.1 Flexible Membrane

After inflation, the stretched membrane is thinner than the printable size, as demonstrated in Section 4.2 and more flexible compared to the as-printed objects. Limited by the nozzle size of FDM printers, printed objects must be designed with a certain thickness greater than 0.4mm, and thus the shells are mostly rigid and hard. The conventional method for FDM printing soft objects is through employing flexible material like TPU, which is difficult and tricky to print with. In contrast, in our method, one can make bouncy and compressible artifacts with the most easy-to-print material – PLA. To demonstrate the flexibility of stretched membranes, we test the pressing stiffness of samples with different thicknesses. We print samples in a planar plate structure with a diameter of 250mm and vary the membrane thicknesses from 0.8mm to 2.4mm. All the samples are then inflated into a hemisphere shape. In the pressing stiffness test setup, the testing samples are not loaded with any internal pressure. The samples are pressed by a 5mm-thick indenter while reaction forces and the indenter displacement are recorded throughout the experiment. As shown in Figure 14 , the change in the slope of the force-displacement curve indicates that the membrane thickness can tune the flexibility of stretched membrane after inflation.

8.2 Dynamic Frame

To enrich the haptic properties, we take the frame as another design component. The flexible membrane can also serve as hinges for disjointed frame components. Rigid frames hold structural properties as they have negligible deformation when subjected to pressure loads, which are isolated from the deformation of the membranes. Yet, the membranes determine the displacement of the frame since they are bonded with the frames. Based on these properties, we leverage the rigid frame as a programmable flexure element to design haptics. We demonstrate how the frame structure determines haptic properties by showing the force-displacement graph in Figure 15 . We print a planar structure with a diameter of 250mm, and a membrane thickness of 0.4 mm. The structure is designed with four concentric circular frames with a width and a thickness of 1 mm and diameters of 4mm, 8mm, 12mm, and 16mm. We fabricate the pneumatic button with the Phase IV technique. As shown in Figure 15 , we illustrate how the pneumatic button with rigid frames can offer staged feedback for pressing with 100KPa internal air pressure applied.

8.3 Internal Air Pressure

Moreover, the airtightness of the fabricated pieces allows them to contain internal air pressure and manipulating the internal air pressure allows us to render the fabricated objects with different stiffness. Using samples with a printed thickness of 0.8mm, we inflated the samples to certain internal pressures and tested the stiffness based on the change in air pressure with the same experiment setup in Section 8.2. As shown in Figure 16 , when the initial internal pressure in the chambers increases, the sample becomes stiff.

Figure 17 shows a haptic proxy for simulating different haptic feedbacks with adjusted air pressure. The proxy stick consists of several chambers. When the pressure in the chambers increases, the stick becomes elastic and bouncy; when pressure decreases, the chambers become soft, and the stick can be deformed to different angles.

9 DESIGN TOOL AND IMPLEMENTATION

To help users understand PneuFab workflow and support design iteration, we developed a parametric design tool with Rhinoceros and Grasshopper as the design environment, Kangaroo as the simulation solver, and HumanUI as the user interface. With the design tool, users can program the target shape of the inflatable structure by manipulating geometry parameters. The visualized simulation of chosen triggering methods facilitates the design iteration. Finally, a model for 3D printing and practical instructions for physical triggering can be generated based on the previously user-defined parameters (Figure 18 ).

9.1 User Interface

9.1.1 Design the Basic Geometries. Firstly, users are allowed to either choose one of the four basic models or pick exemplified models from our predefined library. The basic model is a closed polygon surface and serves as the membrane (displayed in grey) so that users can draw patterns upon it to design the frame components (displayed in red). If users are professionals in 3D modeling, they are also free to import objects of closed polygon surfaces and draw curves on them from scratch in the Rhinoceros environment.

9.1.2 Specify the Structure Parameters. To design the target inflated shape, users can specify global variables such as membrane thickness and adjust the desired constraint performance of the frames. We provide three key variables, constraint width, which defines the constrained area of the frame; constraint thickness, which gives the strength of this area; and object membrane thickness, which controls the surface strength of the membrane. Users can modify these parameters to design the 3D printed parison for blow molding.

9.1.3 Simulation and Iteration. To facilitate design iteration before physical fabrication, the design tool allows users to preview four simulation results in relation to the four phases in the triggering method. The default heating time and inflating time correspond to the selected phase and specified geometry variables. This way, the users can then either further modify the detailed triggering variables or redesign the geometries. After modification, users can refresh the simulation result to iterate the target shape until they achieve desired outcomes.

9.1.4 Printing and Triggering. Lastly, with previously defined geometries and parameters, the software can automatically generate 3D models for 3D printing as well as guidance for triggering. Users can then input the models into slicer software and print them for physical fabrication. The provided guidance allows users to follow the sequential triggering process with instructions on heating and inflating time.

9.2 Implementation

We implement our design tool comprising the simulation function and parametric modeling within the Grasshopper environment. The simulation is performed with the Kangaroo solver, a plugin for Grasshopper, which is based on a position-based dynamics model and works by minimizing the total energy [ 67 ]. Our software applies goals that define different energies as inputs for the solver, including the Length goal for the strength of the frame, the Edge Length goal for the strength of the membrane, and the Volume goal for the expected expansion size. While the Volume goal drives the inflation process, the Edge Length and the Length goals determine how difficult the material can be stretched.

9.2.1 Data Interpolation. As the first step to integrating the expansion behavior, we used polynomial regressions to interpolate data collected through experiments in Section 4. Our material tests showed us how the thickness parameter influences the expansion ratio under certain inflation and heat transference, which determines the required heating time. For each thickness, we utilized a polynomial regression function to predict the volume of the inflated shape at the corresponding time (Section 4.1), which is then set as the constraints on the Length goal, the Edge Lengths goal, and the Volume goal. To find heating time thresholds ( ${t}_m$ and ${t}_f$ ), we establish equations combining the change in thickness upon inflation (Section 4.2) and the relation between the required heating time and the thickness (Section 4.3).

9.2.2 Inflation Simulation. To utilize the position-based dynamics of Kangaroo, we first convert the basic model into a mesh of near-equilateral triangles with the frame patterns preserved (Figure 19 ). The software can deconstruct the mesh into vertices and faces with which we can obtain the goals. The Anchor is pulled with considerable strength to the vertices on the boundary of the model, which ensures the boundary unable to expand. The Edge Lengths goal and the Length goal together act as a spring system following Hooke's law. The mesh of the membrane is an input for the Edge Lengths goal. As the frame constrains and controls the artifact to expand into an expected shape, the software finds all faces in the frame patterns and inputs the edge of these faces as the Length goal to the solver. Due to the large strength, these faces will barely expand in simulation. The last goal is the Volume goal, which defines the expansion of the model. As mentioned above, our software calculates the volume of the inflated shape according to our polynomial regression functions, which drives the solver to generate the corresponding inflated model.

9.2.3 Multi-phase Shape-changing Simulation. To enable the simulation of shape-changing with various configurations, we build our guidelines based on the triggering techniques and morphing structure design. With the triggering rules, the heating time for the Phase I technique is set as ${t}_m$ , and the strength of the Length goal is at maximum. For Phase II , the heating time is the average of ${t}_m$ and ${t}_f$ , and the Length goal and Edge Lengths goal are subject to the interpolated function about the Volume . For Phase III , we view the expanded frame and membrane as new input geometries for simulation. Since the thickness changes upon inflation and the required heating time is a variable parameter, we set the new $t{'}_m$ based on the interpolated data. For Phase IV , we input the frame geometries to the Anchor goal and substitute the Volume goal with the Soap Film goal, which resembles the shrinkage behavior of the membrane.

9.2.4 Printable Model Generation. Given the selected basic model and frame patterns, the software can generate the 3D geometry for printing through a series of transformations. The software first offsets all frame curves on the basic model with the input frame width to form frame surfaces. Then the frame surfaces are extruded by the constraint thicknesses into solid frames. To obtain the membrane, the software offsets the basic model with the membrane thickness, and the result will be subtracted from the basic model. The anchor components will be offset into a thick shell structure. Finally, the four parts, including the membrane, frames, anchor, and barbed connector, are Boolean united, leading to the final 3D model ready for slicing and printing.

10 APPLICATION

To illustrate the potential of PneuFab technique for creative expression, prototyping, and tangible interaction, we designed and created several artifacts in application domains: sculptures, jewelry, illuminations, and haptic devices.

10.1 Modular Sculpture

10.1.1 Modular Design for Grapes. To illustrate PneuFab's ability to complement 3D printing, we created a bunch of grapes by means of modular assembly (Figure 20 ). While the grape stem can be easily printed in a planar shape with the conventional FDM method, printing spherical grape berries is challenging for FDM printers, which require plenty of supporting structures and may end up with rough surfaces. Therefore, we designed the grape berry modular with PneuFab method. There are three basic units of grapes: a single grape, a pair of parallel grapes, and three parallel grapes, and each grape berry is in a flat hollow disk shape with a thickness of 0.8 mm. To make the bunch of grapes more vivid, we also heat and bend the stem. After all the prints are ready, we inflate all the berries and connect them to the stem. The overall printing times for them are 22 minutes, 37 minutes, and 76 minutes respectively. The fabrication process of the whole bunch of grapes, including 30 grape berries and a 3D printed stem, costs ∼11 hours, which equals the time of printing 7∼8 grapes (with a diameter of 30 mm) using a conventional method.

10.1.2 Modular Designed Balloon Dog. We inflated a balloon dog sculpture, a common figure in balloon art, which illustrates the volume expansion feature of PneuFab very well (Figure 21 ). The design consists of two units: three pairs of parallel balloons for ears and legs and four units of balloons in a folded shape for the head and body. We designed the corner parts as nonexpendable frames while the compact balloons membranes unfold after inflation. The deployed structure with round-shaped balloons is about five times larger than the as-printed structure and conventionally hard to print without numerous supporting materials and is time-consuming. With PneuFab method, the overall printing time is 6 hours and 8 minutes, which is reduced by half compared to the conventional method. The compact design also saves space for packaging and transporting.

10.2 Jewelry Design

PneuFab technique empowers designers to explore the aesthetic capabilities of 3D-printed objects. To demonstrate the expressiveness of PneuFab artifacts, we made a set of jewelry with DIY accessories. The PneuFab artifacts are lightweight organic and unique shapes with only a few grams. From opaque to translucent, the pleasing waxy texture, alluring roundedness, and radiance were what make them ideal for use as jewelry (Figure 22 ). We hope PneuFab can facilitate the fabrication of wearables and inspire design in the DIY community.

10.3 Ambient Lights

Lampshades are generally large and fragile, which take up much storage space and are inconvenient for transportation. PneuFab provides designers and artists with a new method for producing illuminations. After inflating, PneuFab artifacts can expand several times and are featured with thin shells which allow light to shine through. In this application, we show how PneuFab innovates illumination design with transformative shapes and delicate textures (Figure 23 ).

10.4 Tangible Devices

Leveraging the flexibility and tunable stiffness of thermoplastic artifacts made with PneuFab, we can create devices for tangible interaction. By changing the internal air pressure, we can alter the haptic feeling and feedback of PneuFab objects to enrich the interactivity.

10.4.1 Pneumatic Keyboard. The PneuFab artifacts are air chambers that can be used for pneumatic devices. Unlike conventionally 3D-printed pneumatic devices that are assembled by parts or with auxiliary material as support [ 50 ], we fabricated the pneumatic buttons in one piece with homogenous material by designing concentric planer structures and applying the shrinkage technique. By connecting them to pressure sensors, information can be input when we interact with PneuFab keyboards (Figure 24 ).

10.4.2 Pneumatic Joystick. Similarly, we designed a pneumatic joystick structure with tunable stiffness, as shown in Figure 25 . The as-printed parison of the joystick is designed in a cylinder shape with a diameter of 20 mm and a height of 40 mm. The thickness of the membrane is 0.8 mm, and the frame is designed with nine wavey curves with a width of 1 mm and a thickness of 1.6 mm. The quadrature points of the curve are shifted up and down to contact the adjacent ones. To enhance the elasticity of the joystick structure, we applied the Phase IV technique and reheated the bulged membrane as flexible hinges. The fabricated piece is like a spring for pressing, stretching, and bending at all degrees of freedom when no internal air pressure is applied. When loaded with an internal pressure of 200 KPa, we can make the joystick rigid and stiff, which can withstand a bending force of 15 N for a deactivated state.

11 EVALUATION

We validate PneuFab from two important aspects: 1) the accuracy of our simulation, and 2) the ease of operation with our design tool and fabrication workflow.

11.1 Simulation Accuracy

To evaluate the geometric accuracy of our simulation, we compared the simulated result with the corresponding physical output, which is fabricated under the generated instructions from the design tool. We chose the Kirigami structure for this comparison, because of the notably different outputs in four triggering phases and the complexity of interconnected frames. We first replicated the physical artifacts into a digital model by 3D scanning. Next, we matched and aligned the models with the anchored components as the benchmark and then compared the differences between the two models using MeshCompare, a Maya plug-in [ 68 ]. The distance error value for displaying fully red is set as 3 mm. As shown in Figure 26 , the simulation outputs and the physical artifacts visually match to a large degree. In Figure 26 c, where the models are mostly in yellow and green color, the worst performing output is the Phase III output. While the frames are largely matched, it is observed both visually and quantitively that the differences are mainly distributed on the membrane part. Although there are some variations, our simulation tool is able to fulfill the function of providing a preview for the designed parison.

11.2 User Experience Investigation

To evaluate PneuFab design tool and workflow, we recruited 6 participants with a mean age of 20 years old. All the participants are familiar with computer-aided design software, including Rhinoceros. We divided the study into two sessions consisting of digital design in session one and physical fabrication in session two. We prepared a consumer-grade FDM 3D printer and triggering tools, as illustrated in Section 5.

After briefly introducing PneuFab method with several primitives as inspiration, in session one, we invited participants to practice and design their parison digitally. Before the design process, the participants were allowed to play and familiarize themselves with the design tool for 10 minutes. We then asked them to complete the digital parison design process with PneuFab design tool within 30 minutes. Eventually, the participants were able to export 3D models for printing and take notes on fabrication instructions according to the workflow. In session two, participants retrieved the 3D-printed items and completed the physical fabrication workflow under the guidance of the generated instructions. At the end of the two sessions, participants filled out a survey with six questions: Q1: The design tool is easy to use and requires little effort to complete a parison design. Q2: The simulation function helps in design iteration. Q3: The provided instruction is understandable and helpful in physical fabrication. Q4: The fabrication process is interesting. Q5: The fabrication process is challenging and complicated. Q6: The fabrication output is as expected and satisfying. The answers were rated on a ten-point scale (1: not at all, 10: strongly agree) by the participants. In addition, we collected some insightful feedback from the participants.

In summary, all the participants were able to complete their parison design with PneuFab design tool (Q1: Mean=8.33, SD=1.03), and three of them finished two designs. They were able to iterate their design with the simulation tool (Q2: Mean=6.5, SD=1.05), especially by “exploring the different triggering phases” (P5). The participants also found that the generated instructions were easy to follow (Q3: Mean=7.33, SD=1.21) and the overall fabrication workflow interesting and playful (Q4: Mean=8.50, SD=0.84). P2 commented that “it was surprisingly exciting to see the objects inflated into a new shape”. While most of the participants rate the fabrication process as medium difficulty (Q5: Mean=4.83, SD=1.60), some expressed their hesitation when facing a novel fabrication method. Lastly, all participants are mostly satisfied with their successfully completed design with physical outputs (Q6: Mean=7.50, SD=1.38), as shown in Figure 27 .

12 DISCUSSION, LIMITATION, AND FUTURE WORK

12.1 Material Consideration

The main factor that influences our choice of material is the ease of operation in both the 3D printing and triggering process. We looked at the three most common and accessible thermoplastic filaments for FDM printing: PLA, ABS, and PETG, with glass transition temperatures of 60°C, 79°C, and 101°C accordingly [ 69 ]. We chose PLA for its lowest glass transition temperature so that we can heat the material above its glass-transition temperature with hot water. Although the glass-transition temperature of ABS is lower than the water boiling temperature, heating and softening ABS require a higher water temperature of ∼95°C. Otherwise, the ABS sample would require a much longer time to be softened with lower-temperature water.

12.2 Reproducibility

We followed the same triggering instructions generated from our design tool to fabricate six kirigami-patterned planar structures with PLA in different colors. While there are some variations, caused by human operation and material properties, the results in Figure 28 show an acceptable reproducibility of PneuFab. To reduce these mismatches with higher reproducibility, we may utilize automated devices (e.g., robotic arms) to ensure consistent triggering conditions, including submerging depth and speed of retrieval. However, we still prefer to view these “imperfections” as potential space for personalized expression and interactive fabrication.

12.3 Airtightness

The main challenge with 3D printing airtight parison is the overhanging structure. Though 3D printing is capable of making various shapes with overhangs, the overhanging structure falls due to gravity. Therefore, the hollow objects may no longer be airtight or even unprintable without supporting structures. The automatically generated supports are superfluous and tightly bonded to the main body. To tackle this issue, we manually design slender struts with a diameter of 1 mm in hollow objects to support the overhanging surfaces, which are easily torn off after heating and inflating.

12.4 Safety Issue

We submerge the PLA parison into 80 °C hot water to quickly reach the glassy state and then apply pressure with an electric air pump. In this process, the operator needs to avoid injuries from direct contact with high-temperature objects, splashed water, and possible noise caused by the explosion. During the whole triggering process, it is recommended to handle the objects with long tweezers and wear protectors (i.e., goggles and earplugs).

12.5 Design Tool Improvements

Compared to other computational software for 3D printing and fabricating inflatable structure [ 27 , 34 ], PneuFab only provides a forward workflow for user-generated basic geometries. An inverse design tool that allows input of complex target shapes and flattens them into printable models is not developed but also desired.

The accuracy of PneuFab design tool can be improved. As described in Section 11.1, the most significant mismatch comes from the membrane part in the Phase III outcome, which can be attributed to the lack of empirical data for the extremely thin membrane. In this example, the stretched membrane is thinner than 0.1 mm and out of predictability, since our interpolated data only covers thickness above 0.4mm. To provide simulation results with higher resolution and accuracy, connecting the design tool with the finite element analysis (FEA) platform can be future work.

13 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we present PneuFab as a novel hybrid fabrication technique, which combines the advantages of 3D printing and blow molding for making artifacts with complex geometries and haptic properties. Built on material experiments in expansion and shrinkage, we meticulously design composite geometry structures and apply controllable trigger techniques. We demonstrate the potential of PneuFab, from decorative sculptures to interactive devices. We also propose an instructive design tool to facilitate the iterative design with visualized simulation. As a democratized method, PneuFab hopes to inspire and invite creative expression to push beyond the boundary of 3D printing.
