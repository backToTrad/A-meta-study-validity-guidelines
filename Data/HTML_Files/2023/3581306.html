Both enterprises and their employees have globally experienced remote work at an unprecedented scale since the outbreak of COVID-19. As the pandemic becomes less of a threat, some companies have called their employees back to a physical office, citing issues related to working remotely, but many employees have refused to return. Thus, working in the metaverse has gained much attention as an alternative that could complement the weaknesses of completely remote work or even offline work. However, we do not know yet what benefits and drawbacks the metaverse has as a legitimate workspace, because there are few real cases of 1) working in the metaverse and 2) working remotely at such an unprecedented scale. Thus, this paper aims to identify real challenges and opportunities the metaverse workspace presents when compared to remote work by conducting semi-structured interviews and participatory workshops with various employees and company stakeholders (e.g., HR managers and CEOs) who have experienced at least two of three work types: working in a physical office, remotely, or in the metaverse. Consequently, we identified 1) advantages and disadvantages of remote work and 2) opportunities and challenges of the metaverse. We further discuss design implications that may overcome the identified challenges of working in the metaverse.

1 INTRODUCTION

Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, enterprises have adapted to fully remote work: hiring, training, and collaborating from the homes of their employees. Surprisingly, working remotely has become increasingly prevalent not only for office work but also for hands-on work (e.g., crime work [ 57 ]) if the tasks and environment allow. Existing tools for remote collaboration (e.g., email, Zoom, and Slack [ 7 ]) helped ease the transition to remote work but also multiplied the workload in the new work-from-home (WFH) environment [ 44 , 58 ]. Accordingly, remote work has many tradeoffs. For example, some people have reported that their productivity and efficiency at work as well as their wellbeing increased because they could spend more time with family and reduce wasted time commuting [ 4 , 22 , 24 ]. Meanwhile, Yang et al. [ 65 ] have reported that social interactions, which potentially lead to work collaboration, knowledge sharing, and creativity at work, significantly decreased, and Glassdoor Economic Research [ 68 ] has demonstrated that newcomers (e.g., interns) struggled the most during the pandemic due to reduced social interaction, because they neither received proper training nor learned effective workflows. Such reduced social interaction also inhibited the sense of belonging, work ethic, and organizational culture [ 34 ]. While organizations benefited from the reduced costs of maintaining a physical workspace and accessing top talent worldwide, procedures for human resource management (HRM) established for physical workspaces were reeling from the completely new working arrangements, where it is harder than ever to train, manage, and evaluate employees [ 34 ].

Nonetheless, workers who are happy or adjusted to the new WFH environment are resisting returning to the physical office as the pandemic is coming to a close [ 49 – 51 , 69 ]. Former Apple director Ian Goodfellow moved to Google after opposing Apple's new policy of going to work three times a week [ 61 ]. Accordingly, it is estimated that fifty-eight percent of the workforce would leave their company if forced to return to a physical office and move to adjacent fields that allow fully remote work [ 49 , 50 , 69 ]. Thus, as workers hop from one job to another following the COVID-19 pandemic, enterprises may need to permit or even support (partial) remote work to prevent workers from leaving or provide inducements to displaced workers.

How then can we help people to communicate and work better in geographically distanced settings? One of the emerging and promising solutions is the metaverse workspace, but case studies of real-world metaverse workplaces at a large organizational scale have not yet shown if such a solution can be feasible or successful. Facebook has changed their company name to Meta and publicized their long-term plan and vision to design a metaverse for work, learning, etc. [ 16 ], and Microsoft has also been working for years to bring their vision for a metaverse workspace to fruition [ 67 ]. They have opened a preview of Microsoft Mesh, which helps people connect, learn, and collaborate through a holographic presence [ 70 ]. However, these tech giants have been experimenting with software internally (i.e., letting their employees use and work in the metaverse during work hours) [ 67 , 71 ], so we do not yet have published results of such internal implementation.

Thus, our research aims to investigate the design and adoption of the metaverse as a solution to the problems of remote and physical workplaces. We aim to ascertain whether the imagined opportunities of the metaverse are real opportunities (i.e., what problems of remote work is the metaverse able to solve?) and further uncover challenges in designing, adopting, and utilizing the metaverse for work (i.e., what problems of remote work is the metaverse unable to solve?). By scrutinizing the opportunities and challenges, we provide design agendas and guidelines that could inform and improve future designs of the metaverse for work.

To do so, we took a qualitative approach and conducted semi-structured in-depth interviews after recruiting a total of 29 workers who had experienced 1) working in a physical office and/or 2) working completely remotely and/or 3) working in the metaverse. However, although there is a wide spectrum of technology (e.g., virtual, extended, or augmented realities) as well as various devices (e.g., VR headsets, HoloLens) that constitute and/or help to define the metaverse, the metaverse that our participants worked in was an early form of a metaverse platform—it was a 2D, avatar-based and/or 3D camera-based workspace that runs on a personal computer without any other high technology devices (e.g., VR headsets, HoloLens). This is reasonable for two reasons. First, we did not find any cases of enterprises that have completely replaced their physical office to a high-tech metaverse by having employees work there while wearing such devices during official work hours (e.g., 9am to 6pm). Second, previous studies on high-tech metaverses in nonwork related contexts (e.g., entertainment) have already demonstrated that it is difficult to use such devices for extended periods because they cause dizziness or (VR) nausea. Notably, a recent study [ 6 ] in a work context reproved that working in an immersive metaverse while wearing a VR device is literally impossible in the technology's current state; two participants among eighteen dropped out as soon as they began participating in the study [ 6 ], and the rest reported negative experiences (e.g., one participant could not even write a note while wearing a VR headset) and/or multiple physical pains (e.g., nausea). Thus, we assume that it is unfeasible for workers at a large scale to work in a high-tech, immersive metaverse for long work hours. As a result, questions on working in such an advanced environment are out of our research scope.

We found that working in the metaverse can mitigate or solve some of the problems of remote work, but challenges of working in the metaverse still exist, including 1) risks of surveillance and resistance, 2) newly emerging threats to workers’ wellbeing, 3) problems of securing private workspaces, 4) irreplaceable nature of jobs, 5) the need to redesign HR systems to better fit the metaverse workspace, and 6) the need to build a new organizational culture. Our work addresses these problems in detail, contributing to the scholarship and practices of both HCI and business:

  • We offer a foundational study that examines real opportunities and challenges of working in the metaverse based on the experiences of users (i.e., workers) who have worked at a physical office and/or remotely and/or in the metaverse at a large scale.
  • We provide a list of design implications for both researchers and practitioners to leverage when designing or adopting a metaverse workspace.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 From Traditional Office Work to Remote Work

Remote work is defined as “a flexible work arrangement whereby workers work in locations, remote from their central offices or production facilities, [in which] the worker has no personal contact with co-workers there but is able to communicate with them using technology” [ 18 ]. A vast variety of research on benefits [ 4 , 22 , 24 , 28 ] and challenges [ 3 , 26 , 35 , 36 , 45 ] of remote work was already conducted before the COVID-19 pandemic, but the research inevitably had to depend on restricted study settings (e.g., in a laboratory) or specific groups of workers, because remote work was not only an uncommon practice [ 37 ] but also regarded as a privilege or luxury for the financially comfortable (e.g., high-income or white-collar workers) [ 19 ]. Many researchers [ 13 , 41 ] have argued that studies conducted before COVID-19 could have looked at only one side of the phenomenon (e.g., individual-level outcomes or benefits [ 4 ]) and/or produced selection bias [ 38 ] by examining a specific group (e.g., office workers [ 19 , 37 ] or those who volunteer for remote work as an option [ 32 ]). This is because “existing knowledge on remote working has mostly been generated from a context in which remote working was only occasionally or infrequently practiced, and was only considered by some, but not all or most, of the workers within an organization” [ 60 ].

Hence, a new line of research [ 13 , 41 , 60 ] has been conducted since COVID-19 by emphasizing contextual relevance to the COVID-19 crisis and its uniqueness; a large number of workers have globally engaged in remote work at an unprecedented scale [ 60 ], and such remote work was no longer an option but mandatory [ 13 , 60 ]. These recent studies have explored what unique challenges people experience while working remotely and how people are handling them [ 13 , 41 , 60 ]. Especially, prior research in the business disciplines (e.g., HRM) have heavily focused on individual work performance or team collaboration [ 59 ] under the WFH circumstance of COVID-19 by taking an organization-centered perspective [ 13 ] (e.g., why the performance of interns [ 68 ] and female researchers was increasingly negative during pandemic-era remote work, what difficulties managers are facing under distance management and how it could affect performance consequences of remote work [ 34 ], whether employees’ perception of leaders’ authenticity impacts employees’ performance [ 17 ], etc.). Accordingly, recent HCI research has scrutinized challenges that remote workers have experienced in non-office environments by emphasizing a user- or employee-centered perspective [ 13 , 41 ] (i.e., the blurred boundary between home and workspace [ 13 ] and distracting moments during videoconferencing [ 41 ]). Meanwhile, several other CHI studies [ 29 , 54 ] have focused on the technological limitations of collaborative tools, such as videoconferencing via Zoom [ 29 , 54 ] or messaging on Slack, that have led to significant decreases in social interaction (e.g., initiating ad-hoc private talks or breaking into smaller groups during a meeting). These studies have further developed new features that help participants to have ad-hoc small talk before or after videoconferencing [ 54 ] and enable an out-group to know another group's current conversation context [ 29 ].

In line with the research mentioned above, our research aims to discover benefits and challenges experienced by an unprecedented number of remote workers across diverse demographics. However, we differentiate from prior works in several aspects. First, we take a macroscopic approach that includes not only an employee-centered perspective but also a stakeholder-centered perspective [ 47 ]. Since all members in an organization, including the CEO, human resource (HR) managers, general managers, staff members, and interns, are employees as individuals, the benefits or challenges that they feel while working remotely as an employee versus as a stakeholder could differ. For instance, an HR manager as an individual employee could benefit from remote work by flexibly adapting their work hours but could also have trouble performing their role as a stakeholder within the organization (e.g., having limited information to evaluate employees as they work via tools for remote collaboration). Second, while we use an organization-centered perspective that mainly focuses on individual or team performance, we further examine how remote work that heavily relies on collaborative tools has affected organizational performance and culture at the corporate level. This is especially important because 1) the sum of performance by individuals or teams is not necessarily equal to organizational performance [ 30 ], and 2) although individual or team performance can be high during a certain period (i.e., during COVID-19), it cannot be guaranteed that the performance will be maintained in the long run. Thus, we identify how various individuals as unique stakeholders and as employees perceive remote work in a broad perspective. Most importantly, the ultimate goal of this research is to discover the opportunities and challenges of working in the metaverse by directly comparing employees who have experienced working remotely and/or in the metaverse.

2.2 The Metaverse for Work

The term metaverse, which comes from “meta” (beyond) and “universe,” was first coined by Neal Stephenson in his science fiction novel Snow Crash in 1992 [ 14 ]. In 2004, the Open Source Metaverse Project made an effort to provide an open source, online, virtual world platform. Later in 2007, the Acceleration Studies Foundation defined the concept of a metaverse as “the convergence of 1) virtually-enhanced physical reality and 2) physically persistent virtual space” [ 72 ]. Since then, the metaverse gained attention from both academia and industry. Starting with early studies on the metaverse that focused on Second Life and its preemptive introduction to the entertainment industry (e.g., gaming or social networking) [ 23 ], many researchers and practitioners have expanded the potential for the metaverse by applying it to different domains or contexts ranging from education to the office [ 48 ].

In fact, even before defining the metaverse, many researchers and practitioners [ 5 , 64 ] had long endeavored to build technology and environments that could enhance computer-supported collaborative work (e.g., collaborating in a virtual workroom using a wearable device). However, such early forms of virtual workspaces (e.g., Virtual and Interactive Environments for Workplaces of the Future [ 64 ]) differ from the current metaverse workspace we portray. First, these early virtual spaces were closer to a rendering that embodies a physical concept rather than a finished product [ 48 ]. The infrastructure (e.g., access on PC only, low penetration rate in mobile devices with constant Internet connectivity through Wi-Fi, 4G/5G, or hotspot) was insufficient for use as an immersive and natural workspace [ 39 , 48 ]. Second, the virtual space barely facilitated various social meanings (e.g., social event, commerce, work) to emerge and bond with one's real life, due to the lack of development and configurable environments (e.g., Unity) that enable more immersive interactions in the virtual space [ 48 ]. Further, such limitations hindered the virtual space from forming “a scalable environment that can accommodate many people,” which is “essential to reinforce social meaning” [ 48 ]. Thus, although many people studied before the outbreak of COVID-19 and envisioned a future where working in a “virtual [work] space” without restriction of time or space would be possible [ 21 ], actual enterprises’ adoption of such technology at scale to the office or management was relatively low, regardless of the level of technology (whether 2D- or 3D-based or virtual, augmented, or extended reality). Hence, the most prominent limitation of prior works is that researchers necessarily relied on laboratory experiments involving a small number of participants who performed a specific task(s) for a limited time (e.g., 24 hours [ 55 ]).

3 DEFINITION OF METAVERSE WORKSPACE

Building upon on new aspects of the present metaverse described in Park and Kim's work [ 48 ] as well as past studies [ 3 , 22 , 34 , 60 , 65 ], we redefine the metaverse workspace in our study by comparing with its neighbors (e.g., remote or hybrid workspaces) and predecessors (e.g., earlier forms of virtual workplaces). While physical work requires workers to commute and work in a designated central office, remote work allows work to be done via technology (e.g., solely using email or any mixture of other tools) without going to the physical office. Hybrid work is a flexible working practice that allows rotation between remote work and physical work (e.g., working in the physical office three times a week and working remotely twice a week). As mentioned in 2.2, the metaverse workspace differs from its predecessors, but both are virtual offices that enable workers to enter and work together in the space. Thus, both current and earlier forms of metaverse workspaces are subsets of remote work that can intersect, but they are not the same. We operationalize the metaverse workspace as “the convergence of 1) virtually-enhanced physical reality and 2) physically persistent virtual space” [ 72 ] where 1) all of the stakeholders can interact in real time via an avatar and/or video camera feed to remotely work together at scale without time or space constraints, 2) an immersive environment facilitates social interactions and meanings like those that emerge in a physical office, or facilitates new ones, and 3) all the tasks and duties seamlessly operate as they do in a physical office. The metaverse workspace we connote is more service-oriented, emphasizing sustainable content and social meaning, and does not necessarily need high levels of technology (i.e., VR, AR, and ER) [ 48 ].

4 METHODS

We conducted semi-structured in-depth interviews well suited to understand workers’ real experiences and/or thoughts on various types of work in detail. Descriptions on recruitment and participants (4.1), interview procedures (4.2), and data analysis (4.3) are explained below.

4.1 Recruitment and Participants

The purpose of our research was to discover and compare the benefits and drawbacks of different work environments from a macroscopic perspective by listening to the voices of not only employees but also company stakeholders (e.g., HR managers or CEOs). Thus, it was important to recruit a representative set of company stakeholders with different positions (e.g., managers, board members) as well as employees with diverse occupational and demographic backgrounds. Thus, we posted and disseminated a recruitment announcement on social media and used a snowballing method to acquire participants.

To recruit a specific group of participants, such as employers or executive-level employees who have adopted or participated in the decision-making of a metaverse workspace, the authors emailed or sent a message (e.g., via LinkedIn) to global business leaders, developers, and company stakeholders via their personal contacts. Some of the first respondents helped recruit potential candidates that satisfied our strict recruitment conditions and/or distributed our recruitment poster to other firms or business leaders.

Consequently, we recruited a total of 29 participants without intentionally excluding anyone, and they all successfully finished the interviews. The participants were compensated with a Starbucks gift card equivalent to 15 USD for their time. Regarding the representativeness of our samples, the age distribution slightly leaned toward younger people in their 30s to 40s, and different experiences of work environments were widely distributed. Among 29 when multiple answers were allowed, a total of 26, 29, and 23 participants had experienced 1) working in a physical office, 2) remotely, and 3) in the metaverse.

The whole research plan and procedures were reviewed by the IRB, and we rigidly adhered to the protocol. In keeping with prior research in which the privacy and security of firms and their employees is sensitively handled, especially in the work context, we have not collected or shared any sensitive information, such as company names and affiliations or employees’ ethnicity, income, and education, with the exception of gender and age range (see Appendix A.2). Because previous research has shown that some employees avoid situations in which people from the same company are coincidentally grouped into the same focus group, we held confidential one-on-one interviews. When participants naturally mentioned the real name of their firm or others when giving examples, we purposely anonymized the disclosed names by using a false name (e.g., Company A).

4.2 Procedures

We conducted a one-hour semi-structured interview with each participant using a qualitative approach [ 12 , 15 ]. Since all of all of our participants had experienced remote work, we first asked, “What are the biggest advantages or disadvantages of remote work?” To those who also had experience of going to a physical office, we further asked them to describe the advantages or disadvantages of remote work in comparison to the physical office. We also asked, “What technology or tools have you or your company used to communicate and collaborate under remote work circumstances?” and “In what ways have technologies or tools satisfied work needs?” Then, we moved to the questions about the metaverse workspace; we first asked whether they have heard of or worked in the metaverse. We proceeded with different questions depending on what they had reported: A) they have not heard of, B) have heard of, or C) have used the metaverse as a workspace. Especially, those who had not heard of or could not grasp what that the metaverse is (group A), we showed them images (Figure 1 ) and video clips 1 that described the metaverse workspace in detail. Figure 1 is a collection of current services and real use cases that support metaverse work. We told our participants to imagine working in the metaverse through a computer , not high-tech devices (e.g., VR), due to the premature stage of its commercialization (explained in sections 1 and 2). This hypothetical method based on real use cases [ 40 , 46 , 66 ] is known to be effective in acquiring users’ perceptions towards a system that they have not experienced. Then, we asked the same list of questions to group A and group B: “Could you tell us what you think the metaverse is?” “Would you work in the metaverse if you had the chance?” “Why would or wouldn't you work in the metaverse?”

To group C who had experienced working in the metaverse, we asked several questions specialized to their experiences of the metaverse workspace: What emotion or thoughts did you have when you decided to work in a company that uses the metaverse as a legitimate workspace? Were you and/or your colleagues excited or resistant toward it, and why so? Were there any technological, functional, psychological, or physical difficulties of working in or using the metaverse? Compared to completely remote work and/or working in the office, what are the clear benefits or drawbacks of working in the metaverse (what issues does metaverse fail to solve)? Do you think companies will keep using metaverse for work, and why or why not? How do you think the metaverse could expand to other workplaces, and what benefits could you get from the expanded metaverse? What are the issues that we should solve to be able to expand metaverse to workplaces? Especially when asking questions about any kind of difficulties or issues of working in the metaverse, we mandatorily asked more open-ended questions: “Do you have any ideas that could solve such difficulties or issues?” All of the interviews were voice recorded after receiving the participants’ consent and then transcribed.

4.3 Data Analysis

We took an inductive and qualitative approach [ 12 , 15 ] to analyze the transcribed data. Specifically, thematic analysis [ 8 ] along with affinity diagramming method [ 27 ] were used to find similar topics or relevant categories and further identify thematic relationships among interconnected concepts. We first opened coded a small set of the interview data and expanded to open coding all of the rest. The authors had a meeting to discuss the distinctive themes they had discovered. If the first coded topics did not belong to specific categories, the authors reorganized the themes and their relationships. We iterated this inductive process multiple times and added new themes as needed until there were no disagreements on the themes. To increase the reliability of the finalized themes, all of the authors screened them until finalization. An intercoder reliability testing was not conducted following Braun and Clarke's justification [ 9 ].

5.2 Working in the Metaverse: Opportunities, Challenges, and Design Implications

Building upon our definition of the metaverse workspace, the metaverse workspace is not a mere tool adopted to support sporadic interactions (e.g., Zoom meetings), but is a “physically persistent virtual space” [ 72 ] that was intentionally designed to substitute physical workspace. Thus, the characteristics that make the metaverse workspace distinguishable from completely remote work or earlier forms of virtual workspaces are presence, natural interaction, and prompt communication . First, all of the workers experience presence by staying in the designated space of the metaverse during work hours. Second, such vivid presence facilitates natural interaction among workers. For instance, while workers need to make an appointment just to have a small chat when working remotely, workers naturally bump into each other in the metaverse workspace. Third, completely remote work is heavily dependent on unsynchronous communication through tools (e.g., Slack and email [ 62 ]), but the metaverse workspace supports prompt, synchronous interaction [ 39 ]. Similarly, the predecessors of the metaverse workspace were mostly renderings [ 48 ] that were effective for sporadic interaction (e.g., meeting up in a virtual space for training) or special events (e.g., annual conference), but they could not support large numbers of workers’ interactions at scale. These three distinctive characteristics create new social meanings and organizational cultures within the metaverse that are different from remote work or the early forms of virtual workspace. In the next section, we explain what opportunities and challenges arise from these prominent characteristics of the metaverse and supplement the participants’ quotes with example images from a metaverse platform that some of our participants were using. Also, where themes match, we present quotes from participants who have not experienced the Metaverse but have heard about it or hypothetically imagined it; these participants are identified by an asterisk throughout the paper (see also our participant information in Appendix A.2).

5.2.1 Opportunities. Better HR Management. We found that presence in the metaverse increased the visibility of workers—that is, the ability to see how everyone (who is logged into the virtual workspace) works—and this in turn can make HRM smoother. While workers have limited knowledge of what others are doing when working remotely through email or collaboration tools [ 11 , 34 , 60 , 62 ], in the metaverse, not only managers but also colleagues are naturally able to see and check on others doing their duties anytime they want (see Figure 2 below). For instance, P05 expressed, “Compared to the completely remote work environment that operates through collaborative tools like Zoom or Slack only, the metaverse has a much greater advantage for seniors who are in the position of managing staff or juniors.” In this regard, P01 shared other firms’ interest in adopting metaverse platforms, “Usually, the top management [of other firms] really like it when we show the metaverse workspace and talk about it with them.” Also, P08 told his experience as the head of the organization, “I can see everyone sitting at the virtual office, working, and talking. […] Because we can see and hear so clearly how each other are working in the metaverse workspace, we can see the process of the result and build trust in each other for sure.”

Likewise, the increased visibility of workers can contribute to fairer and more transparent evaluation processes. P01 said, “In a startup where numerous failures are inevitable to make one big success, even if a worker failed in their tasks, if they did their best, we should give them a compliment instead of making them wholly responsible. I think in the metaverse, in contrast to remote work, we can see the process of how hard we worked, so we can make fairer evaluations.” Similarly, P14 said, “[…] But in the case of tasks that are hard to measure quantitatively, the metaverse could be more helpful in making more accurate task evaluations, because supervisors could see what their teams are doing.”

Increased Interactions and Collaborations. Many participants pointed out efficient interaction and communication for both work and social activities as a major advantage of working in the metaverse (see the examples in Figure 3 ). Surprisingly, participants (P01, P05, P06, P13, P17, P19, P20, P21) naturally compared working in the metaverse with completely remote work, especially with video conferencing tools such as Zoom. P08 mentioned, “When we're about to connect Zoom or Google [Meet], we enter it after scheduling a time. In fact, in the real world, it's not the case that we can communicate only after making an appointment. It's not a discourtesy if someone asks me if I have time to discuss something. We can always rearrange the time if someone says they're not available for some reason. I think that's the biggest strength of the metaverse is that since we're all already logged in, it's more convenient to initiate a conversation.” Similarly, P05 explained her experience as both a superior and subordinate: “But in the metaverse, in the middle of talking, I say, ‘Hey, come see this,’ and just teach them how to do it by immediately sharing the internet search screen of my computer or existing documents that I have” (see Figure 3 , left). P21 said, “In the metaverse, we have the assumption that we can meet someone if we move to a certain spot. In an environment where such features are lacking, when we want to talk or interact, we have to send a private message or make a call. But in the metaverse, everyone is there, so it gives you the feeling that it's close to offline interaction.”

Furthermore, we found that the physical restrictions of an office building are a major drawback, but the metaverse environment does not have physical or spatial barriers, so it can foster even more interactions than at the physical, “offline” office. For instance, P01 pointed out the restrictions of offline offices in terms of scalability saying, “Since the company kept getting bigger, they rented another floor from another building that is half a mile away” Also, P05 said, “When [work] was hard and frustrating, I visited a person on another team and we made the effort to go to the virtual lounge and have a conversation. […] But if you're at the [physical] office, physically visiting the other team itself is difficult.”

Such increased interactions in the metaverse can lead to improved productivity, work collaboration, and knowledge sharing compared to remote work or even offline office work. One big reason why many participants (P05, P09, P10, P15, P23*) felt their productivity increase is the increased social presence attributed to the metaverse (P11). P10 mentioned, “Since I'm around with my team members, I feel like I can easily be absorbed in my work.” P23* called it a “library effect” and P03 said, “The level of concentration on work here [in the metaverse] is really the best.” Additionally, because the metaverse eliminates physical restrictions, workers naturally have more opportunities to meet diverse people and share information. P15 shared his story: “For example, our company does business that intermediates real estate, so sales managers assigned to different districts are on the field all day long. They don't get to bump into each other. But after we started working in the metaverse, physical restrictions kind of disappeared, and sales managers who would probably never meet each other [offline] sat at the same table of the same online office. […] A sales manager in district A gave his customer, who is slightly short of budget, to another sales manager in a different district.” Some participants (P05, P06, P17, P19, P20) emphasized the responsiveness that the metaverse affords. P05 mentioned, “I got really talkative since I started working in the metaverse. […] The more we get to converse, the more chances we get to tell others our work process or achievement through it. Also, when the team members have some successful cases, they just tell the people sitting right next to them. During the process, I think we get to share knowledge and learn something.”

Help Organizations Achieve Goals and Form Culture. We found that working in the metaverse can help improve relationships, build trust, and instill organizations’ values. Because the metaverse is a live arena where all the workers are present to perform their jobs, it facilitates workers’ sense of belonging and helps build organizational culture. This presence at scale combined with virtual space and objects is distinctly different from a tool or virtual space that is used for a specific purpose (e.g., a one-hour Zoom meeting [ 13 , 41 , 54 ] or one-day training). Many participants (including P05, P11, P15, P20, P21) described their experiences by repeatedly using the following words or expressions: “closeness,” “intimate feeling,” “sense of being together,” “feeling that I exist in the 3D space,” etc. P08 directly compared his metaverse experience with other work types (e.g., remote or physical office work): “I told you before that [sharing the feeling of the company's vision and values, workers’ devotion to the company, or sense of belonging] has dropped to 10 when working completely remotely, as compared with 100 when working offline. But if we work in the metaverse, I feel like it comes up again to 80 or over, because working in the metaverse gives you the feeling of working in the same place all together.”

Especially, participants wanted a customized metaverse workspace or object design that reflects the unique characteristics of the work and organization, and customization enhanced organizational culture that was lacking in completely remote work (P05, P12, P24). A world-famous fashion company that works in the metaverse took pride in their installation of “a huge digital sign to advertise their campaign, so that they can deliver the brand identity to their employees and even other people from different companies” (see Figure 4 , left). Meanwhile, at a help-line call center, employees requested to “have a personal phone booth in the metaverse,” although technically, “calling their customers sitting at an open table is not a problem” (see Figure 4 , right). P16 explained, “The callers who are familiar with this physical environment want the same sense or feeling even after moving online. Because humans, since we have experience, want to do things the same way we used to. We naturally and always like what we are familiar with.” These examples demonstrate that, not only the metaverse workspace but also virtual objects help form various social meanings (e.g., organizational identity).

Improved Workers’ Wellbeing. We found that working in the metaverse can partially increase workers’ wellbeing in that 1) the metaverse space creates a feeling of going to the office that supports time management and separates work from daily life better, 2) being and working with other employees in the metaverse creates a feeling of being together, and 3) not only the metaverse office rooms but other spaces designed for rest (e.g., a virtual jogging spot by the beach) or small talk (e.g., a lounge) provide the employees with a positive experience and mitigate loneliness.

Although many workers expressed difficulties with remote work in terms of setting a boundary between work and life, working in the metaverse gave them the feeling of going to and leaving work. Many participants (including P05, P09, P21) expressed: “We can feel that we're commuting.” “I get a message saying, ‘You're at work!’ I do feel I'm at work and feel determined to go to my seat and to work.” In addition, many participants felt positive emotions such as feeling refreshed (P10) and good (P04) or a sense of reality (e.g., feeling of working [P05] and commuting [P21]) by interacting with various virtual objects (i.e., elevator, beach spots, rooftop, etc.). Such findings demonstrate that virtual objects within the metaverse workspace can help increase overall user experience of working or taking a break (see Figure 5 ).

5.2.2 Challenges and Design Implications. Risk of Surveillance and Resistance. Despite the potential, some participants expressed hesitation or resistance toward working in the metaverse due to the risk of surveillance. Logging into the metaverse during work hours could facilitate an ease of surveillance in two ways: 1) by allowing workers to easily monitor each other in virtual space because physical obstacles or distances are removed, and 2) by enabling digital surveillance without any human intervention, since all the workers’ digital traces in the metaverse can be easily datafied. In this context, participants described the metaverse as “Big Brother” and “Panopticon” (P22*) by frequently mentioning “surveillance” and “privacy invasion” (P22*) or using the terms “pressure” (P09) and “burden” (P28*) to express their feelings. Specifically, P28* who refused “an HR job offer from a company that allows working in the metaverse” said, “I have actually entered [the metaverse], and what came into my mind instantly was ‘Isn't this surveillance?’ I had only that thought.” Surprisingly P10, who has been working in the metaverse, also cast doubt on the authenticity of ‘being unrecorded’ saying, “I doubt whether [workers’ activities including video] is really not being recorded.” Regarding this issue, P06 focused more on enterprises’ intended use of the metaverse, saying “If [a company] goes into surveillance [mode], it now has a lot of issues. If a company doesn't trust a worker that much, it's better to not recruit or have that kind of person and save money.” These findings indicate that, regardless of whether workers are in the metaverse or not, the possibility of surveillance and workers’ resistance towards it [ 46 ] could be a great challenge to companies that consider adopting the Metaverse workspace.

[Design Implications] Regarding workers’ resistance toward surveillance, many participants primarily emphasized clarification about the purpose of technology use (e.g., voice or video use) and transparency of company regulations. P15 suggested, “Companies do not need to collect detailed log data of employees’ activities in the metaverse. Of course, it would be hard to refuse. Decision makers with leadership in companies should recognize that the metaverse is a platform designed for better collaboration, not for surveillance.” Similarly, P21 said, “It is important to publicize that the company doesn't store or check workers’ activities in the metaverse.” Other participants like P22* emphasized the role of (UX and system) designers and suggested that the design of the metaverse should focus on better collaboration : “One IT company recently decided that all of the workers should go to the metaverse for work and opened their new system, but they went back to the drawing board because the workers strongly resisted it. There was a rule that all employees should wear a bone conduction headset, and while relying on the sound only, they have to respond within a few seconds if someone calls them.”

Additionally, some participants suggested that acquiring informed consent and receiving external audits can help build trust between firms and workers when adopting the metaverse. P22* said, “I recommend that companies receive informed consent from their employees. It shows the company will do their best to respond and take responsibility of potential problems that could occur due to the technology adoption. If possible, auditing through external experts is a good method, for instance, verifying that the company doesn't collect workers’ log data for the purpose of surveillance by having database experts conduct an audit.”

Newly Emerging Threats to Workers’ Wellbeing. Participants pointed out that working in the metaverse demands higher levels of concentration and work intensity, which can be a critical threat to workers’ wellbeing. In a face-to-face environment, there is necessarily inefficient time involving physical activities (e.g., going to the restroom, smoking and drinking coffee, visiting other offices to meet colleagues), and workers can have chances to take a rest and relax during such intervals [ 46 , 47 ]. In the case of WFH, workers are comparatively free from monitoring or surveillance and can have break time more flexibly. However, working in the metaverse minimizes workers inefficient physical activities and increases mutual surveillance via enhanced visibility among coworkers. This newly emerging problem can threaten workers’ mental and physical wellbeing. P08 mentioned, “Looking back on the past when I physically went to work in the office, […]. Oftentimes, I called executives out for a coffee or asked if we could talk over a smoke on the rooftop. I think there were many times I worked by blurring and crossing the line between duty and breaks. But in the metaverse, we can't do that, right? So working in the metaverse could increase work intensity and workload.” Similarly, P03 mentioned, “When we first came [to the metaverse workspace], our average steps per day were only 300. […] I put my shoe on, and my foot didn't fit.” P05 also said, “When I did completely remote work, I frequently went from place to place, like visiting the bank for business, because people did not keep an eye on me. But, because we put up our faces on the screen, I cannot go to the bank or go see a doctor.”

We also found that increased workloads and work intensity attributed to working in the metaverse led to physical pain or discomfort. P05 continued, “Because of this, I have neck pain and my arm is numb or hurts. My body movements are increasingly decreased. The stiffness of my body posture comes from having to stare at the camera.” Similarly, P08 said, “When I click on the away from the keyboard button, my screen blacks out and the speaker and mic turn off. So after I work for one hour and a half, I click that button and go down to the first floor of my apartment for a smoke and come back. Working in the metaverse has the effect of working so hard that you even need such an intentional break.”

Notably, our findings show that participants were concerned not only with the increased ease of others’ or mutual surveillance but also with self-censorship. P05 said, “Even though nobody surveils me, I care and am conscious of the camera.” Similarly, P04 mentioned, “In the beginning, because the camera is on, I was really conscious that [others] are watching me. I realized I kept looking at myself. I thought, ‘Do I look okay? Ah, I look like this,’ and I got used to being conscious of this.” P28* said “If I see people's faces all the time, I shouldn't ever doze off. I mean, it's really noticeable when I doze off.”

[Design implication] Prior works have also discovered that remote working reduces a chance for physical activities and it causes health-related issues. However, our study highlights that working in the metaverse can even aggravates this situation because being always on the metaverse with other colleagues makes people have a break, like complete remote workers who only uses tools (e.g., Slack or Email) can lay down in bed or walk in their room. To resolve such issues, our participants pointed out that firms should design and provide policies, functions (e.g., screen pop ups for stretching [P05]), or programs to foster workers’ physical activities. Moreover, although most of the participants agreed that “taking a 10-minute break every hour is necessary” (P03), P07 and P09 felt especially uncomfortable stretching with other people: “It would be nice if there are times that we can relax our stiff body on and off while working. But I hate that I should stretch in front of other people. It would be nice if the company makes a button for ‘stretching.’ We can still respond to each other if there's an urgent task because, although the camera's off, we can still hear.” (P07) Thus, we suggest to designers incorporate health-related interventions and functions that workers can use according to their liking.

Furthermore, some participants suggested that gamification could help workers enjoy more physical activities. P03 said, “I shared ideas with the management that the name and affiliation above the avatar could be updated with each worker's average number of steps after connecting [the metaverse platform] with each worker's mobile phone or wearable devices. Then everybody can see their steps, thinking ‘Oh, I've walked 20 steps and the other person has walked 80 steps or something.’” P09 said, “If a gym is placed [in the metaverse] and connect by a sensor, we could work out together in different [physical] rooms. I mean, exercising alone is essentially not fun.” Such workers’ demands for social interaction and activities demonstrate that working in the Metaverse at scale can uniquely maintain or create social meanings (e.g., gamification [ 33 ] by showing workers’ average number of steps) that are different from working remotely or in virtual spaces dedicated to specific tasks.

Problems Securing Private Workspaces. Ironically, we found that workers need their own physical workspace to work in the metaverse workspace (P03, P15, P28*) even though firms might emphasize the convenience of working in the metaverse space without time or space constraints or the elimination of physical offices to remove unnecessary time, energy, and costs. P14 described the phenomenon as “two sides of the same coin” and “light and shadow.” P02 mentioned, “Working in the metaverse space does not mean that a physical workspace is completely unnecessary. Actually, even in my house, I need a private and quiet room that can't be disturbed by my family members during work hours. Acquiring a separate place could be very difficult for someone who has a family, and thus, it's very critical.” These issues of the metaverse workspace have been discussed in previous studies [ 11 , 13 , 41 , 53 ] that focus on the drawbacks of completely remote work. However, owning a private space may become more critical than ever for a metaverse worker. While completely remote work is based on intermittent interactions (e.g., work via email [ 62 ] or one-hour Zoom meetings [ 41 ]), working in the metaverse requires continuous interactions as in a physical workspace, because metaverse colleagues can approach each other at any time. Thus, while a remote worker could arrange a certain time or space in his house by asking for family members’ understanding [ 41 ], metaverse workers may need a dedicated private space for the entire work day (9 to 5).

[Design implication] Participants suggested that firms could financially support employees to secure private working spaces (e.g., supporting expenses for a co-working space near home [P25*]), because working in the metaverse could also allow firms to save the cost of maintaining physical offices. P15 mentioned, “We're aware that some workers have difficulties in securing their own workspace. Even though we have hundreds of employees, since we don't operate a physical office, we're saving lots of expenses. Through the saved costs, we're planning to support employees, such as providing them a shared office near their homes, so that they can have a better work environment. We think of it as an investment rather than a cost. Through this, we expect to be able to invite more talented workers to our company.”

Irreplaceable Nature of Jobs. We found that, even when firms decided to completely switch their physical office to the metaverse workspace and the employees agreed to it, there still are tasks or jobs that cannot be moved to metaverse due to their requirement for (1) physical infrastructure or (2) in-person interaction. For example, there were times when workers should receive documents via a corporate-numbered fax from external firms (P28*), require the firms’ real address to receive packages (P26), or need to imprint the registered legal seal of the corporate (P10). P28* described her friend's story, saying “My friend, she's also an HR manager, once said she had to go to the physical office to send fax documents. Someone might say, ‘Doesn't having a printer at home solve that problem?’ But as you know, the company can only use one fax number within the network.” P10 emphasized the unique characteristics of her legal tasks that required physical objects: “There are many tools that support electronic signature authentication or electronic contracts or conventions. But in fact, since judicial affairs are the most conservative organizations, there are places such as the court or registrar's office that only accept documents signed with the real imprint of the corporate seal. When an actual seal or signature is needed, someone should visit the headquarters and imprint it.”

We also found that there are jobs where offline work is irreplaceable due to the nature of the job and task characteristics. P03 explained, “We are the IoT department, so physical laboratory is needed.” Additionally, some jobs require offline interactions to secure “work efficiency” and “competitiveness” (P19). P14 said, “Especially, in the case of [real estate] sales agency groups, after going to a spot in person, the decision should be made after checking multiple things, such as the purpose of the building, the type of business that the person will be conducting, meeting the store owner, […]. Understanding or experience of the market alone is not yet enough to proceed with such things in the metaverse space.”

[Design Implication] The topic of bridging the metaverse space with the offline world raises two design questions for future studies: 1) how can artifacts be designed to connect offline and online spaces in the metaverse, and 2) how can mechanisms and policies be designed for hybrid work to create synergy between online and offline work? P15 said, “Our company just began operating a concierge service. We first rented a big warehouse and staff to work there. All of the packages and mail are delivered to this warehouse. The concierge staff goes to both the physical warehouse and the concierge in the metaverse. When other workers visit the concierge staff in the metaverse, the staff finds and sends the mail that's been secured in the physical warehouse. So the workers can receive physical mail by visiting the virtual concierge. We're planning to design and develop various services that connect offline and online spaces by applying and extending the concierge service. P14 mentioned, “Currently, we are trying hybrid work that allows offline and online work half and half, but it is still not easy to seamlessly harmonize different forms of work. We're still lacking but experimenting with ways optimized for us through trial and error.” These findings imply that designing hybrid work that matches firms’ unique nature or environment of work is key.

Redesigning HR Systems. Although working virtually in the metaverse could alleviate problems of HR management and supervision attributed to the invisibility of employees’ job processes during fully remote work, working in the metaverse inevitably raises other HR issues. Notably, we found that the HR department needs the most reformation and adjustment when converted to the metaverse work environment. Moreover, HR management plays the most crucial role in all stages of management; the HR team persuades people to work in the metaverse during recruitment, evaluates or fires workers based on the work occurring in the metaverse, and in the event of a merger and acquisition, trains and onboards new workers. P06 explained, “In a specific area of HR, they are experienced. But that is far from the minimum condition or HR level that has emerged in the metaverse workspace. Since rules are not yet set in the beginning, they should make something very neutral from both the company's and the employees' position—but in a way that both of them look together in the same direction. HR workers feel it is sort of a business developer's [role], saying ‘That's too hard.’” Indeed, P21 described the stories of both his HR colleagues’ difficulties and other HR friends who expressed resistance to working in the metaverse: “My HR colleagues and I feel that there are many parts of HR that we need to innovate. They feel it is difficult because there is a lot of HR work to do, rather than experiencing difficulty working in the metaverse.”

[Design Implication] As the metaverse emerges as a new form of workspace, new roles and capabilities of HR managers are demanded from the companies that have adopted or are willing to adopt the metaverse as their workspace. Our participants (including company leaders and department heads, as well as HR managers themselves [e.g., P18 and P21]) emphasized the importance of HRM in building culture within the new metaverse workspace and onboarding employees to better adjust to the environment . Since every interaction that workers make—such as logging in, taking an elevator, using various functions—could be new and challenging to some workers, HR managers will need to understand workers and their behavior and have a higher level of understanding in technology (including UX and UI) to build organizational culture within the Metaverse. Such new HR roles are very unique to the Metaverse workspace compared with the HR roles needed for remote work [ 3 , 34 ], especially because researchers [ 3 , 17 , 34 ] have argued that reduced formal and informal communication with subordinates was a key challenge to managers during remote work.

Additionally, we found that HR managers should play a critical role in collaboration with designers and executives to develop guidelines for “working in the metaverse” (i.e., online manners, guidelines for effective teamwork and communication). P15 explained, “After deciding to work in the metaverse, the first thing we had to do most importantly was making precise guidelines on how to work, communicate, and collaborate in this space. […] Since it's not perfect yet, we keep updating the guidelines by checking the existing rules and make new rules. During this process, reflecting each participant's expertise and diverse opinions is critical.”

Building a New Organizational Culture. Although working in the metaverse is more helpful for building organizational culture and sharing firms’ goals when compared to working remotely and individually without a metaverse space, the metaverse cannot fully replace the presence that physical space and interaction give offline (P13 and P21). As a result, this lack of presence requires a new organizational culture tailored to emerging metaverse workspaces. Particularly, executives (e.g., P06, P08, P15) had three major observations about organizational culture in the metaverse: 1) organizational culture will be more critical in the era of metaverse workplaces, 2) communication among workers should be more horizontal while in decision-making, the role of leadership (e.g., team leaders, executives) will become more important, and 3) the metaverse workplace requires more decentralized leadership (i.e., more high-level leaders).

P28* explained, “The office building, the space, the colleagues, and the location of the company are all elements that constitute the company's culture. […] In the metaverse, such interaction in terms of both quantity or density is certainly lacking. So to better run a company that operates in the metaverse, a stronger organizational culture is essential.” P06 said, “I think the communication in the metaverse should be more horizonal than offline. […] If a senior team member feels uncomfortable with another team member's behavior, it becomes very difficult. It's really important to have a culture where I can yawn in front of my manager or have small talk without hesitation.” P15 said, “Interestingly, the role of teams has become more crucial. In terms of managing and evaluating workers, team-centric operation is more efficient in the metaverse. […] At the company level, the need for stronger leadership that can bind distributed teams into one has gotten bigger. For that, fostering a large number of leaders has become an important goal. However, [leadership] is still lacking, so we are thinking over how we can develop and foster the right people.”

[Design Implication] We found that there is a tension between autonomy (for horizontal communication) and discipline (for strong leadership) when developing organizational culture. Regarding this, participants emphasized setting a clear standard and guidelines to balance autonomy with discipline. Additionally, firms should focus on fostering strong leadership to build trust among organization members. P06 said, “Certainly after we moved to the metaverse, balancing autonomy and discipline became difficult. While we stress more about horizontal communication, we need strong leadership when making decisions. But those could conflict, so the company should establish clear criteria and guidelines to share with the company members on when autonomy or discipline is more important than the other.”

According to P06 and P15, developing a new HR training or onboarding program to “share our company vision and the context and reason why the company began to use the metaverse” becomes more critical. Also, several participants including P15 said, “[it is a great idea to] encourage and have offline meetups regularly to complement insufficient social interaction online.” Such findings are counter-intuitive; the metaverse as a primary workspace rediscovers and stresses the value of offline meetings while prior research [ 34 ] on the challenges of remote work suggests the use of online social platforms to increase the frequency of communication.

Lastly, designing a customized online space can help foster the building and sharing of organizational culture (P04, P06, P12, P13, P15, P24). Specifically, firms can make workers continuously experience the firms’ identity, vision, image, and culture in the online space through customized artifacts (e.g., colors, images, symbols, space planning, etc.). This is a unique organizational potential of the metaverse workspace that remote working tools (e.g., Zoom, Slack, and email) are unable to achieve [ 3 , 11 , 17 , 34 , 53 ].

6 DISCUSSION

Our finding showed that both opportunities and challenges exist when workers work in the metaverse. Then, how can we seize the opportunities while solving the challenges? As a first step toward working in the metaverse and designing it as a legitimate workspace, we discuss three major issues of metaverse; 1) ambivalence: workers like working in the metaverse but are also worried about surveillance coming from the metaverse environment, 2) paradox: workers do feel a sense of reality in the metaverse (e.g., going to or leaving work) but owning a private and physical workspace is a necessary and sufficient condition to work in the metaverse and feel that sense, 3) sacrifice: workers can easily develop new weak ties and maintain them, but barely feel that the weak ties could evolve to strong ties if only relying on meeting people in the metaverse. We explain each of the discussion points in details and further close them with our study limitations and fruitful future studies.

6.1 Ambivalence: Workers Both Love and Hate Metaverse

While much research on metaverse space (generally called as virtual space) has existed in different context, one that took the most similar approach as ours is Erickson et al.’s study [ 20 ]: the authors investigated how hundreds of IBM workers synchronously interact in an avatar-based 3D virtual conference with spatialized audio and further identified how the technology supported, or failed to support the interactions. Although it is a completely different context to use metaverse for a conference meeting that happens once a year as an event [ 20 ] and for general work , some of our findings showed similar results –especially, the strengths of metaverse as a workspace such as increased social presence and communication, possibility of immediate interaction, reduced time/money/energy for commuting/traveling, etc.

However, one big difference is that, participants in Erickson et al.’s study [ 20 ] reported that despite such strengths of avatar-based 3D virtual conference, they still missed traveling and wanted to meet the people in-person like before. But completely differently, many of our participants expressed that they do not want to go to physical office and rather work in the metaverse if they are forced to be back to the office. It is possible that some workers may want to work in the metaverse not because they like it, but because it is too effortful to go to the physical office. Nevertheless, anyhow it is still an alarming discovery as well as an exciting finding that benefits of working in the metaverse even outweighs the challenges of it as well as benefits of working in the physical office. Thus, it became evident that designing metaverse as a better workspace is more important now than ever before.

In fact, either to workers or diverse stakeholders (e.g., employers, HR managers, etc.), working in the metaverse was a compromise between serious problems coming from complete remote work (e.g., company's side: being unable to manage workers, worker's side: lack of self-discipline, decreased performance, loneliness) and drawbacks of offline office work (e.g., company's side: reducing costs on renting or building an expensive office building, worker's side: reducing costs on living in an expensive area near a company). Simultaneously, metaverse workspace aims to maintain benefits of working remotely while it attempts to bring and follow strengths of working offline in-person; by allowing workers and their work visible, increasing social interactions/collaboration, enabling immediate interaction, etc.

Despite the numerous merits of metaverse as a workspace, one of the most challenging hindrances was workers’ concerns with metaverse-afforded surveillance. While the aspect –that metaverse can make both workers and how they work visible to others (e.g., colleagues and managers)– could not only ease HR management but also increase a sense or atmosphere of working together, chances of social interaction (e.g., small talks), such visibility could reversely become a barrier or threat to new workers who consider working in the metaverse but have not experienced it yet. In addition, as workspace is moved to metaverse from physical space where there some rooms for workers’ speech or behavior to volatilize, there are possibilities all of their words and behaviors are remained as digital traces.

Regarding such problems, our findings illustrated the necessity for firms should 1) be clear about the purpose of metaverse workspace in the first place, 2) give and receive an informed consent to gain trust from employees, 3) execute external audit to prove that the firms do not collect data on workers.

6.2 Paradox: Workers Need Physical Workspace to Work in The Metaverse

Consistent with prior works [ 1 , 2 , 10 , 13 , 41 , 52 , 56 ], we found that complete remote work has a critical drawback in that workers find it difficult to manage their time/work, to set a boundary between home and work, which leads to procrastination and lowered work performance, or to change their work mode because always on technology, thus they constantly work. However, notably, working in the metaverse had the advantages in mitigating or resolving many of the problems that result from complete remote work. For instance, participants reported they could finally feel a sense of going to/leaving from work, since they enter the metaverse at 9am and leave at 6pm, and the colleagues are in there with them during the work hours. It is noteworthy that metaverse can indeed act as a design element that separates work from life and make the worker feel like workspace even though he is at home. Also, many participants mentioned increased concentration on work, time/work management, work/life balance as benefits of working in the metaverse compared with complete remote work. Such results contribute to building new knowledge that metaverse as a workspace is not just a mere rosy expectation with intangible benefits, but could indeed support users in many aspects.

However paradoxically, it was inevitable for workers to secure a separate physical space to work in the metaverse and even maximize the benefits of it. This is because, although metaverse has a competitive advantage of extending physical space to virtual environment, metaverse work is still unavoidably a part of remote work, and thus has similar constraints. As many participants expressed in our interview, we suggest the firms plan to acquire shared office space for workers who do not have a separate space at home, by utilizing the expenses saved from not being able to rent, buy, and maintain the real office. For the workers who do not need shared office because they have a space at home, we recommend the firms to aid side payment so that the workers could make their workspace at home more of a place they want to work in such as by buying comfortable chairs or decorate the room. Recent studies demonstrated that people working from home bring personal belongings in the office to home [ 63 ] or decorate their work space at home [ 13 ] to make it more comfortable. In this vein, we believe that our suggestions will be effective in supporting workers both mentally and physical, this in turns benefit the firms by increasing workers’ satisfaction and/or productivity.

6.3 Sacrifice: Workers Appreciate Weak Ties, But What About Strong Ties?

Similar to prior works, our findings showed that complete remote work has dramatically reduced chances of a vast variety of social interactions and collaborations ranging from small talks to sharing knowledge among employees. In contrast with the complete remote work though, working in the metaverse increased workers’ chances of bumping to their colleagues which leads to social interaction, made it easy to make new networks and feel or develop intimacy with other people. These results are related to the power of weak ties and the importance of the environment that supports to build weak ties. In a different context (i.e., virtual conference), Erickson et al. [ 20 ] emphasized the strength of a virtual space that could support social interaction by quoting Granovetter's arguments [ 25 ]; that is, social systems that do not support weak ties will easily be disconnected and inconsistent. Knowledge/idea sharing as well as scientific endeavors will slow down, people will be separated in subgroups (e.g., race, ethnicity, geography) by one another. By extending Erickson et al.’s research [ 20 ] to the more general work context, we discovered that metaverse can increase the points of contacts among workers and make them feel closeness as well as presence in general work context as well.

However, despite the advantages of metaverse that enables to build weak ties, we found that workers felt difficult to develop the weak ties to strong ties only in the metaverse, especially when the people are whom they first met. This finding directly answers to the question –that Erickson et al. has remained unsolved saying “no final answer here” – on why “clearly some aspects of the virtual world support the fluid interaction […], whereas other fall short. [ 20 ]” In fact, metaverse workspace can support fluid interaction, such as maintaining both weak and strong ties with the people that they have met in-person or known of , or even it's possible to build new weak ties with the unknown people. However, workers felt difficult to develop the weak ties – that they have never met in-person –to strong ties by only relying on meeting up in the metaverse . Such our finding is significant and noteworthy in that first, we identified the reason why some of the participants in [ 20 ] reported that they could connect with people while others do not –although there was a hint in participants’ quotes of the study [ 20 ]: “I wonder about long-term ramifications […]” “I may have a name jotted down from someone I ran across […]”. Second, now that we discovered the reason, we can attempt to come up with possible solutions.

As our participants suggested in the interviews, we recommend the firms provide and support a big offline meet up as a special event, and encourage workers to frequently meet up in small groups if possible. This could enable workers who only met in the metaverse to become more intimate, and further increase a sense of belonging to the company which is hard to build only at the metaverse. We believe that this is a unique but insightful implication; in a completely new situation where physical place is no longer a primary space for meet ups, it gives us an opportunity to think about how to approach the weakness of metaverse workspace. At the same time, as members of the HCI community, we hope to keep challenging ourselves to improve the current technology or develop completely new, envisioning one day people are able to develop weak ties to strong ties solely in the metaverse.

6.4 Limitations and Future Research

Some limitations merit note. Although we attempted to recruit diverse participants, it was challenging to acquire workers over 50 who have experienced working in the metaverse. While the metaverse can provide opportunities for marginalized groups (e.g., the elderly or disabled) to work without physical constraints, it also unintentionally excludes such groups due to technological hurdles. Thus, future research is needed in this context. Also, although we mentioned that immersive metaverse workspaces with advanced technology (e.g., VR) were out of our research scope, our study is limited because we excluded this. Thus, we call for future research to examine how to build metaverse workspaces with more usable and useful advanced technology. Finally, our study focuses more on the opportunities and challenges of adopting the Metaverse workspace for general purposes (e.g., having meetings or showing attendance) rather than how Metaverse use directly relates to the participants’ actual work. We expect to see future research that examines how using the Metaverse directly integrates with workers’ roles and tasks.

Interesting future research that expands our findings could answer the following question: Can current technology support the design recommendations we present in our study? And if not, why? For example, our findings show that workers feel inconvenience going to a physical office to imprint a corporate's legal seal, but the technology (e.g., Adobe's electronic seal) designed to solve this issue already exists. Despite the existing technology, incorporating it into the metaverse workspace could be challenging due to firms’ burdens towards security and/or absence of robust infrastructure and ecosystem (e.g., administrative agency may not take an imprint of an electronic seal). Another example is existing technology [ 42 ] that has been implemented to nudge workers to move their body as a health intervention and increase workers’ physical/psychological wellbeing [ 33 , 43 ] (e.g., gamified AR games that could be applied to the work context [ 33 ] and computer-based interventions for computer vision syndrome [ 31 ]).

We expect that both fruitful research questions and new indispensable roles will spring up within the HCI community. For example, how should we design not only the office building or room but also the city (e.g., scale of roads) to help workers feel comfortable and natural? What kinds of point-of-contact spaces (e.g., online Starbucks [P04]) should be designed to increase social interaction in the metaverse when a virtual restroom is unnecessary? These are just a few of many questions that will start to arise. We hope that the HCI community answers such exciting questions by research and practical application.

A APPENDICES

A.1 Advantages and Disadvantages of Working Remotely

A.1.1 Advantages of Remote Work. Many participants listed advantages of remote work or WFH in terms of efficiency, financial benefits, and wellbeing. Furthermore, these advantages can help firms cut costs and recruit better talent.

Work Efficiency. Most participants pointed out that the primary benefit of remote work is saving the time and energy used for commuting. P10 explained, “The time I spend commuting is not the company's time. It's my hours, but I have to invest that four hours just to commute to work. Commuting time is nobody's time, but it is a merit that remote work allows the owner of the time to spend it.” P08 said, “Work efficiency could increase and we [workers] could save time, money, and energy in commuting or something, if we only meet on Zoom for the major agenda for two hours and just use email or phone calls to cover other things. There certainly are advantages.”

Another advantage of remote work is that workers can flexibly manage their work and rest hours. P27 enthusiastically appreciated remote work: “I love it so much. I can work anytime I want to work. I can take a break in the middle. I never want to go back to the physical office.” Even during work hours, workers can schedule their time more freely. P05 said, “I should be at my desk in the [physical] office, so I often missed the bank hours and didn't make it to a bank even though it's a part of my job. Now, I can do bank work within work hours and do other office work at night.”

Financial Benefits. Many participants (P01, P06, P14, P15) emphasized that, whether big or small, remote work has financial benefits. Some argued that they saved lots of money by moving to a cheaper city, because there was no need to live in an expensive city to be near the company. P14 mentioned, “Housing cost is the biggest burden because most of the major companies are located in megacities or expensive areas. Not to mention tech companies in Silicon Valley, all the big companies in the world are generally located where housing is costly, like London, New York, and Hong Kong, among countless others.” Some of our participants (P03 and P05) indeed moved to a city where housing costs are low while others (P08, P15, P27) gave us examples of their friends and colleagues who had left the city where their office was located. Besides the big expenses like housing, many workers mentioned they liked saving money that they used to spend on transportation, food, and even beauty (e.g., clothing and cosmetics). Such financial advantages benefited not only the individual workers but also companies. P28 observed, “In terms of enterprises, firms can save not only the rental fee of operating the office building but also the maintenance fee.” P15 said, “We [the company] will do recruitment more globally. There used to be conditions to meet to choose good personnel, and one of the biggest conditions was that workers should live near the company.”

Worker's Wellbeing. Many participants said remote work helped improve their work-life balance. In particular, remote work allowed workers to choose a preferred work environment and to have more time with their family. P28 made a list of advantages of remote work, saying “Of course, it has lots of advantages as well. It's so good, in terms of commuting and time. And it can reduce costs and allow flexible working hours. But most of all, workers can choose a work environment as well. The tasks become flexible and free. Not to mention that the workers can lie in bed while working remotely. Also, it can give a feeling of psychological stability or safety. You can spend more time with your family. And the work-life balance and there's much more.” Similarly, P08 mentioned that “people can work at a vacation spot for a month and find happiness from it.” P05 also emphasized the advantage of not having time-space restrictions and its positive effect on her well-being: “I was married last year. I wanted to keep my career after marriage but my husband randomly rotated around different states, due to the nature of his job. Since we are newly married, we have family plans, like planning a pregnancy or a child-rearing plan. So, I was thinking about taking a leave of absence from work. But this current company allows fully remote work without physically commuting, so I applied and had a chance to move here. So I'm currently living in the city where my husband works and working remotely to stay with my husband.”

Interestingly, our findings show that remote work can benefit workers’ wellbeing by mitigating unpleasant interactions and workplace politics. For instance, P10 said, “In the case of legal work or judicial affairs, there's stress that comes from face-to-face [offline] interactions, because we frequently have to mention things like critical risks. In the case of a job that has a lot of paperwork and that operates as long as the environment allows free communication, there's nothing uncomfortable regardless of where you work. So I feel that the negative emotions are slightly mitigated when talking in the metaverse.” Regarding relationship building issues, P10 compared his experiences of going to a physical office and working remotely and explained, “The parts that I additionally had to care about besides work, like workplace politics or stress that occurs between humans, dramatically decreased. In reality, we can clearly see who is close to who in the offline office. For example, someone always talks with somebody. Such a situation happens less while working remotely. Although I want to talk with this person only, the remote working environment doesn't allow that. So such stress on human relationships that I had to be concerned with has been significantly lowered.” Similarly, P05 said, “When we're in the office, it's unavoidable to care about personal relationships, like who I should have lunch with, what I should eat, or this subtle tension of relationships between this person and I, like I'm closer to this person and I'm not with that person. But over 90%, I didn't have to care about this anymore after starting remote work.”

A.1.2 Disadvantages of Remote Work. We found that remote work causes four major problems in terms of the following: problems in HRM, reduced interactions and collaborations, obstacles to achieving organizational goals and culture, and reduced worker's wellbeing.

Problems in HRM. Many participants reported problems in HRM, including worker or job monitoring, evaluation, training, and care, that resulted from tremendously reduced information on workers and decreased interactions with workers in remote work.

Because HR or middle managers are not able to observe how employees perform in their job via remote work, they expressed difficulties in monitoring and evaluating workers’ task performance. P08 mentioned, “While working remotely during Covid, only executives and manager-level workers came and physically worked at the office. So it was almost impossible to check what the juniors were doing. We can't use Zoom the whole day and only used it for specific meetings. It was really difficult back then. […] I had to evaluate the workers based on their output only, but I couldn't check it or ask in real time.” Similarly, P28 said, “It becomes really hard to evaluate individuals’ outcome.” And P21 said, “The fact that it's hard to check how members of the company are working, and especially the fact that it was invisible how people are learning was a great challenge. […] It's been known that, while working remotely, workers’ performance evaluations are heavily dependent on the team leader's evaluation of the individuals. We [firms and HR teams] unavoidably delegate workers’ performance evaluations to the team leaders and put weight in the team leader's judgment. So, it [performance evaluation] is inevitably very result-oriented, according to each individual. P28 said, “The hardest part for HR was being unable to manage whether workers are concentrating. Regardless of whether this person looks at the monitor longer, we don't know what the workers are doing during this core time or meeting. Or we [HR managers] question, ‘Is he even sitting at his seat?’” P05 mentioned, “When working remotely, it's uncontrollable. When you work in the practice, it's way more uncontrollable than you expected. I often wondered or was concerned, ‘What in the world is this person [i.e., subordinate] doing and why hasn't he even finished my work?’”

In particular, we found that, when evaluating workers, it is especially important to have enough information on the process by which employees perform their task(s) to reflect in the evaluation. This is because, except for certain tasks that have objective standards for outcomes (e.g., sales), most tasks performed in enterprises cannot be evaluated solely by the result. Thus, observing the worker's process or that the worker is doing their best is critical. However, fragmented interaction using a computer-supported collaborative tool for a specific purpose doesn't give enough visibility of employees performing their tasks. P08 said, “Evaluation on the outcome reflects the task characteristics especially. I've heard that, comparably in tasks like development or sales, results like S, A, or B grades come out pretty obviously. But many other tasks have subjective question scoring, so trust in the process is more important. For example, tasks like media pressing or legislative work don't have a standard that represents a perfect score, so it's important to see the process of how many journalists the junior is contacting or meeting to produce this result.” Similarly, P08 mentioned, “It was extremely hard to maintain trust between one another. It's inevitable for the seniors to raise doubts about whether the juniors are working as hard as the company and I [the head of the organization and the seniors] want. Reversely, the juniors also question why their outcome is nitpicked by a senior, not knowing where [the senior] is at or what they're doing. It's mutual suspicion. This mainly happens because we cannot know the process of the outcome while working remotely. We're not sure whether we've achieved the best result that we [both juniors and seniors] can do. To trust each other, both of us should see and understand how this result has been derived from the process. In this process, trust between each other is built up. The senior should know the junior has worked so hard to make this result. And the junior should see that the senior is not just nitpicking his work all of a sudden after he kept his hands off, but is actually taking it seriously by seeing the senior coaching, sharing concerns together, or making referrals. Then, they both will pass over the result even if it's 50 points out of 100. Since the process is in a gray area, we are unsure of whether it's 50 points or not.” P01 said, “We work in a startup and most of the tasks we do require risk-taking. The essence of our work is doing numerous trials and errors to make one big success. But who would take a risk if we evaluate the employees only based on the outcomes without being able to see how hard they have worked? It is a problem because we cannot see the detailed process of how people work during remote work.”

Reduced information and interactions in remote work creates problems when training workers. P21 recalled, “I was in charge of onboarding. We conducted achievement evaluations after a three-week training for new incoming workers, you know, like a test to measure how well they're trained and ready to work. But what I felt while training was that the scores got significantly lower compared with the ones who trained offline. I didn't statistically analyze it, but just looking at the average, it was over 90 [out of 100] on average; but after remote working, it went down to about 70 out of 100.” P21 further expressed difficulties in the training process, saying “In terms of training, it took so much time to think over how we could possibly check whether the workers were actually concentrating and learning and figure out the ways.”

Human resource managers pointed out that remote work makes it harder to care for their workers. P28 emphasized the role of nonverbal cues in both communication and HR: “We [HR managers] have this saying, ‘HR knows what [the worker] is thinking and doing by just seeing his footsteps.’ In my case, I often sense that if a worker comes to the office early and goes out for lunch hour, then I have this feeling ‘he might be doing job interviews with other firms and leave our company soon.’ This is funny, but when recruiting or evaluating, I can just catch and pick out the candidates or employees who will quit the job. When someone has a bad mood or I read his facial expression, I ask what's going on. So there were many times I had held workers who were going to leave the company. Also, when evaluating workers, I just observe how people interact. For example, this worker was a star player; he excelled in his job. But I found he had some communication issues, and sure enough, the team leader pointed out the worker's communication skills in the performance evaluation report.” P28 added, “The most important and fatal issue in HR is feedback. Regarding performance compensation incentives, there's going to be a person who's not satisfied even if they've got an A. […] In terms of HR feedback, it is important to explain how they received the result, explaining the procedural aspects of their individual outcome, the team's outcome, the communication or collaboration issues, etc.” She further explained, “Some people did well before remote work due to COVID, but all of a sudden, their outcome dramatically decreased during remote work. Then, I asked them what the problem is. Most of the time, they tell me they don't even know why.” [Other cues at the office – feedback] Similarly, P28 said, “I think it's related to relationship building. At the office, I can ask ‘Hey, Jack. Is something going on with you these days?’ while passing by. Or you'll hear of something from people talking at the office pantry, like someone went to a concert last weekend or something. Then, I can ask the person, ‘Hey, I've heard you went to a concert. How was the concert?’ Such a word or two can make the workers feel ‘Oh, this HR manager is more interested in me than I expected.’ Or you can say, ‘Hey, you got a new haircut and it looks good on you!’ I consider such a thing is really important in terms of organizational management. […] In terms of HR, the drawback is huge because it's difficult to care for the workers.”

Reduced Interactions and Collaborations. Many participants, ranging from employees (e.g., staff and interns) to upper management (e.g., CEOs, HR teams), mentioned that reduced interactions and collaborations from remote work led to a decline in productivity, creativity, and knowledge sharing.

Many participants pointed out reduced productivity as a major drawback of remote work. P28 said, “Both the director of the headquarters in company A [game industry] as well as the vice-president of company B [IT industry] told me that they had trusted the workers and let them be, but it [remote work] produced lots of goof-offs.” Also, many participants, ranging from mid- or upper-level workers to staff members, argued that work productivity was lowered. P21 mentioned, “Even though employees turned on the computer and worked, since there was no way to show that an individual was working or for others to be able to check that they were working, we could often see that workers’ concentration on work decreased.” P21 directly compared the scores of HR training between offline versus online courses and said, “The test score was significantly lowered.”

Being unable to observe how other colleagues work also leads to a decline in self-control and productivity. P29 said, “You know, if we work at the office, we see other people working. We could watch another person working really hard, and that gives you an ‘Oh, I should work hard too. I want to do better’ feeling. It builds the environment.” P28 said, “One of our employees said she could concentrate better when working with other people at official places equipped with two or three dual monitors, rather than home.” Similarly, P21 said, “We were thinking about online training last year, but contrarily, the workers said they don't want to do it online. They said they want to see others’ faces and see what the person sitting next to them is doing or drawing during the job training session. They said they like that atmosphere more.”

Also, due to the invisibility (presence) issue (i.e., the absence of colleagues) the flexibility and responsiveness of sudden face-to-face conversations that used to occur at the physical office has been significantly restricted. P28 said, “In the company [at a physical office], there are many times when we have to make rapid decisions. I believe many other workers would relate to that. For instance, while I need to [urgently] concentrate on my task, to somebody else, his work is more urgent or important. Then, he comes to me, saying ‘Hey, Eugene. Have you finished the work I asked for?’ I had purposefully avoided checking his message, but I reply, ‘Oh I was deep in something else.’ Now, imagine how much urgent business could come up in upper management. As far as I know, it's called asynchronous communication. Anyway, even at the shared physical office, we don't read the message if someone communicates through such tools. Do you think people would respond immediately during remote work?” P05 added, “The boss could have something urgent to do. Then, he could come to my desk and say, ‘Eugene, I'm so sorry. This is really urgent. Please do it by 10 am.’ Not only the words he said but the nonverbal cues that represent urgency exist. But when working remotely, even though he texts, saying ‘This is urgent but please do it by 10,’ if the person doesn't see it, he can't answer.”

Also, remote work severely affected spontaneous chances for social interaction and creative collaboration between workers. P08 explained, “If working at the [physical] office, someone could mumble to himself, saying ‘I think something got messed up.’ Then, a colleague right next to him could help him by saying, ‘Isn't there a way to solve that? Why don't you look that up?’ Or when the brain doesn't work, people could ask each other to grab a coffee, and during small talk, some kind of solution comes up.”

We also found that remote work facilitated less social interactions among organizational members. P01 pointed out that reduced chances for small talk were the most critical problem of remote work, saying “We've tried various types of remote work. We tried working all together with Zoom continuously on all day or working without such a thing at all. One of the things that we were mostly concerned with was, while our tasks flew by without a problem, we couldn't form so-called ‘weak links’ based on small talk. I relate to this, because when I was at my first job, we used conference calls, mobile phones, or emails only, since that happened [i.e., Covid and working remotely], when new employees came in, it was literally impossible to build rapport with the newcomers. Since it was based on calls and I couldn't see their face, I felt difficulty, whether this person is from the outsourcing agency or our company, since [rapport] had not been formed. Also, the other company I moved to had a tool, Slack, that we could closely converse in, but even though we were able to see each other's faces and we could have small talk there, we couldn't invite people [to a chat] just to ask if they had lunch. For example, assume that I'm the boss and I send a Slack message to the subordinate, ‘Did you have lunch?’ Now [the subordinate] gets really nervous. What kind of work are they going to give me that they even ask if I had lunch? Because that happens, rather than forming ‘weak links’, people regard [small talk] as an extension of work. In that regard, I also had a hard time.”

What's more problematic, such reduced social interactions at work aggravated knowledge sharing between seniors and juniors or between colleagues and decreased new employees’ opportunities for learning and training. We found that remote work caused especially tight tensions between seniors and juniors. P29 said, “I had just started my job as a newbie when Covid went full swing, so our company switched to fully remote work. I was so stressed learning all the new things. The senior just handed over the text of a manual and told me to get used to the system I'll use.” P05 cynically remarked, “Juniors don't even ask. I mean, I have no idea what they are curious about. They have to ask quickly at all times [i.e., as soon as they have a question], but they don't ask. For God's sake, they don't ask; when I want them to do what I tell them to do, they do what they want to do without even asking.” P25 represented the juniors’ position, “In the beginning, we didn't even see each other's faces. We didn't even have Zoom meetings. How would you ask a question to a superior you're not close enough to? It could depend on the individuals’ personalities or cultural backgrounds. But isn't it common that [people are] a little cautious when asking a question to someone they haven't met?” P29 also said, “There are no tools that help synchronous communication during remote work. Of course, we have a mobile phone, but who would call his boss to ask about a small thing? At the office, we could ask something when bumping into each other or ask if the boss has time to talk when passing by. But to talk on Zoom, we have to email back and forth or write a message on Slack to schedule a time. What's funny is that sometimes they don't even respond. I don't know if they missed it, saw it but forgot it, or ignored it on purpose. Who knows?”

Obstacles to Achieving Organizational Goals and Culture. We found that remote work can negatively impact organizational goals and culture within a firm. This is because working individually from home inevitably reduces chances for social interactions, such as small talk, through which workers build trust in each other and share common values in the organization. Participants recalled being unable to recognize their colleagues’ faces as one of the major drawbacks of remote work. P10 said, “Remote work essentially is a work structure or system where we should unavoidably work without knowing the faces of the company members.” P05 also said, “When I did complete remote work, I would wonder whether I'm really working for the company or not. Although I was working, I only communicated with a few people to whom I had to report and my subordinates right below.” P28, “After experiencing remote work, everybody says ‘I can't recognize newcomers at all.’ Or ‘I can't even say hello.’ It's all strange faces to workers.”

A remote work structure or system that leaves employees to work as strangers diminishes developing organizational culture (e.g., a bond of sympathy between members of the company, a sense of belonging) In this aspect, P28 continued, “[Not knowing colleagues’ faces] also leads to an organizational culture. The strength of a start-up is being able to share the goal together or level with each other freely, sharing ‘our team did well on this and your team didn't make it this time.’ But since workers can't see each other's faces, it becomes uncomfortable. The trust and [the fact that] we're all running in the same direction […] It all comes back to the organizational culture in the end because it's so important. So I'm questioning the remote work system in the aspect of building an organizational culture.” P08 said, “Let's assume it [i.e., sharing the feeling of the company's vision and value, workers’ devotion to the company, or their sense of belonging] was 100 when people came and worked at the physical office before Covid. It has dropped to 10 when working remotely during Covid.” Interestingly, some participants suggested that all employees should work in the same way to avoid organizational conflicts. P08 further said, “Regarding remote work, there are so many conflicts between departments, the old and new, and so on. It happens at each IT company. […] I think hybrid work is even worse. One worker comes to the office, and the other doesn't come. A certain department doesn't come and the other department must come due to the task characteristics. It fosters conflicts within an organization, and trust between one another collapses. Also, we can't really communicate, because the senior doesn't need to talk to someone who is working from home if the senior could just talk to someone approachable at the office.” Similarly, P28 noted, “Everybody talks about the issues of remote work, like why [certain] team members should be physically at the office. Even in the same team, person A is allowed to work remotely while I'm at the office physically. Someone does hybrid work and someone does not. It's a mess.”

Threats to Workers’ Wellbeing. Our findings demonstrate that, as mentioned in Table 1 there are certain parts of a worker's wellbeing (i.e., spending more time with the family) that remote work has positively affected, but other negative parts also exist: a sense of isolation at work, difficulty in time management, and difficulty separating work from daily life.

P05 expressed, “While working remotely, in the aspect of relationship building, the interaction between each other decreases, and features that make me feel intimacy are lacking. I don't think it's possible for workers to feel intimacy or experience interaction while working completely remotely. It's because, oftentimes, people turn on their voices [mics] only, or just show presentation slides. […] In that aspect, I felt and thought that the metaverse is a more social space than remote work that uses Zoom or Slack for certain purposes.”

Also, many participants expressed difficulties in managing their time for work and sustaining a work-life balance. Surprisingly, one group of workers told us that Zoom meetings had stacked up or they constantly had to work because they were logged into online communication services (e.g., Slack, Zoom); the other group said they unavoidably procrastinated due to the WFH environment. On the other hand, P28 spoke for her staff members, saying “the workers spoke candidly of their difficulties in remote work to me, saying ‘I can't help it but I keep going to the bed to lie down.’” Similarly, P25 said, “I put off my work during the daytime and couldn't finish it [within business hours]. So I had to work until night. I think it was better when we went to the office together and finished work and left all together. Back then, I was at least able to rest without concern or guilt after work.” Such participants’ experiences demonstrate that, whether employees work too much or too little, time management is a critical issue of remote work.

The absence of a private workspace at home can not only cause conflicts in the family but also negatively impact workers’ mental and physical wellbeing. P08 commented, “Since the person who used to be always out for work stays at home from 9 to 6, the children don't get it. They often come to my room and ask random stuff.” P25 mentioned, “Making the family members understand my situation is stressful. Ranging from securing a workspace to scheduling arrangements, there are lots of things to share and coordinate.” Also, P28 shared, “Our staff often said that they don't have a workroom or a desk at home, so they work on the bed or even the ground, complaining about their back pain or literally saying their butt hurts. […] If they had worked at the office, they could have used a comfortable chair that doesn't make their butt hurt, or a light company computer, or a clean restroom in the office, and they could even have taken a break in the lounge.”
