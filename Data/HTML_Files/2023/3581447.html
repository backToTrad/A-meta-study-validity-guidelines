“Where is history”: Toward Designing a Voice Assistant to help Older Adults locate Interface Features quickly

Natalie Parde , Computer Science, University of Illinois Chicago, United States, parde@uic.edu
Debaleena Chattopadhyay , Computer Science, University of Illinois Chicago, United States, debchatt@uic.edu

CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Older adults often struggle to locate a function quickly in feature-rich user interfaces (UIs). Mobile UIs not only pack a ton of features in a small screen but also get frequent updates to their visual layouts—thereby exacerbating the problem. This paper explores a design solution where users could search for a UI feature using spoken-word queries. We investigated: 1) what type of questions older users ask when facing interaction challenges in unfamiliar scenarios, 2) how those query types compare with younger users’ inquiries, and 3) how older adults use a voice assistant design probe in a Wizard-of-Oz (WoZ) study. Results reveal five query types when verbally articulating interaction issues: validation, directed and undirected informational, navigational, and conceptual. In the WoZ study, older users typically asked for help following a series of non-unique or off-task feature selections ( n = 13/15), and in 77% of those instances, they completed the task in the next interaction.

1 INTRODUCTION

Owing to the ubiquity of mobile devices (phones, tablets, watches, or rings), the types of mobile applications (apps) continue to rise in number and increase in complexity. These modern mobile apps range from online shopping, food delivery, personal health records, to maps, and offer many functions that are organized under different types of menus, user interface (UI) components, and navigation bars. Although feature-rich mobile apps do not offer hundreds of commands like desktop applications (e.g., word processors or spreadsheets), they continue to pack a ton of features into a small screen. More and more, these sophisticated mobile apps are becoming so essential in daily life that researchers have speculated whether being unable to use online-only government services could soon expedite older adults moving into assisted living [ 33 ]. Last year, the American Association of Retired Persons (AARP), the leading advocacy group for older adults in the United States (U.S.) reported that despite the significant rise in the use of smartphones and tablets among older adults, 50 to 70-plus, two in five 50-plus adults feel that technologies are not designed with older adults in mind, citing the complexities of different apps [ 30 ].

So while we might celebrate the reported uptake of mobile technology among older people—in 2021, 61% of 65-plus and 83% of 50-plus U.S. adults owned a smartphone [ 47 ]—the reality of their actual mobile device use is much more nuanced. Older adults lag behind their younger counterparts in both the number of mobile apps ( breadth ) and the number of features per app ( depth ) they use [ 19 , 36 , 72 ]. A recent study with 35 older adults (55 years or older) reported an average use of 2.86 apps, including 1.97 third-party apps [ 38 ]. Older users may remain in a forever-early adoption stage of feature-rich user interfaces [ 19 , 36 , 72 ] because of a lack of onboarding support and continuing peer-support [ 63 ], challenges in interface exploration [ 42 , 69 ], or as a personal choice [ 33 ].

Before introducing our core research questions, we must acknowledge that no set chronological age makes an individual ‘older’ as opposed to younger [ 34 ]. Such categorization can separate older users into a problematic other as well as imply that design needs to accommodate age-related deficits. Furthermore, older adults do not consider themselves as ‘old’ in all-encompassing life circumstances but only in certain situational aspects of environments, objects, and life changes ( situated elderliness [ 6 ]). In the literature too, older adults are defined from ages 50 and above [ 15 , 61 , 66 ], 55 and above [ 2 , 67 ] and 70 and above [ 59 , 60 ]. Because our focus is on feature-rich apps that could potentially benefit young older adults (50-plus) much more than the oldest old (80-plus)[ 48 ], for example, to get a job, volunteer, or travel, we adopted the definition of older adults from the AARP foundation—people 50 and older [ 1 ]. In this work, we will focus on understanding if/how older adults think and act differently —which in turn can inform mobile UI design differently .

The problem we examine in this paper is how to help older adults locate features in a feature-rich mobile interface. Prior work has reported that older adults frequently struggle with identifying and locating the correct feature in desktop [ 42 ] and mobile interfaces [ 69 ]. While this issue has been identified, and some design implications discussed briefly [ 42 , 69 , 70 ], a detailed design solution has not yet been developed nor examined.

In this paper, we explore a detailed design solution to this interaction problem. We focus on mobile interfaces because they tend to get more frequent updates than desktop apps, and these updates often modify the visual layouts of menus, interface components, and buttons—thereby worsening the problem of quickly locating the correct feature during interface exploration, whether using it for the first time (onboarding) or not. Among the many design trajectories considered in the HCI literature to help older adults locate and select correct features during UI exploration, we chose to pursue the approach of using a voice assistant [ 69 ] because older users tend to verbalize question words, like ‘what’, ‘why’, and ‘how’, when encountering an interaction problem [ 17 ]. HCI researchers are exploring many issues around how older adults interact with voice assistants (VAs) and the technology is still evolving. Nevertheless, studies continue to report that older adults use VAs for finding information [ 31 , 32 , 49 , 57 ]. For instance, 60-plus older adults who already own a smart speaker are more likely to use it daily than people ages 30 or younger [ 64 ].

Other design suggestions include providing tooltips, a heatmap of recently repeated feature selections, using users’ gaze to subtly provide cues about hidden menu options, and showing the user a list of their past actions [ 42 ]. Tooltips, heatmaps, and action lists are better suited for desktop interfaces because mobile devices offer limited-screen real estate. Studies have also shown that older adults fixate more on the central part of the screen than the peripheral top and left parts, where many mobile interface menus often reside [ 37 , 50 ]. Note that these design ideas have been briefly discussed in the prior literature, but none of them have been systematically examined, designed, or evaluated with users.

Our work contributes: (1) an investigation of verbal questions that older users may ask when exploring a feature-rich mobile interface to complete a task; (2) a taxonomy of these queries, and examples from two age cohorts—older and younger adults; and (3) insights from a design probe study with older adults where they could use a voice assistant to find UI features during a task.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Mobile Device use in Older Adults

HCI Researchers have been exploring how older adults use computer technologies and what challenges they encounter since circa 2000 [ 14 , 23 , 53 ]. However, much has changed since the last decade, particularly the stance toward aging research [ 34 ], user perceptions [ 45 ], and the kind of technologies heavily used at present (e.g., smartphones over desktops) [ 47 ]. We have learned that older adults are much less likely to adopt senior-friendly technology variants that are designed specifically for them than people with a different ability to adopt assistive tools [ 33 , 34 ]. That is perhaps because older adults do not view aging as disabling [ 34 ]—at least not in all-encompassing life circumstances but maybe in certain everyday contexts [ 6 ]. Older adults now prefer more social learning methods and have come to rely less on instruction manuals than observed in the past [ 45 ]. Furthermore, user interface guidelines have also been updated to reflect the newer technologies being adopted and used by this demographic [ 13 ].

For instance, at the beginning of the last decade (2013), 18% of 65-plus U.S. adults owned a smartphone. By the year 2021, that number has risen to 61%. So researchers have lately begun to examine how older adults learn, use, and adopt modern mobile apps [ 5 , 24 ], whose functions include personal health management, grocery delivery, public transit, and scheduling COVID-19 vaccines. In spite of the uptake in mobile devices, older adults use way fewer apps and app functions than younger adults [ 38 ]. While non-use could be a deliberate choice [ 33 , 52 ], another reason for limited use repeatedly observed in the HCI literature is a lack of exploration support [ 42 , 69 ]. Most recently, it has been observed that not being able to locate the correct features—within a reasonable amount of time—at either the beginning or during a task constitutes the core struggle of older adults when trying to explore and use a feature-rich UI [ 37 , 45 ].

2.2 Using Feature-Rich User Interfaces

Owing to the new and advanced sensors, better wireless communication infrastructure, and the ubiquity of mobile devices, mobile apps have become the first choice for most businesses to offer their services to end-users. Not only there are more mobile apps offering vital services now, but these apps also have more features than ever before. Although mobile apps do not have hundreds of features as found in desktop interfaces, even tens of features and hidden navigation menu structures can cause usability issues [ 37 ]. In particular, app navigation issues have been reported repeatedly when studying how older adults use feature-rich mobile interfaces [ 37 , 38 ]. For example, when studying how older users accomplish different tasks using a smartphone, researchers observed difficulty in directing attention to tab menus, recognizing scrollable tab menus, and distinguishing between neighboring or similar-appearing icons and buttons [ 37 ]. Users focused more on the content than navigation cues, similar to prior studies [ 50 ], and did not open vertical menus, such as the side drawer and springboard to explore menu options further [ 37 ]. In this work, we examine a design solution to this interaction issue—how to help older adults locate UI features when using or exploring a feature-rich mobile interface.

2.3 Interactive Tutorials for Mobile UIs

Somewhat related but different from the scope of our work are interactive tutorials for mobile UIs. In both accessibility and aging research, there is a distinct line of ongoing work that investigates how to design better interactive tutorials [ 25 ] and task assistants [ 4 , 39 ]. Interactive tutorials address the recent findings that older users have come to rely much less on instruction manuals, be it in a narrative format or video tutorials [ 12 , 25 , 45 ]. Interactive tutorials may provide task-specific instructions along with highlighting the relevant UI components on the screen within the context of a task [ 35 , 58 ]. Such forms of support are most preferred during the onboarding phase, which is provided when the app is launched for the first time [ 35 , 45 , 58 ].

Despite the many advantages of interactive tutorials, creating an interactive tutorial and maintaining it following each and every app update is arduous and resource-intensive. So researchers have been looking into ways to more effectively develop interactive tutorials. For example, inviting a remote helper to compose a tutorial based on a question asked by an older user who needs help while using a smartphone app [ 25 ] or automatically generating a tutorial based on a batch of text instructions about the app and the app's metadata before users explore that app [ 71 ].

In this work, we are not designing tutorials to provide task-specific step-by-step instructions to our users. Our design objective is to help older users locate relevant features in real-time during app exploration—without requiring human assistance or app-specific interventions prior to use. We do not claim a design advantage in our approach over interactive tutorials. Rather, we note that we are designing for a similar but different problem. Both interactive tutorials and feature locators are designed to support older users in UI exploration. But tutorials provide way more guided support than just helping to locate an on-screen feature, for example, finding several correct features and navigating the correct sequence of interaction steps. Studies show that there is a need for both these types of support for older users: tightly– [ 25 ] and loosely–coupled [ 45 , 69 ] with the optimal interaction path.

2.4 Intelligent Voice Assistant Use by Older Adults

Voice-based interfaces continue to garner more and more popularity despite the technological challenges of better speech recognition and search intent recognition [ 56 ]. These interfaces allow users to interact with an application using natural language. They could be embodied in smart speakers or as virtual agents, or available to interact with a desktop GUI (e.g., Apple's Siri or Microsoft's Cortana) or touch-based mobile devices (Apple's Siri or Google Assistant). The body of research examining older adults’ use and perception of voice-based interfaces is still emerging and report both positive results, like user preference in looking up information [ 49 ] and negative results, like cultural barriers to use [ 22 ]. In this paper, we do not argue for any advantage of a voice-based assistant over other interaction modalities to help older adults locate UI features. Rather, we propose a way that is suitable to the size and affordances of mobile devices and does not contribute to ‘othering’ of older users by implying the design of senior-friendly mobile apps. As newer design solutions to the problem at hand emerge, further research will be needed to compare the different solution modalities in terms of usability and accessibility.

In this paper, we propose a design solution to help older adults find the correct features when exploring feature-rich mobile interfaces. Our proposed solution uses a voice-based interaction modality to easily augment existing mobile apps while leveraging current accessibility features [ 68 ]. We inform the design of our voice-based assistant on data collected from our observations with older adults.

3 FINDING FEATURES WITH VERBAL QUESTIONING

To help older users quickly locate features during interface exploration without human intervention, we envisioned a voice assistant system. When asked to think aloud during digital interactions, older users have been found to verbalize question words, like "what", "why", and "how", when encountering an interaction problem [ 17 ]. With a voice assistant, then, users could verbally ask about a function and receive visual cues that would reduce their search space of UI features. But before designing such a voice assistant, we wanted to know what kind of verbal questions older users would ask when exploring a feature-rich mobile interface to complete a task.

Prior studies showed older adults struggle to locate relevant features in both desktops [ 42 ] and mobile interfaces [ 69 ]. However, such struggles did not appear to stem from a lack of understanding of the semantics of icons or labels [ 69 ], but often from a tendency to only look for the features where they would expect them to be (e.g., "Why are there these things [create notebook/section] at the bottom? I would expect them to be at the top, maybe under View" , during a post-study interview, p. 6 [ 42 ]). Despite multiple reports of this problem, there are no data on how older users might ask for assistance when facing the problem. We investigate that in this section.

Older users did not only miss relevant features at the onset of a task but also en route to completing a task. Even when on the right track, older adults could get confused about the correct features, make off-task feature selections, and get stuck into cycles where they would repeat the same sequence of incorrect feature selections over and over [ 42 , 69 ]. In a recent study with Microsoft OneNote, on average, older users missed over twice the features as younger adults, and the difficulty in locating the correct feature did not only occur when using an application for the first time [ 42 ]. Older adults also struggled when familiar with an application but unfamiliar with a particular task or an updated version of the interface [ 69 ]. Several think-aloud studies have reported older users articulating interaction struggles as questions, for example, “What's the problem...why can't we find ice cream...desserts?” [ 69 ] or “Oops. How to go back?” [ 17 ].

The difficulty in quickly locating correct features by older adults has been repeatedly observed, characterized, and described in detail. But we still do not know how older adults might articulate such a difficulty verbally and whether that articulation can serve as an input to a voice assistant. To that aim, we conducted an exploratory study, where older adults verbalized their thoughts as questions when an unfamiliar mobile application caused confusion.

3.1 Study 1: Older Adults

3.1.1 Method. We adopted a think-aloud protocol to conduct a task-focused, qualitative study with feature-rich mobile interfaces. Participants were asked to speak aloud their thoughts anytime the mobile interface caused doubt or confusion. They were encouraged to ask questions. Our rationale for this method was to understand and characterize the kind of questions older users ask during interface exploration. We expected this study to offer insights on whether and how a voice assistant could help older adults locate UI features quickly.

Given the COVID-19 pandemic situation, out of an abundance of caution, our studies (Study 1, 2, and 3) were conducted remotely via a video-calling application (e.g., WhatsApp or Skype). The study was approved by the university's institutional review board. Participants were recruited via word-of-mouth, emails, and flyers at local senior centers and the authors’ institution. Participants shared their mobile screens during the study with the facilitator and both the shared screen and audio were recorded. During recruitment and initial screening, participants were offered onboarding help with their choice of video-calling application but none opted. Recent studies have reported that due to frequent use of video calling during the pandemic, more older users have become familiar with their use [ 10 , 29 ]. When participants asked a question, the facilitator would answer verbally and then let them continue exploring. Unlike the traditional think-aloud protocol, facilitators did not repeatedly prompt participants to think aloud or ask questions during the study. To measure participants’ proficiency in mobile device use, we asked them to complete the mobile device proficiency questionnaire (MDPQ-14), which is an objective measure of smartphone skills and has been validated with older adults (Appendix, Table 6 ) [ 46 ].

Given the exploratory objective of this study, we opted for ecological and external validity over internal validity. Each participant (1) chose the place, time, mobile device, and video-calling app, while (2) we chose the particular app (after an initial discussion with the participant) which was from a category familiar to them (e.g., online shopping) but never been used before (e.g., Target shopping app). Using the same application and same tasks, irrespective of users’ familiarity with them would not be a valid UI exploration scenario. Each participant used an unfamiliar mobile app from a familiar category to ensure they were unfamiliar with the app interface but familiar with the app concept.

We tried using as many unique mobile apps as possible to collect a diverse corpus of unique user questions during interface exploration. Each participant completed three to four tasks with the elected app on their mobile devices (Appendix, Table 7 ). Each study session lasted for about 30 to 45 minutes and was conducted between 2020 and 2022.

3.1.2 Data analysis. The objective of our study was to identify, collate, and analyze the questions asked during interface exploration. We started that by first identifying the questions from each video. The entire video corpus was 202 minutes long. The first and last authors worked on identifying and recording these questions over a period of two months. First, the two authors inspected two full videos together to decide which articulations should be considered a question. Because our data were collected in natural settings as a conversation between the participant and facilitator around the action of exploring a mobile interface, we took inspiration from conversation analysis (CA) to decide what talk should be considered as a question [ 11 , 54 ]. One goal of CA is to identify the meaning of actions that participants in interaction do, e.g., asking, telling, requesting, complaining, etc. So other than extracting the wh -interrogatives, we identified when the final intonation of a turn construction unit (TCU)—i.e., a speech segment in CA—was a question mark (?), indicated by a strongly rising intonation [ 54 ]. Wh -interrogatives start with what, when, where, who, whom, which, whose, why, and how, and are used to ask for information that cannot be answered in yes or no.

After the authors agreed on what constitutes a question, the first author independently went through the entire video corpus and recorded all questions. These questions were repeatedly examined by the last author for clarity of description and completeness. Disagreements were discussed among the authors and resolved during revisions. After user questions were logged, the first author analyzed them inductively using open coding [ 7 ]. Then, the codes were discussed and iterated in group data analysis sessions until key themes were identified. To identify the open codes, we grounded our analysis in prior classification of search queries in web search, e.g., navigational, informational, and transactional [ 8 ] and voice search, e.g., "how" and "what" queries and "yes/no" questions [ 20 ]. Although prior taxonomies exist for search queries—typed or spoken—to our knowledge, no prior HCI work has characterized the questions asked during interface exploration by older adults.

3.1.3 Results. We recruited 19 participants (11 females, Mdn = 54 years, IQR = 9), all of whom owned a touch-enabled smartphone. Each participant completed three to four tasks with sixteen unique mobile apps (Appendix, Table 7 ). These apps—although never used before by the respective participant—belonged to a category familiar to them. All of the participants asked questions during interface exploration ( Mdn = 5). MDPQ-14 scores were reported by 12/19 participants ( Mdn = 44.5, M = 44.75). They were not significantly different ( p = 0.51) from MDPQ-14 scores reported in the literature from 60-plus adults ( Mdn = 41.5, M = 41.3) [ 46 ]—which suggests that the mobile device proficiency of our participants were similar to people considered older adults elsewhere in the literature. Table 1 lists the demographics, MDPQ-14 scores, and the number of questions asked per participant. Table 2 lists some query examples.

Following data analysis, we identified four key types of questions asked during interface exploration: validation , informational , navigational , and conceptual . Questions asked to verify whether the user is doing the right action were classified as validation, e.g., “Should I click on the ‘no changes’ option?” . This type of question would require a yes/no answer and may immediately follow any other type of question as the user deliberates on different interaction options, e.g., “What do I do now? Do I need to click the restaurant?” . In this example, “What do I do now?” is an undirected informational question, which is discussed next. Questions that asked for a particular piece of information were classified as informational. Informational questions could be directed , i.e., having a clear idea about the next action, but not what next features to be selected (e.g., “How do I highlight it?” ), or undirected , i.e., lacking any intuition about what specific UI features to use next (e.g., “What am I supposed to do with this map?” ). Questions asked about where to go next or where a particular UI feature is were classified as navigational, e.g., “Where do I find the ‘edit’ button?” . Finally, questions that were not directly related to the UI structure but were about the general workflow or system model of an app were classified as conceptual, e.g., “Why is it saying ‘couldn't send SMS’?” . A question could only be classified as one of the five types. We did not encounter examples in our study that would make such a classification problematic. But future studies may suggest revisions to this taxonomy as more research is done in this area.

We also classified user questions as “how", “what”, “where/which”, “why” and “yes/no”. User questions could be about interface features (e.g., “How to click a picture?” ) or other concerns (e.g., “Why does it say ‘error’ when I enter the address in the text box?” ). Examples of this characterization are shown in Table 2 . Finally, we identified if a question was about finding a correct interface feature or not. While we cannot presume the user intent of a question, we considered “where/which” and “how” questions as the most compelling evidence of a study participant intending to locate a correct feature during interface exploration. We acknowledge that there are some limitations of this approach and discuss them in Section 3.3. On average, older adults asked two questions about finding correct interface features (Table 1 , # UI Qs ).

Given that this was an open-ended exploratory study, no patterns of a common sequence of questions or question preferences per demographic were identified. 3.2 Study 2: Verbal Questioning by Younger Users

While we use the terms older and younger to differentiate users on the basis of age, we note that there is no set age that makes someone “older” [ 34 ]. We neither argue that age should categorically separate users into a problematic other nor interface design should strive to make up for age-related deficits. We were, rather, interested in uncovering if older adults think and act differently , which in turn could inform design differently . Following the same method as Study 1, each study participant completed 3–4 different tasks with an unfamiliar app (Appendix, Table 8 ). Participants were also asked to respond to the MDPQ-14 survey to contrast their mobile device proficiency with older adults.

3.2.1 Results. Twelve younger users participated in the study (6 females, Mdn = 31 years, IQR = 8.5). 8 out of 12 participants completed the MDPQ-14 questionnaire ( Mdn = 64, M = 62.1). As expected, the mobile device proficiency of our younger participants was significantly more than our older participants in Study 1 ( U = 20, p = 0.03, r = 0.48).

The total length of videos recorded was 133 minutes. The number of questions asked by each participant during interface exploration is listed in Table 8 (Appendix). All the questions collected in Study 2 could be categorized using the taxonomy created in Study 1. No new categories were identified. For example, “This is ‘direct chat’. Is this what a live chat is?” (validation), “I can only see the Split Equally option, how do I add an unequal split?” (informational, directed), “How to identify my account information?” ( informational, undirected ), “Where exactly is the option to add stories?” (navigational), and “How is the application aware of people I know?” (conceptual). Wh -interrogatives (e.g., “Which one represents the cart?” , “How to record a video?” ) and yes/no questions were also captured (e.g., “Is this public?” , “Is this what a live chat is?” ).

While younger users asked the same types of questions as older adults, how frequently they asked these questions varied significantly. When compared between subjects, we found that older users asked significantly more validation questions than their younger counterparts ( U = 46, p = 0.004, r = 0.6), as well as more directed informational questions ( U = 53, p = 0.007, r = 0.53). The total number of questions asked by both cohorts is presented in Table 3 . The most common type of questions that older adults asked was validation , followed by informational, directed and navigational . In contrast, the most common type of questions asked by younger users was conceptual . However, we had a greater number of older ( n = 19) than younger participants ( n = 12) in our study which may have had an effect on the total number of questions asked.

3.3 Summary

We presented qualitative data from two studies where users articulated their doubts and confusions when exploring a mobile interface as questions. Similar to a previous study, older users sometimes used question words, like "what", "why", and "how", when encountering an interaction problem [ 17 ]. We contributed a characterization of user queries to aid in the future design of helper solutions (Table 2 ). The study tasks or apps were unfamiliar to the participants, but they were familiar with the type of apps (e.g., social media, online shopping, food delivery) and touchscreen smartphones. In this way, we tried to focus on issues around interface exploration rather than any issues with forming a mental model about the app category (e.g., How does online shopping work?), touch interactions (e.g., What is a tap or pinch to zoom?), or smartphones (all 31 participants were regular smartphone users).

Although both older and younger users asked questions that ranged all across our taxonomy, the frequency of different types of questions differed significantly (Table 3 ). For example, older users asked significantly more directed informational questions (e.g., “How can I zoom in?” ) than younger users. This suggests that younger adults are less likely to ask interface-related questions, which could be interpreted as either they encounter relatively few problems, or they find the answer themselves by exploring the UI. Younger users may often quickly find the relevant features because studies show that they are more comfortable exploring different menus compared to older users [ 42 ]. Drawing from this new knowledge about older users’ question types during interface exploration, we wanted to provide them assistance in quickly locating correct UI features. In the next section, we describe how we designed a probe and got feedback from older users about their usefulness.

3.4 Limitations

It is important to note here some limitations of our categorization, particularly how we identify user interface (UI) related questions. We observed that queries might be about UI features but not "where" or "how" questions, e.g., “Is this same as a friend request?”, “I can't see a remove button.” , or “Do I do this with the filter or sort button?” . So the number of UI-queries we report here is a conservative estimate—and done to ensure the rigor of our analysis. Second, we did not conduct a controlled study to compare the question types of older and younger users and thus, cannot rule out any contextual confounding factors that may have played a role. Our objective here was not a strict comparison, but an exploration of how older users would articulate their doubts during mobile interface exploration.

4 DESIGN PROBE STUDY

While prior literature reported older users struggling to locate features in feature-rich user interfaces [ 42 , 69 ], our Study 1 provided two new pieces of empirical evidence: (1) when encountering such problems, older users use question words, like "where" and "how", and (2) UI-related queries were among the top three most frequent types of questions asked by older adults (directed informational, and navigational, Tables 3 and 2 ). Based on these results, we established the following design requirements: (1) allow the user to ask an interface-feature-related question verbally, and (2) offer visual cues on the mobile interface to narrow down the search space. To realize these requirements, we would need to process users’ natural language input (the query) and output the coordinates of a UI component that could then be visually highlighted. Processing the users’ query would include speech recognition and intent recognition.

Before getting the design right (i.e., testing the usability), we wanted to investigate if our proposed solution addresses the users’ requirement of quickly locating a UI feature in feature-rich mobile interfaces. To that end, we decided to develop a design probe and conduct a Wizard-of-Oz study to examine how older adults would use our design solution. Using the design probe, we wanted to examine if users find the design intervention useful. Particularly, we investigated the following three research questions:

  1. Do users use the voice assistant?
  2. When do users use the voice assistant?
  3. How does the voice assistant intervention influence subsequent user interactions?

Next, we describe the design probe and discuss the study.

4.1 Design Probe

Our proposed design solution would work as follows. Imagine a user exploring a feature-rich UI to accomplish a task at hand. Anytime during that interaction session, the user could invoke the voice assistant and ask a question while remaining on their current UI screen or view ( just-in-time , Figure 1 a). The assistant will first process the user's query using speech recognition and intent recognition techniques—the output of which would be a set of keywords (Figure 1 b). The assistant will also process the current UI view and obtain the coordinates, labels, and descriptions of all UI components attached to the current view (e.g., Table 4 ). A second set of keywords will be obtained for each UI component from their associated labels and descriptions (Figure 1 c). Using these two sets of keywords, the assistant will then find up to three UI components that are similar to the user query and highlight them on the screen ( just-in-place , Figure 1 d). Highlighting up to three UI components was a design decision, which was made as a trade-off between a need for guidance and additional visual clutter. We limited our UI-feature search space to the current page, i.e., if the feature most relevant to the user's query was located on a different page, the assistant would not automatically take the user to a different page and highlight that relevant feature. Rather, it would highlight less relevant features—if any—on the user's current page.

To obtain the UI structure of the current page and the labels and descriptions of the UI components, we used Android's Assist API. The set of keywords for each component was extracted using a TF-IDF algorithm [ 3 ] (Figure 1 c). For training the TF-IDF algorithm, we used our own dataset of 240 typical in-app queries scrapped from YouTube how-to videos, 35 mobile apps’ help pages [categories: map (13), shopping (12), social media (10)], and literature [ 69 ]. The set of keywords from the user's voice input was extracted using a Transformer model [ 40 , 43 ] (Figure 1 b). For training the keyword-matching algorithms we used a raw corpus of  30K English sentences. We matched the two sets of keywords and identified up to the top three relevant UI components using ConceptNet's numerical similarity score [ 55 ] (F1 score = .8). We could then use the coordinates of those UI components, as extracted from Android's Assist API, to visually highlight them on the current screen (Figure 1 d).

In translating our design solution into a fully functional prototype that could be used by older adults independently, we faced a number of pragmatic limitations. First, since UI labels and descriptions are optional, not all mobile apps have them in a comprehensive manner. Second, we would have to exclusively use an Android device and develop a custom-made mobile application. So any iPhone users would need to use an Android device in an in-person setting during the COVID-19 pandemic. Even Android users would need to install the custom-made mobile application and provide certain app permissions which could result in additional onboarding issues for any remote evaluation study. Finally, our design solution uses speech recognition and natural language processing algorithms—technologies that are still maturing [ 16 , 41 ]. Although the F1-score for our best-performing algorithm was .8 and accuracy 85%, we decided to conduct a design probe study using the Wizard-of-Oz (WoZ) method and get some feedback on our design idea that was not affected by associated technological limitations.

4.2 Method

A WoZ study with our design probe was conducted to get user feedback about the proposed design solution. When participants invoked the voice assistant, a facilitator (or wizard) would listen to the speech input and highlight up to three relevant UI components on the participants’ current screen—to emulate interaction with our proposed voice assistant (Figure 5 shows the wizard's interface and controls). The study was conducted online using a video-calling application on a smartphone or tablet. Recruitment methods and criteria were similar to Study 1. Participants were asked to complete five tasks while exploring two feature-rich mobile websites. Following task completion, they completed a demographics survey and MDPQ-14 survey and shared their feedback about the design probe.

At the beginning of the study, participants watched a demonstration of the voice assistant (VA). They were instructed to ask questions to the VA when in doubt. When a participant asked a question, the facilitator visually highlighted up to three most relevant features on the web page. We did not use the annotation tools of the video-calling app to highlight features, rather the wizard's version of the website which included a GUI was used to quickly add highlights and arrows as necessary (Figures 2 and 5 ). The participant's screen and audio were recorded for data analysis.

We informed the participants up front that there may be UI loading delays during the experiment. Nevertheless, to minimize the wizard's response time, we created a set of possible questions for each task before the experiment and used our algorithm to pre-identify the relevant UI components (Section 4.1). This list was neither shared with the participants nor incorporated in the interface—but used privately by the wizard. Doing so allowed the wizard to quickly highlight relevant UI components during the experiment. For participant questions that were not identified before, the wizard (first author) used her judgment based on the question, the task at hand, and the participant's current view of the web page. Because no visual feedback of a voice assistant listening to the participant was available, we provided user feedback to confirm a successful query delivery with an audio notification tone. The same audio cue was played again after rendering the visual highlights on the web page. The highlights would disappear as soon as the participant began interacting again. Participants used their smartphones or tablets for this study.

4.3 Results

Fifteen older adults participated in the study (8 females, Mdn = 65 years, IQR = 8). None of them reported being familiar with either of our study websites. Their MDPQ-14 scores ( Mdn = 47.6, M = 48) were not significantly different from our Study 1 participants ( p = 0.56), nor from 60-plus older adults reported in the literature [ 46 ] ( p = 0.56). Table 9 lists the demographics of our participants.

All participants successfully completed the five tasks and in total 33 questions ( Mdn = 2) were recorded during the study (Appendix, Table 9 ). Thirteen participants asked at least one question, and at most, seven questions were asked by one participant. Two questions could not be answered by the wizard because there was no relevant UI component on the screen (a prompt showed “no results were found”), and one question could not be answered because it was a validation-type question ( “Am I doing correctly?” ).

Our video corpus was tagged and classified using an existing codebook [ 42 ] by the first author and supervised by the last author. Given that our experimental setting and participant tasks were different from the original study [ 42 ], we used the following seven events: unique selection, non-unique selection, successful selection, off-task selection, revert, cycle, retry—and introduced two additional events: slip and questioning (Table 5 ).

A total of 931 events were tagged. A single interaction could be tagged with multiple events because events are not mutually exclusive. We marked users as ‘getting lost’ when they would repeat non-unique selections or off-task selections more than once in a row. These criteria were able to capture the cycle, retry, repeated reverts, and other inefficient paths that a user might generate while exploring, which reflects the characteristics of navigational disorientation—looping, inefficient pathways, and inverse operations [ 18 , 69 ]. Getting lost primarily appeared in the form of repeatedly scrolling the screen up and down or moving back and forth through several pages.

Once the user got lost, they could stay lost and abandon the task or complete the task by getting back on track. If they were able to complete the task successfully, we would consider them ‘ recovered ’. 67 off-task selections and 64 non-unique selections were observed, and a total of 41 ‘getting lost’ were identified. 26 out of 41 ‘getting lost’ events were followed by the participant asking the VA a question. In the rest of the 15 cases, users ‘recovered’ by themselves (Figure 4 , right). In 20 instances out of 26 questionings, the participant recovered immediately after the intervention. When examining individual-level data, we found that all participants except for P7 and P15 asked at least one question, and seven of them recovered only when they used the VA (Figure 4 , right).

Participants used the VA significantly more after getting lost (26) than when not lost ( Z = 2.3 p = 0.01, r = 0.71; Figure 4 , left). Participants (P2 and P5) who used the VA without getting lost asked questions right after beginning the task at hand, which could have been a novelty effect, with participants wanting to try out new technology. Figure 3 shows two examples where users used the VA after ‘getting lost’. Among participants who used the VA at least once, VA was used significantly more frequently than self-exploration when recovering from getting lost in an interaction sequence ( Z = 2.4 p = 0.009, r = 0.86; Figure 4 , right).

Another interesting finding from this study, that we did not anticipate, was how participants used the assistance to learn about an unfamiliar app and reuse the features highlighted by the VA in a previous task. For example, often participants repeated the suggested features from one task to the next once they found success with them in the former task. Below are some such instances:

  1. After successfully completing Task 3 on the schedule page, P2 and P6 went directly to the schedule page at the beginning of Task 4.
  2. After successfully completing Task 1 on the event search page, P3 went to the event page to find the answer for Task 2.
  3. After successfully completing Task 1 using the search box, P4 chose to use it in Task 2.
  4. After successfully completing Task 1 using the explore menu, P5 kept trying to use the explore menu for Tasks 2 and 3.
  5. After VA highlighted the search icon in Task 2, P11 used the search function in all subsequent tasks (Tasks 3, 4, and 5).

Note that this was not a think-aloud study. So we did not get many insights into the participant's decision-making process. But during the post-study interview participants mentioned that VA helped them to learn about new features (P11: "VA made me realize that the search function is quite useful" ) and when stuck (P1: " It helped me when I got stuck." ; P5: "It's nice that it reduces my options" ; P14: "It was good. It gave me a hint." ). Participants did not report noticing a substantial delay in getting VA assistance during the post-study interview.

5 DISCUSSION

While mobile device ownership has been steadily growing among older users, both 50-plus and 70-plus [ 26 ], the COVID-19 pandemic has catapulted older adults’ mobile device use and created new habits. Smartphones are now used daily by 97% of 50-plus adults and 95% of 70-plus adults [ 26 ]. Not only mobile devices are now being used for most common activities like browsing, texting, or emailing, but owing to the pandemic more people are using video chat to stay connected to friends and family. The numbers have gone up from 52% in 2019 to 70% in 2020 [ 28 ]. Despite increased tech ownership, older adults’ biggest frustration with fully adopting today's technology is a lack of knowledge ( “Not knowing how to use it. Who can teach me. Also, everything changes so quick” , p. 5, [ 27 ]). 33% of 50-plus and 40% of 65-plus older adults recently reported user interface experience as a key barrier to tech use. With the aging population predicted to rise sharply in the coming decades, it is important that we help older users effectively use feature-rich mobile apps. Given that more and more older users are adopting home assistant devices like Amazon's Echo and Google Home (35% of 50-plus older adults owned smart home devices in 2021, [ 28 ]), can we use voice assistants to improve older users’ experience of exploring continually-changing, feature-rich mobile apps?

5.1 Primary Findings

In this paper, we found that, when encountering interaction problems, older users ask three main types of questions, validation questions (whether they are doing the right thing, clicking the right button, or on the right track to accomplish a task), directed informational questions (how to do a particular action, like saving, or going back, or searching), and navigational questions (where is a particular UI feature or function or button). Directed informational and navigational questions were mostly verbalized using question words, like ‘how’, or ‘where’ (Table 2 ). A prior study looking into older users’ think-aloud (TA) verbalization and speech features found that when encountering an interaction problem, older users tend to verbalize question words, like ‘what’, ‘why’, and ‘how’ [ 17 ]. We extend this finding to show how different question words could further indicate different types of interaction problems (or information needs), e.g., ‘how’ and ‘where’ questions to express a lack of knowledge about the UI location of a particular function. This type of UI-related query appeared to be unique to older adults; older users asked significantly more directed informational questions than younger adults. Our taxonomy (Table 2 ) continues the line of prior work on classifying web search engine queries [ 8 , 51 ] and general question-answering systems [ 9 ].

On average, older users asked two questions about finding correct interface features (Table 1 ). Although this number might seem low, studies have shown that failing to locate the correct feature even once during a task often leads to looping, several back-and-forths, and ultimately task abandonment and user frustration [ 37 , 69 ]. This type of repeated non-unique and off-task selections has also been found in older users’ interactions with desktop applications [ 42 ]. Still, prior studies did not examine how users would articulate those struggles verbally as they happened. We close that research gap and show the potential of a question-answering assistant to provide older users with just-in-time help.

We informed our design solution with past findings and observations about older users’ interaction challenges and changes in abilities. For example, it has been suggested that the failure to notice UI components at certain parts of the screen may stem from older adults’ narrow useful field of view (UFOV) and difficulty rapidly shifting their UFOV [ 69 ]. Or it could be because as we age, our fluid intelligence declines and so increases the difficulty in processing multiple information cues in parallel [ 21 , 44 ]. In a similar vein, post-study interviews found that older users tend to only look for features where they would expect them to be without scanning the current view comprehensively [ 42 ]. Because of these existing observations, we decided to stay away from aural guidance which would be ephemeral and could be easily missed. However, that meant adding visual cues to an already busy UI of a feature-rich interface, which could add clutter and hinder rather than better user experiences. We plan to test different visual feedback designs in a future study to address this problem.

Results from our design probe study found 13/15 users used the voice assistant (VA). Non-use might have been a personal preference, e.g., P7 mentioned post-study that "Talking to a machine always makes me cringe." . Of the users who used the VA, 11/13 used it only when they were lost during a task. The two participants who preemptively used the VA reported a lot of enthusiasm about the technology in the post-study interview, e.g., P2 said, "I was not sure whether it understood my question, but it actually worked!" and P5 said, "It's nice that it reduces my options" . When lost, 63% of the time users used the voice assistant to ask a question. When a voice assistant was used after getting lost, 77% of the time users were able to recover immediately and successfully complete the task. This solves a hard problem reported in the literature: once lost, older adults can keep going back and forth on the same interaction flow until they are frustrated enough to give up (Figure 3, [ 69 ]).

5.2 Research Implications

Older adults are rarely adopting simplified, senior-purpose apps [ 62 , 65 ]. They want to use technologies with family and friends across generations. They also would prefer to learn about new technology from and with family and friends [ 28 , 45 ]. Yet, depending on their technology proficiency, older users need support—not in terms of better manuals, but just-in-time, just-in-place. Assistance, which not only supports older users with a task at hand but also helps them learn and become better at using sophisticated applications. We distill three research implications from our studies.

5.2.1 Design considerations for the visual output and voice input. First, any additional visual information on a UI would add to users’ cognitive load and could make older users inefficient while trying to improve learnability. How do we trade off between efficiency and learnability? The choice to offer three options was made to accommodate algorithmic errors but users may want the correct answer right away, e.g., P5 said, "It's nice that it reduces my options, but I wish it could give you a clear answer." . Understanding how much user control and freedom could be given up to improve efficiency will depend on future advances in natural language processing and algorithmic robustness. Future studies will also need to examine the shape and size of visual emphasis to be offered on the UI and how that affects user experience. While from our study, it appeared that simple shapes like circles, rectangles, and arrows are sufficiently helpful, it remains to be investigated if additional cues like instructions snippets would be more helpful.

Second, voice input is not without its challenges—e.g., problems include knowing what to say, how to say [ 22 ], whether the input was correctly recognized (P2: "I was not sure whether it understood my question, but it actually worked!" ), difficulty in error recognition, and difficulty in error correction. Whether the usability challenges of voice assistants will reduce the possible benefit of our design solution remains to be seen in a future in-the-wild study.

5.2.2 Learning while doing. A major limitation of our design solution is that we only provide relevant features on the current UI view. Scaling our design solution to multiple views without negatively affecting the user's orientation would be a challenge. In that case, the solution must help users understand how the transition happened between the user's current view to the final view.

Elsewhere, researchers have explored the concept of users teaching a virtual assistant to completely automate complex tasks on mobile devices by offering natural language instructions [ 4 , 39 ]. This learning-by-demonstration or programming-by-demonstration approach takes a user's natural language query (e.g., "I want to find a steakhouse in West loop" ) and automatically completes the task based on a prior similar task (e.g., a cafe in Hyde Park). While there are certainly benefits of automating similar tasks done using the same or similar mobile interfaces, our design objective is different. Rather than automating tasks and making the underlying interaction flow opaque , we are trying to make the interaction flow transparent and not only support older adults to troubleshoot but also learn how to use a feature-rich mobile application.

Indeed, in our WoZ experiment, we found that users indirectly learned from the output of the voice assistant (VA) and used them in consecutive tasks. Note that this was not our direct design goal. Furthermore, users mostly invoked the VA for help when lost in the interaction steps (Figure 4 ) which suggests that our design can help in the exploration of feature-rich mobile interfaces without first identifying and then prescribing a complete sequence of steps.

5.2.3 Supporting other types of interaction problems. In this paper, we were only concerned with designing a solution that could help older users with finding features, i.e., address their directed informational and navigational questions. However, another type of question among the top three frequently asked during interactions was validation-type questions. These types of articulations have also been reported in the literature when older users encountered interaction problems [ 17 , 69 ]. Furthermore, in our Study 3, one user specifically mentioned "It would be good if it answers the question when I ask if I'm doing well." This problem might be addressed by making users aware when they are getting lost, i.e., making repeated non-unique selections or off-task selections. Now that may also decrease user confidence and increase the chances of abandonment. Another design approach could be to help users arrive at a directional informational or navigational question when starting from a validation question through a sequence of question-answers with the voice assistant.

6 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we presented a taxonomy of questions that older users tend to ask when encountering interaction problems with feature-rich mobile apps. The three most common types of questions were validation, directed informational, and navigational. Older users asked significantly more UI-related questions than their younger counterparts. Building on that, we proposed a design solution to address the problem of quickly locating relevant UI features when exploring a feature-rich user interface (or answers to directed informational and navigational-type questions). In our proposed solution, the user could verbally ask a question to a voice assistant, which would then highlight up to three relevant features on the user's current page. In our design probe study, 87% of our participants used the voice assistant, mostly when getting lost during exploration, and were able to recover immediately and successfully complete the task 77% of the time assistance was requested.
