Empathic Accuracy and Mental Effort During Remote Assessments of Emotions

Stephan Huber , Psychological Ergonomics, Julius-Maximilians-Universität, Germany, stephan.huber@uni-wuerzburg.de
Natalie Rathß , Psychological Ergonomics, Julius-Maximilians-Universität, Germany, natalie.rathss@web.de

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3580824
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Observing users in remote settings is unfavorable because it adds filters altering the information that underlie judgement. Still, the COVID pandemic led to an unprecedented popularity of remote user experience tests. In this work, we revisited the question, which information is most important for evaluators to assess users’ emotions successfully and efficiently. In an online study, we asked N =55 participants to assess users’ emotions from short videos of 30 interaction situations. As independent variable, we manipulated the combination of the information channels video of users, video of the interactive technology, and audio within subjects. Our findings indicate that empathic accuracy is highest and mental effort is lowest when all stimuli are present. Surprisingly, empathic accuracy was lowest and mental effort highest, when only video of users was available. We discuss these findings in the light of emotion literature focusing on persons’ facial expressions and derive practical implications for remote observations.

1 INTRODUCTION

Since the onset of the COVID pandemic, remote communication via video platforms increased by more than factor 30 [ 16 ] and user experience practitioners conducted up to 90 % of their user tests remotely [ 3 ]. To maintain quality of research despite this development, HCI researchers and practitioners published success stories or guides on how to perform user research in remote settings. Related work comprises the adoption of existing methods to work in a remote setting [ 39 ; 80 ], the advice to entirely move studies to virtual environments [ 56 ], as well as recommendations for a desktop setup rich in hardware to facilitate participative involvement of people with dementia over distance [ 55 ]. Following a phenomenological investigation, Osler [ 57 ] claims that streams of video or audio are sufficient to empathize with the user. So far, it is unclear whether her abilities generalize to other researchers and no reductionist approaches have been made to thoroughly evaluate the data quality achievable through remote methods. In this work, we address this research gap and contribute observers’ empathic accuracy and mental effort when assessing emotions in remote settings from audio and video of users and their interface.

Conducting user research in context and face to face gives us subjective, yet direct impressions of users’ body posture, as well as facial and vocal expressions that allow us to infer their emotions. When switching to a remote setup, external factors that corrupt the transmission of sound or picture may limit the quality of a video conference. Consequently, the researcher's impression of the user's emotional experience is biased (Figure 1 ). In this work, we pursue the overarching research question under which conditions (i.e., lacking information) we can still trust the quality of remote results. Expressed more practically and in short, we strive to learn whether video streams of the users’ face, video material of the interactive technology under study, or the audio channel are most expendable. The guiding research questions were: (1) which channels give us the most accurate impression of users’ emotions and (2) at what cost with respect to observers’ mental effort. We conducted an online study where we controlled for factors on the user side (emotional expressivity, light & noise condition, hardware quality, degree of data compression; Figure 1 .1-4) and asked participants taking the role of user researchers to optimize factors on their side (Figure 1 .5-7). In theory, this makes all remaining differences in emotion recognition attributable to perceptual abilities and approximates an upper limit for each condition. To set the frame for this research, in the next paragraphs we begin with definitions and associations between user experience, emotions and empathy.

1.1 Definitions of user experience and emotion

User experience is described by theorists as either holistic with intertwined threads [ 31 ; 81 ] or reductionist with separately measurable components [ 74 ] or qualities [ 18 ; 34 ]. The uniting part of these conceptions of user experience is, that they entail emotions as a central part [ 35 ; 74 ; 81 ]. From the perspective of user experience, an emotion is one result of an interaction that, for example, satisfied a need [ 17 ; 33 ]. In contrast to the underlying need, an emotional reaction is directly observable from users’ behavior and utterances. Thus, starting from an observation of users’ behavior or utterances and in order to gain an understanding of users’ underlying emotional experience, researchers deploy specific interview techniques to dig up the foundations of user reactions [ 2 ]. Follow up questions to understand observed behavior can be posed and answered directly in context [ 40 ] or from videotaped interactions in the aftermath, following up on timestamps set by users [ 10 ] or researchers [ 41 ]. While definitions of emotions vary as well, affective psychologists agree upon three aspects that are critical to user research:

  • In contrast to mood, emotions can be attributed to an identifiable trigger [ 67 ].
  • The experiencing person expresses their emotions which makes them observable from facial movement, which we refer to as facial expression [e.g., 26 ], body posture [e.g., 49 ] and audible from vocal notions of the voice [ 68 ].
  • Similar to mood, emotions can affect behavior or decision making [ 66 ; 71 ].

Affect – the feeling component of emotions that lacks reflective aspects [ 65 ] and hence serves as umbrella term for all affective phenomena [ 66 ]– is intentionally left out of our study since there is no way of measuring it from observations alone. One common way of documenting and communicating emotions are categories with descriptive names [ 23 ; 24 ]. Some authors map those categories based on shared dimensions such as valence and arousal [ 82 ], intensity [ 59 ; 72 ], or undisclosed dependencies [ 13 ]. Advocating for dimensionality within or between emotions may help to describe dependencies but still allows for a distinction of emotion categories. Hence, for the sake of simplicity, we stick to categories in this work. Having summarized definitions of emotions and their centrality to conceptions of user experience brings us to the more practical challenge of how to recognize them in users.

1.2 Cognitive empathy

Scientific interest in the human ability to perceive and recognize emotions in other people reaches back to Darwin [ 15 ]. Over the past decades, psychologists found evidence for the recognition of basic emotions across cultures [ 24 ] and contexts [ 14 ], thus supporting the evolutionary argument for innate abilities. Today, social psychologists title this ability as emotion recognition ability [ 70 ] or cognitive empathy [ 51 ]. As a personality trait, cognitive empathy has shown to be stable over time [ 61 ] and compromised rather by disinterest than increased age [ 62 ]. The ability to recognize emotions is best contextualized “as sensory-cognitive ability that is distinct from, yet related to, intelligence” [ 70 ]. On a situational level, we are better in recognizing emotions if the other person has the same cultural background [ 53 ] or is otherwise familiar and the trigger of the emotion is known [ 21 ]. Observations of participants who mirrored their conversation partners’ smile already within 200 milliseconds – an interval too short to be explained through a reaction – hint towards an anticipation component [ 37 ; 63 ]. Cues for anticipation again may be drawn from the content of speech or expected behavior of familiar conversation partners [ 37 ]. In sum, the common understanding is that apart from generally paying attention to the situation, observers’ judgment of other humans’ emotions benefits from familiarity of the partner and information about their context.

Measuring cognitive empathy as a personality trait has been primarily researched for diagnosing cognitive impairments and affective spectrum disorders such as autism [e.g., 32 ; 36 ]. For the determination of the entire spectrum of cognitive empathy, behavioral operationalizations are established [ 6 ]. More central to our work than the trait of cognitive empathy is empathic accuracy. Empathic accuracy is a term that has been used in several fields of study (and was also challenged [ 7 ]). Our definition and operationalization are in line with “perceivers' accuracy […] in inferring the emotional state(s) of one or more target persons” (p.588) in a specific situation [ 44 ] but does not go as far as guessing another persons’ momentary thoughts, needs, personality traits [ 36 ] or accurately name the triggers of emotions [ 54 ]. In terminology of user research, empathic accuracy operationalizes the effectiveness of emotion recognition (“accuracy and completeness with which users achieve specified goals” [ 1 ]).

How does this translate to remote settings? Borrowing from a phenomenological perspective again, recognizing (or feeling) another persons’ emotions solely via chat is harder, because we lack a perception of their reactions in bodily presence [ 73 ]. Consequentially, our perception of other persons’ emotions should improve the closer we come to bodily presence, that is, the more sensory information we perceive. In addition to perceiving our counterpart's bodily signals, Eickers and Prinz [ 21 ] argue about real life encounters, that it helps to know the trigger of an emotion. In line with those indications for the benefit of more detailed information, we follow the assumption the more information the better for empathic accuracy:

H1. Participants have the highest empathic accuracy when they have access to the combination of all three information channels user, sound, and user interface .

While most studies on empathic accuracy focus on (partial) facial expressions presented in isolation, Barrett, Adolphs, Marsella, Martinez and Pollak [ 7 ] highlight the criticality of context for understanding an emotion. They argue that unlike fingerprints, facial expressions are ambiguous signals that become unreliable when observed in isolation. Neuroscientific findings support this perspective by showing that human observers combine facial expressions with their context already in early processing stages [ 64 ]. Israelashvili et al. [ 45 ] even found that in selected mixed-emotions scenarios, participants were not able to identify a happy emotion from video recorded faces if the context was hidden. In our simulated remote setup, having access to context information means being able to see the user interface. This leads us to the following second hypothesis for empathic accuracy in remote settings:

H2. Participants have a higher empathic accuracy when they can see the user interface .

Whereas empathic accuracy translates to the effectiveness of emotion recognition, we are interested in exploring participants’ mental workload during this task as well, representing the efficiency (“resources used in relation to the results achieved” [ 1 ]). Apart from the null hypothesis that the amount of available information has no impact on subjective workload there is evidence in literature that could point towards an increase or a decrease of workload. Dyck and Denver [ 20 ] trained deaf children in recognizing emotions over several sessions. While the children's comprehension and emotion related vocabulary improved, their accuracy or speed in emotion recognition did not. This hints towards an inefficiency that increases workload if one channel is impacted. In turn, having all information available should not only produce the best but also the most efficient outcome as observers can select critical cues by themselves from the channels of their choice. On the other hand, the multiple resource model by Wickens [ 79 ] suggests that processing perceptual information of two visual foci (face and context) competes for resources. Depending on the task, shared attention resources can compete between sensory modalities [ 78 ]. Shared resources to allocate between user, interface, and sound, as well as the additional task of attentional resource allocation would add to the workload. We stick with the resource driven line of argument and hypothesize with respect to mental workload:

H3. Participants experience the highest mental workload when processing the combination of all three information channels user, sound, and user interface .

2 METHOD

At the time of data collection, lab studies that would have increased the level of control were advised against due to the pandemic. Therefore, to test our hypotheses on empathic accuracy and mental effort, we conducted an online study. In short, we asked participants to rate emotions based on their perception of video from the user, the user interface and sounds resulting from that interaction.

2.1 Design

The online study followed a within-subjects design with the factor information on user manipulated on six levels. In detail, we presented the three independent variables User, Sound, and Interface in the following six combinations: User-only, User-Sound, User-Interface, User-Sound-Interface, Sound-only, Sound-Interface; examining the Interface alone was left out on purpose because when documenting emotions based on observations of a context without any visual or auditory cues of the user, one measures merely the evaluators’ own emotional associations with this context but no user emotions. For the definition of interface, we oriented on video conference settings. In order to convey a visual or tangible user interface, a screen needs to be shared or an additional webcam positioned to show the interaction. Hence, in this study we define shared screens and videorecorded robots as the “user interface”. In a video conference, audible aspects of the interface (e.g., game sounds or text to speech), would be captured by the microphone anyway and transmitted along with vocal user expressions.

Due to limited availability of emotional video material, we restricted the choice to the six observation categories anger, despair, fear, joy, pride, and no identifiable emotion. Our two dependent variables were empathic accuracy, operationalized as the number of successfully recognized emotions and mental effort, operationalized as participant ratings on the Subjective Mental Effort Questionnaire (SMEQ, [ 84 ]). Additionally, we collected demographic data to describe our sample and captured participants’ cognitive empathy as well as information on their hardware setup to control for biasing effects.

2.2 Material

Adopting the methodology from emotion recognition research, we used video material of persons in their context but presented the two channels distinct [ 45 ]. We refrained from producing new material with actors who would artificially express a certain emotion. Instead, we searched appropriate video material on YouTube where users displayed genuine emotions during interactions with technology (see Figure 2 for an example), thus sampling stimulus material from publicly accessible interactions resembling mostly natural behavior. While this approach theoretically limited our control over video content opposed to self-produced, artificial scenes, it allowed us to choose from a large amount of publicly available material resembling natural behavior and included a range of user groups we would not have had access to. Our explorative search for emotional interactions produced primarily recordings from gamers who capture their screen as they play but also video recorded human robot or voice bot interactions. Most prevalent in this exploration were the categories anger, despair, fear, joy, and pride, to which we added instances of no identifiable emotion. All videos were in German, the prospective participants’ native language, and we cut the scenes into short sequences lasting between 4 and 19 seconds ( Mdn = 10 seconds, see Table 1 in the appendix). A full description of video sequences with translated transcriptions of audible content and links to original content are available in the online supplement. We did not intentionally vary scene length but restricted the stimulus material to show emotional scenes beginning with their trigger until the emotional reaction faded out, the scene was cut by the uploaders, or another trigger occurred. Consequentially, scene length varied as, for instance, the sight of a frightening robot or the realization of losing a game may evoke a clear and immediate negative reaction whereas extracting a slight notion of joy from a user's thinking aloud utterances may take multiple sentences and to distinguish pride from joy, insights on users’ self-achieved success need to be presented to observers. While this variation of stimulus length limits the experimental control, deploying a range of situationally occurring emotions come with variations of length and intensity approximates facial and vocal expressions during real-time remote user studies and thus increases ecological validity [ 7 ; 47 ] and generalizability.

With respect to psychometric instruments, we used the German translation [ 22 ] of the SMEQ [ 84 ]. The SMEQ prompts participants to self-report their perceived mental effort for the previously completed task on a visual scale from 0-220, discretized in steps of 20. Additionally, we deployed the German version [ 58 ] of the Eyes-Test [ 6 ] as a validated measure to check for participants’ empathic abilities. The Eyes-Test operationalizes cognitive empathy, that is a person's ability to recognize emotions from another person's facial expressions. The Eyes-Test owes its name to the set of 36 pairs of clipped photographs revealing only the area around human eyes, from which participants must identify the depicted person's emotion out of four suggestions per item. An equivalent test for measuring cognitive empathy from voice samples exists and moderately correlates with the Eyes-Test [ 32 ]. We refrained from its usage, because it is optimized for diagnostic purposes and to the best of our knowledge has not been translated into German.

2.3 Procedure

Data collection took place between January and February 2022. After registering for a slot in the participation administration system, participants received a personal link to the online study platform Gorilla (Cauldron Ltd, Cambridge, UK). On the landing page of the online study, we thanked participants for their interest and communicated them a short description, the procedure and approximate duration of the study. Each participant signed informed consent. To familiarize participants with the online study interface, data collection started with demographical data.

In the first part of the experiment, each participant completed the Eyes-Test. Following one instructive item, the 36 test items occurred in randomized order. In the main part of the experiment, we asked participants to view short videos of user-technology-interactions and after each video rate the user's emotions. At the beginning, we presented participants with an exemplary video clip and asked them to optimize their audio- and screen settings once but then not alter them over the course of the study. We also warned participants that some videos were intentionally without sound or image material. Our recommendation was to complete the study on a desktop setup and use headphones for optimal conditions. Videos were only playable once and participants had to start them manually. After viewing the video, participants entered their ratings as single choice item from six predefined categories, including “no emotion recognizable”. Participants saw each scene only once and received no feedback regarding the correctness of their response. Instead, we asked them after each video to rank their mental effort on the SMEQ by drawing a visual mark with their cursor on a vertical scale. Each participant viewed 30 clips in total (5 videos per condition). The order of conditions and videos was randomized, and the entire study took approximately 40 minutes.

2.4 Participants

We recruited N = 55 participants (8 male) over the local university's pool without setting further constraints or preconditions. Their mean age was 20.73 years ( SD = 2.15), ranging from 18-30 years. They were enrolled in degree courses of media communication (48) or HCI (7) and participated in exchange for course credit. All participants ran the online study with desktop operating systems and the logged resolution of their screens ranged from 1024 x 768 pixels to 2560 x 1440 pixels with a mode (n=14) of 1440 x 900 pixels.

3 RESULTS

Aim of the study was to examine the effect of information availability in stimulus material on empathic accuracy and perceived effort to recognize emotions. Data were preprocessed in Microsoft Excel and all statistical tests were calculated with JASP (Version 0.16.3, Amsterdam, Netherlands) against an α of .05.

3.1 Eyes-Test

One participant's score in the Eyes-Test lay 3.09 standard deviations below the mean. Since her data with respect to other dependent variables was inconspicuous, we did not exclude her from further analysis. The other n= 54 participants recognized M = 25.85 items correctly ( SD = 3.15), with female participants scoring descriptively higher ( M = 26.11, SD = 3.08) than male participants ( M = 24.38, SD = 3.38), t (52) = 1.45, p = .15. As these numbers are in line with a standardized sample of the respective age group by [ 50 ], we can uphold the assumption that our participants were empathically capable and proceed with the data analysis.

3.2 Check of Manipulation and Assumptions

Since video material had been categorized by researchers (“expert ratings”) not users, we firstly inspected the difficulty of cue material for the participants of our study. Given the 6 predefined categories, chance alone explains 17% of correct choices. Hence, we examined items, which were more difficult than .2 across all emotion categories and conditions. This led to the exclusion of one item each from the categories pride and despair, which were hardly recognized by participants, even when all cues were present (see Table 1 in the appendix). Scene length did not significantly correlate with item difficulty ( r = -.27, p = .17) or perceived workload ( r = -.22, p = .26). According to Shapiro-Wilk tests, the assumption of normal distribution cannot be upheld across all conditions. We still report ANOVAs for our three main hypotheses for three reasons. First, ANOVAs have shown to be robust to non-normally distributed data in terms of Type I errors [ 8 ]. Second, the assumption of sphericity could be upheld. And third, exploratively conducted non-parametric tests did not change the direction of outcomes or significance for any test.

3.3 Empathic accuracy

For each condition, we computed the mean of correctly identified emotions over the five [four] video clips. A repeated measures ANOVA on the consolidated data indicated a significant difference of mean empathic accuracy between conditions, F (5, 270) = 2.6, p = .024, η² p = .05. Regarding our first hypothesis, there was a statistically significant difference in empathic accuracy scores between the User-Sound-Interface condition ( M = .68, SD = .24, Range = [.2; 1.0]) and all other conditions ( M = .57, SD = .08) of .11 ( SE = .04), p = .004. Explorative Bonferroni post-hoc comparisons indicate that the contrast to the User-Sound-Interface condition was largest and statistically significant ( t (54) = 3.2, p bonf = .023, d = .63) in the condition where only the user was visible ( M = .52, SD = .23), followed by the condition where only sound was audible ( M = .55, SD = .25), t (54) = 2.62, p bonf = .14, d = .52.

With respect to our second hypothesis, empathic accuracy scores were only descriptively higher in conditions where the user interface was visible ( M = .61, SD = .13) in comparison to when it was not ( M = .57, SD = .12), .04 ( SE = .03), p = .13. Adding participants’ score achieved in the Eyes-Test and their screen resolution as covariates did not change the main effect of condition, F (5, 265) = 2.56, p = .027, η² p = .05. Neither did the score of the Eyes-Test, participants’ screen resolution or any of the interaction effects indicate significance, all F < 1. Descriptive values are visualized in Figure 3 and textually presented in Table 2 in the appendix.

3.4 Subjective effort

For each condition, we extracted the effort of accurately recognizing an emotion. We computed subjective effort as the mean over the SMEQ values reported for each of the videos, including only values where emotions were accurately identified. On the consolidated data, we calculated a repeated measures ANOVA. Since six participants did not accomplish to identify at least one emotion in each condition, cells remained empty, and we had to base the calculation on n = 49 participants. Results indicated a statistically significant difference of subjective mental effort between conditions, F (5, 240) = 8.33, p < .001, η² p = .15. There was a non-significant difference in subjective mental effort ratings between the User-Sound-Interface condition ( M = 52.69, SD = 39.55) and all other conditions ( M = 61.94, SD = 29.19) of -9.26 ( SE = 5.18), p = .075. All descriptive values are visualized in Figure 3 and textually presented in Table 2 in the appendix.

When adding screen resolution and the Eyes-Test score as covariates, the main effect of condition is no longer significant, F (5, 230) = 2.22, p = .054, η² p = .046. Instead, screen resolution appears to have a significant main effect between participants, F (1, 46) = 10.01, p = .003. The between effect of Eyes-Test is not significant, F (1, 46) = 1.70, p = .2, and neither are the interactions condition × screen, F (5, 230) = 2.14, p = .06, or condition × Eyes-Test, F (5, 230) = 1.07, p = .2. The visualization of descriptive data in Figure 4 depicts how the participants with the highest screen resolution reported systematically higher SMEQ values than participants with lower screen resolution.

3.5 Explorative analysis

Exploratively correlating our dependent variables indicated no general relationship between empathic accuracy and subjective effort, r = .23, p = .09. When distinguishing by emotion, the relationship between empathic accuracy and subjective effort closest to significance occurs during the identification of fearful user emotions ( r = .25, p = .07). Whereas subjective effort correlated moderately across all emotion categories ( r = .36-.58, all p < .018) except for pride × despair, no such relationship was observable for empathic accuracy across emotion categories. Finally, there were not any correlations of age with performance, neither in empathic accuracy ( r = -.18, p = .2) nor subjective effort ( r = .09, p = .51).

Further descriptive exploration revealed that not all emotions were named equally often. For all six emotion categories we presented five videos each to 55 participants, resulting in 5×55= 275 videos per category [and 220 for the two categories where we excluded one cue each post experiment due to their difficulty]. Our observation is, that participants’ false responses are descriptively not equally distributed across all categories. As can be seen in Figure 5 , the categories despair and joy were evoked more frequently than others. The detailed data in Table 3 in the appendix reveals that the most common error was misjudging the category pride as joy. Looking into the raw data, this happened across all conditions, from 18 occurrences when only sound was audible to 28 occurrences when only the user was visible.

4 DISCUSSION

When observing users in remote settings, technical constraints can limit the availability of sound or image material and bias the researcher's impression which ultimately confines data quality. To estimate the boundaries of empathizing with users remotely, we conducted an online study in which we tested empathic accuracy and mental effort during the assessment of user emotions. To our knowledge, this is the first work (1) considering the effect of context awareness in emotion recognition and (2) specifically focusing on the application domain of remote user research. In our study, participants were assigned the role of observing user researchers and had to extract emotions from short videos of interaction situations. We manipulated the degree of information available in the videos within participants. Confirming our first hypothesis, participants had a higher empathic accuracy when they had access to the combination of all three information channels user, sound, and user interface. Non-significant trends in the data of all conditions indicate that participants’ empathic accuracy gradually increased with the information they had. The effect was stable with respect to the possible covariates of participants’ cognitive empathy and the screen resolution of their device. Against the assumption of our second hypothesis, the visibility of the user interface had no significant effect on emotion recognition.

Regarding our third hypothesis on subjective effort, our results are inconclusive. Contrary to our expectation, participants did not experience the highest mental effort when processing the combination of all three information channels. Descriptively, users’ mental workload was even highest when focusing solely on the users’ facial expressions. However, in the light of a between-subjects effect caused by participants’ screen resolution, all trends of mental effort in our data need to be considered with care. One explanation is that the screen resolution had a direct effect on how the video material was displayed. Since the difference is also visible in the condition where only sound was available – on which the screen resolution should not have any impact – but screen resolution did not impact empathic accuracy it is also possible that an unknown variable moderated the effect. For instance, participants still operating on a laptop with a resolution of 1440 x 900 pixels or less might be more tolerant with respect to subjective mental effort. To eradicate uncertainties on mental effort during emotion recognition, we plan to repeat the study in a controlled environment as soon as pandemic restrictions allow.

4.1 Implications

Our study confirmed that for the most accurate impression of users, observers should exploit all the information channels they can access. With pre-selected video material, we optimized conditions for the user side from emotional expressivity to video and audio quality, which resulted in empathic accuracy rates of 68 % on average, ranging from 20–100 % accuracy for individuals. This compares well to empathic accuracy ratings from studies that lay slightly lower but exceeded judgments of users’ emotional reactions to include the naming of triggers [ 12 ; 53 ; 54 ]. Even in suboptimal conditions with only one of three information channels available, our participants inferred at least 52 % of emotions correctly. This implies that untrained observers can recognize user emotions even in suboptimal conditions way above chance.

4.1.1 Emotions are more than facial expressions. Emotion recognition literature mostly focuses on facial expressions in behavioral observations [ 25 ] as well as neuroscience [ 29 ]. Typically, authors use static or dynamic image material of facially expressed basic emotions. Even at “full intensity”, recognition rates may range from 45-98% [ 4 ]. Experiments showed that seeing a person's body posture contributes to emotion recognition as well [ 76 ]. Interestingly, in our data participants were descriptively worse in rating users’ emotions from their face and body-language than from the sound of their voice while reporting a higher mental effort. This implies the vastly underestimated role of sound for emotion recognition. In contrast to our findings that hint to the importance of audio, Bänziger et al. [ 5 ] found that meaningless audio resembling utterances in an unknown language hardly increased the recognition rate when presented together with video and is even worse than still images when presented alone. A reason for the low performance of audio in their study may be that without understanding the content of language, prosodic qualities alone are not sufficient to support empathic accuracy. Isolating prosodic cues teaches us about human processing of emotions, however, the more authentic cues used in our study may better translate to practical implications for emotion recognition. After all, healthy adults can process both aspects of speech in parallel, the emotional content as well as the intonation [ 32 ].

It is known, that the abilities to recognize emotions from audio and visual cues correlate [ 9 ] and likely share related skills or even a common resource [ 32 ; 69 ]. We know from research with deaf children that the absence of sound perception impairs emotion recognition to a level that cannot be compensated through visual training [ 20 ]. On the other side of the sensory ability spectrum, air traffic controllers who only have a radio connection into the cockpit can infer the emotional state of pilots from the tacit information on this audio channel [ 42 ]. Future work could investigate how emotions are recognized from various constellations of audio and visual channels in less extreme environments.

4.1.2 The role of sound. Field studies show that only a fraction of a person's emotion is reflected in their facial expressions [ 28 ]. Therefore, perceiving a persons' facial expressions is not sufficient to infer an emotion and our results support higher empathic accuracy (effectiveness) when sound is available. Our non-significant findings do not allow for a conclusive statement on the direct comparison between video conferencing and pure audio signals regarding emotion transmission. However, descriptive trends hint towards an advantage of two channels over one, which should be further examined in future work. The model by Young et al. [ 83 ] in which voice and facial cues equally contribute to affect analysis may serve as a useful theoretical foundation. In terms of efficiency, neuro-scientific approaches suggest that extracting emotions from voices “promote[s] higher order perceptual and evaluative processing” [ 68 , p11]. However, according to our results, this cognitive extra effort is not expressed in participants’ ratings of perceived workload, which is a strong argument for providing the extra information. Despite these advantages, so far, the potential of sound in conveying and inferring emotions has not been fully exhausted in HCI. For instance, in interfaces whose main purpose is to transmit emotions, voice interaction takes only the third place behind haptic interaction and visual output (with non-speech sound following on the sixth [ 52 ]).

Towards the automatic recognition of emotions from voice, the last years have seen remarkable progress in arranging voice samples along dimensions (positive/negative sentiment) or categorizing them, for instance through directly analyzing parameters of voice signals [ 47 ], machine learning approaches or entropy [ 38 ]. A recent study even achieved accuracies of 61 % in the categorization of voices from TV debates – mostly due to high performance in the emotion category “calm” that made up almost three quarters of the data [ 19 ]. In the future, joining recognition and documentation abilities of automated systems and human researchers may yield the best results. For that goal, our understanding of which parameters can be better detected by machines and which combinations – that for instance occur only rarely and are thus not suitable for systems requiring large amount of training data – can be better judged by humans. In terms of practical implications beyond user research, beneficiaries of increased effectivity in emotion recognition from voice samples may be users of telephone hotlines that aim to support mental health [ 43 ] and require high levels of empathy on the provider side [ 46 ].

4.1.3 The role of context. Young, Frühholz and Schweinberger [ 83 ] argue from an evolutionary perspective that emotion is always ambiguous and the more information we have about our counterpart and their situation, the better for our understanding of their emotions. Knowledge about the trigger may improve empathic accuracy [ 21 ]. A reason for this phenomenon is, that an observer's affective state triggered by the observation of certain interactions may cause an egocentric bias when judging the observees emotion [ 75 ], also referred to as emotional contagion in theory of mind literature [ 60 ]. Therefore, if the cultural background and perspective on the interaction are in line with the observed user, shared knowledge of the context is beneficial for emotional inference. In our study, we did not ask participants to report their own affective reaction and, hence, are not able to determine egocentric bias. Due to the similarity of dispersion between conditions with and without information on the interface, we can only assume that our participants’ affective reaction to the interactive system was aligned with the users’ emotional response. Thus, one reasonable explanation for the descriptive improvement of empathic accuracy in the presence of interface information remains: participants were rather successful in classifying emotional reactions from facial or audio cues alone. While seeing the interface supported this process, the ambiguous situations were too scarce to make a significant difference. A significant benefit of context awareness might only show in more emotionally complex situations, where the critical cues for empathically deciphering the situation can only be found in context (for an example see the manipulation in [ 45 ]).

4.1.4 Complex emotions. The misjudgment of pride as joy is interesting, as it reveals a critical lack of knowledge about the users’ situation. Superficially, joy and pride cannot be distinguished in facial expressions. As the often-joyful consequence of achievement, pride is a rather complex emotion. To correctly infer pride in a user, observers require knowledge about the user's competences, motives, and goals. For practitioners, acquiring this level of insight involves guessing the user's thoughts [ 54 ] or simply asking the user [ 2 ]. Similarly, Chang-Arana et al. [ 11 ] warn that the borders of designer-user empathy need to be acknowledged. Our results give some descriptive statistical orientation of where those borders are for conclusions from observations alone. Thereby we want to highlight that we cannot just breathe the users and their context – empathic abilities are a start, but thorough questioning is crucial to validate seemingly intuitive impressions. Still, exploring interactions between emotion categories and available information channels are a promising anchor to foster the theoretical understanding of cognitive empathy and improve methods for user researchers.

4.2 Limitations

Our selection process and categorization of video material poses a limitation of internal validity. In contrast to fabricated material for experiments [ 76 ] or standardized tests (e.g., MERT [ 5 ]) our video material did not show actors whom we explicitly instructed to portray a certain emotion, which reduced our level of control. On the other hand, streamers filming themselves for the entertainment of viewers may not act entirely naturalistic. Towards optimizing generalizability, future work should source video material from real user research scenarios. Ideally, for capturing authentic emotions instead of acting, users need to be initially blinded to the purpose of the recording, which of course poses an ethical difficulty. For internal validity, critical situations in this material should then be validated by the users themselves before shown to observing participants, as has already been proposed by Ickes [ 44 ] for studies on empathic accuracy. Ickes [ 44 ] also lists caveats that may lead to biased judgements of emotions. He warns about the effects of historic or desired relationships between the observer and the user. While we did not check for these situations, they are unlikely due to our video selection process. Finally, Ickes [ 44 ] warns about observers’ deliberate inaccuracy in reporting – a possibility that we cannot rule out for individual items but that did not become apparent as systematic error in our data.

Our intention was to replicate participatory observations in real-time remote settings, where user researchers identify emotional reactions and directly validate their assumption about the situation with the users. Therefore, we chose to present videos to participants once and then prompt them to log their judgement of the most prevalent emotion observable. Results from the mode of video presentation and documentation do not generalize to studies involving hour-long multimodal analyses from video recordings applying several lenses [e.g., 48 ]. Additionally, the curated stimulus material in our simulated remote setup involved high levels of control over the data quality shared by the user (see filters 1-4 in Figure 1 ). Since such high levels of control over the remote setup are not always given, we possibly overestimated empathic accuracy. With the important role of sound for emotion inference in mind, future replications should instead of the Eyes Test [ 6 ] preferably deploy auditive instruments to check English speaking participants’ empathic abilities.

Participants stated their workload after each classified video. Under the assumption that participants honestly stated their perceived workload we dare to speculate on low workload serving as one indicator for the ease of recognizing emotions accurately. However, in some instances the low intensity of changes in facial, postural, and vocal expressions, ambiguity between channels or sequence effects may have complicated differentiation between emotions for participants, thus causing a higher workload. In these cases, it is unclear from our data whether participants ended their reflection process with an informed decision or a lucky guess. Unfortunately, we did not explicitly ask participants how certain they were with their observations, which should be done in future studies.

Another limitation in terms of generalization is the selection of the emotional categories joy, pride, anger, fear, and despair. Even though this selection represents the most prevalent emotions produced by our search for suitable video material, the limited emotional range does not represent the rich variety and complexity of emotions evoked by human computer interaction. Our focus lay on emotions in timely proximity to their trigger. The short video sequences did not facilitate the inclusion of emotions that are caused by past technology use and arise later [ 77 ]. We also made no distinction between emotions that are directly induced by the interaction design versus emotions that emerge need satisfaction during the interaction [ 77 ]. Finally, observers’ ability to recognize and categorize an emotion in a user does not necessarily imply that they experience the emotion the same way [ 27 ]. Thus, observations should never replace users’ words but rather build the foundation to remind users of telling us about their experience in critical situations.

5 CONCLUSION

This is the first work quantifying the effect of context in emotion recognition that specifically focuses on the application domain of remote user research. Descriptively, sound was the most important channel for emotion recognition. Enriching information with visibility of the user and their interface enabled participants to accurately identify two thirds of displayed emotions. Interestingly, the visibility of users alone contributed least while descriptively causing the highest effort, which stands in contrast to the focus on facial expressions of emotion literature. Our results point towards tackling the underestimated and under-researched role that sound plays in human emotion recognition during situations of HCI in future work. For practitioners who cannot facilitate face-to-face encounters, our message is that remote sessions offer a promising alternative with respect to emotion recognition, if all three channels can be transmitted in high quality – the user, their interface, and apparently most important: sound!
