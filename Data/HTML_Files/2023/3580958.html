Design, Mould, Grow!: A Fabrication Pipeline for Growing 3D Designs Using Myco-Materials

There is a growing interest in sustainable fabrication approaches, including the exploration of material conservation and utilisation of waste materials. Particularly, recent work has applied organic myco-materials, made from fungi, to develop tangible, interactive devices. However, a systematic approach for 3D fabrication using myco-materials is under-explored. In this paper, we present a parametric design tool and a fabrication pipeline to grow 3D designs using the mycelia of edible fungi species, such as Reishi or Oyster mushrooms. The proposed tool is designed based on empirical results from a series of technical evaluations of the geometric and material qualities of 3D-grown myco-objects. Furthermore, the paper introduces an easy-to-replicate fabrication process that can recycle different organic waste material combinations such as sawdust and coffee grounds to grow mycelia. Through a series of demonstration applications, we identify the challenges and opportunities for working with myco-materials in the HCI context.

1 INTRODUCTION

With increasing scarcity of natural resources and environmental concerns, there is a growing interest in sustainable fabrication approaches such as the exploration of recyclable and organic materials. Sustainability has also been in focus in human-computer interaction (HCI) through several fronts, including studying sustainable behavior [ 27 ], developing persuasive technologies [ 56 ], investigating material conservation [ 24 ], re-usability in fabrication and prototyping [ 10 , 16 , 38 , 55 , 57 , 66 ] and using organic materials in designs [ 20 , 21 , 37 ]. Furthermore, there are compelling arguments made regarding the importance of sustainable interaction design [ 7 ]. For instance, organic materials such as fungi have shown to be very promising in tangible computing applications [ 60 , 61 ].

Fungi are our planet's mass-recycling system, able to reduce organic compounds into simpler forms that are usable in the broader ecosystem [ 52 ]. Particularly, some species of fungi will bind together raw organic matter in their network of interconnected hyphae (roots), creating structures known as mycelium networks. This natural process can be used to create new forms of organic materials referred to as myco-materials , which can be used to create rigid physical objects or flexible leather-like materials. Applications have demonstrated and exploited various “bio-affordances” [ 21 , 66 ] of fungi to produce sound and heat insulation, moulded forms, architectural materials, and animal-free leather alternatives [ 29 , 30 ]. Myco-materials can vary in texture, colour and appearance in ways that increase its applicability–even when a single species of mushroom is used, as is seen in Figure 1 B and C. Myco-materials are also being developed for mass-manufacture by Adidas for Stan Smith style shoes 1 and by Hermès for handbags 2 . These examples show that the designers can utilise myco-materials for highly desirable, luxury products [ 36 ].

In the context of HCI research and prototyping, myco-materials can create opportunities through their natural variation. For example, Vasquez et. al. demonstrated that the outer layer of mycelium can be utilised to create interactive, wearable accessories [ 60 , 61 ]. Furthermore, commercially available grow-at-home kits such as Ecovative 3 and recent work in HCI explore simple composites shapes of mycelium such as rectangular cuboid shapes [ 6 , 20 ]. However, a systematic exploration of how mycelium can be directly grown into 3D objects are under-explored, except in DIY community groups 4 . Even then, much of the work focuses on replicating lab conditions to grow mycelium and reverse-engineer commercial growth processes, rather than a reproducible design to fabrication pipeline. Furthermore, they lack a deeper understanding of the characteristics and limitations of the growth process, possible inclusions of other organic waste, and how they influence a given 3D design. Particularly in HCI context, an accessible workflow to translate a 3D design to a myco-object without expertise in mycelium growth, specialised equipment or materials is lacking.

In this paper, we present a reproducible approach for growing myco-materials into 3D objects from the design to fabrication. Using empirical results from a series of studies, we characterise the growing process of myco-materials and developed a 3D design tool as a plugin for the popular CAD software Rhino 3D 5 (see Figure 1 A). We further explored accessible organic waste material to demonstrate approaches to remanufacturing waste into myco-materials that grow around embedded electronics. The paper documents an easily reproducible fabrication process in an HCI lab without specialised lab conditions usually used in fungi research labs. We further present both qualitative and quantitative evaluation of the resulting designs compared to the original 3D models. To demonstrate uses of myco-materials, we present results from the 3D growing process (see Figure 1 B) and application examples of 3D objects grown using myco-materials are used for tangible and interactive applications. We believe the findings and insights of this paper will benefit future research in sustainable HCI application in personal fabrication, tangible interface and pervasive computing.

Contributions of this paper include:

  • A fabrication pipeline for design and growing 3D myco-objects in a HCI lab setting without specialised equipment and an exploration of accessible organic waste material combinations for remanufacturing.
  • Characterisation of the 3D myco-material growth process through empirical evaluations of the geometric and material properties, and developing a 3D design tool to assist the fabrication process using the results.
  • Demonstrations of approaches to utilise affordances of myco-material properties in interactive applications and evaluating their functionality.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Sustainability and HCI

Sustainable Interaction Design, or Sustainable Human Computer Interaction, was first discussed in 2007 [ 7 ], and in the following years has produced important discussion through workshops (such as [ 3 , 4 , 17 ]), design methods [ 49 , 62 ], examples of sustainable interactive designs (such as [ 5 , 18 , 61 , 64 ]), and connections to other sustainability movements in computing, such as Sustainable Digital Infrastructure [ 8 ].

To view HCI through a critical lens, researchers have used sustainability, the circular economy and responsible innovation to interrogate designs [ 3 , 8 , 13 , 62 ]. The goal of these is to consider how design could have a positive, even restorative, impact on the environment when analysing the design of an interactive object.

A recurring theme in the literature is that some projects, as well as some models of the circular economy, focus on changes in behaviour of the user [ 25 , 65 ] in a model of Sustainability Through Design [ 44 ]. This is where sustainable choices and decisions are supported through technology, and sustainable behaviour is encouraged by the designer [ 13 , 44 ], rather than solely focusing on the sustainability of the designs themselves. This approach is not all-encompassing, due to the complex challenges of sustainability [ 33 ],

Life-Centered Design approaches sustainability by expanding the stakeholders in design to include all peoples, animals, ecosystems and the planet [ 43 ]. Similarly the agency of mycelium in the growing process also captures the non-human role of the living designs [ 63 ]. The frameworks for achieving this are currently emerging, but they aim to support a thriving existence for all. These approaches represent a more-than human centered approach to designing interactive systems, and strive to represent the non-human concerns in the design process [ 58 ], as well as using circular and regenerative design approaches [ 19 , 32 ].

An alternative approach is to consider the sustainability of the material design, particularly related to energy and reduction of material impacts (reducing, reusing and recycling materials) [ 44 ] but also considering social and economic issues related to sustainable HCI [ 12 ]. Sustainable materials an area that is important for further research on sustainability in design. One study of millennial's attitudes towards sustainability showed positive responses [ 27 ]. Sustainability in design can take a circular approach by identifying sources of waste material, which can be given new economic value, and re-manufactured so that the material is kept in use.

2.2 Designing with Living Materials

Through biodesign [ 23 , 34 ] and bio-HCI [ 47 , 48 ], a range of different living organisms have been functionalised to operate as design materials or interactive systems. Living things have been applied to create novel concepts and designs for interactive textiles [ 66 ], growable robotics [ 46 ], bio-digital hybrid systems [ 50 ], sexual health kits [ 21 ], and for a range of fabrication methods, materials, sensors, actuators and logic processors that are enabled by advances in the field of synthetic biology [ 54 ]. The opportunities that are opened up by biodesign are exciting, and are an ongoing topic of discussion in HCI communities [ 21 , 22 ]. While synthetic biology may be out of reach for many HCI labs, myco-materials are a living material that requires less specialist knowledge to work with.

The evolutionary niche of some fungi is to break down lignocellulosic (wood-based) material into basic compounds, so that they can be reused in the natural environment. During this process, some species create a network of roots, called mycelia, that binds together the substrate material—a pile of leaves, or fallen tree in the wild—as it is digested. When provided with lignocellulosic waste material such as paper, cardboard or other organic waste, myco-material can be formed into desirable designs for a range of purposes. Some applications from research in engineering and architecture include alternative to synthetic foams, for packaging, acoustic or thermal foams, as well as fire-resistant materials, and panelling, flooring or furnishing [ 14 , 31 ], and promising alternatives to animal leather [ 29 ].

Myco-materials have been evaluated for tunable growth properties [ 26 ] and in comparison to synthetic materials [ 31 ], but designers also need to develop a tacit understanding of materials in order to apply them, and there is also a lack of software tools to help designers turn 3D designs into 3D objects using myco-materials. Using Material-Driven Design, by Karana et al. [ 35 ], designers can build material knowledge through: (1) understanding the material , where it is characterised according to its technical and experiential qualities; (2) creating a materials experience vision , where materials are considered relative to their function and user experience, and how it relates to the broader context of society and the planet; (3) manifesting materials experience patterns , through user testing, designers can understand perceived and actual interactions with the object through the materials; and (4) creating material and product concepts that can be developed through iterative evaluation.

In this paper, we present an investigation of the first two steps of the material-driven design process. We developed a pipeline for reliably producing and forming myco-materials, and evaluated the material for its technical and experiential qualities. Our materials experience vision is presented through speculative concepts for how myco-materials can be made interactive, using the properties of the material that we evaluated. We also present a software tool which assist to appropriate 3D designs into the myco-material based fabrication process.

2.3 Myco-materials in HCI

There are a limited, but growing, number of examples of myco-materials being used in HCI. Some of these projects are explorations during the development of a concept, or presented as final works.

The project Drone Chi [ 39 ] describes an interactive design process that explored the possibility of making an interactive drone from myco-materials. The authors described the texture as having an “earthy feel,” but did not develop this material into a prototype. The Myco-Accessories project by Lazaro Vasquez and Vega [ 61 ] created a mycelium “skin” as a medium for interactive wearables. This project is interesting as it involved removing the outer layer of mycelium that grows when a substrate is exposed to air, and forms a leathery layer, using a commercially available grow-it-yourself (GIY) kit 6 . Though the paper and the kit producers’ website 7 do not name the species used, this approach could be taken with reishi mushroom species used in our process, given the right conditions. This paper also describes the process of growing, drying and moisturising the material to give it leather-like qualities.

As a biodegradeable material, and using reprogrammable electronics, Myco-Accessories provides some insight into how sustainable GIY approaches could be used in a maker community. Several projects demonstrate electronics prototyping with biomaterials as an alternative to plastics [ 20 , 41 , 60 ]. In this project the authors present myco-materials as an alternative to plastic breadboards by carving the grown and dried myco-material using CNC routing and laser cutting. This approach allowed them to create housings for common electronics frameworks (Ardino Uno and BBC Microbit) when combined with 3D printed forms. Device housing was also explored in the Mycelium Artefacts project [ 64 ], to create disposable, low-fidelity enclosures for rapid prototypes, and demonstrates applicability of 3D printing to creating myco-materials.

The Material-Driven Design process was used with myco-materials, demonstrating an interesting applications of the material affordances in the project second skin : “it can grow into any shape; it has high insulation and protection qualities; it can be composted; and it makes you pluck and pick it.” [ 36 , p.130]. Using this materials experience, the designers created a packaging concept where a wine bottle was entirely cased in mycelium.

These examples show an initial exploration for interaction with digital systems and the myco-material itself. We will extend these examples by providing a detailed process of designing for myco-materials and present new ways that they can be applied, based on the properties of the material that we were able to grow.

3 FABRICATION METHOD

This section outlines our workflow for growing myco-materials. We start with an overview of fungi used and the process followed in a biology lab, how we transitioned to an HCI lab context, and a step-by-step description of the process.

3.1 Fungal Species and Characteristics

The mushroom species and growth substrate used to make myco-materials has a strong influence on the material properties, such as compressive and tensile strength [ 15 ], it is important to understand different species and their suitability in HCI applications. Over thirty different fungal species have been used in the past for development of myco-materials [ 9 ]. Commonly used fungi are all wood-degrading Basidiomycetes such as oyster mushroom ( Pleurotus spp. ), turkeytail ( Trametes versicolor ), polypore ( Fomes fomentarius ) and reishi ( Ganoderma lucidum ).

These species differ in the types of hyphae, the branching filaments that make up the mycelium (root network) of the fungus. All these species contain generative hyphae, which are responsible for development of fruiting bodies (the mushroom). Many wood-growing fungi also contain skeletal hyphae, which are thick walled and provide additional strength to the mycelium. Trimitic fungi also contain binding hyphae, which are highly branched and increase the compressive strength of the mycelium. Since the strength of 3D objects is an important factor to consider, we chose to work primarily with the trimitic fungus. We ultimately chose a reishi variant, Ganoderma steyaertanum , since it is fast-growing, generates a strong mycelium, and does not rapidly form the edible fruiting bodies under the growth conditions we were able to provide [ 67 ].

3.2 Growing Process in the Mushroom Lab

To understand the established growing processes for fungi in mushroom labs, we conducted several growing-cycles at a research mushroom lab. Our goal was to identify critical factors that affect mushroom growth that would present challenges in the growing process when transitioning to our HCI lab. We conducted two growing sessions with the assistance of microbiologists from the Marsh Lawson Mushroom Research Centre at the University of Sydney.

The growing process involves four main material types: 1) the mushroom species , usually purchased as a grain inoculated with the desired mushroom species; 2) substrate which the mycelium grows on and binds together; 3) supplements that act as fertiliser for healthy growth; and 4) inclusions , any other material that can be added to change properties of the final 3D object.

3.2.1 Materials. Our first trial included two mushroom species , turkeytail and oyster; three types of sawdust, Pine, Oregon Ash wood and Blackwood ( Acacia melanoxylon ) used as a substrate ; and included 5, 10 and 15% nutrient, nitrogen-rich soy meal supplement by weight. We did not use any inclusions at this stage. The first trial had 18 sample conditions. For our second trial, we used reishi mushroom species along with turkeytail and oyster, and only used Blackwood as substrate . We used the same supplement but added straw as an inclusion (75%, 50% and 25%). The second trial had 27 sample conditions. Table 1 outlines the material choices for trials.

3.2.2 Process. The spawn was millet grain inoculated with the mushroom fungi, purchased from a commercial supplier for the mushroom lab. For the substrate, we collected sawdust from our school workshop, collected through the extractor system from the Planer Thicknesser machines. Pieces of sawdust were consistently sized, approximately 10mm 2 × 1mm thick.

The first step after securing materials to autoclave the substrate, a common process in biological sciences, which involves using heat and pressure to pasteurise the material. This will kill any dangerous pathogens or wild mould (which the mushroom growers call weeds) on the substrate which could be harmful to human health if able to flourish. The sawdust was then hydrated to 65% by weight (assuming that the wood was 10% water) and left overnight to absorb the added water. The following day, 5% by dry sawdust weight of grain spawn was added to the mixture, as well as the nitrogen supplement, which acts like a fertiliser. We used separate mixtures with supplement 5, 10 and 15% by dry sawdust weight. We used plastic takeaway containers to grow the mushrooms, which were sterilised using ethanol to prevent mould contamination. Mixtures were put in the containers and placed in growth chambers , which are small rooms with controlled levels of CO 2 , humidity, temperature and light. The samples were grown under “spawn run” conditions with CO 2 at high levels (20%), 95% humidity, 25ºC in darkness as specified in [ 51 , p. 191]. This is to mimic the underground conditions, so that the mushroom produces mycelia to bind the substrate, instead of attempting to produce the fruit (the edible mushroom). After 2 weeks, the colonised mushrooms were taken out of the containers and dried in a very low oven (varying between 50-80ºC).

3.2.3 Results. Our first trial was largely unsuccessful. After 2 weeks in the takeaway containers, only 3 of the 18 samples successfully grew on the sawdust and were dried to make a partially-completed form. Samples failed by being unable to fully colonise the substrate sawdust, particularly for Oregon wood. We also observed wild mould growth as well as the desired mushroom fungus. The three successful samples were all on blackwood, but after 2 weeks, they were only partly colonised, and were fragile once dried, which is undesirable for our purposes.

In our second trial reishi showed the most promise (Table 1 ), producing a thick layer of mycelium around the outside of the container, with the internal network of mycelium able to hold the objects together after they were dried. This test also showed a thicker mycelium layer on the sample with the best coverage being reishi mushroom on a substrate with 75% blackwood and 25% straw. This is a very desirable outcome. Contributing to the success of this trial was the straw inclusion since it is easily digested by the mushroom than the blackwood, which provided nutrients to then consume the blackwood.

3.3 Growing Designs Workflow

This section outline our workflow for growing myco-materials in our HCI lab. We start by identifying key challenges and alternative approaches we followed with a step-by-step guide to help replicate the process.

3.3.1 Challenges in the HCI lab. Reflecting on the process of growing in the mushroom lab, we identified three key challenges that need to be addressed to replicate the process outside the biology lab. First, We needed an alternative approach for the autoclave to pasteurise the substrate which is important to ensure that no pathogenic mould grows. The initial step of inoculating the substrate with the mushroom is where the greatest risk of infection lies, as it is only after the substrate is fully colonised, that the healthy mushroom mycelia will best compete against wild mould for the nutrients in the substrate [ 67 ]. As an alternative approach, we could have used a pressure cooker to pasteurise the substrate, but we decided to use commercially available “grow kits”; pre-colonised mushroom and substrate that is commercially available from suppliers. This is ultimately more expensive, but we found the cost to still be reasonable for our purposes, as we are not mass-producing myco-materials, and pre-colonised kits have much lower risk of infection, even with inclusions, leading to less waste.

The second challenge we faced was to reduce the risk of wild mould from contaminating the samples during the growth phase. In addition to using pre-inoculated grow kits, we adopted the following strategies: use ethanol or sanitising wipes to create a sterile work area, anything that touches this area must be sanitised before coming into contact with it, including gloved hands, scales, containers, utensils, outer packaging of grow kits; to minimise risk of infecting our grow kit, any spilled substrate was discarded, rather than being placed back into the bag with substrate that may be used in the future; any inclusions should be boiled and allowed to cool before adding to the substrate. Pressure is needed to kill spores and totally sterilise the inclusions, but bringing to boiling temperature will be sufficient to kill active mould or other pathogens that could cause human harm, and reduce the chance that wild mould spores can compete with the already healthy mycelia.

The third challenge was to develop environmental controls to replicate growth chambers . Controlling the CO 2 levels in our HCI lab setting was not possible. However, we found that we can closely replicate a growth chamber using a 700L cool-box purchased from a hardware store. We kept an open container of water in the cool-box to keep a humid environment inside. The inside of the cool-box is entirely dark, doesn't require extra coverings over the material and had a stable temperature. This setup effectively replicate and act as a DIY growth chamber . Furthermore, we were able to successfully grow myco-materials in an apartment laundry using the same setup, proving the approach can be used in a home setting as well.

3.3.2 Step-by-Step Growing Design Process. The materials we used in our process were a commercially purchased grow kit, a desktop 3D printer and PLA filament, latex gloves, sanitising wipes, cling film and micropore tape (available from a pharmacy), a kettle and a very fine strainer. All the other materials we used were waste material (e.g. sawdust, waste cardboard, coffee grounds, etc.), which was readily available. The grow kit we used included approximately 2kg of inoculated sawdust as the main substrate (cost 23USD). To prevent mycelia from fully colonising its substrate, the grow kit should be refrigerated below 4ºC where growth will be slowed, but not stopped completely. Figure 2 provides an overview of the process we describe below.

1. Digital design . Designing for myco-material growing is similar in principle to designing for casting or moulding processes. As long as the design can be removed from the mould, then many forms can be successfully grown. For our designs, we used Autodesk Fusion 360 to create negative forms, into which the substrate is pressed. This part of the design process presents an opportunity for improvement, as highligted in Figure 2 , for example author 1 has experience designing moulds for fabrication, while (like many interaction designers and researchers) other authors do not. A software intervention was identified as an opportunity here, so that an arbritrary 3D model can be turned into a 3D printable mould.

2. Mould forming . We have used 4 materials as moulds: (1) takeaway containers made of polypropylene (PP) were used as bulk growth containers; (2) high-impact polystyrene (HIPS) vacuum formed moulds were used as a test for bulky designs with smooth surfaces; (3) Poly-lactic Acid (PLA) moulds from 3D prints were used successfully for creating fine details; and (4) acrylic, or Poly(methyl methacrylate), was laser cut for use as a barrier for flat designs. Mycelia did not seem to attempt to digest PP, HIPS or acrylic, but mycelial networks did show a tendency to grow between small imperfections in the 3D printed PLA mould, and “hold on” to the shell during demoulding. In our experience, we also noted the mycelia grew between gaps in our 3D printed forms, using the energy from the substrate to reach out to try and find more food to consume. When dried, this mycelia became prominent part lines, which can be trimmed. Additionally, complex shapes could potentially be grown in multiple, smaller parts, as mycelia of the same species will join networks, however, we have not tested the strength of these joints. Figure 2 shows that this involves design of a 3D model, which can take minutes to hours to complete, depending on complexity. After this a negative mould design is created, which may also take hours to produce, as considerations such as overhangs, which would make the myco-material break when removing from the mould. The negative mould is then digitally fabricated, and for interactive applications, micro-controllers can be programmed to embed hardware.

3. Substrate preparation . If adding other substrate materials, such as waste cardboard or sawdust, it is important to make sure that the material is as free as possible from any active pathogens, as noted above. In the biology lab, this is achieved by autoclaving, which can be done with a pressure cooker, but active pathogens can be killed by bringing inclusion material over 72ºC for at least 15 seconds, or bringing it to boiling temperature.

For samples when we included spent coffee grounds, with 10:3 substrate:coffee ratio, we used a kitchen strainer (a cheap pour-over coffee maker) and boiling water to heat the coffee grounds and raise the temperature of the material enough to kill active pathogens. Inclusions should be covered and allowed to cool before use, but shouldn't be stored, as this method will not kill dormant spores of wild mould, only active ones. Once cooled, they can be mixed by (gloved) hands or other clean utensils. Additional water may need to be added if using a dry inclusion (e.g. oats) so the mushroom has enough moisture to grow.

4. Bulk Growth (1 Week - Figure 2 A.) . Substrate preparation and bulk growth can potentially be started before digital design process, as this stage takes time. A large container can be sterilised using ethanol or sanitising wipes. The containers we used at this stage were polypropylene takeaway food containers. To facilitate some air transfer, we cut a hole (1cm × 2cm for a 500ml food container was sufficient) in the top of the lid. This was patched with micropore tape to prevent wild mould spores in the environment from landing on the substrate, while allowing oxygen to pass through. If the container does not have a lid that can be cut, cling film or aluminium foil will be suitable replacements, with small, evenly spaced holes, or a resealable bag with a hole covered in micropore tape. Figure 2 shows this as part of a material cycle.

By the end of this stage the mycelia will grow throughout the material, and will be ready to be moulded when the surface exposed to air is fully covered by mycelia. As the growth will be exponential, it will appear to happen slowly at first, and accelerate. Inclusions such as cardboard or coffee grounds may mean this takes longer, as the mycelia will have new substrate to infect. Mycelia may be white, but is not exclusively white, depending on the species used (as seen in Figure 1 ). Water droplets (sometimes discoloured) may appear on the surface of the mycelia at this stage, and is normal. In warm, low-humidity environments, monitoring the weight of the container will show if significant amounts of moisture are evaporating away. Our grow kit substrates was around 66% water by weight.

5. Forming Growth (3 days- Figure 2 B) . After bulk growth, the material should be broken up as finely as possible, which was easy to do by hand. The fabricated moulds can be sterilised using ethanol or wipes, and material can be pressed firmly into the mould. The mycelial network will grow into the shape of the mould. Exposed surfaces can be covered with cling film, aluminium foil or the mould kept to a resealable bag, if not in a sealed cool-box. This stage is faster than bulk growth, as the mycelia is active and has already spread through the growth substrate.

6. Demoulding for Open Growth (3 days - Figure 2 C, D) . After the exposed areas of material is fully colonised and the mycelia is visible, the object may be able to be removed from the mould and retain its shape, and placed in the cool-box under same conditions to continue growth. At this stage the myco-material feels like a damp sponge, but can still crack. If it is not ready, or breaks during demoulding, it can be pressed back into the mould and allowed to grow and repair. Depending on the volume of the material, and the area exposed to air, the duration of this stage will vary.

7. Drying (1 day to 1 week) . The myco-material will feel soft and spongy, but able to hold its shape when it is ready to dry. At this stage the material can be soaked in a 10% glycerine solution for an hour (glycerine can be purchased from some supermarkets for around 6USD) to have a softening effect on the final product. We dried our designs in a very low oven, which fluctuated between 50-80ºC, for several hours (depending on the material volume). The materials continued to dry over several days after removing from the oven. Thin samples tend to warp, even if there are no textures on the outside surface, but weighing down while drying may mitigate this. The final material will reduce in weight by about two thirds as the water evaporates, and will also shrink in volume.

4 CHARACTERISATION AND DESIGN TOOL

We conducted a series of studies to apply the growing process, and understand material properties. The goals of the study were to: 1) characterise and compare geometric features of the final grown object to the original 3D design; and 2) qualitatively and quantitatively evaluate how different design and material choices affected the properties of the finished model. These are presented as 2 case studies, the first using trays which include surface features, and the second using rectangular cuboids. Finally, we utilise the results to create a 3D design tool to assist with the fabrication.

4.1 Case Study 1: Geometric Features

To evaluate how different geometric features are translated into the grown object, we designed three 3D models, first with a variety of features, the ramps and patterns trays shown in Figure 3 A and B respectively, and a tray with circular step features of varying heights shown in Figure 3 C. Each tray design was used to grow two 3D models, and with two different conditions (with and without inclusions) as shown in Figure 3 -Table. Grown models were scanned and cross-sections of the scans were compared to the original design. Figure 4 A to D shows a circular tray with step structure of varying heights (1mm to 5mm) as the design, 3D print, a grown model and a scanned model. Figures 3 A and B further show the details of the 3D printed trays with different geometric features such as spherical , corners , ramps , repeating lines and different kinds of textures . The radius of the spherical geometries ranged from 2mm to 7mm and the smallest cuboid features were 5 × 5mm. The features we compare included convex and concave shapes. For each tray two variants were grown, first using only the substrate in the grow kit and another that includes waste coffee grounds at a ratio of 10:3 by weight. From the circular design, an third object was grown and treated with glycerine, which has been shown to alter the properties of the material by retaining moisture [ 15 ].

4.1.1 Geometric Properties. To further analyse geometric changes after the drying process, samples were 3D scanned using a Schining EinScan Pro 2X with models were captured using EXScan Pro software (Figure 4 D). We extracted a series of profiles (cross-sections) from each 3D scan and quantitatively compared them to the corresponding profile of the original design. For the circular tray, 11 slices of profiles were extracted at rotational displacement of 7.5º as shown in Figure 4 E. For the patterns tray, 16 slices of profiles were extracted at linear displacement of 0.1mm between each other showing concave and convex patterns visible in Figure 4 F.

For the circular tray, the 3D scans of models with different treatments were separately analysed. The three materials samples are named for the substrate materials: Plain Substrate (PS), Coffee-inclusion Substrate (CS), and the Glycerine-plain Substrate (GS). For the patterns tray, only the outline of the sample using coffee inclusions was analysed for Concave and Convex (CC) sections, as it showed noticeably superior performance to the sample with no coffee inclusion.

The evaluation was performed by overlapping the profiles of final samples over the original 3D design. Along the outline of the grown material, the absolute error with respect to the original design (in mm) is averaged along evenly spaced intervals across the cross-section. Fig 4 G, H, and I show the profiles of cross-sections with CS, GS and CC conditions respectively. The outlines in each comparison show the mean pattern generated by all the profiles (purple), and the corresponding 95% confidence interval at each point (dashed line). These outlines indicate the ability of each material to replicate the intended pattern. The average mean absolute error (MAE) is shown in Figure 4 J. It is evident that including coffee grounds improves the ability of the material to replicate sharp edges and flat surfaces. We further compared the MAE on different curvature radii and types (convex, protruding out vs. concave, denting in) along the condition CC. CX represents convex and CV1 to CV3 represents three concave radii 7mm, 5mm, and 3mm. Results show that convex features have higher conformity with the original design than the concave, and we recommend a minimum feature size of 5mm (Fig 4 K).

For the ramps tray in Figure 3 A and its outcome Fig. 5 A Left, the tray was measured to be 128 × 127.5mm, and the myco-material showed a reduction of 93.0% × 92.5% and 92.2% × 91.8% for no inclusion and coffee inclusion respectively. The patterns tray shown in Figure 3 B (outcome in Figure 5 A Right) was measured to be 128 × 123mm, with the myco-material showing a reduction in volume of 92.2% × 91.9% and 90.6% × 91.1% for PS and CS respectively. The circular tray shown in Figure 3 C (outcome Fig. 4 C and E) has a radius of 118mm. All three myco-material samples were measured to be 109 × 109mm, a 92.4% reduction in length along the two straight edges.

4.1.3 Qualitative Observations. Two grown designs are shown in Figure 5 A. We observed slight warping occurred during the drying phase. Previous studies have compared the results when the material is pressed as it is drying [ 2 ], which would reduce warping. However, as the object shrinks during drying, this would put strain on some fine details on the material if it were to be dried inside the 3D printed mould, which may also start to soften at drying temperatures, depending on the filament used. It's also worth noting that the 3D prints itself had some geometric imperfections, which occurred during printing that were rendered in the material.

Samples with coffee ground inclusions showed significantly more detail in the final product. The walls between the square indentations, measuring 1.33mm width, can be clearly seen in Figure 5 A-I, but are not visible in the sample grown without the coffee ground inclusion. Additionally, the layers from the 3D prints are clearly visible in both types (layer height was set to 0.30mm during slicing), but more pronounced in the form with inclusions as seen in Figure 5 A-II. The Patterns sample in Figure 5 A-III & -IV shows larger protrusions being rendered clearly by the material, as well as surface textures. Fine details were possible, but can be difficult to remove from a 3D printed mould as mycelia may find its way between the layers of the mould. It may be possible to use other methods to improve fine details by using a higher resolution 3D print technology (e.g. SLA resin). We also noted that if pieces of the designed object are stuck or broken during the demoulding stage, they can be repaired by simply putting them back in place, and the mycelial network will fuse the pieces together during the open growth stage. We did not evaluate the strength of this bond.

4.2.1 Surface Quality. Samples A - H had formed a white layer of mycelia around the outside of the sample, which is often seen in myco-material examples (see [ 15 ]), however Samples I - P had a darker appearance, and the shape of the underlying substrate is more easily visible. This is explained by natural variations of the fungus to the specific conditions of the two growth runs. Under mushroom lab conditions where we would be able to precisely control ambient humidity, temperature and CO 2 , this may be able to be controlled, but in our HCI lab, some variation was inevitable. It is also observable that samples I - P also had some evidence that they were starting to try to produce fruit, with small white points visible in samples I - P in Figure 5 B (Bottom). These points feel dense or hard when dried, whereas the overall white coating seen on samples A - D in Figure 5 B (Top) feels softer and similar to paper.

4.2.2 Mechanical properties. We conducted a compression and relaxation study under an external load to test the elasticity of the material using the cuboids. The load testing was performed using a Starr FDG-1000 digital force gauge, loaded on a manual testing stand and digital distance reading. Samples were placed on a plastic block, and the myco-material blocks were pressed using a flat push attachment and the force from the material measured at 0.5mm increments to a maximum depth of 5mm. The reading was taken using a custom Python script. Compression and relaxation cycles were repeated 5 times on each cuboid.

Figure 6 A shows the results of the mechanical test. Subplots a) and b) show samples A - H (without inclusion), c) and d) show samples I - P (coffee ground inclusion). The two curves represent compression (loading, blue) and relaxation (unloading, orange), as a typical elastic material, requiring less force to achieve the same displacement when relaxing. Comparing Figure 6 A subplots a vs c and b vs d, we can see that the coffee inclusion increased the slope or the modulus of elasticity. Comparing Figure 6 A subplots a vs b and c vs d reveals that treating with glycerine has a negligible effect on the elastic properties of the material without coffee inclusion. However, the glycerine treated coffee mixture has better elasticity, since the compression and relaxation cycle almost overlap with low hysteresis loss in a complete cycle. This result indicates that careful selection and tuning of inclusion material can be used to manipulate mechanical properties. This may be relevant to a tangible application where the elastic modulus of the object needs to be adjusted, for example, in squeezable interfaces or pressure sensors.

4.3 Software Tool

Our material characterisation studies and 3D growing experience revealed that there are several considerations that need to be taken when transitioning from a digital 3D design to a physical 3D object using myco-materials. First, since the drying process creates a volumetric change in myco-material objects, the design dimensions need to be adjusted to compensate for these changes. Second, when negative mould is created for the myco-materials to grow, it should to include draft angles and air vents to allow the mushroom to breathe and be easily removed. And, finally, it is desirable to remove any smaller and finer features that has a radii 2.35mm (see Figure 4 K) or smaller. In our initial designs, using 3D CAD tools, we found that this process can take a long time (hours), and volumetric changes can create erroneous dimensions in the final object, and the draft angles can render de-moulding process difficult. Furthermore, when it comes to complex 3D objects, mould designing process needs experience and expertise to generate an accurate model. Therefore, to assist with the design process, we developed a design tool, which can take an.OBJ/.STL file and systematically co-create the mould for myco-material fabrication.

The tool uses Grasshopper, a visual programming environment for parametric 3D modelling, which runs on Rhino 3D. The UI was created within grasshopper using the Human UI plugin 8 similar to  [ 53 ]. Figure 7 A shows a screenshot of the design tool and the UI. Our approach of generating moulds for a given target 3D object geometry G , using the design tool can be described in four steps:

1. Volumetric Adjustment for Myco-Materials: First, the user imports a Given a 3D geometry G to Rhino 3D. This geometry will then be linearly scaled in three dimensions to generate a new mesh Ĝ , to compensate for the volumetric shrinkage due to the myco-material drying process (Table 2 ). We calculated the linear shrinkage in a given axis to be $~92\%$ of its original dimensions through our empirical studies, thus making the scaling factor f = 1/0.92 = 1.09. From this point, the mould design will be done for object Ĝ , which will ensure the final, dried design will approximate the dimensions of G . To remove any problematic finer points (radii less than 2.35mm), the tool can assist the user by running a surface smoothing. The smoothed area of the geometry will be highlighted as shown in Figure 7 D. Furthermore, the tool can add a texture to the object to help designers see an approximate resemblance of the grown object.

2. Creating Mould Box: Then we create the bounding body object B , on which the negative mould will be created. There are two choices for the bounding body, namely the Cuboid and Rough Shape . The Cuboid option gives a box-shaped enclosure to generate the mould cast, which will consume more 3D printing materials and increases the fabrication time of a mould. However, we found that for small objects (to approximately 5cm tall), handling is easier with a box-shaped mould. Moreover, the box type has the advantage of processing faster within the software tool platform and maintaining stability in the fabrication pipeline. Alternatively, the Rough Shape bounding object generates a shell-like enclosing object (Figures 7 A and 1 A) which completely encloses Ĝ with a uniform thickness. This approach reduces a large amount of 3D printing material and minimizes the 3D printing time. The drawback of this approach is that the user needs to have a more experience in printing complex overhanging shapes and need to handle the object carefully when growing.

3. Slicing and Air Inlet Adjustment: Next, the user is given the ability to align up to 5 clipping planes (Figure 7 B) on Ĝ to minimize the draft angle α i between the outer surface of Ĝ and the clipping plane P i where i = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 . In this phase, the draft angle is shown as a heat-map on the geometry and the aim is to minimize the red regions (problematic areas) on the body (Figure 7 C) by adjusting the plane location or angle. Adding multiple planes could be used to isolate the problematic draft angles and remove them. As shown in Figure 7 C the red areas on the ear of the bunny can be removed by adding a new plane. Users can turn on and off the visualization of the plane to better see problematic areas. The level of complexity supported by the software tool is relative and depends on the user's ability to manipulate the discrete number of clipping planes making sure there are no problematic areas within the 3D object. An air inlet is added at the bottom of the mould and can be changed in diameter by the user, to make sure that the growing fungi have enough access to air. Following this, each of the aligned planes P i is used to split B which results in N separate components b j where j = 1,2,..N (see Figures 7 A and 1 A). To further assist moulding, the tool provides keys to connect different part of the mould together in the growing process as shown in Figure 7 A inset.

4. Generating Mould Cavity and Export: Next, we use the solid difference component in Grasshopper to generate mould cavity as shown in Figures 7 A, corresponding to each b j as follows; r j = b j − ( b j ∩ B ). Finally, the user can manually select single r j or multiple r j components as a group and generate 3D mesh geometry for the selected components. These can then be exported from Rhino 3D for printing.

4.3.1 Evaluation and Limitations of the Software Tool. To evaluate the functionality of the tool, we used it to generate a series of moulds for a sphere with radius 20mm and followed the myco-material fabrication process to grow three myco-material spheres. The sphere was chosen because it would reduce in size uniformly in each direction and helps us evaluate performance from different angles. We measured each of the sphere across the diameter at six different locations (See Figure 8 E). The 18 measurements ( M = 39.92 mm , σ = 0.74) show that the final form was within 0.2% of the target size on average.

The fabricable geometries and shapes are inherently limited by the moulding/demoulding process. While our tool assist with slicing to optimize for draft angles, some shapes, such as hollow concave shapes are extremely difficult to mould. While our tool can create up to 5 slicing planes, this increases the complexity of the physical moulding process. As can be seen in Figure 1 B, even after refining the mould with the tool, some complicated areas such as the ear of the bunny, did not grow as expected. Additionally, Rhinoceros and Grasshopper are costly and this will limit the usage of the tool.

While we show integrating electronics and other interactive elements in the growing process, our tool does not assist the design of the housing. The heterogeneity of possible elements made it difficult for us to integrate the embedded elements in the tool. We see possible pathways to assist with the process through enclosed skeletal structures, however, we believe this should be done through a different process, that integrates empirical understanding of the effects of growing process on embedded elements. For instance, how much growth process move internal elements? What pressure and tension forces are exerted while growing and drying? These not very well understood.

5 EXAMPLE APPLICATIONS

To demonstrate the capabilities of myco-material based fabrication, we created several tangible and interactive application examples.

1. Flower Pot and Interactive Plant-Stand . We designed and grew a flower pot (Figure 8 A) and a plant-stand (Figure 8 D) using myco-materials to demonstrate: 1) detailed textures and patterns that can be grown, and 2) embedded interactive systems. The flower pot mould was designed with a repeating surface texture in CAD software and 3D printed with a desktop FDM printer, meaning the mould can be re-used. For the plant stand mould, we used two cylindrical plastic containers. Using myco-material in a flower pot has interesting implications. Since the flower pot itself is organic, when the plant out-grows its pot, it can be broken down and used to recycle nutrients to the plant it once held. A larger pot can be grown to for plant and the previous pot as the first is decaying.

The plant-stand (Figure 8 D) allowed use to explore how to embed electronics in myco-materials, and grow the object around the electronics. We enclosed a simple moisture sensor setup (Stemma soil sensor, Adafruit Industries) connected to Adafruit Feather nRF52840 Express micro controller, two LEDs, a Li-Po battery and a wireless charger. We sealed the electronics in a zip-lock bag to prevent moisture damage, and were able to communicate with the micro-controller using BLE protocol while the mushroom was growing, and after the drying process. We programmed the device to measure moisture from the flower pot, indicate dry conditions using blinking LEDs (Figure 8 D) and through BLE to a mobile app. The application shows that the myco-materials can be grown around active interactive elements such as electronics without significant effort.

2. Coffee Cup Holder . Myco-materials exhibit excellent thermal insulation properties [ 31 ]. We leveraged this property to create a coffee cup holder application using myco-materials. The mould for the cup holder was 3D printed to fit common coffee cups (Figure 8 B).

To understand the thermal insulation of the cup holder, we measured the temperature of transition from inside the cup to outside the holder over 10 minutes. We measure the temperature change under two conditions, 1) without a lid and 2) with a lid on. We poured hot water into the cup (90ºC) and measured the temperature of the water and outside the cup-holder using IR thermometers. Figure 6 B shows the temperature variation over 10 minutes. We observed that the peak temperature reached on the holder was slightly over 50ºC, which is warm but comfortable to hold.

To indicate the temperature inside the cup to the user, we added a thermochromic display on the cup. The design included a flame symbol, which we painted using red acrylic paint that was covered with a layer of thermochromic paint. At ambient temperatures, the thermochromic in has a yellow appearance, and transitions to a darker violet, causing the red flame to appear to change colour from orange-yellow to purple, so the user can see the liquid inside is still hot.

3. Pressure-Responsive Coaster . Utilising the mechanical properties of myco-materials, we developed an interactive pressure sensitive coaster by embedding a force sensitive resistor (FSR). A coaster was grown around the FSR (Figure 8 C), that was connected to an external microcontroller. Coaster was able to estimate the amount of liquid in the mug using the FSR readings, which could be used to track water intake over the course of a day.

4. 3D form growth . The examples above show designs with thin cross-sections, which several previous examples show, such as creating myco-material boards for laser cutting or electronics prototyping [ 20 , 41 , 60 ]. However, myco-materials are also suitable for use as lightweight housing for interactive systems, and can also create complex shapes, such as the Stanford Bunny shown in Figure 1 D.

6 DISCUSSION AND FUTURE WORK

6.1 Prototyping with Myco-Materials

Our experience growing myco-materials provides some implications for prototyping in HCI labs. Working with the mushroom research lab to grow the myco-material from the master spawn highlighted challenges for designing with living organisms. Even with controls over growth environments, we still experienced issues with wild mould making our products unusable. In comparison, our approach of using pre-inoculated grow kits was very successful, with no instances of contamination from other mould species. We used gloved hands to handle material, and prepared 3D printed moulds with antibacterial wipes. Using ready-to-grow mushroom kits, rather than pasteurising our own growth substrate, allowed us to manage the need for a clean environment, and greatly reduce risk of infection, as the mushroom is the dominant species in its environment by the time it arrives in our HCI lab. For us, this provides the best opportunity for supporting the material's development.

We did experience some other challenges in our workflow. For instance, removing the myco-material from the cast can be a delicate procedure, however, the material can be self-repairing, held in place with wooden skewers during the open growth phase, but it's unclear if this would impact the material performance.

Though there are some industrial design details to consider, such as the need for draft angles and to hide part lines, our study shows that the barrier to entry is surprisingly low. The cool-box we purchased as an incubator can maintain an even temperature and high humidity. While more expensive than spawning mycelia in the biology lab, the grow kits were still economical, particularly as the substrate has less overall wastage due to contamination. We were able to re-use the 3D printed moulds, though the mycelial network did reach in between the layers of the print, making removal more difficult. An alternative method may be to use vacuum-formed negative moulds, however our design tool needs adjustments to support the design process. Vacuum forming would lead to smoother surfaces, as there are no printed layers to alter surface textures, but lower potential for detailed designs, and a positive form still has to be created. Our approach works directly with the negative mould from a digital 3D model, and can produce multi-part forms that would be difficult to replicate using vacuum forming.

The cost of using myco-materials is higher than 3D printing with plastic. For only the volume of myco-material, with 30% coffee inclusion, we calculated that the myco-material is between 2.4-3.2 times more expensive than printing the same form in PLA, based on “out-of-the box” slicer settings and basic filament costs, without taking into consideration the cost of 3D printing a growth mould (which can be used multiple times). However, the resulting materials are difficult to compare, and they should be utilised for different purposes.

Though it may be more costly than 3D printing, there are several opportunities for future applications that are created from working with myco-materials for prototyping. Complex 3D designs can be grown from multiple pieces, which are allowed to fuse together permanently before the drying phase. This negates the need for glue, screws or snap-fit designs, reducing the number of parts used. Our initial exploration of this area shows that the quality and strength of multi-part designs, such as for electronic housing, is an area for future research. Additionally, myco-material prototypes can be designed to engage the values of the user: by utilising waste and creating compostable designs, it can appeal to a value of sustainability; by allowing a user to grow a design at home, it can appeal to a sense of care mindfulness for wellbeing the living organism; as a short-term material, and using a reprogrammable microcontroller, the myco-material can connect to the temporary nature of a research prototype, in contrast with using a plastic that may ultimately last for centuries in landfill.

6.2 Implications for User Experience

These implications for user experience, when compared to 3D printing or other fabrication methods, are worth exploring further. When working with a living material it is problematic to think of the designer as entirely in control of the design process [ 63 ], rather than working with a material stakeholder. However, it may also be easy to think of myco-materials as “unpredictable” or “hard to control,” when compared to engineered materials. One example of treating the material as a stakeholder is by maintaining a clean environment: so that the desirable mycelium is the dominant organism in the designed environment. Another example is through the organism's autonomy. The limited autonomy of the fungi has a noticeable impact on the final appearance, texture, and colour of the myco-materials, which led to surprising results. For example, some objects produced without the coffee grounds inclusion were a much darker colour than those with, which initially seemed counter-intuitive, but is likely the result of slight changes in humidity, temperature or duration of growth. The level of detail visible in the final objects was surprisingly fine, and improved with the use of coffee ground inclusions, perhaps as it was finer than the sawdust in the grow kits.

Rather than shying away from relinquishing control of fine design details, an area of further research is to explore the relationship between users and materials. The Wabi-Sabi nature of the material—its naturalness, simplicity and slight imperfections [ 42 ]—could be used as a resource in the design process, reinforcing alternative patterns of consuming materials and resources, emphasising that ‘nothing lasts’, ‘nothing is finished’, and ‘nothing is perfect’ [ 59 ]. The examples we present demonstrate great variation in appearance, even as objects were grown sequentially, using the same mould, grow kits, and growth conditions, and have very similar dimensions. The unpredictable, but not random, nature of the material points toward some autonomy of the mushroom fungus as it grows the artifact, through its quality of livingness [ 34 ]. This presents an opportunity for design to provoke responses from the user, through aesthetics, care and the habitats of the living material [ 34 , 66 ].

6.3 Remanufacturing Waste

Our exploration into myco-materials shows promise for creating new value from remanufacturing waste. This could be improved further by identifying other commonplace waste materials to remanufacture into myco-materials, such as waste paper or cardboard, which would be generally accessible to HCI labs. Opportunities to use wood waste would be accessible where lumber is processed, as up only around 45% of wood may be turned into useful lumber by a sawmill [ 45 ]. While strategies of engaging with other parts of the economy in this way may work at commercial scales, in a research context, this may be more complex, as an HCI lab may need a trivial amount of material compared to what a sawmill produces.

FDM 3D printing using a bioplastic, such as PLA has potential to be recycled into filament and reused in 3D printers with satisfactory results [ 1 , 40 ]. Recycling PLA is preferable to composting the bioplastic in terms of environmental impact, though it is biodegradable [ 11 ]. After use as myco-material moulds, the PLA may not be suitable for recycling, as the mushroom hyphae may find its way between layers, and not be able to be separated from the PLA. However, while we focused on reishi mushroom, another species able to create myco-materials, Pleurotus ostreatus (oyster mushroom) has been shown to degrade PLA composite materials [ 28 ]. While we were unsuccessful, The possibility of breaking down PLA scaffold from 3D printing, and failed or discarded 3D printed objects to create new myco-materials is an interesting opportunity for further research.

6.4 Limitations and Future Work

The primary fabrication strategy we propose in this paper was based on moulding forms. It is not the most desirable and if possible, direct 3D printing will be the preference of many HCI researchers. Our initial experiments with a 3D Plotterbot, usually used for ceramic 3D printing, have shown promise. We have experimented using a 6mm extruder, with a mixture of substrate from the grow kits, spent coffee grounds, finely ground oats and additional water. Our objective was to produce a paste with similar consistency to clay prepared for the ceramic printer. The oats are a useful binding agent for the material for this proof-of-concept. Future work will explore sources of waste materials that hold water, to give a paste consistency to the mixture.

Another consideration is the impact that the growing process can have on a fabrication timeline. 3D printing a complex object can take hours or even days, but the growing process can take weeks. While an equivalent volume of a myco-material may be cheaper than PLA, and would use less energy, the time cost, and reduced control over the final product is a green premium that HCI researchers would have to be prepared to pay.

7 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we have shown how myco-materials can be created and used in the HCI lab as a sustainable fabrication material. We demonstrated an affordable and a reliable process to grow and fabricate tangible interactive objects using myco-materials, and presented a software application that can help designers more effectively create negative moulds for myco-material fabrication. By understanding the properties of the materials, we were able to provide speculative prototypes that apply the affordances of the myco-material, and create new opportunities for research, particularly related to sustainable HCI practices.
