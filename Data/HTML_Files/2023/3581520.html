Digital technologies play an increasingly prominent role in memorialisation, but their use at cemeteries has been criticised for lack of sensitivity. Designers of digital cemetery technologies (cemtech) face a high risk of causing offence or failing to attract users due to social norms of memorial sites. To map this area of design and its pitfalls, we first developed a typology of cemtech through a review of examples from around the world. We then evaluated social acceptance of various types of cemtech through a survey of 1,053 Australian residents. Younger people were more accepting of cemtech than older people. Acceptance was highest for cemtech with three characteristics: familiarity, intimacy of user group and peacefulness. Through a reflexive thematic analysis, we identified four attitudinal dichotomies that explain divergent reactions to cemtech: Expands/Impedes, Public/Private, Lively/Restful and Pragmatic/Affective. We conclude with a discussion of how this work can assist designers of public memorialisation technologies.

1 INTRODUCTION

“I appreciate that technology has evolved but there are some places that it doesn't belong. Cemeteries are one of them.” [comment from survey respondent]

Cemeteries are widely thought of as bastions of tradition: places dedicated to remembering the past, and thus looking backward rather than looking forward to innovation and change. Nevertheless, new technologies are being introduced to cemeteries, as they are to virtually all urban public spaces. They are brought in by visitors who use digital media in their everyday lives and mourning practices [ 78 ], by entrepreneurs who aim to sell new memorial products and services [ 2 ], and by cemetery owners who seek to engage cemetery users or improve cemetery management in response to pressure from urban land shortages and rising maintenance costs [ 79 ].

The clash of tradition and technology at the cemetery is frequently a source of controversy. Within the past few years, public debates have broken out over the use of technologies including Pokémon Go [ 34 ], TikTok [ 82 ], Instagram [ 45 ], QR codes [ 42 ], electronic music [ 61 ] and media-enhanced grave monuments [ 20 ] in cemeteries. Tensions over online memorial practices are well-documented in the academic literature [ 22 , 60 , 75 ], but little research has been done on social acceptance of technology at cemeteries, although small studies have found varying levels of approval for technologies of different types [ 29 , 30 ].

Social acceptability has become a more pressing concern for HCI researchers over time as computing technologies have proliferated and spread from private to public spaces [ 5 , 44 ]. Cemeteries are among the most contentious of public spaces for technology acceptance due to their significant cultural purpose and place, which implies strong social norms around appropriate atmosphere and conduct (although the details of these norms vary across time [ 46 , 64 ] and place [ 74 ]). Consequently, social acceptability is an acute problem for cemetery technologies, as it is for death-related technologies in general, which face a high risk of public disapproval or distrust [ 55 ].

In this paper, we map the broad landscape of public attitudes to cemetery technologies. To delimit the scope of the research, we define the term cemtech to refer to digital technologies that are designed to augment the user experience of interacting with a cemetery, whether on-site as a visitor or remotely through the internet . 1 We first conducted a systematic review and identified 72 unique examples of cemtech from around the world, and used them to develop a typology of cemtech. Informed by this typology, we designed a survey to present 13 cemtech concepts to a representative sample of adult Australians ( n =1,053) and collected both quantitative and qualitative feedback on each one. We compared the quantitative responses between age groups, and conducted a reflexive thematic analysis on the qualitative responses.

The paper makes four main contributions to knowledge on social attitudes to cemtech. First, we find that social acceptance varies widely between different types of cemtech. Second, we observe that younger people are more positive towards nearly all forms of cemtech than older people (with the exception of funeral webcasts). Third, we identify three qualities that characterise the most socially accepted forms of cemtech: familiarity, intimacy of user group and peacefulness. Fourth, we describe four areas of public disagreement about cemtech: whether it facilitates or impedes memorialisation; whether it should or should not activate the cemetery space for increased visitation; whether it should or should not animate the deceased; and whether it should be judged on primarily functional or emotional terms. These dichotomies reflect the contentious nature of death, mourning, technology and the cemetery, and mark out challenges for thanatosensitive design [ 49 ] in the cemetery context.

2 BACKGROUND

2.1 The cemetery as a modern technology

When considering attitudes to technology at the cemetery, it should first be acknowledged that the cemetery is itself an urban technology. The cemetery in its modern, Western form—typically a large space with a distinct perimeter, laid out with clearly demarcated burial sites that afford individual memorialisation, usually serving an entire local community [ 62 ]—is a relatively recent invention, developed in Europe in the late eighteenth century as a rational solution to concerns about disease and overcrowding at churchyard burial grounds [ 46 ]. Like other technologies, design standards for cemeteries have changed over time, including a shift from ornamented garden cemeteries to more park-like lawn cemeteries in the twentieth century [ 63 ]. In turn, cemeteries are also spaces already populated by digital technologies through the ubiquity of personal mobile devices [ 2 ].

Notably, the modern cemetery introduced the principle (in some countries) that bodies would remain undisturbed in perpetuity [ 65 ]. Today this principle is being challenged by the pressures of urban land shortage [ 27 ] and the spectacle of older cemeteries falling into ruin [ 79 ]. Cemetery owners are increasingly encouraged to “activate” the space by making it available for a wider range of purposes, “to encourage visitors to see it as more than a space for burial and cremation” [ 79 ]. Early proponents of cemeteries explicitly intended them to be places for public recreation [ 47 ], but over time they came to be seen as the exclusive domain of the dead and the bereaved [ 62 ]. Cemeteries are nevertheless used for a range of leisure activities, from jogging [ 19 , 26 ], picnicking [ 17 ] and tourism [ 19 , 81 ] to more transgressive activities such as drug-taking, sex and vandalism [ 17 ]. The latter are unsurprisingly viewed with disapproval, but even the legal recreational activities have moderate to very low social acceptance [ 17 , 24 , 26 ].

To date there has been little direct academic study of public preferences for the design of cemeteries [ 66 ], but there are a few exceptions. Nordh et al. [ 56 ] interviewed 59 visitors to a cemetery in Oslo, Norway, and found that they most valued the orderly, quiet, secluded, historical and natural qualities of the cemetery environment. Al-Akl et al. [ 1 ] interviewed a convenience sample of 29 university students and staff (primarily young tertiary-educated women) in Beirut, Lebanon, about cemetery design, and found similar preferences for orderliness, nature and familiarity. (What was considered familiar differed between participants.) They summarised the most preferred cemetery environment as “a private garden where one can get away from the city” [ 1 ]. That preference for orderly solitude appears to conflict with the high-usage, multi-purpose cemetery model advocated in urban design, and with the cemtech that has been developed to support a broader range of cemetery interactions. This suggests a pitfall that needs to be managed in design—the contours of which are mapped in this study.

2.2 From sequestration to social media mourning

In the twentieth century, cemeteries played a central role in a broad cultural effort in the Western world to sequester the dead from the living and to remove real death (as opposed to fictional death) from public view [ 46 , 62 ]. The cultural silence around death in this period is well documented, and has been attributed to the reduction in child mortality, the medicalisation of dying and the trauma of the two world wars [ 37 , 46 , 52 ]. Since the 1980s, however, this sequestration of the dead has been challenged by a combination of social and technological factors [ 77 ]. One of the most significant factors has been the internet, which has simultaneously given the dead an extended presence in social life [ 76 ] and created unprecedent opportunities for public displays of mourning [ 10 , 78 ].

Online digital media push back against social death—“the withering and eventual extinction of social identity and social interaction” [ 78 ]—by allowing the dead to continue to have a dynamic social presence, whether accidentally through legacy social media pages or deliberately through platforms such as posthumous messaging services [ 38 ] or AI-powered digital replicas [ 12 ]. The latter have attracted a great deal of attention (perhaps most famously in the Black Mirror TV series episode “Be Right Back” [ 33 ]), but also strong disapproval from some potential users [ 12 ]—a finding that is reaffirmed in our study. These phenomena have been referred to as “postmortem identities” [ 10 ], “posthumous persons” [ 51 ], or more provocatively as “digital zombies” [ 4 ]. These terms reflect how digital media have created new ways for mourners to maintain continuing bonds with the dead [ 23 , 40 ], challenging the previously dominant norm that saw grief as a state to be “worked through” with the goal of allowing the mourner to “move on” and leave the deceased person behind [ 41 , 72 ].

Conflicts between such norms are a common feature of online mourning spaces. Social media profiles of the dead may be open to comments by anyone from relatives to acquaintances and even strangers, who may have very different ideas about appropriate conduct [ 10 ]. Consequently, as Tony Walter describes, “online social media commemorations can resemble a conflict-ridden cemetery” [ 75 ]. Onlookers who disapprove of public displays of mourning may engage in “grief policing” [ 22 ] or outright trolling [ 48 , 58 ] to discourage the practice. While these conflicting norms and behaviours are visible online, they are more elusive, transient and difficult to observe in physical memorial spaces such as the cemetery—hence the need for research that proactively asks about potentially provocative or contentious activities at cemeteries.

2.3 HCI and the digital cemetery

In the past decade, HCI researchers have paid increasing attention to technologies for after death and bereavement, as evidenced by a workshop on “HCI at End of Life and Beyond” at CHI 2020 [ 71 ]. Massimi et al. [ 50 ] identified the end of life and bereavement as important but previously under-examined life stages for HCI research, and Massimi and Charise [ 49 ] coined the term “thanatosensitive design” to describe design that seeks to address the sensitive issues raised by death. The majority of HCI research conducted in this space has concerned online memorialisation (as noted above), digital inheritances [ 21 , 28 ] and digital memorialisation in domestic spaces [ 57 , 68 , 70 ]. Moncur and Kirk [ 53 ] put forward a set of questions for digital memorial design that includes consideration of the memorial's placement in public or private space. The Covid-19 pandemic has prompted a rapid escalation in funeral webcasting, and this phenomenon has been studied by scholars including Uriu et al. [ 69 ] and Pitsillides and Wallace [ 59 ]. These studies emphasise the appeal and the benefits of simpler and more familiar technologies for webcasting over more feature-rich but complex and unfamiliar technologies.

A handful of studies have addressed the cemetery as a specific design context for digital technology. Nansen et al. [ 54 ] contrast the media-rich, interactive, informal and fleeting content of digital cemetery technologies (i.e. cemtech) with the terse, formal and durable content of traditional stone grave markers. They note the conflict between these new “lively” technologies and the long-held notion of the cemetery as a sleeping place: “The use of rich multimedia may help ‘bring to life’ that person at the grave, but it also disturbs the traditional and sequestered notions and associated imagery of rest and repose” [ 54 ].

Several HCI researchers have conducted studies with cemtech prototypes. Dow et al. [ 18 ] and Sabra et al. [ 67 ] both developed location-based cemetery audio tour systems, and conducted small-scale user experience tests to determine their viability. Coughlan et al. [ 15 ] co-designed a mobile discovery system for a historical cemetery in collaboration with cemetery volunteers and local students. Similarly, Ciolfi and Petrelli [ 13 ] engaged with cemetery volunteers to inform the design of cemtech prototypes for wayfinding, narrative and augmented reality displays. Häkkilä et al. [ 31 ] created a virtual reality replica of a second world war cemetery and conducted a small-scale user experience test. These are valuable examples of cemtech design and research, but their insights do not extend to whether these or other cemetery technologies would be socially acceptable to a broader public.

There is more recent work by Häkkilä et al. [ 29 ], however, that does give some preliminary indications. They conducted three small user studies with participants in Finland to elicit reactions to several cemtech design concepts. In these studies, digital wayfinding systems received broad approval, whereas digital gravestone displays were criticised for being tasteless and largely unnecessary (although some participants noted they could give children new memories of their grandparents). The idea of an AI-driven chat with an avatar of the deceased person was considered inappropriate and potentially harmful to mourners. Some participants worried about the idea of an AI representation “capturing” the deceased's personality in a gravestone, “so it would never get away again” [ 29 ]. Notably, each one of these findings is directly reflected in the results of our survey, which offers a larger dataset and a different national context for the analysis of attitudes to cemtech.

2.4 Social acceptance research in HCI

Technology acceptance is a central concern of HCI research. Studies of technology acceptance have most commonly focused on the concerns of the individual user, as in user experience evaluations and the Technology Acceptance Model [ 16 , 35 ]. However, as computing has spread to more public spaces, it has become increasingly necessary to anticipate social acceptance: the reception that a technology will receive from both users and non-users in its context of use. Koelle et al. [ 44 ] make a distinction between social acceptance —the empirically measurable state of public (dis)approval of a technology—and social acceptability —a subjective assessment of how the use of a technology will activate the social judgements of those who encounter it. They argue that both social acceptance and social acceptability need to be a more explicit focus of HCI research, as too often a lack of social acceptance is discovered only by accident when a product or service fails in the market.

In a recent study, Koelle et al. [ 43 ] conducted a systematic review of HCI papers that reported on social acceptance (or acceptability) research. They found that surveys were among the most common methods used, employing a mix of quantitative and qualitative questions. There was no commonly agreed-upon way to ask about social acceptance, and many of the measures used would be difficult to generalise as “questions are often phrased to exactly match the to-be-evaluated prototype or interaction style” [ 43 ]. We sought to avoid this limitation in our survey by asking for general ratings of sentiment followed by an open-ended explanation, as detailed in the research design (section 3.2). Moreover, they observed that the most popular suggested strategy for social acceptability was subtle or unobtrusive technologies. These qualities are also reflected in the results of our cemtech survey, which we unpack in our results (section 4) and discussion (section 5) below.

3 RESEARCH DESIGN

3.1 Typology of cemtech

To begin our investigation, we conducted a review of cemtech designs that have been documented in research literature and deathcare industry publications. We began by searching the online archives of the Association for Computing Machinery and the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers using the search terms “cemetery OR cemeteries”, which produced 339 results. From these results, we selected all those that substantively documented an example of cemtech, which resulted in 40 academic papers. We further explored the literature through forward and backward chaining of references to identify additional relevant academic papers. We then searched the online archives of several deathcare industry publications: Funeral Service Insider, American Funeral Director, Memento Mori and Connecting Directors . We also searched online records related to deathcare events: trade conferences hosted by the National Funeral Directors Associations of the USA, UK and Australia as well as the International Cemetery, Cremation & Funeral Association; the Asia Funeral & Cemetery Expo & Conference; and a design competition run by the Future Cemetery Research Centre at the University of Bath. We supplemented this with field notes and examples the authors had collected over years of prior research on deathcare innovations. We used Google Translate to review sources in languages other than English.

From all of these sources, we identified 72 unique examples of cemtech. Most of the examples (57) were from majority English-speaking countries, which may be a result of the bias towards English-language sources in our search process. Bearing that caveat in mind, we believe this represents a broad and comprehensive cross-section of global cemtech design as of 2022.

We conducted an iterative data-driven coding process to classify the cemtechs according to their primary applications—that is, the main functions they were designed to fulfil. We identified 13 primary application types, summarised in Table 1 . The types are non-exclusive, as specific examples of cemtech were often designed to fulfil multiple applications.

3.2 Online survey

To understand social acceptance of cemtech in Australia, we conducted an online survey with a mix of open-ended and multiple-choice questions. A total of 1,053 respondents completed the survey, which is a very large sample size for a survey with substantial qualitative elements [ 8 ]. Respondents were recruited from a commercial survey research panel, and screened to ensure a representative sample of the Australian adult population stratified by age, gender and state/territory of residence. All respondents were 18 years of age or older. The survey preamble identified it as part of a university research project on cemeteries and new technology, and warned that it contained questions on potentially sensitive topics relating to the treatment and commemoration of the dead. Ethics approval for the research was granted by the University of Melbourne.

3.2.1 Survey design. The survey presented descriptions of 13 hypothetical cemtech concepts, representing a cross-section of the typology outlined above in Table 1 . The full list of cemtech concepts is shown in Table 2 , alongside the descriptions that were presented in the survey and the corresponding typology categorisations. The first five concepts were web-based technologies designed to support cemetery-based memorialisation practices remotely. For these, the survey asked: “How would you feel about using an online service like this?” These five concepts were presented first, with their order randomised. The remaining eight concepts were designed to be used at the cemetery itself. For these, the survey asked: “How would you feel about this technology being used in a cemetery?” These eight concepts were presented second, with their order randomised. Respondents rated their feelings on a seven-point scale ranging from “very negative” to “very positive”, with an option for “don't know”.

The survey then asked respondents to explain each rating in their own words (“Why did you give the answer above?”). Responses to these questions were optional, but encouraged. If a respondent did not enter any text, they were shown a pop-up window to confirm that they wished to continue without entering a comment. Out of 13,688 opportunities for comment, 12,507 contained a response (91%), the vast majority of which were relevant and consisted of at least a few words. All survey comments quoted in this paper are verbatim, except where a clarifying word has been added in square brackets.

The list of concepts was informed by the review of existing cemtech described in section 3.1, with input from our industry research partner, a large cemetery management trust. Funeral webcast is included because funerals are often held on cemetery grounds. Online memorial is not strictly cemtech as it lacks an inherent connection to a cemetery, but it is a component of one of the most widely used forms of cemtech: a technological fixture on a gravestone, such as a QR code, that links it to an online memorial [ 11 , 25 ]. We asked about the online memorial component in isolation to distinguish it from the other concepts and to compare the acceptability of digital memorial technology that is not linked to the cemetery grounds. Historical game and ghost game were inspired by the controversy around Pokémon Go at cemeteries [ 34 ]. We tested two versions to gauge how much of the backlash may be based on the theme versus the form of a game. AR avatar was inspired by a real project designed to recreate the experience of speaking to a person who may have died: USC Shoah Foundation's Dimensions in Testimony. 2 The augmented reality glasses component was inspired by an augmented reality cemtech prototyped by Ciolfi et al. [ 14 ], and this was combined with a mention of an “interactive hologram” to explore whether more advanced technologies would be less socially accepted.

The survey also contained questions about body disposal preferences, funeral attendance and cemetery usage, which are not reported in this paper. The complete survey questionnaire is provided in the supplemental materials.

3.2.2 Quantitative analysis. In addition to reporting quantitative responses at the overall level, we segmented the sample by age to determine how social acceptance of cemtech differed across generational lines. We split the sample into three age groups of roughly equal size, reflecting the age composition of the Australian adult population: 18–34 years ( n =352), 35–54 years ( n =341) and 55 years or older ( n =360). For each cemtech rating question, we performed a chi-square test of independence to examine the relationship between age and sentiment. A p-value of less than .01 was chosen to indicate statistical significance. The full table of ratings by age group is included in Appendix A.

3.2.3 Thematic analysis. To understand the explanatory survey comments, we conducted a reflexive thematic analysis [ 7 ] using an inductive approach to code and theme development. As the sample size was very large for a survey with substantial qualitative elements [ 8 ], the qualitative dataset was correspondingly large, comprising 109,967 words across 12,507 individual comments. To make this manageable for qualitative analysis, we began the thematic analysis on a subset of the comments before expanding to the full dataset. We first randomly selected a sub-sample of 100 respondents, who provided a total of 1,166 comments, for initial line-by-line coding and theme development. Once the initial themes were developed, we extended the analysis to the full dataset through browsing and searching for relevant keywords, in order to test the themes against the remaining data, to flesh them out with additional details and to gauge the prevalence of the attitudes they represent.

We began by familiarising ourselves with the data by reading over the initial sub-sample. The first author then coded each comment with semantic codes to reflect the explanations the respondents had provided. We iterated upon these codes throughout the analysis process until we had developed a final list of 43 codes to summarise the survey comments (which form the basis of the qualitative reporting in section 4.2.)

Once we had coded the entire initial sub-sample, we developed the codes into themes. Following the precepts of reflexive thematic analysis, the themes represent “patterns of shared meaning underpinned or united by a core concept” [ 6 ]: underlying ideas that we see expressed in different ways across a variety of survey responses. The themes were elaborated upon in discussion with all of the authors. It was at this stage that we began to compare and contrast our initial codes and themes against the full dataset, browsing and searching for relevant keywords to look for comments that contradicted or elaborated upon the ideas they expressed.

During this process, we noted that our initial themes were in opposing pairs, which reflected underlying differences in the values by which respondents explained their judgements of the cemtech concepts. We formalised this in our analysis by developing the themes into four binary oppositions. Each pair of themes describes a thematic dichotomy: two contradictory ways of judging cemetery technology on which respondents expressed divergent views. For instance, the Expands/Impedes dichotomy consists of the opposing themes technology expands opportunities for memorialisation and technology impedes proper memorialisation . The former theme is informed by the codes “Improves accessibility”, “Convenient”, “Next best thing to in-person”, “Good to share memories with others”, “Good to see messages from others”, “Fairer” and “Covid safe”, and is generally associated with positive reactions to cemtech. The latter theme is informed by the codes “Should attend in person”, “Prefer to do it myself”, “Virtual is not the same”, “Technology is impersonal or unnatural”, “Prefer tradition or simplicity” and “Anti-technology”, and is generally associated with negative reactions to cemtech. The themes are described in section 4.3.

3.2.4 Reflexive statement. Reflexive thematic analysis is a “fully qualitative approach” [ 9 ] to thematic analysis in which the researcher's subjectivity is acknowledged as an essential part of the meaning-making process. In this section, we reflect on the perspectives that we bring to this work and how it may have shaped our conclusions.

All of the authors are from a Western European (historically Protestant Christian) family background and do not have a religious affiliation or belief. One influence this may have had upon our analysis is to lessen the salience of spirituality. Consistent with Australia's status as a relatively unreligious country [ 3 ], we noted few explicit mentions of religion or afterlife in the survey responses, but different researchers may have read more spiritual connotations into the comments.

The authors also share a familiarity with and curiosity about death-related technology, novel death practices and the global diversity of funerary cultures. Given all of the above, on an emotional level we may be less sensitive to the negative sentiments some people feel towards cemtech, and more favourably inclined towards responses that express an interest similar to our own. We have sought to ensure that both kinds of responses get a fair representation in this paper, by supplementing the qualitative results with quantitative results (section 4.1) and by capturing opposing viewpoints in the thematic analysis (section 4.3).

4 RESULTS

In this section, we report the survey results in three stages. First, we briefly compare the quantitative sentiment ratings for each cemtech concept, at the overall level and by age group (section 4.1). Second, we examine and compare the qualitative responses to the most and least socially accepted cemtech (section 4.2). Based on this comparison, we identify three qualities for a cemtech to have that make it most likely to find the broadest possible social acceptance. Third, we describe the four themes that we developed through reflexive thematic analysis, which highlight the divisions in public attitudes to cemtech and the ways in which social acceptance of cemtech is not monolithic (section 4.3).

4.1 Comparison of cemtech social acceptance

We found sharp differences in the level of support for each cemtech (Figure 1 ). Mostly positive ratings were given for audio guide, funeral webcast, online memorial and grave-tending service . Roughly similar proportions of positive and negative ratings were given for gravesite webcam, gravestone video, virtual grave, narrative audio beacons and narrative audio speakers . Mostly negative responses were given for autobiographical audio, AR avatar, historical game and ghost game .

The chi-square independence test showed that sentiment ratings differed significantly (p<.01) between age groups for all cemtech concepts except funeral webcast (see Appendix A). Negative sentiment increased with age: respondents aged 18–34 gave the most positive responses, whereas respondents aged 55+ gave the most negative responses.

Funeral webcast was unique in that it received similar—and largely positive—ratings from all three age groups. The qualitative responses indicate that it was the most familiar form of cemtech, particularly among older people, which may explain its outlier status. It was the only cemtech that more than a handful of respondents said they had personally used (or even heard about second-hand) and these comments were most common among older respondents. The prevalence of webcasted funerals in Australia has clearly been boosted by the Covid-19 pandemic, during which state governments placed limitations on funeral attendance.

4.2 Characteristics of socially accepted cemtech

In this section we summarise commonly mentioned reasons for positive and negative ratings of cemtech. We begin with the explanations that were most frequently provided for the positive ratings of the five highest-rated concepts. Following this, we describe several common criticisms that were levelled at various concepts. Finally, based on all of these comments, we draw out three perceived design characteristics that differentiate more socially accepted cemtech from less socially accepted cemtech.

The top five cemtech concepts— audio guide, funeral webcast, online memorial, grave-tending service and gravesite webcam —were typically praised for providing bereaved family and friends with access to pre-existing memorial rituals, such as attending the funeral and laying flowers at the gravesite. For most respondents, the ideal purpose of a cemtech was to broaden participation in these kinds of memorial rituals, and the ideal user was a close friend or relative who would have preferred to attend in person but was unable to do so due to distance, infirmity (such as visual impairment in the case of audio guide ) or some other barrier. This was expressed in a comment about the grave-tending service :

“It's a lovely idea to be able to leave flowers at a gravesite when you are unable to get there yourself. This may make individuals who have lost a relative feel like they are still able to be involved in important dates/anniversaries somewhat.”

Conversely, many of the negative responses criticised these technologies for discouraging in-person participation in mourning rituals (e.g. “People should be showing respects by attending in person”), or for opening up those rituals to people other than close friends and relatives (e.g. “I don't want the whole world to be able to view something which is very private”). The dichotomy between expanding and impeding existing memorialisation practices is explored in section 6.1.

Respondents were typically less favourable towards technologies that were thought to create new interaction opportunities for people who had not known the deceased. This was a frequent complaint in the responses to narrative audio beacons, narrative audio speakers, autobiographical audio, AR avatar, historical game and ghost game . Respondents generally expected these technologies to draw more people into the cemetery who would treat it like the wrong kind of public space. “A cemetery is not a museum / amusement park” was a common and often vehement refrain. For many respondents, the ideal cemtech was as unobtrusive as possible, creating the minimum of noise and activity at the cemetery. As one comment on the gravesite video concept put it:

“If people would appreciate a video display like that they should have it in their own home for personal reflection.”

It was evident that the public nature of the cemetery was in tension with a widely held principle that those experiencing grief should be given privacy and quiet. This principle was often equated with showing respect, both to mourners and to the dead. Cemtechs that were designed to make the cemetery a more interesting place to visit were perceived as a threat to privacy and solitude, and accordingly were often criticised as “disrespectful”. The tensions between public and private notions of the cemetery and between lively and restful representations of the dead are discussed in sections 6.2 and 6.3, respectively.

Four of the cemtech concepts received negative ratings from more than half of respondents: autobiographical audio, AR avatar, historical game and ghost game . These four cemtech concepts were criticised for creating new forms of interaction that were disconnected from traditional memorialisation rituals; they were often perceived to serve visitors who did not know the deceased more than family and friends; and they were expected to generate noise or lively activity that would disrupt the preferred atmosphere of quiet and solemn reflection. In all three of these respects, these unpopular cemtech concepts were the opposite of the technologies that received mostly positive responses ( audio guide, funeral webcast, online memorial and grave-tending service ).

Based on this analysis, we have identified three qualities that distinguish the most socially accepted cemtech concepts from the least socially accepted cemtech concepts. The most socially accepted forms of cemtech were those that:

  1. Extend familiar memorialisation practices rather than create new forms of interaction
  2. Serve relatives and friends of the deceased rather than wider publics
  3. Preserve a quiet and restful cemetery atmosphere rather than create conspicuous noise and activity

We summarise these qualities as familiarity, intimacy of user group and peacefulness. If a designer's primary goal is to maximise the social acceptability of their cemetery technology, they should take them as design principles. However, not every potential user will be best served by following these principles. This is a simplification of attitudes that obscures deep schisms in what respondents said they considered appropriate, desirable and beneficial in cemetery technology. In the following section, we explore four such schisms.

4.3.1 Expands/Impedes dichotomy.

“In circumstances where I was unable to undertake the tasks myself, I would feel I have honoured the person's memory” [expands]

“If you can't be bothered to do it yourself do you really care?” [impedes]

Respondents were conflicted over how technology affects the meaning and value of memorialisation practices. This was particularly prevalent in comments about cemtech that enabled remote access to cemetery locations (e.g. gravesite webcam, grave-tending service ) and events (e.g. funeral webcast ). It was also apparent in comments about on-site cemtech that altered the experience of visiting a loved one's grave (e.g. gravestone video ).

On the positive side, many respondents agreed that technology expands opportunities for memorialisation . For these people, using a digital service may not be as good as visiting the cemetery in person, but it was better than nothing. Comments frequently mentioned the Covid-19 pandemic as a time when it was sometimes impossible to visit a funeral or grave, and noted that remote-access cemtech was the next best thing in this situation. Funeral webcast was the only type of cemtech that many survey respondents said they had used personally, and nearly all of those respondents described it as beneficial. For example: “I have previously done this for a school friends funeral service and I felt like I was there.”

An oft-mentioned benefit of cemtech that had an online component (e.g. online memorial, virtual grave ) was that it could enable communal mourning practices between people who might rarely see each other in person. Some respondents particularly appreciated being able to share memories and stories. For example: “I would love a page to visit and keep updated for family that live further away and cannot visit often”. Others spoke about cemtech as a way to “feel present” with others even when unable to attend—or simply unwilling. A few participants admitted that they simply found it inconvenient or tiresome to visit the cemetery in person and would appreciate a lower-effort way to do so.

On the negative side of this dichotomy, many people said that technology impedes proper memorialisation . For these respondents, digital technology is not just inferior to physical practices, it actively interferes with them. A common complaint was that remote-attendance cemtech would displace in-person attendance, by allowing people to stay away who would otherwise have attended. This was described as disrespectful to the dead. That complaint was often paired with the idea that online communication is inherently inauthentic or impersonal. For example:

“it doesn't feel right to me. if you really love the person, you should be there instead. online is like for showing off only”

A similar sentiment was widely expressed about digital technologies at the cemetery itself, whether those were dedicated cemtech or personal devices such as smartphones. Quite a few respondents felt that technology was antithetical to the cemetery regardless of its contents. For example: “Technology and gravesites don't mix for me”. For some, this attitude was part of a general weariness with digital technology and a sense that it was “invading” all areas of life and “interfering with traditional things”. The idea that technology is in some sense “not real” was frequently mentioned.

Quite a few of the negative responses expressed the concern that cemtech would disrupt a healthy mourning process, leading to prolonged or unresolved grief for the user. This was generally framed in terms of the belief that mourners need to “move on”, and that it is harmful to dwell too long on memories of the dead. For these respondents, the purpose of visiting a grave was to learn to “accept and let go”, an ultimately to stop grieving for the deceased person; technology was thought to prevent this by discouraging the mourner from visiting the grave or by creating a sense in their mind that the dead person was still alive and present. (See also section 6.4.) This concern reflects a model of grief that was dominant in Australia in the twentieth century but has been challenged in recent decades [ 37 , 72 ].

4.3.2 Public/Private dichotomy.

“Graves should not be alienated. They should be places where more people visit and enjoy” [public]

“A grave is a personal thing not for the public” [private]

Underlying many of the responses were strongly held views about whether the cemetery should be a multi-use public space or a more constrained and narrowly utilised space for pseudo-private mourning. This was most apparent in comments about on-site cemtech (e.g. gravestone video ) and cemtech that catered to audiences other than relatives and friends of the deceased (e.g. historical game ).

On one side of this dichotomy, a substantial minority of respondents expressed the view that the cemetery should be an enjoyable public space . For them, creating new activities to do at the cemetery was appealing because it made the cemetery a more pleasant place to spend time. Narrative installations and mobile games were acceptable from this perspective as they would prompt people to visit the cemetery more often, encourage exercise, or give children something to do during family visits, as one respondent said of the ghost game : “[it] might entertain or distract the kids during a live interment”. Others commented positively about making the cemetery a more communal space. For example:

“It creates a sense of community and makes a cemetery a celebration of peoples lives. Without sharing these stories these deceased people exist only in the ground”

Learning about history was the most widely approved activity at the cemetery besides mourning a loved one. Cemeteries were recognised as heritage sites, where it made sense to hear stories about the past. This was evident in the higher level of support for the historical game compared to the ghost game . As one respondent said: “I love the idea of spending time in a cemetery and looking for historical graves”. Some respondents specified that they would only countenance game-playing if it was tied to learning history. Quite a few respondents said they would accept personal-narrative cemtech (e.g. narrative audio speakers ) at historical graveyards but not at active cemeteries where people still visited their loved ones. One such comment, in response to the narrative audio beacons idea:

“If it is a historical cemetery it makes sense but otherwise they an invasion of a families privacy and creepy”

On the opposite side of this dichotomy was the view that the cemetery should be a protected space for private grief . These respondents asserted that mourners needed to be left alone while visiting the cemetery so that they could grieve in quiet isolation. The judgement of this group was that cemtech should not be designed to attract people into the cemetery or towards the graves of anyone other than the user's own loved ones. Many described visiting a stranger's gravesite as snooping on grief. Some even considered the grave-tending service (through which a professional could be hired to maintain a grave) to be too intrusive: “I wouldn't want a stranger or random going to a loved ones site”. Similarly, many respondents considered it inappropriate for cemtech to publicly display or narrate details of a deceased's person's life. For example:

“Why should other people be told about the dead person. This should only be for family members”

The anti-public-space viewpoint was often asserted in opposition to the audio cemtech concepts ( narrative audio beacons, narrative audio speakers and autobiographical audio ), and even more so in opposition to the game concepts ( historical game and ghost game ). A common objection was that these technologies would turn the cemetery from a “place of solitude and reflection” into a busier type of public space—namely a “museum” or an “amusement park”. A representative example is this comment on the ghost game :

“A cemetery is place of peace and quiet for those grieving to reflect on the connection of the life they have experienced with the deceased. It is not a fun or theme park.”

It was not necessarily the appearance of the technology itself that was thought to change the cemetery atmosphere, as most of the audio and game concepts (with the exception of narrative audio speakers ) were described as being visually unobtrusive except in the form of their users’ phones, and the comments indicated this unobtrusiveness was generally understood. 3 Rather, the concern was that these cemtechs would change users’ behaviour at the cemetery by encouraging light-hearted activity, loud chatter and possibly a lack of care for the graves.

Finally on this theme, we note that there was a substantial third group of respondents who did not fit into this public/private dichotomy because they did not much care for the cemetery at all. This group tended to disapprove of cemtech not out of respect for the sanctity of graveyards or the privacy of mourners, but out of a general hostility to cemeteries and burials. They often disparaged cemeteries, funerals and cemtech as a “waste of money” or a “scam”. Some criticised burial as a poor way to come to terms with death. These respondents frequently mentioned that they or their relatives had chosen to be cremated rather than buried, and so found the idea of most cemtech to be superfluous to their experience of death and bereavement. For example:

“My loved one would disapprove of all of these. My loved ones chose cremation, I took their ashes into the country, stood on a big hill and let their ashes blow in the wind.”

This group generally held a low view of cemtech because it would encourage the continued use of the cemetery, rather than because it would interfere with the cemetery.

4.3.3 Lively/Restful dichotomy.

“Sometimes I wish I could see them in front of me just one more time” [lively]

“It is like you are trying to bring deceased people back to life and I find this disturbing” [restful]

Each of the cemtech concepts described in the survey represent the dead in some way, but the immediacy with which they would do so varied. The types of cemtech that provided remote access to the cemetery, such as grave-tending service and funeral webcast , represented the deceased person indirectly through the gravestone or the funeral, which are themselves relatively abstract forms of representation. The cemtech that presented digital narratives, such as narrative audio speakers and gravestone video , provided representations of the dead that were a more immediate part of the technology, although still generally second-hand through text or the voice of a storyteller. At the far end of the scale were cemtech that represented a deceased person in a higher fidelity, more interactive and more agential form—most notably AR avatar and autobiographical audio . The greater verisimilitude of these latter cemtechs produced strongly polarised comments, which stemmed from respondents’ views about how the dead should be represented.

Those who favoured these technologies said that the dead should be enlivened , in the sense of being presented in richer media forms, as interactive, or reanimated through a technological medium. They welcomed the chance to see the animated face or to hear the voice of someone who had passed, especially a person who had been dear to them. This was expected to bring happiness and comfort, and to soothe feelings of loss and disconnection. A typical positive comment on the autobiographical audio concept said:

“If it is your loved one you are visiting, makes you happy to hear their voice and feel connected”

A common sentiment was that these technologies could create a subjective sense that the deceased person was socially present and engaged in conversation with you. As one respondent said of the autobiographical audio concept: “you would feel like they are there with you [and] that could be special and beautiful for some”. Even less richly mediated cemtech received praise for the potential for an experience of social presence, such as this comment on the online memorial :

“I would love to see photos and videos of my loved one's life. Whenever I felt like I missed them I could jump online and watch it and visit their page and just leave messages as if I were speaking to them”

Positive comments also noted the ability of these representational technologies to create new memories or to preserve and strengthen existing memories. Often the expected beneficiaries were the bereaved. For example: “Could help a lot of people talk to their loved one once they are gone and have a deeper memory of that person”. Some also commended the prospect of creating memories of the deceased for people who had not known them, such as grandchildren. For instance, one respondent said that the AR avatar “would be awesome especially [for] those that never met the loved one”.

Not everyone favoured lifelike representations of the dead, however. Many of the most vociferous objections in the survey came from respondents who felt that the dead should be left to rest . In accordance with the sleep metaphor that permeates Western burial culture [ 32 ], these respondents primarily spoke about the dead as “resting”. They often attributed to the dead a sense of continuing awareness (e.g. “Let them have peace in the grave”) or continuing preferences (e.g. “They want to Rest In Peace not be displayed like a show”), and equated the act of representing a deceased person with rousing them from their slumber (e.g. “I personally don't like it, it likes waking up the death”). Regardless of how literally these statements were meant, it is evident that this is a mental model many people used to evaluate cemtech: viewing the dead as peaceful sleepers who can be rudely awakened by technology (“revived at will like a puppet show”). Through that mental model, the cemetery's purpose as a place of quiet slumber is antithetical to animating cemtech such as AR avatar and autobiographical audio :

“Rest In Peace (RIP) is the ultimate guiding factor here.”

A closely related attitude was that the correct way to show respect for the dead was to preserve an atmosphere conducive to slumber at their gravesite. This tied in with the view that silence and stillness were respectful to mourners, as bereaved people need quiet reflection while visiting the cemetery. Consequently, lively representations of the dead such as AR avatar and noisy media such as narrative audio speakers were frequently described as disrespectful both to those who were buried and to those who came to visit their graves.

4.3.4 Pragmatic/Affective dichotomy.

“Each individual to their own and that is up to the family. The question is how much will these extras cost and what are the ongoing maintenance costs for this feature?” [pragmatic]

“This would creep me out and make me feel that the cemetary was full of ghosts” [affective]

The final dichotomy concerns the degree of sentimentality in judgements about cemtech. For some respondents, it was implicit that cemtech should be judged on practical considerations . Their comments were often equivocal, acknowledging the appeal of the technology (for others if not for themselves) but querying what would be required to keep it running: operating costs, maintenance, compatibility with changing mobile devices, preventing vandalism and so on. Energy usage and potential energy savings were mentioned with some regularity, as in this response to the gravestone video :

“It does not have to run continuously and I would prefer a more environmental option, eg. using solar energy to have tombstone activate name of deceased”

Practical benefits were acknowledged as well as practical constraints. A few respondents praised the cemtech that provided a remote view of the grave (e.g. gravesite webcam ) or encouraged visitation (e.g. historical game ) as a way to deter vandalism or detect damage. Audio guide was widely lauded as a navigation aid, and a handful of comments noted that getting exercise was a benefit of the historical game or ghost game .

Respondents who gave relatively neutral ratings to a technology often said their opinion depended on how much it cost and who had to pay for it. These responses were typically cautiously positive, but wary about the financial implications, e.g. “It might be nice to ‘see’ the deceased but I think it would be expensive”. The grave-tending service was the only cemtech whose description mentioned money (“a professional service that you can hire for a small fee”), so this concept attracted a particularly high proportion of cost-related comments, such as “It would all depend on the cost”.

Some respondents acknowledged the possibility that a cemtech would elicit uncomfortable feelings or be perceived by others as disrespectful, but expressed a liberal attitude towards its use rather than deferring to those negative emotions. “Each to their own” was a frequent expression of this position. The subject of the memorial and the deceased's relatives were generally given priority, however (e.g. “As long as the deceased family agree to them being involved”). A few respondents noted that initial emotional reactions were likely to change as the technology ceased to be novel, as in this reaction to the virtual grave : “it feels a bit weird but maybe thats only because it is something new”. Some connected this to the Covid-19 pandemic as an event that has obliged the whole of society to adjust to an increased role for digital technology. For example:

“it may feel unnatural as it is different to the ‘norm’—but the ‘norm’ is significantly different now due to COVID and we need to adapt”

On the opposite side of this dichotomy were many who asserted that cemtech should be judged on emotional considerations . This was often expressed through reactions of disgust or unease. The word “creepy” alone was used by nearly a quarter of respondents 4 to describe at least one cemtech, usually accompanied by a “very negative” rating. Similarly affective terms such as “weird”, “unsettling”, “disturbing”, “spooky” and “scary” were also commonly used, often with no further explanation (e.g. “This is weird”). Some compared the more lifelike cemtech ideas to the experience of seeing a ghost, as in this response to the autobiographical audio :

“This concept sounds creepy as if you are sneaking up on a ghost who will later frighten you.”

Another concern was the emotional harm that cemtech might inflict on the bereaved. Respondents often worried that seeing, hearing or being reminded of the deceased would prolong feelings of grief. Some said that it was not healthy to “dwell on death”—sometimes extending this objection beyond cemtech to the cemetery in general, reflecting the common notion that grief is a process to be “worked through” so that the mourner can “move on” [ 72 ]. The problem of emotional harm was raised for both on-site and online cemtech, as in this comment about the virtual grave :

“I think it could become extremely toxic and make people obsessed with the idea of death”

Positive emotional effects were also cited. Various cemtech were described as providing feelings of comfort or relief from sadness, as in this response to the online memorial concept: “It would give me internal peace and help ease the ongoing sense of loss.” Some said that participating in a mourning ritual through technology, such as funeral webcast , would make it less overwhelming. A small number of comments mentioned fun and enjoyment, mostly in relation to the game concepts and the narrative audio concepts. Quite a few respondents noted that cemtech would help to relieve the guilt and loss they felt at not being able to visit loved ones who were buried far away. For example:

“Having loved ones buried in another country it would make me feel more content and happy to know that I could visit them this way and not feel so guilty about not being able to visit and take care of them more.”

There was not always a strict binary separation between pragmatic and affective ways of thinking about cemtech. Respondents sometimes switched between the two types of concerns in their responses to different concepts, and occasionally acknowledged both practical and emotional concerns within a single comment. For example:

“I think the technology would become outdated whereas the traditional grave is not. I think its a little creepy in some aspects but it would also make me a little sad.”

Nevertheless, there was a clear bimodal distribution in the sentimentality of the comments, and respondents often showed a tendency to favour one mindset over the other across all of their explanations.

5 DISCUSSION

The aim of this study is to measure and understand social acceptance of cemtech, a growing and often controversial area of human-computer interaction, through a survey of the Australian public. It makes four key contributions to knowledge on this topic. First, it establishes that different forms of cemtech have widely varying levels of social acceptance, from general approval to general disapproval. Second, it shows that younger people are more positive than older people towards nearly all forms of cemtech, with the exception of funeral webcasts . Third, it identifies three characteristics that make cemtech more socially accepted: familiarity, intimacy of user group and peacefulness. Fourth, it brings to light four core areas of public disagreement over what is appropriate and beneficial in cemtech design (summarised as Expands/Impedes, Public/Private, Lively/Restful and Pragmatic/Affective). Together, these findings provide a clear picture of the potential sources of controversy in cemtech design, and an indication of how social acceptance of a cemtech design may change across time and place or in the presence of different audiences.

For a designer of any kind of cemtech, these results contain reasons for both optimism and caution. Every concept presented in the survey received at least some enthusiastic support. Even the least popular, ghost game , was received positively by one in eight respondents, and was hailed in superlative terms (“excellent”, “amazing”, “a great idea!”, “I would 100% purchase this game and use it!”) by a few—including several who expressed a general enthusiasm for cemeteries and one who identified themselves as a cemetery caretaker. However, the results also show that cemtech users may risk encountering disapproval from wider publics, and that this disapproval could be exceedingly hostile. One response to the ghost game even hinted at violence: “It would be difficult to restrain myself from aggression against any person seen ‘playing’ this way”. It will be important for designers to be sensitive to these negative sentiments, to minimise both the risk of emotional distress for onlookers and the risk of unpleasant social interactions for users.

Technologies materialise an imagined world, and making this world explicit is important in technology assessment. Our thematic binaries have made an important, novel contribution in identifying the imagined worlds (and their cemtech) that are particularly polarising. This contribution will be of interest to cemetery designers and to cemtech designers. Polarising cemtech are those that imagine a world in which cemeteries expand their reach beyond their physical boundaries, or do not; activate the space for increased visitation, or do not; animate the biographies and personhood of the dead, or do not; and imagine the cemetery in functional terms or in emotional terms.

The divided social attitudes reflected in these dichotomies complicate the task of engaging in “thanatosensitive design” [ 49 ] for cemtech. Just as Massimi et al. [ 50 ] note that the needs of bereaved individuals vary over time, our research shows that these needs also vary between bereaved individuals. Different respondents gave directly contradictory statements about how cemtech design would affect their grief. This may lead to a sense that designers should simply defer to those who are most resistant to innovation and change—but as we have discussed in section 2.1, the cemetery is not and cannot be the timeless memorial landscape that many would prefer it to be. The history of death and burial is a story of contentious social and technological changes [ 46 , 76 ]. Disputes over the proper use of graveyards and memorial rites have been a recurrent feature of Australian social life since European colonisation [ 36 ], and analog technologies that are now mainstays of “traditional” practice—such as lawn cemeteries, crematoria and mausolea—were the subjects of vociferous debate within the past century [ 37 ]. But not all innovations are eventually accepted, and many memorial technologies have foundered on the rocks of public disapproval [ 55 ]. This research provides a map of those shoals so that designers may avoid or prepare for them.

5.1 Design strategies for socially acceptable cemtech

If a designer's goal is to maximise social acceptance of their cemtech, our findings suggest three clear principles to follow in most cases. First, the technology should extend or expand access to familiar memorialisation practices rather than create an unfamiliar new practice. Second, it should be seen to cater to family and friends rather than people who did not share a social bond with the deceased. Third, it should preserve a restful and quiet atmosphere at the cemetery rather than create conspicuous activity or noise. These principles all broadly speak to an ethos of unobtrusiveness and subtlety, which is a common design strategy for social acceptability in HCI research [ 43 ].

Another strategy, suggested by respondents, is to deploy cemtech in places where it is only likely to be encountered by a narrow and appropriate audience—whomever that may be. For instance, many respondents expressed discomfort at an education-oriented cemtech such as narrative audio speakers being used near graves that mourners visit, but indicated that it would be appropriate at a historical cemetery without recent burials. Conversely, some respondents said that personal representations of the dead, such as the gravestone video , were fine for family members but not for strangers, and so should not be in a public cemetery space. If a cemtech is going to be used at an active cemetery still visited by the bereaved, it should be cautiously designed to minimise its effects on the ambiance of the site. That does not only mean making the technology itself unobtrusive or consonant with the existing atmosphere, such as by delivering sound through headphones rather than speakers; it also means thinking about the technology's influence on the behaviour of its users, and trying to avoid prompting users to act in ways that break social norms about conduct at the cemetery.

5.2 Will cemtech become more acceptable?

The survey provides a snapshot of beliefs and attitudes about cemtech during a particular moment in time, notably in the midst of a global pandemic, and the results it describes may not remain stable over time. Indeed, our analysis provides two reasons to predict that the types of technology presented in the study will become more broadly accepted. The fact that younger respondents were more positive than older respondents towards all but one cemtech concept means that generational change will make these ideas more accepted, if people simply retain the attitudes they currently hold. The exception to this rule— funeral webcast —is also instructive, as it appears to have been so broadly accepted precisely because it was familiar, for older respondents as well as younger ones. This is congruent with the finding that technologies were more accepted when they facilitated familiar, expected cemetery activities. (It is also tentatively supported by Uriu et al.’s [ 69 ] finding that a funeral webcast through Zoom was favoured over a 360-degree video version, although the result in that study could be due to technical difficulties with the latter.) From this, we may predict that other forms of cemtech could also receive a warmer response after they have a chance to become familiar—as funeral webcast received during the pandemic.

5.3 How generalisable are the findings?

As our survey was conducted in Australia, its results may differ from the prevailing social attitudes of other places. Australia's death culture is influenced by a range of factors, including: having been a colony of the British Empire on land that was taken violently from its existing inhabitants; being relatively unreligious; having a high proportion of first-generation and second-generation migrants from a diverse range of cultures; and having few historical cemeteries (of the modern European kind) where new burials are no longer taking place [ 36 , 39 ]. Funerary practices vary considerably between and within countries [ 73 ], and social acceptance of cemetery practices and technologies may vary as well.

However, as we have noted above, our findings do accord with those of some smaller studies conducted in other countries. The three design qualities we found to be most socially accepted (i.e. familiarity, intimacy of user group and peacefulness) were also found to be highly valued features of the cemetery in studies conducted in Norway [ 56 ] and Lebanon [ 1 ]. Our findings also reflect many of the results of Häkkilä et al.’s [ 29 ] three user studies in Finland, from the overall pattern of cemtech acceptance to more specific participant comments (such as the suggested utility of digital gravestone displays for allowing children to form new memories of their deceased grandparents). This indicates that the conclusions of our research are at least somewhat cross-cultural.

Conversely, our results will not be perfectly representative of every location and cultural group within Australia. Our survey sample ensures that the results may be generalised to the country as a whole, but individual cemeteries serve local communities that differ from the makeup of our sample. Designing technology for a cemetery that serves a minority community, in particular, would require its own study. There is scope for additional research on social acceptance of cemtech across demographic variables including, age, gender, religion and culture.

5.4 Limitations and future work

The design review that informed our survey (detailed in section 3.1) was conducted in English, using Google Translate to read sources that were written in other languages. Possibly as a result of this, the majority of cemtech examples we found were from majority English-speaking countries. This may have biased the range of cemtech concepts reflected in the typology and therefore in the survey.

The cemtech ratings could be influenced to some degree by an order effect. We randomised the order in which the survey presented individual concepts, but displayed all the remote-access (web-based) concepts first, before the on-site (cemetery-based) concepts. This could have influenced the ratings of the latter in a positive or negative direction.

Future work could provide a deeper understanding of the social acceptability of cemtech through research methods that present the concepts in a more detailed or realistic form, as well as through methods that allow participants to ask follow-up questions and explore alternative versions of the idea. Such methods could include field interviews, co-design workshops, prototyping studies or cultural probes. In particular, research conducted on-site at cemeteries may prompt different kinds of responses than an online survey.

The prevalence of words such as “creepy”, “weird”, “spooky” and “scary” in the survey responses merits further investigation. These terms were primarily used to explain negative ratings—often presented as self-evident descriptions with no further elaboration. A similar reaction was observed by Chen et al. [ 12 ] in response to an AI-driven memorial avatar. A follow-up study using an instrument such as the Perceived Creepiness of Technology Scale [ 80 ] could provide a better picture of why certain forms of cemtech elicit such a reaction, and thereby help designers to avoid it (unless it is their intention).

6 CONCLUSION

We surveyed a representative sample of the Australian population ( n =1,053) to measure and understand social acceptance of cemtech. Our analysis of the survey responses makes four key contributions that will be of interest to cemetery managers and cemtech designers. First, we show that different types of cemtech have widely varying levels of social acceptance. Second, we find that younger people are more approving of nearly all forms of cemtech than older people, with the exception of the most familiar form: funeral webcasts. Third, we derive three design principles for making cemtech more socially acceptable: familiarity, intimacy of user group and peacefulness. Fourth, we identify four areas of polarised opinion about cemtech: whether it facilitates or impedes memorialisation; whether it should or should not activate the cemetery space for increased visitation; whether it should or should not animate the deceased; and whether it should be judged on primarily functional or emotional terms. These dichotomies map the potential controversies designers need to navigate and address when developing new cemetery technologies. The findings of this research will help designers to achieve greater social acceptance for digital technologies in the cemetery and other public commemorative spaces.
