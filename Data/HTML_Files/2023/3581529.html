Matching Mind and Method
Augmented Decision-Making with Digital Companions based on Regulatory Mode Theory

Stefan Tretter , LMU Munich, Germany, stefan.tretter@lmu.de
Axel Platz , Siemens AG, Germany, axel.platz@siemens.com
Sarah Diefenbach , LMU Munich, Germany, sarah.diefenbach@psy.lmu.de

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581529
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Digital companions shall augment complex human processes like extensive decision-making. However, their acceptance may depend upon their ability to adapt to individuals’ psychological states and preferred decision strategies. Regulatory Mode Theory divides human self-regulation into assessment (i.e., making comparisons) and locomotion (i.e., movement from state to state). These regulatory modes are more or less compatible with different decision strategies. In an experimental study (N=81, 2x2-between-subjects design) we explored whether digital companions can gain higher acceptance by considering these compatibilities. Participants were confronted with a decision task. The assisting digital companion first induced a regulatory mode (assessment vs. locomotion) and subsequently presented information according to one of two decision strategies (full evaluation vs. progressive elimination). We show that a fit between regulatory mode and decision strategy (assessment/full evaluation or locomotion/progressive elimination) leads to a favorable evaluation of decisions and the digital companion. No differences regarding decision accuracy and speed were observed.

1 INTRODUCTION

A major theme in the integration of technologies into human behavior is the balance between automation and augmentation [ 3 , 9 , 30 ]. As technologies get more and more elaborate, they become capable of taking over well-defined tasks, leading to the increasing automation of former manual processes. But at the same time, there will always be tasks that the human user has to—or wants to—be in charge of. Here, the aim of assistant technologies is not to take over but purposefully augment the user just like a collaborator. The question of how to augment “the right way” is heavily discussed in the work context (e.g., [ 16 , 22 ]) but extends as well to everyday applications, as the proliferation of smart devices and assistant technologies indicates (e.g., [ 17 , 31 , 52 ]).

To describe the notion of a symbiosis between human and artificial supporter, the term “digital companions” has been coined, which captures technologies that are “able to smartly adapt their functionality to the individual user's requirements and current needs; adequately react to changes of context and changes of the environment that might impair an effective user-system interaction; be sensitive to the user's emotional state and disposition; and conduct helpful and informative dialogs.“ (p. 11; [ 6 ]).

A promising field of such human-technology-collaborations is the decision-making process [ 16 ]. While there are many everyday situations, trivial (e.g., what to pick from a menu) or substantial (e.g., who to marry), that call for intuitive decision-making, we often also encounter decisions that require the consideration of data in conjunction with our subjective evaluations (e.g., which car to buy). According to standard decision-stage-models (e.g., [ 21 ]), four stages lead up to a decision: (1) problem recognition, (2) information search, (3) formation of a consideration set, (4) evaluation of alternatives, (5) choice. For example, people who are looking for an apartment to rent are used to browsing the web for information and more or less quickly end up with a defined set of potential alternatives. But they are seldom supported in the more difficult part of the process of evaluating similar alternatives and coming to a decision. Sometimes, comparison portals at least provide a comprehensive table and filters, but they do not consider relative importance ratings of individuals and the mindset they are making their decision in. This is where a supporting system that incorporates users’ individual preferences and adapts to their psychological state (i.e., a digital companion) would ensure positive user experiences and comfort in the rather complex task of decision-making [ 8 ].

We conducted an experimental study to investigate how a digital companion that considers psychological principles of human self-regulation affects the decision-making process, its evaluation, and the attitude toward the employed technology. Based on Regulatory Mode Theory [ 20 ], which proposes two independent self-regulatory processes that operate in human goal pursuit, we implemented a text-based companion that, first, induces a preference for one of those regulatory modes within the user, and, second, presents the available information according to one of two decision strategies. We hypothesize and subsequently show that matching the induced regulatory mode with a complementing decision strategy has significant effects on the subjective evaluation of the decision made and the digital companion itself.

In general, our work offers several contributions to HCI that are located at the intersection of digital companions, augmented decision-making, and Regulatory Mode Theory (Figure 1 ). First, it brings Regulatory Mode Theory to HCI, particularly the design of digital companions, and explores how the adaptation of supportive actions to the psychological state of a user can improve the system's evaluation. Second, it demonstrates how the premise of sensitivity to the user that characterizes digital companions can inform the design of augmented decision-making systems. And third, it provides a case study of why augmented decision-making should not only focus on (allegedly) objective decision quality, but also subjective evaluation criteria like the users’ confidence in their decisions.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Augmented Decision-Making

2.1.1 Decision-Making Paradigms. . Within behavioral decision theory, two different theoretical strands can be distinguished, the normative and the descriptive [ 47 ]. Normative theory revolves around prescribing the procedure that leads to the best decision, i.e., the optimal outcome in terms of the respective individual's values and beliefs. Descriptive theory, on the other hand, describes the manner in which those values and beliefs are incorporated into individuals’ decision-making. In other words, normative theory is concerned with how decisions should be made, whereas descriptive theory is about how decisions are actually made. In order to aid human decision-making in the sense of digital companions, both strands have to be integrated. Knowledge about how people make decisions and what psychological processes take place is necessary to configure technological systems that support optimal courses of action.

Another important distinction to be made when dealing with the circumstances of human decision-making is the pre-existing knowledge about possible outcomes and their probabilities. Conceptually, decisions can be either made under certainty, risk, or uncertainty [ 24 , 26 , 51 ]. In case of certainty, outcomes of choices are clearly defined, we just have to decide on the choice that maximizes our outcome, e.g., which dish on the menu brings us the most pleasure [ 24 ]. Decisions under risk occur in situations, where the probabilities of outcomes for all options are known, e.g., lotteries or poker. In these cases, it is possible to calculate optimal courses of action under given circumstances [ 51 ]. Then there are decisions under uncertainty, where probabilities are unknown or even unknowable. Most decisions in our everyday life fall into this category. In contrast to decisions under risk, which happen in a confined space, we do not know all alternatives, consequences, and probabilities when we, for example, plan a date, choose our partner, or schedule a wedding [ 51 ].

The examples above already illustrate why the adaptive nature of a digital companion is a necessary capacity for successful human-technology interaction. Decisions under certainty might not require any interference by a companion, as situations are relatively easy to process and/or dependent on the subjective preferences of the decision maker. Decisions under risk are probably more mentally challenging but since there is an optimal choice, processing various options could be taken over by a companion. Decisions under uncertainty, though, would most likely profit from an interplay between the computational capacity of a digital companion and the intuition, empathy, broad judgment, and complex reasoning capability of its human user [ 16 , 22 ].

2.1.2 Acceptance of Augmentation. . One challenge accompanying the development of productive and ethically sound human-computer collaboration is the tension between augmentation and automation, i.e., which tasks or how much of a task can be delegated to technologies [ 41 ]. Users can employ digital assistants that either augment a task (i.e., support the user) or automate it (i.e., perform the task autonomously) [ 3 ]. According to AI job replacement theory, artificial intelligence will even develop intuitive and empathetic intelligence and take over corresponding tasks, but for the time being, they are rather limited to application areas of mechanical and analytical intelligence [ 22 ]. Beyond that, they are best used by augmenting people with their computational capacity, complementing areas of human expertise [ 12 ].

One of the most important aspects of human life is making decisions. The notion of augmenting (or automating) human decision-making has been around for at least half a century and today finds itself in practice in areas like diagnostic medicine or business management. The current ideal is that of a human actor in the “driver seat”, while artificial intelligence provides input or substitutes individual tasks, thereby complementing rather than replacing its user [ 16 ]. Likewise, the requirement for a digital companion is that it enhances the decision-making capabilities of its user instead of just taking over [ 27 ]. However, this stands and falls with the individual willingness to accept such decision aids. In their systematic review, Burton and colleagues [ 9 ] identify five pitfalls for the acceptance of algorithmic decision-making: expectations and expertise (e.g., false expectations about what an algorithmic aid is capable of), decision autonomy (e.g., the proportion of human control within the process), divergent rationalities (e.g., different conceptions of what makes a rational choice), incentivization (e.g., lack of motivation to use the system), and cognitive compatibility (e.g., inherent differences in how human and algorithm end up at a decision).

The present study addresses the last two challenges. By varying how an assistant addresses the user and provides information, we investigate how this shapes a user's satisfaction with the system, which arguably fosters their willingness to use it, regardless of external incentivization. Furthermore, we apply psychological theory to establish compatibility between the cognitive state of a user and the way the system presents available information. We thereby follow the notion that “for real-world progress to be made there needs to be at least an equal emphasis on theory integration as there has been on theory generation” (p. 10; [ 9 ]).

2.2 Regulatory Mode Theory

2.2.1 Assessment and Locomotion. . Regulatory Mode Theory is a theory of the constituent functions that underlie human self-regulation. To “self-regulate”, in this context, means to govern or direct one's attention, resources, or action in terms of goal pursuits [ 20 ]. There are basically two processes in self-regulation: on the one hand, comparing and selecting desired end-states and means to attain them, and on the other hand, initiating and maintaining movement from the current state towards the desired end-state [ 29 ]. In other words, there is the process of deciding what we want and how to get it (i.e., making comparisons), referred to as “assessment mode”, and the process of actually doing something about it (i.e., movement from state to state), referred to as “locomotion mode” [ 20 ]. Or, as Kruglanski and colleagues [ 29 ] framed it, whether people follow the motto of “Do the Right Thing” or “Just Do It”.

Regulatory Mode Theory considers assessment and locomotion as independent motivational orientations [ 29 ]. That means that both regulatory modes can be high (or low) at the same time, but people may also emphasize one over the other. The strength of their activation at any point in time depends on chronic individual differences—people may be high assessors or locomotors by nature—but also on people's current activity or situation [ 20 ]. This is important, as it allows us to shape contextual factors in a way that promotes either assessment or locomotion mode.

The consideration of regulatory modes is crucial here, as it has direct implications for judgment and decision-making [ 34 ]. For example, people in a dominant assessment—compared to locomotion—mode act less impulsive and more far-sighted [ 32 ], take fewer risks because of anticipated regret [ 38 ], and prioritize accuracy (over speed) in processing information to maximize feelings of thoroughness [ 33 ]. Paramount to the present study though is that regulatory mode also affects our preferences toward different decision-making strategies [ 20 ].

2.2.2 Strategic Fit. . Two central features that decision strategies can be classified according to are whether they are (1) alternative- or attribute-based (i.e., process one attribute across several alternatives or vice versa) and (2) compensatory or non-compensatory (i.e., let advantages of an alternative compensate for its disadvantages—or not) [ 39 ]. An example of an alternative-based, compensatory strategy is the so-called weighted additive rule, where the overall value of an alternative is “calculated” by adding up the relative importance of all its attributes. The alternative with the highest overall value is chosen [ 5 ]. A representative of the attribute-based, non-compensatory strategies is the elimination-by-aspects rule, where the most important attribute and a cut-off value for this attribute are defined first. Then all alternatives below the threshold are discarded and the same procedure is applied to the second most important attribute. This continues until only one alternative is left [ 5 ].

Regulatory Mode Theory suggests that each of the two distinct regulatory modes favors another strategic principle [ 20 ]. A “full evaluation” strategy, where all available alternatives are simultaneously compared, better suits the assessment orientation, which emphasizes exhaustive and thorough consideration of all alternatives. A “progressive elimination” strategy, where alternatives are successively compared and eliminated due to single attributes, better suits the locomotion orientation, as this pronounces a feeling of progressive movement. Avnet and Higgins [ 1 ] once put this association under scrutiny by experimentally inducing participants with an assessment or locomotion mode and instructing them to either use a full evaluation or progressive elimination strategy to choose among five different brands of travel booklights. They assumed that people would experience a “regulatory fit” [ 19 ] when regulatory mode and decision strategy would match accordingly.

This regulatory fit is a well-documented phenomenon that occurs when the means of goal pursuit fit the currently dominant regulatory orientation [ 2 ]. In those cases, people experience a sense of “feeling right” about what they are doing, which gives subjective value to their current activity. This value may also transfer to its outcome, so that the positive feeling with “how” a choice is made spills over to “what” was chosen [ 20 ]. The aforementioned study by Avnet and Higgins [ 1 ] showed exactly that. People in assessment mode who used a full evaluation strategy as well as people in locomotion mode who used a progressive elimination strategy were willing to pay over 40% more for the same book-light compared to people who applied a “non-fit” strategy (note: the sample of available book lights was pre-tested so that all participants ended up choosing the same book light to ensure comparability).

We aim to bring this effect to HCI by investigating whether such a positive transfer effect also applies to the means involved (i.e., the technology), not only the end (i.e., the chosen alternative). Research has shown that regulatory fit may give subjective value to an activity itself and its outcome, but does this also apply to the tools used? Additionally, our study differs from previous works as it employs an organic induction of regulatory fit through the technology itself and, even more importantly, provides a more realistic decision task, where not one single option is unanimously the best but people can vary in their personal preferences.

3 THE PRESENT STUDY

With the goal in mind that digital companions should adapt to the user's inner states and support them with their decision-making, they could, on the one hand, exert influence on the current regulatory mode of users, and, on the other hand, shape the decision architecture in a way that supports a certain strategy. If companions were able to create a regulatory fit by matching mode and strategy, the resulting positive feeling could not only increase decision satisfaction but also transfer to an improved user experience.

To investigate this, we conducted a study where a prototypical companion system induced one of the two regulatory modes by advising the user accordingly and afterward presented information for a decision task in a way that corresponds to a full evaluation or progressive elimination strategy. Based on prior research, we propose two hypotheses, stating that such a companion-created regulatory fit leads to a better subjective evaluation of decisions (H1) and the technology itself (H2) compared to non-fit conditions.

H1 – Decision Evaluation : Users in an assessment mode that follow a full evaluation strategy and users in a locomotion mode that follow a progressive elimination strategy evaluate their decisions more positively than users in an assessment mode that follow a progressive elimination strategy and users in a locomotion mode that follow a full evaluation strategy.

H2 – Companion Evaluation : Users in an assessment mode that follow a full evaluation strategy and users in a locomotion mode that follow a progressive elimination strategy evaluate the companion more positively than users in an assessment mode that follow a progressive elimination strategy and users in a locomotion mode that follow a full evaluation strategy.

Figure 2 contains a comprehensive graphical depiction of expected fit conditions. As regulatory fit effects are solely subjective phenomena, but “feeling right” in what you do might also affect how accurately and fast people act, we are also interested in investigating corresponding research questions.

RQ1 – Decision Accuracy : Are decisions more accurate when made under conditions of regulatory fit ?

RQ2 – Decision Speed : Are decisions faster when made under conditions of regulatory fit ?

4 METHOD

4.1 Participants

The present study was conducted as an online experiment with the software EFS Survey from Tivian . A total of 81 participants from Germany, Austria, and Switzerland were recruited with the crowdsourcing platform clickworker.de . The study was conducted in German, all reported materials were later translated into English. Prerequisites were fluency in German and an age of at least 18 years. The average age was 38 years ( M = 38.10; SD = 11.59; Max = 63). 56.8% of participants were male and 43.2% were female. The average time to completion was 572 seconds, for which participants received a compensation of 1.40€. To ensure data quality, only those participants were included who passed an attention check item that asked them to skip a following dummy question on what is the most important attribute of an apartment for them.

4.2 Design

Participants were randomly assigned to one of four conditions in a 2x2 between-subjects design. The two independent variables were (1) the regulatory mode (assessment vs. locomotion) the companion induced and (2) the decision strategy (full evaluation vs. progressive elimination) the companion supported. Thus, there were two conditions of companion-created regulatory fit (i.e., assessment and full evaluation; locomotion and progressive elimination) and two non-fit conditions (i.e., assessment and progressive elimination; locomotion and full evaluation) (Figure 2 ).

4.3 Procedure

After giving a consent agreement according to data protection laws, participants were introduced to the details of the study. They were told that they would have to decide between several apartment offerings. In making their decision, they would be supported by a text-based assistant system called “COMPANION” that interacts with them and is able to individually adjust the decision process to its respective user. To this end, COMPANION was first going to ask them for some personal information, before giving advice on how to approach the decision and presenting available data in a customized way. The term COMPANION was also used in the original German study, as it provides a product-like name and conveys a certain meaning due to its similarity to the French-German loan word “Kompagnon”.

Initially, COMPANION asked participants for information about themselves by rating several statements according to how much they agreed with them. Those statements were drawn from the German Locomotion-Assessment-Questionnaire and were actually used to measure each participant's chronic regulatory mode as a control variable (see Materials – Chronic Regulatory Mode). Subsequently, the system also asked them to rate several criteria of an apartment (e.g., size, rent, commute) according to how important they are to them personally.

After this request phase, the decision-making phase was initiated. At first, COMPANION addressed its user with a piece of advice on which mindset they should approach their decision process with. This was used to induce assessment or locomotion mode (see Materials – Regulatory Mode Induction). Then the system proceeded with the decision task. In both conditions, COMPANION presented participants with information on the potential apartments, sorted according to their previous importance ratings. But in the full evaluation conditions, people were shown all of the available information at once and should choose their preferred option. In the progressive elimination conditions, however, people were successively presented with parts of information, and could exclude options in each round until only one was left (see Materials – Decision Task).

After coming up with their decision, participants were asked to rate their mood during the decision phase, as well as how much they agree to statements regarding the evaluation of their decision and the COMPANION technology. Finally, participants reported their age and gender.

4.4 Materials

4.4.1 Chronic Regulatory Mode. . The German Locomotion-Assessment-Questionnaire [ 46 ] was used to assess the dispositional strength of both regulatory modes within an individual. The questionnaire comprises 22 statements to which participants report their agreement on a six-point scale (1 – strongly disagree; 6 – strongly agree). An example item for the locomotion dimension is “When I decide to do something, I can't wait to get started.”, for the assessment dimension “I like evaluating other people's plans.” The attained scores were later applied as control variables to ensure that observed effects were due to the experimental manipulation and not personality traits.

4.4.2 Regulatory Mode Induction. . In order to manipulate the currently dominant regulatory mode, we adapted the appeal used by Scholer and Higgins [ 45 ], who quoted an expert on decision-making in their study (p. 121). Accordingly, our companion advised its users in a certain way. In the assessment condition, the companion gave the following advice:

“Think on the end before you begin! When individuals are stuck in an ambivalent state, it's most effective to adopt a ‘do it right’ attitude. This attitude helps you to get to the ‘right place’ by allowing for careful contemplation about whether to stay in the current state or make a change.”

Conversely, in the locomotion condition, the companion advised the user:

“One who moves not forward goes backward! When individuals are stuck in an ambivalent state, it's most effective to adopt a ‘just do it’ attitude. This attitude helps you to get ‘unstuck’ by prompting action and progress—whether to maintain action in the current state or to make a change.”

4.4.3 Decision Task. . The decision task resembled paradigms applied in the study of unconscious thought effects [ 14 , 49 ] and closely that of Newell and colleagues [ 35 ]. In the present study, we created a table of data on six different apartments, with each row representing an attribute according to which the alternatives are evaluated (Table 1 ). The premise of this decision board was to comprise enough information to require an extensive deliberation process, while covering a subject that people can relate to.

Before the actual decision phase, participants rated those criteria in isolation depending on how important they generally weigh them in their opinion about an apartment (ten-point scale; 1 – not important at all; 10 – extremely important). In the full evaluation conditions, this table was presented as a whole, with the personally most important attribute at the top and the least important attribute at the bottom. On this basis, participants made their decision. In the progressive elimination conditions, the information was also sorted according to its importance for the individual, but the table was presented successively in smaller chunks. First, participants saw how all of the apartments were rated on their most important attribute. Then, they decided which apartments to keep before continuing with the second most important attribute. If two attributes were rated equally important previously, both were presented at the same time. This process continued until only one apartment was left (or no more information was available, in which case participants would have had to choose between the remaining apartments).

4.4.4 Mood. . Mood during the choice task was assessed with five items taken from Avnet and Higgins [ 1 ]. Participants responded on a seven-point scale on how positive, pleasant, content, happy, and good they felt (1 – not at all; 7 – extremely). This was used to control for simple affect-induced variance in the statistical analyses.

4.4.5 Decision Evaluation. . Participants evaluated their decision by indicating their consent to three statements on a seven-point scale (1 – not at all; 7 – extremely): “I am satisfied with the decision I made.”, “I am convinced that I made the best choice.”, and “It was easy for me to make a decision.”

4.4.6 Companion Evaluation. . Participants evaluated the digital companion by reporting how much they agreed to another five statements on a seven-point scale (1 – not at all; 7 – extremely): “COMPANION helped me to make a decision.”, “COMPANION was useful in the decision-making process.”, “I consider COMPANION to be trustworthy.”, “I would use COMPANION for further decisions.”, and “I took COMPANION's advice to heart when making my decision.”

4.4.7 Decision Accuracy. . Decision accuracy was captured by comparing the actual decision with the decision a rational actor with infinite computational capacity would choose. This has been considered in many ways the gold standard of decision analysis [ 9 ]. To this end, we calculated for each individual an overall value score of each apartment. We did this by adding the individual positive attribute ratings and then subtracting the negative attribute ratings [ 35 ]. For example, suppose an individual rated the importance of apartment size with nine points. If an apartment is spacious, this would add nine points to the overall score of that particular apartment. On the contrary, if an apartment is small, these nine points would instead be deducted from its overall score. This way, we ended up with a comparable score for the apartment an individual chose and all the apartments they could have chosen (with the optimal choice having the highest score). If someone chose the objectively highest-scoring apartment, then the difference score between the actual and optimal choice would have been zero. If someone deviated from their optimal choice, this difference score would have been higher the further the chosen alternative is away from the optimal choice. This allowed us to put a number on an individual's decision accuracy (the lower, the better).

4.4.8 Decision Speed. . The objective criterion of decision speed was calculated by using time stamps generated by the utilized survey software. We subtracted the time when participants started the actual decision task from the time when they arrived at a final decision.

Self-report variables (i.e., decision evaluation, companion evaluation, mood) were normally distributed in each cell according to Shapiro-Wilk tests. Although Likert-type scales are basically of ordinal nature, we conducted parametric tests, as this is still common practice in current HCI research (e.g., [ 42 , 43 , 53 ]). However, we also performed these tests on average-rank-transformed data [ 11 ], which led to the same conclusions.

The exploratory variables of decision accuracy and speed did not follow a clear normal distribution according to Shapiro-Wilk tests (which is not surprising as they were calculated by subtraction of two indicators). However, simulation studies show that ANOVA is robust against violation of the normality assumption [ 7 , 44 ] and bears lesser risks than more sophisticated approaches [ 25 ]. Therefore, all the following results are based on two-way analyses of variance. Additional correlations are given as Pearson correlation coefficients.

5.1 Decision Evaluation (H1)

We analyzed the evaluation of made decisions with a 2 (regulatory mode: assessment vs. locomotion) by 2 (decision strategy: full evaluation vs. progressive elimination) ANOVA. As expected, we found a significant interaction effect between both variables ( F (1, 77) = 5.303, p = .024, partial η2 = .064). Thus, people evaluated their companion-supported decision more favorably, when the decision strategy matched the induced regulatory mode. This effect even increased when we controlled for people's mood and their chronic regulatory mode orientations ( F (1, 74) = 9.441, p = .003, partial η2 = .113).

5.2 Companion Evaluation (H2)

We conducted the same analysis for H2 with companion evaluation as the dependent variable. Again, as predicted, there was a significant effect of the interaction between regulatory mode and decision strategy ( F (1, 77) = 4.937, p = .029, partial η2 = .060). This interaction effect also persisted when we controlled for mood and chronic regulatory mode orientation ( F (1, 74) = 5.250, p = .025, partial η2 = .066). Thus, people were more satisfied with the companion system in the fit conditions than in the non-fit conditions.

5.3 Decision Accuracy (RQ1)

In order to explore potential effects on the objective quality of decisions we re-ran our analytic procedure with regard to the difference between the calculated value scores of optimal and actual apartment choices. Regulatory mode and decision strategy did not show a significant interaction effect on decision accuracy ( F (1, 76) = 0.101, p = .751, partial η2 = .001). This was still the case when we controlled for mood and chronic regulatory mode orientations ( F (1, 73) = 0.030, p = .863, partial η2 < .001).

5.4 Decision Speed (RQ2)

We repeated the analysis another time to gauge potential differences in the time it took participants to come to a decision. Analogous to decision accuracy, decision speed also showed no interaction effect of regulatory mode and decision strategy, regardless of whether we controlled for mood and chronic regulatory mode orientation ( F (1, 74) = 2.873, p = .094, partial η2 = .037) or not ( F (1, 77) = 2.527, p = .116, partial η2 = .032).

5.5 Exploratory Analysis

An additional inspection of correlations among the dependent variables brings up complementary evidence for our theoretical rationale and empirical data. Our previous results show that a “mode-strategy-fit” boosts a favorable evaluation of the decision as well as the digital companion. Accordingly, decision and companion evaluation are positively related within the data ( r = .461, p < .001). While both might also influence one another, we argue that this is due to the transfer of value from how a decision is made (under fit or non-fit conditions) to its outcomes (the decision itself) and the means that are employed thereby (the companion). Moreover, it is not surprising that the experience of such a fit, i.e., a feeling of “being right”, can come with a corresponding affective reaction, which is reflected in the positive correlation of mood with decision evaluation ( r = .587, p < .001) and companion evaluation ( r = .423, p < .001). That observation also emphasizes the importance of controlling for mood in statistical analyses to prove that there is an effect of cognitive compatibility that goes beyond positive affect. On a side note, we can only speculate about why positive mood is higher in people with a stronger chronic locomotion orientation ( r = .387, p < .001). Maybe making decisions, or participating in the experiment in general, gives people a feeling of progress that appeals to locomotion-oriented individuals in particular, thereby lifting their mood.

6 DISCUSSION

Our study applied the principles of Regulatory Mode Theory [ 20 ] to the design of decision-augmenting digital companions and investigated effects of regulatory (non-)fit on subjective as well as objective criteria. Results show that people evaluated their decisions and the supporting companion more favorably, when they followed a full evaluation strategy in assessment mode or a progressive elimination strategy in locomotion mode (compared to cases where the other strategy was used, respectively). With regard to the objective criteria of decision accuracy and speed, we did not observe any significant differences.

6.1 Matching Mindset and Decision Method

Given that decisions were not objectively worse or slower, a human-centered design approach could fully focus on the subjective upsides of bringing a user's regulatory orientation in line with the way the decision-assisting technology curates available information. Each user could thus be supported with a matching strategy, eliciting a positive user experience without expecting major drawbacks in performance. While it is imaginable that a companion system is aware of the individual's chronic regulatory mode preferences, we also illustrated how the technology itself can induce a situationally dominant mode. This becomes increasingly important, if the decision task is known to require a certain strategy. For example, in cases where the available information exceeds the human capacity for conducting a full evaluation, the companion could foster a locomotion mode that fits the necessary progressive elimination of data, even though the user might chronically lean towards an assessment orientation. This notion of task-dependent adaptation finds support in the observation that the right mixture of assessment and locomotion fosters task performance in highly complex tasks [ 10 ].

Such a deliberate creation of regulatory fit can have two positive implications. First, when people are more convinced of their decision, they are more committed to following the path they have taken. While it is, obviously, important to question former decisions when their outcomes reveal themselves to be detrimental, rumination and anticipated regret about whether one “was right” are psychologically taxing and counterproductive. Accordingly, a conscious alignment of regulatory mode and strategy via the companion might add to decision-maker's psychological well-being. Similarly, we observed a favorable evaluation of the companion itself. Our approach apparently creates positive user experiences and might contribute to people's trust and willingness to use pertinent systems, thereby bridging the gap between their theoretical value and their actual use [ 9 ]. In sum, our study can inspire the design of positive companion experiences that might not only foster immediate user satisfaction but possibly also long-term technology acceptance (see Limitations and Future Research).

6.2 Absence of Behavioral Effects

While we were able to find support for our predictions regarding subjective decision and companion evaluation (H1 and H2), we did not see any interaction effect on the objective criteria of decision accuracy and speed (RQ1 and RQ2). A theoretically related study on fit effects between employees’ regulatory orientations and the way their leaders exert influence suggests that job performance is affected by an interplay of regulatory mode and the leaders’ kind of power [ 18 ]. Accordingly, the performance of locomotion-oriented employees improves when they are led with expert power (i.e., by providing superior knowledge and information), while coercive power (i.e., administering negative consequences) improves the performance of assessment-orientated individuals. We may not have observed any interaction effect here because participants perceived the companion as an expert in all conditions, as the technology was framed as a knowledgeable supporter rather than a commanding superior. A comparative framing, i.e., expert vs. coercive companion, might have yielded interaction effects on performance indicators. Alternatively, the absence of interaction effects could be explained with the singular observation of a cross-sectional design, while performance effects might develop long-term.

Moreover, we even did not observe any main effects, neither of strategy nor regulatory mode (see https://osf.io/4pey8/ for those analyses). Apparently, we were able to design a task that did not favor one of the two strategies per se, which improves comparability. Nevertheless, it is still remarkable that people who used the same strategy, but in different induced modes, were similarly fast and accurate, although locomotion mode is associated with a higher concern for speed and assessment mode with a higher concern for accuracy [ 34 ]. This indicates that the induced regulatory mode that led to a preference for one of the decision strategies did not transfer to behavioral outcomes like deciding faster or processing information more thoroughly. But since the absence of evidence for an effect must not be treated as evidence of its absence, we yet advocate for not over-interpreting this observation.

6.3 Contributions

Beyond the application of regulatory mode theory in a new context and a more realistic decision situation [ 1 ], our study also provides contributions to the acceptance of augmented decision-making and companion design.

6.3.1 Acceptance of Augmented Decision-Making. . We contribute to the acceptance of augmented decision-making by addressing two of its challenges: cognitive compatibility and incentivization [ 9 ]. We began with establishing compatibility between the cognitive approach of the users (particularly their regulatory mode) and the way they perceive the system to process and present information (expressed in the supported decision strategy). The compatibility in turn was responsible, in line with regulatory fit theory, for an overall positive evaluation of the means and ends of the decision-making process. This provides an example of an innovative approach to create positive user experiences and thereby an internal incentive for technology acceptance that transcends economic pressure or social norms [ 9 ].

Another theme in the acceptance of such technologies is the fulfillment of users’ needs for competence and autonomy [ 30 , 36 ]. By not only augmenting the user with the curation of information but also eliciting a feeling of “being right” through regulatory fit, users are enabled to make more informed decisions while preserving the impression of being in the “driver seat” [ 16 ]. Moreover, as we also showed that our observed effects occur beyond mood changes, this cognitive component might also serve as a valuable addendum to a model of digital assistant ability proposed recently by Beeler and colleagues [ 3 ].

Taken together, we made a first step in demonstrating an effect whose impact can now be confidently examined in a less controlled, real-world scenario (see Limitations and Future Research). This constitutes a proof of concept that adds to current research on the adoption of augmented decision-making [ 9 ] and the liking of digital companions [ 8 ].

6.3.2 Regulatory Mode Theory in Companion Design. . Our study also provides a case study that highlights the relevance of considering regulatory modes in the design of digital companions, especially as decision-aid systems (e.g., [ 23 ]). There are several more or less elaborate definitions in the literature on what makes a digital companion and which roles it may play in relation to its user [ 6 , 27 , 37 , 48 ]. Their common notion, however, is that a companion system has to be sensitive to the individual user's requirements and needs but also his personal states and dispositions [ 6 ]. In other words, the technology ought to support users’ objectives while also being empathetic to their subjective states. Our mock-up prototype was no functional companion, but participants perceived it as one, and it focused on one core functionality that more elaborate systems could be equipped with—decision-making support with empathy for the fact that humans vary in their regulatory mode.

From a more theoretical perspective, our study yields generalizable knowledge that advances the application of Regulatory Mode Theory to technological means. Regulatory fit effects occur when there is a transfer of positive value from how something is done to what is dealt with. Leadership research shows that regulatory modes are associated with preferences towards certain leadership styles [ 28 ] and that a fit increases evaluations of the person who leads [ 4 ]. What has not been shown yet, and what we demonstrated, is that this positive “spill-over” effect can be employed to foster the evaluation of technological means. This is all the more relevant as technologies are way more flexible in how they act compared to people and their leadership styles.

6.4 Limitations and Future Research

One of the shortcomings of our study is that we implemented a low-fidelity companion system. People only received text messages and information from the companion and were not able to interact with the system apart from giving input through rating scales. A more interactive, voice-based system in particular would be an interesting starting point for further investigations, as this change of modality can change decision-making processes in consumers [ 13 ].

Another limitation pertains to the use of self-reports. We only made an initial step by assessing the immediate positive reaction to the technology and the outcome of its use, which probably fosters technology acceptance [ 50 ]. And although we also addressed two solutions to algorithmic aversion (i.e., cognitive compatibility and incentivization), it remains to be examined how much a companion, sensitive towards regulatory mode and decision strategy, would prevail in a real-world context with numerous other influences.

Moreover, our application scenario might seem rather simplistic and limited to a specific area. While the underlying principles are universal and have been proven in a variety of contexts, there are many more effects of regulatory modes on aspects of decision-making like impulsiveness, riskiness, or counterfactual thinking [ 32 , 34 , 38 ], that would all have to be verified in the context of augmented decision-making. In addition, the scenario we used could be considered a decision under certainty (or risk), as all circumstances and outcomes (and arguably probabilities) are known. Whether the effects hold up under conditions of uncertainty is still to be investigated. However, we do not assume that this would significantly change the effects on subjective experiences at the core of this study.

Another question that could not be answered yet is how the companion's adjustment to the regulatory orientation of its user can work out in the long run. As companion systems are intended to be long-time collaborators, they might also have knowledge about their user's natural disposition regarding regulatory modes and adapt to it. The case of an mHealth app [ 15 ], for example, shows that its effectiveness is dependent on the user's chronic regulatory orientation and that a “one size fits all” approach is suboptimal. In the future, companion technologies might even make use of their knowledge of a user's tendencies and intentionally promote a balance of activity between regulatory modes, since research indicates that best performances are achieved with high locomotion and high assessment concerns [ 29 , 33 , 40 ].

7 CONCLUSION

While in the future more and more duties will be automated with intelligent technologies, there still remain tasks that require humans to be in control. In cases of “small world” decision tasks [ 51 ], i.e., worlds of risk, where options and their probabilities are known and optimization is feasible, computational devices might have the upper hand. But human decision-makers often act in a world of uncertainty, in which alternatives, consequences, and probabilities are unknown [ 9 ]. The best decision under risk does not have to be the best decision under uncertainty [ 51 ] and since humans as well as algorithms both have their relative strengths [ 9 , 16 ], we should explore how augmentation processes that consider the individual user can look like. The question is: Which leverages exist to design a collaborative context, where people experience their technological counterpart as a helpful companion? We contributed to this endeavor by designing the augmenting companion in a way that considers basic functions of human self-regulation and even exerts influence on their activation. While there are definitely ethical implications that have to be considered as soon as technologies affect our cognitive processing, we see this study as a further step towards a symbiotic human-computer interaction that creates positive user experiences.
