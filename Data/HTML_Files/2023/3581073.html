“ It can bring you in the right direction ”: Episode-Driven Data Narratives to Help Patients Navigate Multidimensional Diabetes Data to Make Care Decisions

Engaging with multiple streams of personal health data to inform self-care of chronic health conditions remains a challenge. Existing informatics tools provide limited support for patients to make data actionable. To design better tools, we conducted two studies with Type 1 diabetes patients and their clinicians. In the first study, we observed data review sessions between patients and clinicians to articulate the tasks involved in assessing different types of data from diabetes devices to make care decisions. Drawing upon these tasks, we designed novel data interfaces called episode-driven data narratives and performed a task-driven evaluation. We found that as compared to the commercially available diabetes data reports, episode-driven data narratives improved engagement and decision-making with data. We discuss implications for designing data interfaces to support interaction with multidimensional health data to inform self-care.

1 INTRODUCTION

Continuous self-monitoring and reflection on data is known to improve the management of chronic health conditions like diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, and depression [ 15 ]. With improved ability to self-monitor different types of data using medical wearables and consumer sensing technology, patients are increasingly relying on personally-generated health data to inform self-care [ 42 ]. However, in the absence of appropriate tools, leveraging data to inform care remains challenging [ 38 ]. Patient-facing informatics tools that present health data or insights from the data are often critiqued for being cognitively burdensome and for their poor support to make data actionable [ 3 , 9 ].

The use of data is even more challenging for conditions that are simultaneously influenced by several factors as these require users to track and make sense of multiple data streams to assess the impact of those factors on health outcomes. Type 1 diabetes is one such condition that is influenced by multiple factors and requires patients to track different types of data, including glycemic outcomes and the associated factors (e.g., carbs, insulin, physical activity). Continuously available data on blood glucose and the related factors offers opportunities for patients to adjust different dimensions of self-care (e.g., eating behavior, insulin) based on data [ 36 ]. However, owing to the unsuitability of existing tools, patients choose not to engage with their data, resulting in poor health outcomes and over-reliance on care providers [ 19 , 49 ]. These issues suggest the need to design and study tools to scaffold patient engagement with multidimensional data towards the management of chronic health conditions, especially those where tracking different types of data is now feasible and necessary.

To address this need, we conducted two studies in the context of Type 1 diabetes to a) obtain an empirical understanding of how multiple streams of data are used to inform care decisions and b) design and evaluate novel interfaces for patients to support engagement and decision-making with multidimensional data. In the first study, we observed 71 data review sessions between Type 1 diabetes patients and clinicians to identify different steps involved in making decisions from data (blood glucose, carbohydrates, insulin) that is tracked using medical wearables (continuous glucose monitors and insulin pumps) for diabetes management. Here, we note the crucial role of glycemic episodes and domain heuristics in enabling analytic focus when navigating the different types of data. Additionally, we note the different levels of multiplicity involved in making decisions – prioritizing among multiple glycemic episodes and understanding the different possible explanations for an episode in terms of the multiple causal factors. Based on this empirical understanding of tasks and strategies involved in assessing data, we designed novel data interfaces called episode-driven data narratives to support patients in engaging with multidimensional data to make care decisions. In study two, using task-driven data walkthroughs, we evaluated episode-driven data narratives by comparing them with the tools provided by commercial diabetes platforms. We found that episode-driven narratives, as compared to the commercial platforms, improved engagement, and decision-making with different types of data in multiple ways. Particularly, episode-driven narratives demonstrated the potential to increase participants’ comprehension of the data and lowered the burden of engaging with data. They prompted participants to exercise their agency in navigating the complex multiplicity involved in making care decisions from data. Lastly, they demonstrated the potential to improve participants’ ability to articulate plans for action. Unlike the commercial tools, participants’ engagement with the episode-driven narratives also helped them articulate their needs for data that they wanted to see when making decisions, such as the number of days of evidence considered sufficient, the type of factors they wanted to know about, and the number of factors they considered including in the care decisions. Drawing upon our findings, we discuss implications for designing interfaces to support engagement and decision-making with multidimensional health data.

Our contributions in this paper are as follows. First, we articulate the different data-based tasks performed by patients and their clinicians to make decisions from multidimensional diabetes data. Second, anchored in these tasks, we design and evaluate novel data interfaces, called episode-driven data narratives, to support episode-based interaction with complex health data. We show that episode-driven data narratives have the potential to improve data comprehension, lower the burden of engaging with data, and improve participants’ ability to articulate action plans as compared to the tools provided by the commercial diabetes platforms. Lastly, we identify design considerations for presenting multidimensional health data in informatics tools for patients to aid data-based management of chronic health conditions that require tracking different types of data.

2 RELATED WORK

In this section, we describe research on different types of systems designed to present multiples streams of health data for reflection. We also summarize research on understanding and supporting data-based decision-making, especially for diabetes management.

2.1 Presenting Multidimensional Health Data for Reflection

To help people reflect on multiple streams of personal health data, studies in personal informatics and chronic condition management have explored systems to support free form reflection by having users collect relevant data and by presenting their data using a variety of visualizations. Data collection and presentation approaches have included visualization-based approaches and insight-based approaches. Visualization-based approaches include capture and display of contextual data in addition to biomedical data – images, location, social context, mood, and personally defined tags [ 32 , 34 , 43 , 45 , 50 ]; visualizing several subsets of self-tracked data (called visual cuts) to provide more than one perspective [ 13 ]; presentation of multiple streams as stacked time series [ 27 ]; and translating lifestyle data into slideshow based stories to communicate the temporal relevance of data (what data becomes important at what time) [ 35 ]. Insight-based approaches include presentation of correlations using graphs and natural language to communicate insights from multiple data streams [ 5 , 23 , 29 , 47 ]; and presentation of outcomes categorized by certain factors affecting them [ 7 , 29 , 50 ]. Most of these approaches focus on presenting information that users want but have paid limited attention to the different ways in which users could obtain insights and make decisions by engaging with data [ 4 ]. Consequently, there is limited empirical understanding of how people could engage with multidimensional health data to make care decisions; and systems to support decision-making through engagement with personal health data remain largely underexplored.

2.2 Understanding and Supporting Decision-Making with Health Data

Much research on value generation from patient-generated data has focused on designing tools for clinicians. For example, Zhang et al. identified tasks performed by clinicians in using data from continuous glucose monitors (CGMs) and insulin pumps. Given the challenges of making nuanced temporal inferences from multiple streams of data, they designed IDMVis, a tool to support temporal folding and alignment [ 51 ]. They evaluated this tool through think aloud sessions and interviews to find that IDMVis helped identify trends, data quality issues, and variability which helped make treatment decisions. In addition to tools designed by prior research, the commercially available tools for diabetes data are also clinician-centric, making them less useful for patients [ 38 ]. There has been limited work on designing visualizations and decision-support tools for patients [ 46 ]. Most patient-facing tools are cognitively burdensome and provide limited actionable information [ 24 ]. Interfaces designed for patients focus on single data streams, such as presenting forecasts or predictions of blood sugar values to patients [ 10 , 11 ], and suggesting goals based on data [ 16 ]. There is limited exploration of data interfaces that support interpretation of multidimensional health data and tools that support decision-making from multiple streams of data.

One of the ways to support decision-making through engagement with data is to systematically understand data reasoning practices involved in leveraging data to inform care and design systems around these practices [ 30 ]. In the context of diabetes, several models have been proposed to describe the high-level data reasoning tasks involved in managing diabetes, such as problem-solving [ 20 ], sensemaking framework [ 33 ], and fluid contextual reasoning [ 25 ]. While these models improve empirical understanding, it is unclear how features of a system to support engagement with data can be instantiated based on them. For instance, the sensemaking framework by Mamykina et al. describes three high-level tasks involved in managing diabetes - perception, inference, and action. However, it is unclear how patients use health data to perform these tasks, which challenges conceptualizing features for data interfaces. In another work, Mamykina et al. identified the analytical tasks patients perform in making personal discoveries using their data in guidance with diabetes educators (e.g., feature selection, hypothesis formulation, hypothesis evaluation, and goal specification) [ 31 ]. Mamykina et al. also developed MoDD, a web-based tool to engage patients in different problem-solving steps using blood glucose data and educational materials. Specifically, patients were instructed to do the following – a) pick a problematic glycemic pattern that they wanted to fix (e.g., high BG number after breakfast), b) pick a causal behavioral pattern from a knowledge base created by researchers (e.g., lack of protein for breakfast), c) select an alternative behavior and set an action-oriented goal every week (e.g., have eggs for breakfast), and d) implement new behavior and monitor changes in BG number to see if the goal is being achieved [ 30 ]. This body of work did not study engagement and decision-making with multiple types of data. Our work builds on this research with the aim to understand and scaffold diabetes-related decision-making using multiple streams of data as diabetes is affected by multiple factors on an ongoing basis and requires frequent decision-making.

3 STUDY CONTEXT: TYPE 1 DIABETES

Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune condition in which the pancreas produces little to no insulin resulting in abnormal blood glucose (BG) levels. As a result, T1D patients depend on insulin to control their blood sugar levels. T1D is usually diagnosed in childhood but it can develop at any age. This condition cannot be cured requiring life-long self-management. Regular self-management tasks involve monitoring blood sugar levels multiple times during the day, counting carbohydrates and taking insulin when eating, taking insulin in between meals, and adjusting the dose of insulin based on several factors affecting blood sugar levels, including the amount of carbohydrates eaten, the activity levels, and the overall routine [ 1 ].

There are two types of insulin that patients need to take based on a regimen decided in collaboration with their endocrinologists– long-acting insulin (basal insulin) and short acting insulin (bolus insulin) [ 12 ]. These differ in how quickly they act and how long they remain effective in reducing blood glucose levels. The long-acting insulin acts over multiple hours after taking it whereas the short acting insulin starts to work within fifteen minutes of administration and is usually taken to counter the blood glucose spike that happens when eating food [ 2 ]. Medical devices, such as the continuous glucose monitor (CGM) and the insulin pump, automate some of the abovementioned tasks. Specifically, the insulin pump can be configured to automate the calculation and administration of basal insulin and bolus insulin. The two parameters used to configure insulin pumps are insulin to carb ratio and correction factor or insulin sensitivity factor, which need to be manually determined for each patient and are set in the pump [ 12 ]. The insulin to carb ratio is the amount of insulin to be administered for every 10 grams of carbohydrates consumed. Different meals during the day could use a different insulin to carb ratio depending on what is eaten and how it affects the patient's blood glucose levels. The other configuration parameter, the correction factor, is the amount of insulin to be administered for every unit increase in blood sugar above the target blood sugar number or the amount of insulin to be deducted for every unit decrease in blood sugar below the target number. When using the insulin pump, the patients need to record two data points in their pump at the beginning of a meal - the current BG number and the carbohydrate amount. The pump then calculates the amount of insulin required by using the insulin to carb ratio to adjust for food and the correction factor to adjust for the difference between the current BG number and the target BG number [ 12 ]. The pump can also be configured to deliver bursts of basal insulin often scheduled using several different infusion rates over a 24-hour duration [ 41 ].

The two parameters described above, and the basal insulin settings are continuously assessed and changed in response to the patient's glycemic performance after reviewing their data. Data is primarily reviewed using data platforms provided by the device manufacturers, such as Dexcom 1 , Tandem 2 , and Medtronic 3 or by using data aggregation platforms, such as Glooko 4 , and Tidepool 5 . These platforms provide different types of displays of data as a PDF report that can be downloaded from their portals. These reports contain limited data interpretation, such as identification of coarse glycemic patterns. These patterns need to be further interpreted by patients and/or clinicians to identify problems with management, their causes, and the potential care actions to fix the problems. Since multiple factors could be influencing blood glucose numbers simultaneously, patients need to periodically make sense of the impact of different factors to identify changes they could make to improve glycemic control. Prior work has reported that the existing displays are complex, clinician-centric, and unsuitable for use on mobile devices, making it difficult for patients to engage with their data on a regular basis to make changes to their insulin pump configurations [ 38 ].

4 STUDY 1: UNPACKING DATA-INFORMED DECISION-MAKING FOR T1D MANAGEMENT

As noted above, prior work on presenting health data provides limited guidance on how to design patient-facing interfaces to support engagement with multiple streams of data [ 48 ]. Empirical identification of the tasks involved in data-based decision-making can help identify the requirements for interfaces to support the presentation of data [ 51 ]. To identify tasks involved in making Type 1 diabetes (T1D) data actionable, we observed 71 data review sessions between T1D patients, caregivers, and their clinicians. Through these observations, we found five analytical tasks involved in making decisions. These tasks inform the design of our novel prototypes, called episode-driven data narratives , which we designed and evaluated in study two. In this section, we describe the data collection, data analysis, and the key findings from study one that provide a basis for the design and evaluation of our novel data interfaces.

4.1 Data Collection

We wanted to understand how multiple streams of data are understood to make decisions for care. For this, we observed 71 data review sessions held as a part of a telemedicine program at a large university's medical center. The study was approved by the university's Institutional Review Board (IRB). Six patients (age range 9 – 17 years old), their caregivers, and three healthcare providers (two endocrinologists (Endo) and one certified diabetes educator (CDE)) participated in the study. Patients and their caregivers were recruited by the clinic research staff. Informed assent and consent were obtained from all the patients and caregivers.

These sessions were held on Zoom and were video recorded and transcribed. The average length of a session was 25 minutes. During these sessions, patients, caregivers, and providers reviewed two weeks of data from patients’ diabetes devices that included insulin pumps and continuous glucose monitors. Commercially available data reports provided by the diabetes device manufacturers, such as Dexcom 1 and Tandem 2 were used for data review. These reports provide different types of data visualizations but do not guide users in connecting different types of data. For instance, they provide an overview of glycemic trends in the data but do not help connect those with the data on carbs and insulin from the insulin pump. Additionally, these reports provide a lot of data but do not guide users in efficiently navigating the data, which makes data review cognitively burdensome and time consuming, especially for patients.

4.2 Data Analysis

The transcripts of the video recordings of data review sessions were analyzed using a mix of descriptive and process coding [ 40 ]. The first author coded the transcripts with words describing the analytical tasks being performed in reviewing data and the challenges associated with the tasks. Coding was performed iteratively until we reached saturation [ 39 ] where different tasks started to appear repeatedly within and across data review sessions and no new steps were identified. The coded data was then grouped into broader themes describing analytical tasks and challenges in the data review process. These themes were discussed with the study team members for further refinement.

4.3 Findings

We found five tasks involved in assessing multidimensional data to make decisions for improving T1D management. These include identifying one or multiple episodes of suboptimal management, explaining them in terms of potential causal factors, invoking domain heuristics to find relevant evidence from data, assessing the need and feasibility of an intervention, and selecting a factor to work on to prevent an episode from recurring. In executing these tasks, we observed that participants employed episodes as an analytic lens to reduce the complexity of navigating multiple types of data. The episode being analyzed determined the data exploration strategy, the domain heuristic to apply, and the factors to consider for exploration.

4.3.1 Identifying one or multiple episodes of suboptimal management. Patients and clinicians began data assessment by identifying glycemic episodes, that is phases of suboptimal management typically depicted by blood glucose patterns. For instance, the CDE identified an episode of low BG numbers by counting the number of time lows happened, “I have the last 14 days here and it's a handful of times [of low numbers] and its usually between 3 and 4 am” (CDE in P07’s CDE visit 7). Once an episode was identified, it was characterized for severity and concern using measures such as the average BG during the time duration of the episode, “The night average is right around 150 [i.e., in range] but the day average is, is probably pushing 220-240 [with 80-120 as the expected range]” (Endo 2 in P04’s Endo visit 2).

4.3.2 Explaining an episode in terms of potential causal factors. After noting an episode, it was explained using different types of available data to identify the potential factors involved in causing the episode. Since blood glucose could be impacted by several factors simultaneously, multiple explanations corresponding to all possible causal factors were typically discussed and compared before making decisions. It was often difficult to isolate causal factors with certainty and required judgment based on the available evidence and based on inputs from the patients. For example, the CDE while trying to establish the potential cause of a high BG pattern in the afternoon mentioned, “I almost wonder if you're missing a couple carbs [food] here and there or if that's your basal [long- acting insulin]. ” (CDE in P01’s CDE visit 08). Based on the data, multiple narratives were crafted to assess an episode in the context of different influential factors.

4.3.3 Invoking domain heuristics to find relevant evidence for an episode. The process of creating explanations was often guided by heuristics that clinicians drew upon based on the episode being assessed. These heuristics defined the typical causes of an episode and the corresponding corrective interventions (e.g., overnight high blood glucose is usually caused by insufficient long-acting insulin. Increase long-acting insulin to fix overnight highs). They helped connect the different types of data, that is an episode to one or more factors. Additionally, these heuristics guided the search for relevant evidence supporting or refuting the different emerging connections between an episode and different factors.

The reports contained multiple days of data that could be used to identify the episodes and specific episode-factor connections. Given the short time of the data review sessions, it was not possible to discuss all days of data. The evidence was unpacked by discussing a few days as representative examples supporting a narrative. For instance, in one of P03’s sessions, the CDE noted an episode of high BG numbers post lunch and by looking at specific days she established that a few of these happened despite the patient taking insulin for lunch, “even when you do bolus, like on Sunday you bloused [took insulin for lunch] and still went sky high afterwards. Same thing with last Friday, last Thursday. ” Given the heuristic that daytime high numbers are typically related to meal insulin, she decided to increase lunch insulin in this case.

4.3.4 Assessing episodes for interventional need and feasibility. For some observed episodes, an initial assessment did not result in the clarity required to identify interventions upfront because of the nature of the available evidence (e.g., weak, or inconsistent evidence). To develop more clarity, different characteristics of the episode, such as frequency and confidence with which it could be explained in terms of different factors, were considered to gauge the need and feasibility of an intervention. Moreover, there could be multiple episodes requiring intervention. Explicit classification in terms of the need and possibility of an intervention helped prioritize and determine which episodes could be left unattended. As described in the vignette below, the CDE was not able to identify evidence supporting an explanation for an episode of post dinner high BG numbers. She decided to monitor the episode instead of making a change to fix it.

The educator points to an episode of post dinner high BG numbers and asks if the patient has any snacks after dinner. The caregiver mentions that the snacks are covered by insulin and are mostly without carbohydrates. The CDE does not make any changes and asks them to wait until after school starts. She asks the caregiver to keep an eye on post dinner numbers. (P04’s CDE visit 4)

4.3.5 Selecting potential causes to work on to fix an episode. Because multiple factors were associated with most episodes, there was a need to unpack the relative influence of these factors and identify those that should be the basis of change(s). While at times, the choice of intervention was straightforward given the strong evidence in favor of a causal factor, often, it wasn't, particularly when several potential causes seemed equally influential. In that case, one or multiple changes were identified for implementation, as described in the vignette below:

The CDE notes post-dinner high BG numbers and shows them to the patient (P01), focusing on examples of highs after 5 pm continuing overnight. The spike continues overnight despite taking correction insulin. After seeing this data, the CDE thinks more insulin could be used for dinner and for corrections after dinner. The CDE makes two changes - increases the insulin to carb ratio at dinner and increases the midnight correction factor for P01 to get more insulin at dinner and overnight. (P01’s CDE visit 3)

The above-described tasks, in most cases, were driven by clinicians using commercial reports. These reports are known to be complex for patients and often discourage independent use of data [ 38 ]. Additionally, they are unsuitable for mobile interfaces, which further restricts easy engagement. Thus, we set out to design a new set of interfaces, which we call episode-driven data narratives, to help patients independently engage with multiple streams of data on a mobile device. In the next section, we describe the design of episode-driven data narratives.

5 DESIGN OF NOVEL INTERFACES: EPISODE-DRIVEN DATA NARRATIVES

In this section, we describe the design goals we had in creating novel interfaces and the different components of the episode-driven data narratives .

5.1 Design Goals

In designing episode-driven data narratives, we wanted to support the five tasks described above, for which we had three design goals – engaging patients with multiplicity by presenting multiple episodes and causal factors, enabling analytic focus using an episode as a basis for data presentation, and contextualizing representative data with domain heuristics to explain typical connections between different data streams.

5.1.1 Multiplicity: Presenting multiple episodes and causal factors to engage patients with multiplicity. As described above, navigating different levels of multiplicity was central to making decisions from T1D data. There was often more than one episode present in the data which meant understanding and prioritizing the episodes that need attention based on their properties, such as severity and frequency. While all episodes were assessed, not all of them resulted in a decision for an intervention. Moreover, each episode was linked to more than one factor which meant that it could be explained and worked upon in more than one way. Lastly, there were multiple days of evidence depicting an episode and specific episode-factor connections. Navigating multiplicity involved the creation of several narratives corresponding to different factors and relied on human judgment of each narrative based on representative evidence depicting episode-factor association. Hence, we wanted our prototype to present this multiplicity and engage users with it. Towards this goal, we chose to show multiple episodes that were present in the data along with their properties, and the multiple factors related to each episode, as determined by heuristics that clinicians used. Given the study context, we chose not to show users directive recommendations or one specific factor that seemed the most influential. Instead, we chose to show all possible factors and the corresponding interventional options that could be considered.

5.1.2 Focus: Using episodes as a basis for data presentation to enable analytic focus. Given the need to pay attention to multiple types of information, it is important to help users maintain analytic focus during the review of multiple episodes and of multiple factors associated with each episode. In study one, we saw that the tasks performed to make care decisions were anchored in episodes. Episodes guided the identification of heuristics to apply to the data, the factors to consider, and the subsets of data to explore, thereby constraining and structuring the exploration involved. We chose to embody this episode-driven engagement with data in the design of our interfaces. We cater to this goal by curating and presenting a list of factors, domain-heuristics, and data based on episodes present in a data set.

5.1.3 Heuristics: Contextualizing representative data with domain heuristics to explain connections between data streams. Episode-specific domain heuristics help specify typical causal factors of an episode, narrowing down the number of factors to explore. They provided a frame to identify connections between an episode and a factor and filter the day-to-day data for examples that depict those connections. To ensure that patients saw and understood the connection between the different types of data, we chose to annotate representative data with episode-specific heuristics. We chose to show only a few days of data as representative examples to constrain burdensome data exploration.

5.2 Components of Episode-Driven Data Narratives

To support the three design goals described above, we included the following components in our interfaces – 1) an overview of the data, 2) a detailed view of each episode, and 3) a detailed view of each factor associated with an episode. Figure 1 shows the design of episode-driven data narratives – mobile interfaces that bring together information on episodes, episode's characteristics, potential causal factors, domain heuristics, and examples from data. We call these interfaces episode-driven data narratives as they surface multiple narratives for an episode to help users understand the relative influence of different factors on the episode and make decision(s) in a context where several interventional possibilities exist, making it difficult to identify the best intervention.

5.2.1 Overview of the data. Following design goals 5.1.1 and 5.1.2, the first interface of the episode-driven data narratives presents a list of episodes from the CGM data along with overall glycemic performance for the duration of the data (time in range, time above range, time below range, and number of days CGM was worn). The user can click on one of the episodes and go to the second screen that shows an episode in detail.

5.2.2 Detailed view of an episode. Following design goal 5.1.1, the second screen displays a detailed visualization of an episode showing the various percentiles in a time series of the CGM data (10, 25, 50, 75, and 90), also known as the ambulatory glucose profile [4]. It also presents several episode properties (average BG during the episode, range of BG numbers, and the number of days the episode happened) and a list of factors potentially causing the episode as identified from the insulin pump data. For each factor, the interface showed the number of days the factor was influential. The user can click on one of the factors and go to the third screen to see more information about the factor.

5.2.3 Detailed view of a factor. Following design goal 5.1.1, and 5.1.3, the third screen provides an understanding of the factor in the context of the episode. It first briefly describes the episode in natural language and presents a domain heuristic to explain the role of the factor in causing the episode. This is followed by a presentation of exemplary data from the CGM, the insulin pump (BG, carbs, insulin) and insulin pump configuration (correction factor, carb ratio, basal rates) where needed.

The visualization formats of the different components in the design of episode-driven data narratives were selected based on the data or the insight to be presented. The review of existing tools, literature on patient-facing visualization formats [ 48 ], and evidence on effective task-visualization mappings [ 6 ] informed the choices here. To organize and communicate different components, we sought guidance from research on narrative visualizations to make design choices for creating visual narratives that bring different components together [ 22 ].

We created preliminary designs for presenting the different components and then discussed it over several iterations with the core study team members. Following suggestions from our clinical expert, we included aggregate measures of glycemic performance (time in range, time above range, time below range, and CGM wear time) as another component in the first screen that presents a list of episodes. Later in the process, we obtained feedback from the peripheral members of the study team and from one patient. Iterations resulted in identifying gaps in the presented information, such as the type of factors to include or exclude from presentation (factors with or without available evidence), the number of days of evidence to present corresponding to an episode, the time granularity to use for presenting episode and the factors (one day and 7 – 30 days using a 24 hour scale), and the types of episode characteristics to present (average, range, number of days episode was seen in the daily traces).

6 STUDY 2: EVALUATION OF EPISODE-DRIVEN DATA NARRATIVES

In a preliminary evaluation of episode-driven data narratives, our aim was to understand how patients use diabetes data to perform specific tasks involved in making care decisions, and in the context of these tasks, how episode-driven narratives might affect data comprehension and decision-making as compared to the commercial diabetes data reports. Towards this aim, we first recruited participants and obtained data from their CGM, insulin pump, and electronic medical record. Next, we created candidate prototypes for evaluation using diabetes device data from five participants. For each patient's data, we created prototypes following the design of episode-driven data narratives. As the final step, we conducted two task-driven think-aloud sessions, one using the commercial diabetes data reports and another using episode-driven data narratives with eight participants. Next, we describe these steps in detail.

6.1 Recruitment

Patients were recruited through the adult and pediatric endocrinology clinic and a health research platform of a large university's health system. Patients who expressed interest through the clinic or the online platform were contacted to assess eligibility. To be eligible for the study participants should have been diagnosed at least 2 years prior to the time of enrollment (to ensure prior experience reviewing and understanding data), should have been using a continuous glucose monitor and an insulin pump, should have downloaded and reviewed diabetes device data during their last clinic visit with a certified diabetes educator or an endocrinologist, and should have been willing to allow the use of their diabetes device data for research. We excluded newly diagnosed patients as they would still be learning to engage with diabetes data, would not have established data practices, and their needs would vary from those who have had more exposure to data. The study was reviewed and approved by the IRB of our university. Eligible patients were provided with the informed consent form. Participants who signed the form were considered enrolled. Once enrolled, participants filled out a baseline survey to reconfirm eligibility and to gather demographic and general information about their diabetes management. Next, we obtained the data from their medical devices (CGM and insulin pump) and their electronic health records. These data included the PDF data reports downloaded from the commercial data platforms and interpreted in the last clinic visit, the corresponding clinical notes with an interpretation of the reports, and raw data from the CGM and the insulin pump for the time duration captured in the PDF reports.

Enrollment happened in two phases over a duration of eight months. In the first phase, 12 participants enrolled followed by 4 participants in the second phase. Only 8 patients went ahead to participate in the study and completed all the activities. Among the 8 patients who did not participate, two were considered ineligible because of our inability to access their data and one withdrew from the study. The remaining participants stopped responding to communication shortly after enrollment. Despite multiple attempts, we could not establish contact with them. 6.2 Patient Data Selection for Prototypes

We used data from five participants to create five prototype instances embodying episode-driven data narratives. We began patient data selection process after the first phase of enrollment, which resulted in recruiting 12 participants. From these, we were able to use the data from nine participants (P01, P03, P04, P05, P07, P08, P09, P10, P12). These participants consented to having their data used for prototype creation (data from CGM, insulin pump, and clinician notes on data from electronic medical records). We had to exclude the use of data from three participants (P02, P06, and P11) because of different reasons. P06 withdrew from the study, and we could not download P02’s CGM data and P11’s pump data. We did not consider the remaining patients’ (P13-16) data for prototype creation because by the time we enrolled them, we already had sufficient data sets to create several instances of episode-driven data narratives for evaluation.

Our clinical expert reviewed the commercial data reports for each of the nine patients. We also had an interpretation of the data documented in the electronic medical records from the last clinic visit for each patient. Based on these two largely overlapping interpretations, for each patient's data, we documented the episodes present in the data, the time window of the episode, the time duration over which the data was assessed, the potential factors leading to the episode, the representative days that were used by the clinician to establish the influence of a factor, and the possible changes that could be done to prevent the episode. From these nine patients, data from five patients (P01, P04, P05, P07, P09) was selected for the creation of five prototypes embodying the design of episode-driven data narratives. In selecting the five data sets, our aim was to ensure diversity along the following dimensions – the type of episode (high or low), the time duration of episode, (morning, afternoon, evening, night, overnight), the potential factors (behavior related – carb counting, insulin timing, meal timing, etc. and insulin related – carb ratio, correction factor, basal insulin), and the potential solutions being represented. We call these five annotated data sets “cases”. For brevity, Table 2 shows a summary of two of the five cases. The five cases we chose were largely representative of the data from the nine participants and the remaining data did not add any more diversity to the different episodes, factors, and interventions that we captured from these five cases.

We employed the Wizard of Oz [ 8 ] technique to implement five prototype instances corresponding to the five cases. Using the documentation created for each case and the raw data (CGM and insulin pump including data on BG, carb, insulin, and insulin pump events), we created individual visualizations for specific episodes and factors in Python and brought them together in an interactive mobile prototype on Figma. For each case, we identified the episodes and factors by collaborating with our clinical expert who annotated the data to establish the ground truth. The five prototype instances represented seven episodes. Because our aim was to investigate user engagement through interface design, we did not focus on automating the detection of episodes and factors, although it is feasible to do so as suggested by prior work. For instance, combining machine learning to predict glycemic outcomes with a template-based expert system has been used to generate nutrition recommendations [ 16 ]. Additionally, algorithmic techniques to identify causal moderators in time series data can be used to identify potential factors influencing glycemic patterns [ 52 ].

We wanted to evaluate the prototypes in the context of the specific tasks noted in study 1, particularly identifying episodes, explaining them in terms of potential causal factors, and identifying an intervention. For this purpose, we prepared questions corresponding to two broad categories – questions about the data in general (time in range, number of episodes) and questions specific to each episode present in the data (characteristics, potential causes, and solutions). Our questions represented the different tasks observed in study 1 and were used for assessing participants’ comprehension of the data based on the prototypes, as is done in task-based evaluations of information visualizations [ 6 ]. For each of the seven episodes (and the corresponding cases) represented in the five prototype instances, questions specific to different tasks were put together in seven data comprehension tests. Multiple choice questions corresponded to the following tasks – 1) identify the time in range for the duration of the data, 2) identify all the episodes present in the data, and given an episode, 3) identify its characteristics, 4) all the potential causes of that episode based on the data, 5) all the potential fixes possible based on the causes identified, 6) one cause that seemed the most evident and that the participant would like to work on, and 7) one fix they would like to implement. The comprehension tests also elicited free text response on what would be the specific change(s) patients would actually make given all the potential causes and solutions they could identify. While the comprehension test was not statistically validated, all the comprehension tests were reviewed by another study team member for clarity. The expert interpretation of the data sets and educational material used by our clinical expert for training her patients were used as the ground truth to guide the choice of correct responses to the questions in the data comprehension tests. The responses to the questions were scored based on their correspondence to expert generated ground truth except the free text responses and the questions corresponding to items 6 and 7 in the above-mentioned list. For each correct answer chosen, one point was awarded. For each comprehension test that a participant took, the final score was a percentage of the total possible points.

6.3 Data Collection: Task-Driven Evaluation of Two Platforms

We conducted two sessions - one using the PDF reports downloaded from commercial diabetes platforms (Dexcom and Tandem) and another using the episode-driven data narratives, to assess participants’ ability to use data for specific tasks and answer questions related to those tasks. Both the sessions lasted for 60-90 minutes. Participants engaged in a task-based walk through of the data while taking the comprehension tests for the cases (and the specific episodes) they were reviewing. While doing so, participants were asked to think aloud. After completing the comprehension tests, participants were asked to take the cognitive load assessment (using the NASA task load index) [ 18 ] while also thinking aloud. The sessions concluded with a semi-structured interview where we asked about their overall experience reviewing the data and probed them on specific aspects observed during the think aloud sessions. The semi-structured interviews in both sessions focused on understanding to what extent and how participants engaged in different tasks noted in study 1 (identifying episodes, explaining them, and assessing and prioritizing multiple episodes for intervention). Additionally, participants were asked about positive and negative aspects of both the platforms.

In the first session, participants were able to review only one case using the commercial PDF reports in the duration of the session. Figure 2 shows a few visualizations from the commercial reports that were reviewed by the participants. These reports include reports from Dexcom and Tandem. In the second session, participants were able to review two to three cases. Cases were assigned to participants to balance the number of participants across the available cases. Additionally, we manually assigned cases to ensure that each participant saw variations across the time duration of the episode (morning, afternoon, evening, night, overnight), the type of episode (high BG, low BG), and the number of factors associated with the episode.

Across the two sessions, participants saw at least one identical case (and the corresponding episodes) to enable comparative assessment through the comprehension tests. To avoid order effect, in the second session, we showed participants a case they had not seen in the previous session before showing them the case they had seen in the previous session. To avoid recall of information from the first session, the second session was conducted at least two weeks after the first session. Participants did not see their own data across any of the sessions and only reviewed other participants’ data to enable objective assessment of the data interfaces, given our focus on measuring data comprehension based on the interfaces.

All the sessions were conducted on Zoom. Participants used their laptop for both the sessions. All the sessions were video recorded and transcribed using the live transcription feature of Zoom. Transcripts were edited to correct the issues with the automated transcription.

6.4 Data Analysis

For each participant, data was analyzed in multiple steps. We first scored the comprehension test for the case that was common across the two sessions for a participant. Next, we analyzed the videos to make detailed notes about their responses to each question on the test. We documented their responses, identified why they selected or omitted certain answers and which questions seemed particularly challenging. The responses related to the common case seen in both the sessions by a participant were compared to identify differences and similarities in how participants answered the test questions. Lastly, we analyzed the transcript of each session guided by the different decision-making tasks identified in study 1 (identifying episodes, explaining them in terms of potential causes, invoking domain knowledge to find relevant evidence, assessing the need and feasibility of an intervention, and selecting a cause to work on) to examine how participants engaged with the different analytical steps using the two platforms. For this, we performed descriptive coding [ 39 ] to identify instances of the different tasks. The data specific to each instance was then analyzed and coded across all participants for each platform using in-vivo coding to label the types of information participants used for specific activities, the challenges they faced, and the suggestions they provided for improving the design. Additionally, we coded participants’ descriptions of the helpful and unhelpful aspects of the different platforms using in-vivo coding.

7 FINDINGS

We found that as compared to the commercial diabetes data reports, episode-driven data narratives improved engagement and decision-making with multidimensional data in three ways. First, episode-driven narratives resulted in better data comprehension as assessed through episode-specific comprehension tests we created; and they lowered the burden of engaging with data as assessed through the NASA TLX questionnaire. Second, they also prompted participants to exercise their agency in navigating the complex multiplicity involved in making care decisions from data. Third, they demonstrated the potential to improve participants’ ability to articulate plans for action. Unlike the commercial tools, participants’ engagement with the episode-driven narratives also helped identify their needs in terms of the data that they wanted to see when making decisions. Specifically, we found that participants’ need for data varied across three dimensions. These include the number of days of evidence considered sufficient, the type of factors participants wanted to know about, and the number of factors they wished to include in the care decisions. Next, we describe these themes in detail.

7.1 Episode-Driven Narratives Resulted in Better Data Comprehension and Lower Task Load

As compared to the commercial tools, episode-driven narratives improved data comprehension as assessed through the scores obtained on episode-specific comprehension tests; and lowered the burden of engagement with data as assessed using NASA TLX questionnaire. Based on a paired samples t-test, we found a significant difference in the comprehension test scores between the research prototypes (M = 91.375, SD =11.078) and the commercial platforms (M = 69.75, SD = 24.340); p =0.0252. That is, comprehension test score based on research prototypes was significantly greater than that based on the commercial tools. We also found a significant difference in the task load scores between the research prototypes (M =37.625, SD =10.463) and the commercial platforms (M =60.125, SD =15.390); p =0.00321. That is, task load based on research prototypes was significantly smaller than the task load based on commercial tools. Figure 3 shows the boxplots of the two types of scores for each of the two platforms. Because of the small sample size, these results may or may not extrapolate to a larger sample. While we did not set out to establish statistical significance, these results point to the potential of the episode-driven narratives in improving participants’ ability to engage with data.

7.2 Episode-Driven Narratives Enabled Agency in Navigating Multiplicity

As described above, often, multiple episodes and multiple factors associated with each episode need to be carefully considered in making decisions about which episodes to correct and which factors to include as a part of corrective interventions. In doing so with the commercial platforms, participants engaged in much guesswork, made several assumptions, and had little engagement with the available data. With the episode-driven narratives, participants handled multiplicity relatively more systematically by leveraging episode-specific information (measures associated with the episode, factors associated with the episodes, exemplary evidence, domain knowledge), which also prompted them to use their own perception of risks and benefits. Below we detail two ways in which episode-driven narratives helped participants navigate multiplicity better than the commercial platforms.

7.2.1 By prompting careful classification and prioritization of episodes. When looking at the blood glucose (BG) episodes using the commercial reports, several participants (P05, P09, P10, P13) deprioritized the low BG patterns upfront in favor of the high BG patterns without looking at the detailed evidence, such as potential causes of the low BG numbers or their frequency. This was particularly surprising as in study one, we observed that clinicians often prioritized low BG patterns for interventions.

When looking at the glycemic episodes using the research prototypes, participants did not dismiss any of the episodes upfront as was done when reviewing the commercial data reports. Evidence presented in the prototypes, particularly the episode-specific measures (e.g., average, and range) and factors, prompted them to carefully analyze, compare, and choose one or more episode for an intervention. For instance, in selecting which of the two episodes to pay more attention to, P04 mentioned the information he was considering, “ I'd probably be looking at the average BG so like, this one's a 195 this one's 185 so I'd probably say this one would be more severe if the average is higher. I would also look at the timeframe because being high for longer is worse – you want to come back down ”.

Instead of only considering the measures associated with an episode, episode-specific factors were also considered to choose which episode to fix. P01 chose to fix the episode that was linked to a factor that seemed influential on all days of the week versus the one that was linked to a factor that was influential on fewer days of the week, “ I would start with the one that was happening every day and try to fix that first then move down to one that maybe you know wasn't happening as frequently. ”

The engagement prompted by the episode-specific measures and factors further resulted in participants considering the risks associated with not fixing an episode and the benefit of fixing an episode, particularly when two episodes seemed similar based on the information presented. For instance, P13 chose an episode based on his understanding of what would have the greatest benefit when fixed. Specifically, he chose to fix the nighttime pattern because that could result in having better control the entire night, “ They're both [the two episodes] pretty similar. The length is quite similar like being highest they're both four hours long. And also, like their peaks. but I would go with the nighttime one just because I know that if you're high when you go to sleep, you're going to be high the entire night until you wake up and correct. ” Similarly, P10 chose to fix the overnight pattern over the daytime pattern because of the risks of having hyperglycemia while asleep and the benefit of starting the next day with better numbers, “ you're sleeping and that's kinda scary and then that's going to set up the rest of your day to kind of flow better. ”

Unlike the commercial data reports, episode-driven narratives facilitated participants’ agency by prompting them to engage in careful analysis and comparison of different episodes using not only the information presented but also their own perceptions of risks and benefits related to an episode.

7.2.2 By enabling careful and informed choice of factors to change for an episode. Participants acknowledged the challenge of identifying the best change to make in response to an episode, given that multiple factors influence glycemic control. Consequently, a list of possible factors presented in the prototypes was found helpful in directing their exploration for identifying what factors to consider for a change, “ there's no way to know exactly what the best change is but it [the prototype] can definitely hint towards it or bring you in the right direction which is why these potential causes are great ” (P13). Given the challenge of understanding the influence of multiple factors, the list of factors along with detailed information on each factor gave them an opportunity for controlled and deeper exploration of the different possibilities. It prompted them to carefully assess each possible cause and exercise their agency to align their choice of factors with their preferences. Contrary to our expectation, these included not only the number of days a factor was influential but also the ease with which a factor could be worked upon, and a factor for which there was evidence on what worked. P05 and P10 choose to work on the factor that was influential on the greatest number of days, “ Even though evening carb ratio is listed first [in the list of factors] I'm going to pick basal insulin for 14 of 14 days, because that seems to have the highest impact on like every day. It's not just like occasional user error or something. ” (P05)

Not all participants chose the factor that was influential on the maximum number of days. P01 chose a factor that would have been easier for her to work with. She saw two factors – carb ratio and frequent meals. She chose to change the carb ratio instead of avoiding frequent meals because she had a condition which required her to divide her meals into smaller portions instead of eating all at once, making it harder for her to avoid frequent meals, “ so I had run into a problem where I couldn't bolus before my meals, because I was never quite sure how much I would be able to eat. I might probably do the carb ratio, because it would seem like it would be the easier of the two. ”

Where possible, the interfaces presented an example of a good day and a bad day to explain the influence of a factor. Participants found such explanations more convincing. Comparing the good day with the bad day gave an indication of what needed to be done to reproduce the outcomes from the good day. Hence, instead of relying on a factor that was influential on the greatest number of days, a factor that had concrete evidence on what worked was considered more useful, as mentioned by P04, “ I was kind of more convinced by the fact that this had like an example of where you did what you would do to fix it and it worked. ”

In selecting a factor to work on, participants also leveraged their perception of risks and benefits associated with each factor. P14, for instance, took a conservative approach in exploring the different factors presented. Instead of selecting the factor that was the most influential based on the data, she chose to work on factors that did not involve making changes to insulin, such as ensuring carb estimates are correct, and ensuring insulin is being taken ahead of the meals, “ my initial thought is ‘Am I counting carbs wrong?’ before I do anything else [change insulin]. I'm either miscounting carbs or I just don't take my bolus ahead of time. ” In contrast, P15 took an aggressive approach wherein he wanted to start by changing the basal insulin, which is the long-acting insulin and is considered relatively riskier to work on by most patients, “ the easiest starting point would be that basal because if you're 48% of the time in range then that's almost definitely wrong somewhere. ”

In summary, participants used a variety of criteria in selecting factors to work on. This was made possible by the information presented in the episode-driven data narratives - the overview of multiple factors associated to an episode, and the details on each factor that included examples from the data showing its association with the episode, and the number of days a factor was involved. Participants relied on this information and their own perceptions of risks and benefits in nuanced ways and exercised their agency to select factors to consider in their decisions. Such engagement was not observed with the commercial data reports.

7.3 Episode-Driven Narratives Have the Potential to Improve Actionability

For three participants (P04, P10, P13), research prototypes improved their ability to identify and articulate possible interventions and to prioritize the several possible interventions to fix an episode. This was evident from the free text responses recorded by participants to articulate the change they would make to their management based on the data they saw. In two cases, participants were unable to identify any intervention using the commercial reports. In another case, the intervention suggested with the prototype was more methodical than that suggested with the commercial tool.

P10 and P13 were unable to identify interventions using the commercial data reports. For the same case, they were able to articulate an interventional plan when reviewing the data with the prototypes. The following examples show the interventions that were noted in the free text response by P10 and P13.

P10’s interventional plan based on review of commercial reports: “ N/A ”.

P10’s interventional plan based on review of episode-driven data narratives: “ Increase basal insulin gradually and see if changes improve over several days. Also, take dinner meals earlier and take insulin 20 minutes before dinner. ”

P13’s interventional plan based on review of commercial reports: “ Nothing ”.

P13’s interventional plan based on review of episode-driven data narratives: “ Change it [dinner carb ratio] from 1:9.2 to 1:8.5. ”

P13 saw two cases. For the other case, while he was able to correctly identify what needs to change, that is the carb ratio, he chose the incorrect carb ratio specification (1:8) to change when using the commercial reports. With the prototype, he chose the correct carb ratio specification (1:6) to change for lunch. Based on the review of commercial reports, P13 wrote, “ Increase lunch carb ratio - 1:8 -> 1:7 ”. However, the correct carb ratio to change for lunch was 1:6 and not 1:8. With the episode-driven data narratives, P13 identified the correct carb ratio that needed to be changed. In the free text response, he wrote, “ Change it from 1:6 to 1:5.7 ”.

Because of their exploratory nature, the commercial data reports did not help participants compartmentalize different issues and identify the few most important issues to work on. This resulted in participants suggesting multiple changes despite having a general preference to make only one change at a time. For instance, as mentioned below, P04 suggested multiple changes with the commercial reports as compared to episode-driven data narratives.

P04’s interventional plan based on commercial reports: “ I would eat all of the nighttime snacks as one. I would wait 15 minutes after my bolus to eat. I would up the correction factor from 7-12 to 1:20 to start with. I would also increase the carb ratio in this time frame to 1:6 to start. ” Here, P04 was unable to prioritize the different action items he listed.

With episode-driven data narratives, P04 came up with fewer changes and a methodical plan for changing his regimen wherein he not only listed the changes to make but ranked the different possible changes by specifying the order in which he would implement them, as noted below.

P04’s interventional plan based on review of episode-driven data narratives: “ I would switch to only having one meal in this time frame to start with and see if this fixes the issue. If the issue persists, I would then change the carb ratio during this time period as necessary .”

7.4 Episode-Driven Narratives Helped Identify Data Needs for Decision-Making

Engagement with episode-driven narratives also helped participants articulate their needs for the data they wanted to have when making decisions, which was not observed with the commercial data reports. We found that participants’ need for data varied across three categories – number of days of exemplary evidence considered sufficient, type of factors they wanted to know about, and the number of factors they wanted to include in care decisions.

7.4.1 Number of days of exemplary evidence: Seeing all versus a fewer number of days. Most of the participants felt that having just one or two days of data as a representative example of an issue is enough and reasonable, “ I think having just a few days is better. Your day to day can be different. What really matters is the average of those days or issues that persist across all days despite all those days being different ” (P04). Three participants (P01, P09, P13) wanted to see more data, all the days, if possible, because of their general preferences and their need to account for the system's mistakes, “ I would want to be able to see different occurrences of this [the issue] happening, as opposed to just this one random day. The computer could have messed up somewhere or something or could be misinterpreting it .” (P13).

7.4.2 Type of factors: Seeing factors related as well as unrelated to an episode. In addition to knowing the potential causes of an episode that were listed in the prototype, participants also wanted to know information on the other factors, particularly those that were not considered related to the episode by the system and those that were related to patient's compliance to self-care behaviors, such as recording data in the insulin pump. For instance, P13, who tended to think very comprehensively about all possible causes, struggled to believe that the factors not listed under potential causes were not an issue and later indicated that he would want more information, “ It doesn't say that this [not recording certain data in the insulin pump] is an issue, but just from looking at this [the presented data], you can't tell if they're putting it in [on all days], which is why it's interesting that it doesn't say that that. ” Because the patient was recording data as expected, the interface did not list it as a potential cause for the episode but P13 wanted to check for himself.

7.4.3 Number of factors to include in decisions: Considering one factor versus a combination of factors. Most of the participants noted that they preferred to make only one change at a time. However, after having looked at the data using episode-driven narratives, they felt comfortable thinking about making more than one change. P10, who was not comfortable making changes with the commercial reports, felt comfortable making two changes with the porotypes, “ they both have equal weight to me and they're both convincing. And I think combined if you're having less meals and giving the correct carb ratio that's a perfect thing there. ”

Because multiple factors were listed as potential causes of a BG pattern, P09 and P15 wanted the interface to provide a ranking for the factors so that they could progressively try different factors one by one, in case their first choice did not produce the desired results. P09 mentioned, “ I would like it if the user interface could rank the potential causes by likelihood. Well, so the most likely culprit is the bolus timing. Then the second most likely culprit is the carb ratio, and potentially the third most likely culprit is the auto correction, so that people know let's work on that but if it's not enough I know I need to make adjustment to let's say the carb ratio, eventually to the auto correction. ”

8 DISCUSSION

In this paper, we identified the nuanced tasks and challenges involved in making decisions from multiple streams of diabetes data. Drawing upon these tasks, we designed and evaluated a set of novel data interfaces called episode-driven data narratives to help Type 1 diabetes (T1D) patients engage with multiple streams of data and make care decisions. In comparing episode-driven narratives with commercial tools used for diabetes data review, we found that episode-driven narratives improved engagement and decision-making with data in three ways. First, episode-driven narratives resulted in better data comprehension and lowered the burden of engaging with data for specific tasks, such as identifying episodes, understanding the impact of different factors on those episodes, and identifying which factors to work on to fix the episode. Second, episode-driven narratives prompted participants to exercise their agency in navigating the complex multiplicity involved in making care decisions from data. Third, episode-driven narratives showed the potential to improve actionability by improving participants’ ability to articulate plans for action. Unlike the commercial tools, participants’ engagement with episode-driven narratives also helped them articulate their needs for data that they wanted to see when making decisions. Specifically, we found that participants’ need for data varied across three dimensions – the number of days of evidence considered sufficient, the type of factors participants wanted to know about, and the number of factors they considered including in the care decisions.

Prior work on personal health informatics has focused on designing systems around the data that users want to track or the insights that users want from the data (e.g., correlation, distribution) but have paid limited attention to the different ways in which users could obtain those insights by engaging with data to make decisions, particularly with multiple streams of data [ 4 ]. This work articulates the nuanced data-based tasks involved in generating insights and making decisions from multidimensional data in the context of Type 1 diabetes. Leveraging the understanding of these tasks, it designs and evaluates novel interfaces to enable the use of health data.

When designing systems to support the use of patient-generated data, most of prior work has focused on supporting clinicians. For instance, Zhang et al. created IDMVis for clinicians to better understand temporal interactions between different data streams [ 51 ]. There is limited work on patient-facing systems, which primarily focus on helping interpret single variables [ 48 ]. In this work, we designed interfaces to enable patient engagement with multidimensional data. We also provide an empirical understanding of how patients leveraged data through these interfaces to identify plans for action. Drawing upon our findings, we now provide design implications for tools to support the use of multidimensional data to navigate decisions that involve the consideration of multiple factors corresponding to different types of data. To support the use of multidimensional data, tools need to constrain the space for exploration and enable just enough exploration of choices and possibilities. Additionally, tools need to leverage data-based explanations to improve the utilization of data by users.

8.1 Constraining the Space for Exploration

With the commercial data reports, having too much data to explore without any structure overwhelmed the participants. Instead of engaging with the data, they made guesses and assumptions. Prior work has explored several approaches to reduce the complexity of interfacing with multidimensional data and making it useful. These include visualization-based approaches and insight-based approaches. Visualization based approached include presenting multiple visual cuts from the data [ 13 ], and presenting multiple data streams as stacked time series [ 27 , 37 ]. Insight-based approaches include presenting correlations between multiple factors and an outcome measure, often using natural language summaries, and presenting outcomes categorized by certain factors affecting them [ 7 , 23 , 29 , 47 , 50 ]. Visualization-based approaches are critiqued for not being actionable enough and insight-based approaches have been critiqued for not providing the raw data to the users, affecting the interpretation of system-generated insights [ 5 , 14 , 21 , 26 ]. We add to this body of work by providing another approach for designing multidimensional data interfaces, which we call episode-driven data narratives , to reduce the complexity of engaging with different types of data while combining high-level insights with the raw data. In designing to present multiple types of data, episodes, and episode-specific domain-heuristics helped us scope down the data that needed to be shown to patients, while still being informative enough. Having fewer days of data to look at, and having the data anchored in episodes and contextualized with domain heuristics enabled participants to engage with the data less overwhelmingly and more productively as compared to the commercial platforms. These findings suggest that by constraining the exploration that users might have to engage in before being able to make inferences from the data, tools can support deeper and productive engagement with multiple types of data.

8.2 Enabling Just Enough Exploration of Various Choices and Possibilities

For each episode, episode-driven narratives presented multiple possible factors (along with data and suggestions) that could have influenced a given episode as indicated by the data. The existence of several possibilities required the users to engage in a certain amount of exploration to better understand each of them. While this could have challenged engagement, we saw that participants used it as an opportunity to better align their choices for self-care actions with their needs and preferences. For instance, they did not necessarily choose to work on the factor that seemed involved on the greatest number of days. They chose the factor that was the easiest for them to work on or what seemed to have the most benefit. Additionally, despite their general preference to make one change at a time, looking at multiple narratives in our interfaces made them comfortable thinking of two changes at a time. On one hand providing multiple potential factors for an episode enabled choices, on the other hand excluding factors that did not seem to be relevant for an episode resulted in skepticism. That is, half the participants wanted information on factors that were deemed unrelated to an episode and not presented in the prototype. Taken together, these experiences suggest that providing a very specific insight (e.g., an episode-factor association) or a recommendation as has been the focus of recent research [ 16 ], even if feasible, might restrict the exploration that is necessary for users to feel comfortable making decisions with data, especially in this specific context where several possibilities exist, making it difficult to identify the best decision. Presenting several possibilities would not only facilitate user autonomy and choices, as seen in this study, but would also help users ensure that the system did not miss anything, possibly improving users’ trust and willingness to use the system.

8.3 Leveraging Evidence-Based Explanations Targeting Different Benefits

Our prototypes explained episode-factor connection using domain heuristics (e.g., daytime high episodes are typically due to carb ratio of meals) and exemplary evidence from the data (e.g., a day with post lunch high BG numbers). Based on the available data, different types of evidence were included in the explanations – data from a single day showing an issue, data from a good day and a bad day in relation to an episode-factor combination, and data to show whether a factor met the guidelines. Each of these examples were leveraged by participants for different benefits. For instance, examples of individual days helped show the presence or absence of a problem. Comparisons between good and bad days in relation to a factor enabled identifying not only the problem but also the solution by showing what worked, increasing participants’ confidence in a particular choice. This suggests that using detailed explanations that leverage different types of data as exemplary evidence can improve engagement with data for making decisions. This observation is in contrast with prior work's findings that providing detailed explanation for a recommendation can negatively affect users’ experiences with the system [ 28 , 44 ]. The differences between our work and prior work can perhaps be attributed to the decisional context. More detailed explanations may be less useful in low-risk decisional situations, such as general sleep or stress management. For high-risk decisional situations, such as changing insulin dose, our work suggests that detailed explanations are relatively more useful and perhaps necessary to avoid assumptions and misinterpretation. Additionally, the existence of explanations might not be enough to enable deeper engagement by users as reported in a recent study [ 17 ]. User engagement can be improved by including insight-specific evidence, as observed in this study, to support careful understanding of system-generated insights.

9 LIMITATIONS

Given the challenges of recruiting from a clinical population, the study involved a small number of participants. Our findings may or may not generalize to a larger sample. Our participants were being seen at one of the two clinics at the same university's health system. Their experiences with the research prototypes and with diabetes management tools may not be representative of the experiences of patients with other chronic conditions, particularly those that do not require tracking multiple types of data and those where the effect of factors does not become easily apparent and is not easily measurable. More work is needed to understand the applicability of this approach for chronic conditions other than diabetes. Patients’ needs may differ from one health condition to another. For instance, some conditions, such as depression, may involve a longer lag between causal events and episode occurrence, making it harder to establish associations. The concept of episode-driven review of data explored in this paper could be applied to the assessment of health data for chronic conditions that are marked by well-defined and easily detectable symptomatic episodes, where episodes are influenced by multiple factors, and where data on different factors and biomarkers is readily available.

10 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we designed and evaluated episode-driven data narratives , a novel prototype to help patients engage with multiple streams of diabetes data (blood glucose, carbs, insulin). When compared with the commercially available diabetes data reports, episode-driven narratives resulted in better data comprehension and lowered the task load of engaging with data to identify suboptimal glycemic episodes, the potential factors causing the episode, and the potential interventions to prevent the episode from recurring. Additionally, episode-driven narratives improved participants’ ability to navigate the multiplicity involved in making decisions (choosing which episode and factor among the many to work on) and to articulate plans for action. Drawing upon our findings, we suggest that tools to support the use of multiple data streams need to limit unnecessary exploration but must also engage users in just enough exploration of the different possibilities to help users exercise their agency and feel comfortable in making decisions. Additionally, there is an opportunity for tools to leverage evidence-based explanations to improve how users utilize the data. Episode-driven data narratives provide an approach to reduce the complexity of interfacing with data while enabling user agency by anchoring data presentation in episodes to scaffold data navigation, by presenting examples from data contextualized with domain knowledge as explanations, and by presenting different possibilities as opposed to directive recommendations.
