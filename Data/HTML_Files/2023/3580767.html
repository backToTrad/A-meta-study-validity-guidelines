Sensorimotor Simulation of Redirected Reaching using Stochastic Optimal Feedback Control

Eric J Gonzalez , Mechanical Engineering, Stanford University, United States, ejgonz@stanford.edu
Sean Follmer , Mechanical Engineering, Stanford University, United States, sfollmer@stanford.edu

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3580767
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Illusory VR interaction techniques such as hand redirection work because humans use vision to adjust their motor commands during movement (e.g., reaching). Existing simulations of redirected reaching are limited, however, and have not yet incorporated important stochastic characteristics like sensorimotor noise, nor captured redirection's effect on movement duration. In this work, we propose adapting a stochastic optimal feedback control (SOFC) model of normal reach to simulate redirection by augmenting sensory feedback at run-time. We present a summary of our simulation and validate it against user data gathered in multiple redirection conditions. We also evaluate the impacts of visual attention on the effectiveness of redirection in real users and replicate the effects in simulation. Our results show that an infinite-horizon SOFC model is able to reproduce key characteristics of redirected reaches and highlight the benefits of SOFC as a tool for simulating, evaluating, and gaining insights about redirection techniques.

1 INTRODUCTION

In recent years, there has been increasing interest in illusory interaction techniques for virtual reality (VR). These techniques leverage the fact that vision strongly influences human perception to augment physical experiences in VR [ 2 , 28 , 40 ]. Hand redirection is one such technique that influences the user's perceived hand position by gradually introducing visual offsets between their real and virtual hands. This has been used for improving the ergonomics of spatial interactions [ 48 ], enabling non-collocated haptic feedback for virtual objects [ 6 , 13 ], and improving the perceived capabilities of haptic devices [ 1 , 25 , 72 ]. Beyond demonstrating new applications, research in this area has largely focused on understanding the perceptibility of redirection [ 27 , 70 ], developing new implementation strategies [ 14 , 26 , 71 ], and evaluating its impact on factors such as user experience [ 7 ] and task performance [ 29 ]. However, there has not yet been significant consideration of the sensorimotor processes involved during redirection, which are critical to understanding the potential and challenges of illusory interactions [ 2 ].

Specifically, with a sensorimotor model of hand redirection, we can simulate important movement features (e.g., hand trajectories, velocities, duration) for different redirection strategies. Such a model could be a powerful computational tool for both developing new implementations (e.g., model predictive control [ 26 ]) and evaluating them more rapidly by reducing the need for user testing. Furthermore, models which consider the noise (i.e., uncertainty) in the sensorimotor system could also capture how redirection is affected by different sensory and interaction contexts (e.g., visual distraction). We believe that developing such models, however, requires incorporating knowledge from outside traditional human-computer interaction (HCI) research.

In the fields of neuroscience and motor control, optimal feedback control (OFC) is the prevailing theory of human movement generation [ 64 , 65 , 68 ]. In OFC, the human body is considered a dynamical system in which control actions (e.g., muscle activations) effect a change in the body's state (e.g., hand position), which is observed through sensory measurements such as vision and proprioception. By comparing these observations with those expected based on an internal model of the body dynamics, humans update their estimate of their body's state. This theory assumes that humans continuously determine the optimal control action by minimizing an overall cost function for the given task (e.g., accurately reach a desired target). Stochastic OFC (SOFC) additionally considers noise in the sensorimotor system, which is a critical aspect of motor planning and control [ 30 , 64 ]. SOFC models in particular have been shown to explain well-known empirically observed phenomena such as the speed-accuracy tradeoff (i.e., Fitts’ Law [ 21 ]) [ 42 , 56 , 64 ], and naturally capture variability across repeated movements. Within the context of HCI, OFC has recently been explored for modeling mouse movements and point-and-click interactions [ 17 , 20 , 46 , 50 ].

In this work, we apply this same approach in the domain of hand redirection – specifically redirected reaching. Leveraging an infinite-horizon SOFC model first presented by Li et al. [ 42 ] – which, unlike the majority of SOFC models, does not require a pre-specified movement duration – we present a simulation of redirected reaching which accounts for the noise characteristics of the sensorimotor system (in contrast with previous redirection simulations, e.g. [ 26 ]). We validate this simulation against real reach trajectories and evaluate its use to predict movement duration and accuracy. To highlight the value of a stoachastic approach in this context, we also present the first evaluation of the effects of visual attention on the effectiveness of redirection and replicate key results in simulation.

1.1 Contributions

In summary, in this work we contribute:

  • A method for simulating redirected reaching based on SOFC that captures trial-to-trial variability, multisensory integration, and the effects of sensorimotor noise.
  • A validation of our simulation method against a dataset of real reaches made with multiple types of redirection.
  • The first evaluation of visual attention on the effectiveness of reach redirection in real users, and replication of key results in simulation.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 OFC Models of Human Movement

Models based on OFC have long been used to describe goal-directed human movement [ 34 , 63 , 68 ]. Early models were deterministic and based on minimizing non-intuitive movement features such as jerk [ 22 , 32 ] and muscle torque [ 66 ]. In contrast, stochastic OFC (SOFC) models capture the noise characteristics of the sensorimotor system (i.e., the multiplicative nature of motor noise [ 30 ] and the uncertainties of our different senses), support more intuitive objectives (e.g., accuracy), and naturally reproduce movement variability. Todorov presented one of the first SOFC methods for simulating sensorimotor control using a modified Linear Quadratic Gaussian (LQG) model [ 64 ]. Later work showed how this approach can be adapted to nonlinear dynamics [ 41 ] and different task requirements [ 44 ]. A significant limitation of this approach, however, is that it assumes the duration of any given movement is known ahead of time. To address this, Li et al. recently proposed an infinite-horizon SOFC formulation in which reach duration is not pre-specified, and found that a single continuously applied model could explain duration differences in normal and target-perturbed reaches (i.e, where the target is shifted mid-reach) [ 42 ]. In this work, we build on this model by extending its use to simulate redirected reaching and its influence on movement duration.

There has been recent interest in OFC models within the HCI community as well. Fischer et al. presented a framework for leveraging OFC for common HCI contexts, and presented a comparison of deterministic and stochastic OFC models of mouse pointing [ 20 ]. Do et al. recently proposed an intermittent control model to simulate point-and-click behavior in users [ 17 ], while Bachynskyi et al. explored dynamical models of aimed mid-air movements [ 8 ]. Within the context of VR interactions and perceptual illusions, however, the use of OFC has not yet been widely explored, though Abtahi et al. recently advocated for using an OFC-based sensorimotor control perspective as a means of understanding, designing, and evaluating illusory interaction techniques [ 2 ]. In this work, we apply this framing to better simulate redirected reaching using SOFC.

2.2 Hand Redirection

Hand redirection gradually displaces the user's virtual hand from their real hand during movement, typically through a linear transformation [ 7 , 29 ]. This technique has been used to alter the perceived shape [ 35 , 73 ], location [ 7 , 13 , 29 ], and orientation [ 1 ] of real-world objects (referred to as passive haptic props ). Other applications include improving the ergonomics of virtual interactions [ 48 ], inducing weight perception [ 57 ], and improving the perceived capabilities of active haptic devices [ 1 , 3 , 25 , 72 ].

Beyond evaluations of user experience [ 13 , 14 ] and task performance [ 29 , 36 ], research on hand redirection has largely focused on understanding its perceptibility [ 1 , 10 , 19 , 27 , 31 , 51 , 70 ] and developing new methodologies [ 13 , 14 , 23 , 25 , 71 , 72 , 73 ]. While such work is essential to understanding and developing effective redirection techniques, several aspects of hand redirection have not yet been thoroughly explored, including its impact on movement duration and the effects of visual attention — both of which we examine here through the lens of sensorimotor control.

Practical models of hand redirection are also just beginning to be explored. Lebrun et al. recently presented a trajectory model which predicts the shape of redirected reach trajectories, but does not consider the dynamics of trajectory generation [ 39 ]. We previously evaluated the minimum jerk model in the context of redirection [ 24 ] and subsequently presented a dynamic OFC model of redirected reaching used within a broader model predictive control framework [ 26 ]; importantly, however, this approach assumed a known movement time and did not consider sensorimotor noise. We build upon this work here by leveraging an infinite-horizon SOFC model to more accurately simulate redirected reaching.

3 Sensorimotor Model & Simulation

In this section, we detail the sensorimotor model of goal-directed reaching used in this work and describe how we apply it to simulate redirected reaching.

3.1 Model Overview

In general, SOFC models are defined by the system dynamics and sensory measurement equations, state-estimation and control laws, and a cost function (e.g., [ 55 , 64 ]). Following previous work [ 42 , 44 ], we model the hand as a linear mass-damper system ( m =1 kg, b =10 Ns/m) driven by a three-dimensional neural control signal. The control signal is converted to muscle force through a first-order low-pass filter ( τ =0.5 s). Note that τ ≈ 0.05 s is thought to be more consistent with human biomechanics [ 64 , 67 ]; we modified this parameter to better fit experimentally found reach durations and discuss the trade-offs in Section 6 . The dynamics of the system (without noise) are characterized by:

\begin{align} \dot{\mathbf {p}}(t) &= \mathbf {v}(t) \end{align}
(1)
\begin{align} m\dot{\mathbf {v}}(t) &= -b\mathbf {v}(t) + \mathbf {f}(t) \end{align}
(2)
\begin{align} \tau \dot{\mathbf {f}}(t) &= -\mathbf {f}(t) + \mathbf {u}(t) \end{align}
(3)
where p ( t ), v ( t ), and f ( t ) are the 3D position, velocity, and muscle force of the hand, respectively, while u ( t ) is the 3D neural control signal. Note that position is measured relative to the reach target, which is assumed to be static. We consider the system state vector to be $\mathbf {x}(t) = [\mathbf {p}(t); \hspace{3.00003pt} \mathbf {v}(t); \hspace{3.00003pt} \mathbf {f}(t)]$ . The complete dynamics including sensorimotor noise are then given by the following stochastic differential equation:
\begin{equation} \text{d}{\bf x} = (A{\bf x} + B{\bf u})\text{d}t + \sum _{i}{C_i{\bf u}}\text{d}\epsilon _i + F \text{d}\boldsymbol {\omega } \end{equation}
(4)
where A and B are derived from ( 1 - 3 ), and C i and F represent the control-dependent and additive noise covariances (see Appendix A.1 for details). Note that multiple matrices C i are required to account for the covariance of motor noise in different dimensions [ 42 , 44 ]. Here ϵ i and ω are scalar and vector Wiener processes, respectively (i.e., their increments are zero-mean Gaussian white noise with variance d t ).

As in [ 42 ], we consider noisy and delayed sensory feedback from both vision and proprioception. The observation equation for each modality is given by:

\begin{equation} \text{d}{\bf y}_s = H_s{\bf x}_s\text{d}t + D_s{\bf x}_s\text{d}\upsilon _s + G_s\text{d}\boldsymbol {\xi }_s \end{equation}
(5)
where the subscript s = v , p represent observations from vision and proprioception, respectively. H s is the observation matrix, which we assume to be the identity matrix. D s and G s represent the state-dependent and additive noise covariances in sensory channel s . Similar to the dynamics, $\upsilon _s$ and ξ s are scalar and vector Wiener processes, respectively. To account for sensory delays, sensory measurements d y s are taken from a delayed state vector x s , obtained by applying a first-order low pass filter to the true state x :
\begin{equation} \tau _s\text{d}{\bf x}_s = (-{\bf x}_s + {\bf x})\text{d}t \end{equation}
(6)
where τ s represents the time constant of the delay for each sensory channel. Based on previous work [ 16 , 42 ], the delays for vision and proprioception were set to τ v = 0.1 s and τ p = 0.05 s. These delays are incorporated into the system dynamics by defining new augmented state and observation variables [ 33 ]:
\begin{align} {\bf x}^* \equiv [\mathbf {x}; \hspace{3.00003pt} \mathbf {x}_v; \hspace{3.00003pt} \mathbf {x}_p]\text{ }, \hspace{10.0pt} \text{d}{\bf y}^* \equiv [\text{d}\mathbf {y}_v; \hspace{3.00003pt} \text{d}\mathbf {y}_p] \end{align}
(7)

Similar to previous SOFC models, the state-estimation and control law equations are given by:

\begin{align} \text{d}\mathbf {\hat{x}}^* &= (A^*\mathbf {\hat{x}}^* + B^*\mathbf {u})\text{d}t + K(\text{d}\mathbf {y}^* - H^*\mathbf {\hat{x}}^*\text{d}t) \end{align}
(8)
\begin{align} \mathbf {u} &= -L\mathbf {\hat{x}}^* \end{align}
(9)
where the starred matrices are the updated dynamics and measurement matrices adjusted to account for the augmented state and measurements (see Appendix A.4 for complete definitions).

The optimal Kalman gain matrix K and control gain matrix L which complete the SOFC model are found by minimizing a given cost function. In the case of goal-directed reach, this cost typically includes the hand's displacement from the reach target [ 44 , 64 ]. The majority of SOFC sensorimotor models perform this optimization over a predefined reach time (i.e., finite horizon ). In contrast, by optimizing over an indefinite reach time (i.e., infinite horizon ), movement duration becomes an emergent property of the model. Critically, a noisy infinite-horizon model also accounts for natural variation in movement duration [ 11 , 56 ]. Similar to previous work [ 42 ], we therefore consider the following steady-state cost:

\begin{equation} J = \lim _{t \rightarrow \infty } \mathbb {E}\left[\left(q_p \Vert {\bf p}(t) \Vert ^2 + q_v \Vert {\bf v}(t) \Vert ^2 \right) + r \Vert {\bf u}(t) \Vert ^2 \right] \end{equation}
(10)
where q p penalizes distance from the target (assumed to be at the origin), q v penalizes hand velocity, and r penalizes control effort. The SOFC problem is therefore to determine the Kalman gain matrix K and control gain matrix L which minimize J , subject to the constraints imposed by ( 4 - 9 ). Details on the derivation of the optimal K and L which solve this problem are given in [ 42 ].

3.2 Relevant Parameters

Complete details of the sensorimotor model including the structure and values of the noise covariance matrices (i.e., C i , F , D p , G p , D v , G v ) are given in Appendix A . Here we highlight the most relevant free parameters as they relate to simulating redirection.

3.2.1 Motor Noise. As control effort in a given direction increases, motor noise in that direction increases as well [ 65 ]. In the present model, the motor noise scale factor σ m determines the strength of this relationship. This parameter scales the elements within the control-dependent noise covariance matrices C i (details given in Appendix A.1 ).

3.2.2 Sensory Noise. The relative magnitudes of noise in vision and proprioception determine how these different signals are integrated into a single estimate [ 18 , 38 ]. The extent to which users are relying on vision to estimate their hand position is highly relevant to redirection, since the technique relies solely on modifying visual feedback of the hand. In general, sensory noise has both state-dependent and additive components: D s and G s , respectively, in this model. State-dependent noise can be used to account for Weber's Law, which states that uncertainty about a sensory stimulus scales with its magnitude [ 60 ]. In this work, we found additive sensory noise to have a larger impact on redirection and so focus our discussion there. Details on the state-dependency of sensory noise in the present model can be found in Appendix A.2 .

In this model, two scaling factors σ v and σ p determine the overall magnitude of additive noise in vision and proprioception, respectively. As in previous work, we assume that under normal conditions proprioception is noisier than vision [ 16 , 42 ]. To accurately model redirection at noticable levels and ensure vision dominates sensory integration, we scale proprioceptive noise to be considerably larger than visual noise:

\begin{equation} \sigma _p = 100\sigma _{v} \end{equation}
(11)
These parameters scale G v and G p accordingly (see Appendix A.2 ). In practice, we manually fit σ v via comparison with experimental data under normal reaching conditions . To simulate interactions under increased visual noise (such as blurred vision or reduced visual attention), we can further scale G v using a noise amplification factor γ .

3.2.3 Cost Weights. The free parameters of the cost function significantly influence simulated reach characteristics. Intuitively, larger values of target distance penalty q p lead to the simulated hand more rapidly approaching the target. Conversely, larger values of the velocity and effort penalties ( q v and r ) tend to slow down reaches and reduce overshoot. We fit these parameters on a per-user basis to better capture variation in reaching strategy between individuals.

3.3 Simulating Redirection

A diagram of the redirected reaching process as modeled by SOFC is shown in Figure 1 . Given a set of model parameters, we can use the resulting optimal feedback controller and estimator to simulate reaching under any redirection algorithm.

We do this by modifying the visual feedback of the hand at simulation runtime. At each timestep, based on the desired redirection method we can easily compute the state of the virtual hand x virtual from the true hand state x . To simulate delayed feedback of the virtual hand, we substitute x virtual for x in Equation ( 6 ) when computing x v . The new update equation for x v and its corresponding noisy observation become:

\begin{align} \tau _v\text{d}{\bf x}_v &= (-{\bf x}_v + {\bf x}_{virtual})\text{d}t \end{align}
(12)
\begin{align} \text{d}{\bf y}_v &= H_v{\bf x}_v\text{d}t + D_v{\bf x}_v\text{d}\upsilon _v + G_v\text{d}\boldsymbol {\xi }_v \end{align}
(13)

With this modification, the state of the real hand can be propagated over any length of time given an initial state, redirection algorithm, and discretization timestep. We discretize our SOFC model with a timestep of 10 ms – details on discretizing continuous stochastic systems can be found in previous work (e.g., [ 41 ]). Model optimization and simulation were performed in MATLAB.

3.3.1 Example Simulation. An example simulation of 20 reaches made under 12° of horizontal angular redirection (detailed in Section 4.2.2 ) is shown in Figure 2 . Looking at the real hand position and speed profiles over time, we see that this model produces the characteristic invariant features of human movement – smooth, S-shaped position profiles and bell-shaped hand speed profiles [ 34 ]. The paths of the real hand in space (blue) are also qualitatively consistent with experimentally observed redirected reach trajectories: initially moving towards the visual target before smoothly curving away in the latter part of the reach [ 7 , 23 , 26 , 39 ].

Unlike previous models of redirected reaching (e.g., [ 23 , 26 ]), however, an SOFC model replicates the trial-to-trial variability we expect to see when making repeated movements, illustrated by the translucent lines in Figure 2 each representing an individual reach. The variance of these trajectories is influenced by the levels of motor noise ( σ m ) and sensory noise ( σ v , σ p ) in the model. By simulating many different trials, we are able to analyze the distributions of different reach characteristics (e.g., reaching error, peak speed, etc.) and simulate the effects of different redirection conditions on these distributions as a whole. Furthermore, since movement time is an emergent (although implicit) property of the model, we are also able to simulate the effects of redirection on reach duration.

4 STUDY 1: MODEL VALIDATION

We conducted a human subjects study to validate the proposed SOFC simulation method as a general model of redirected reaching. We collected data from real users reaching under multiple redirection conditions in VR as they performed a simple target acquisition task. We recorded hand trajectory data as well as measures of accuracy and duration for each reach. Following this, we fit a single model to each participant based only on data from their non-redirected reaches, and evaluate the fit of the simulated reaches to real data in all redirection conditions. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Stanford University.

4.1 Participants

We recruited 12 participants (5M, 4F, 3NB), ages 21-33 ( μ = 26, σ = 3.8) for this study; all had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no sensorimotor impairments. Participants received $ 30 for their involvement.

4.2 Experimental Setup

4.2.1 Task. A diagram of the target acquisition task is shown in Figure 3 . Participants were asked to reach from an origin point (orange sphere) and touch a 2cm diameter target (blue sphere) with the right index finger of their virtual hand. The origin was positioned 20cm below the headset and 20cm in front of the user, while the target was positioned 30cm in front of the origin. Participants were instructed to reach smoothly and naturally to the target in approximately 1 second ( $\pm 10\%$ ); this timing goal was used to ensure consistency between different trials and users. Participants were aware that the mapping between their real and virtual hand could be altered between trials and were instructed to smoothly reach their virtual hand to the target even if they noticed an offset.

4.2.2 Conditions. We gathered data in 4 different redirection conditions. Figure 4 (top) illustrates each method by showing the resulting virtual trajectory for a straight-ahead path in physical space.

Gain, horizontal, and vertical redirection were all implemented using the algorithms described by Zenner et al. [ 70 ]. Gain redirection ( GR ) scales the displacement of physical hand from the reach origin by a factor g to generate the virtual hand position. We tested the following scale factors: g = 0.75, 0.85, 0.95, 1.05, 1.15, 1.25. Horizontal redirection ( HR ) rotates the virtual hand away from the real hand about the reach origin in the horizontal plane (i.e., about the z -axis) by an angle θ . Vertical redirection ( VR ) similarly rotates the virtual hand about the origin in the sagittal plane (i.e., about the x -axis) by an angle ϕ , resulting in vertical displacement. For both HR and VR we collected data for the offsets θ = ϕ = ± 2°, ± 6°, ± 12°.

To test the proposed model more generally beyond the rotation and scaling of common redirection techniques, we also gathered data for a step redirection ( SR ) condition. Here, halfway through the reach the virtual hand would instantaneously shift horizontally by an offset of δ . We tested shifts of δ = ± 1cm and ± 4cm. The magnitudes within each method were selected to span the range of unnoticeable redirection to highly noticeable redirection, based on previous work [ 70 ] and pilot testing. Note that the orientation of the virtual hand was not altered by redirection in any condition.

For further comparison, we included a fifth condition GR* which consisted of non-redirected reaches of the same physical distance as in condition GR . In this condition, the distance of the virtual target to the reach origin was varied to match the nominal physical reach endpoint performed in GR . For example, for g = 0.75 in condition GR , the participant's hand had to travel 40 cm in physical space to reach the virtual target placed 30 cm in front of them in virtual space; for the same magnitude g * = 0.75 in GR* , the virtual target would be positioned at 40 cm with no redirection present. (A diagram comparing nominal real and virtual hand trajectories in conditions GR and GR* is shown in Figure 9 a.) We collected this data to explicitly compare the trajectory and duration differences between normal and redirected reaches which nominally traverse the same physical distance.

4.2.3 Apparatus. Participants wore an HTC Vive Pro Eye head-mounted display (HMD) and held a Vive controller in their left hand to provide input and advance to the different sections of the study. On their right hand, participants wore a splint with retro-reflective markers to enable tracking of their hand without holding a controller or wearing a bulky tracking device. The splint was used to comfortably keep the participant's hand in the pointing position (see Figure 3 ). We used an OptiTrack Prime 13 motion capture system to track the hand and HMD (overriding the Vive tracking system). The virtual study environment was programmed in Unity and run on a PC with an NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1070 GPU.

4.3 Data Collection Procedure

Participants were first seated in a chair and given a brief introduction to the study and concept of hand redirection. The experimenter then helped the participant don the finger splint and HMD.

Upon entering the virtual scene, participants first performed a training session to familiarize themselves with the target acquisition task. During this training, the target changed color based on the participants reach duration at the end of each reach, with the goal of having participants complete their reach in approximately 1 s ( $\pm 10\%$ ). Following the training session, participants then briefly experienced the largest level of each redirection condition to reduce any unexpected novelty effects during data collection.

The study consisted of five blocks of trials, with the first four blocks grouped by the four redirection conditions. Within each block, the first 10 reaches did not have redirection and included the color-coded speed feedback to remind participants of the desired speed. In the remaining trials, participants experienced each redirection magnitude 10 times randomly presented among non-redirected trials, with the specification that 60% of all reaches (excluding the first 10) had no redirection. This was done so participants could not predict the level of redirection prior to movement and thus would begin each reach with the assumption that no redirection was present. The GR , HR , and VR blocks therefore each had 10 + (6 levels × 10 repetitions)/0.4 = 160 trials, and the step redirection block had 10 + (4 levels × 10 repetitions)/0.4 = 110 trials. The four redirection blocks were presented in random order. The fifth block of trials consisted of condition GR* . Here participants completed 10 reaches to each target location (including that of g * = 1), in random order, in addition to the 10 preliminary reaches for a total of 10 + (7 target positions × 10 repetitions) = 80 trials.

In all trials, participants were instructed to press and hold down the trigger of the Vive controller in their left hand while reaching to the target in each trial to provide bounds for when they begin and end their reach. Participants were given a 1-minute break halfway through each block, and 2-5 minute breaks between blocks. The overall duration of the study was approximately 90 minutes.

4.4 Simulation Procedure

4.4.1 Per-user Model Fitting. To account for individual differences, we fit one model per participant. Importantly, we use only data from non-redirected reaches to fit each model. This is consistent with the experimental design, as the majority of reaches (60%) were non-redirected, and redirected trials were presented randomly (i.e., unpredictably) – together, these conditions support the assumption that participants plan and execute their movements initially assuming no redirection. We also assume that this motor plan does not change across trials or conditions, motivated by the fact that the task goal remained the same. In the SOFC framework, this corresponds to using the same controller and estimator (optimized assuming no redirection) across all reaches. The effects of redirection in simulation are therefore purely a response to unmodeled bias in the visual feedback influencing the user's internal estimate of their hand state.

The motor noise scale σ m and visual noise scale σ v parameters were set to 0.1 and 0.01 for all models, respectively, based on comparison with experimental data. These values consistently produced simulated non-redirected reaches with comparable variability to real participants. We fit the cost parameters q p , q v , and r per participant using a grid search with bounds q p ∈ [0.5, 5], q v ∈ [0.0001, 0.01], and r ∈ [0.0001, 0.001]. For each parameter set, we simulated 100 reaches over 2 s ( dt = 10 ms). Following Fischer et al. [ 20 ], we used mean Wasserstein distance (MWD) as our evaluation criteria. To compute this, we first compute the 2-Wasserstein distance (2WD) [ 52 ] between the distributions of real and simulated positions at each timestep. The 2WD, or earth mover's distance , provides a measure of effort required to transform one distribution into another. For two 1D distributions d 1 and d 2 (with quantile functions Q 1 and Q 2 , respectively) the 2WD is given by the following [ 54 ]:

\begin{equation} {\color {black}W_2(d_1,d_2) = \left(\int _0^1 \left| Q_1(p) - Q_2(p) \right|^2 \text{d}p \right)^{1/2}} \end{equation}
(14)
Here, d 1 = d real and d 2 = d sim are the empirically observed distributions of the real and simulated positions at a given timestep, respectively. We compute the 2WD between these distributions using the ECDF-based Distance Measures package in MATLAB [ 5 ] . The MWD of a given parameter set is computed as the average 2WD across all timesteps.

The final q p , q v , and r for each participant were selected such that MWD was minimized. All other model parameters were identical across participants and are listed in Table 1 and Appendix A .

4.4.2 Simulating Reaches. Using the best fit model for each participant, we simulated 100 reach trajectories per magnitude in each study condition (i.e., GR , HR , VR , SR , and GR* ). We generated hand position and velocity trajectories over a simulation period of 2 s with a timestep of 10 ms. At each timestep, the simulated virtual hand position was computed using the appropriate redirection algorithm and the virtual hand state was sampled to simulate visual feedback, as described in Section 3.3 .

4.5 Measures

For each real and simulated reach, we recorded the position of the fingertip at 100 Hz. Position data was smoothed using a zero-phase Butterworth filter (2Hz cut-off frequency) to reduce noise, as commonly done in biomechanics [ 15 , 67 ]. Velocity data was computed using a Savitzky-Golay filter on the smoothed positions.

We then computed the duration and accuracy of each reach. The start time of each reach was considered the time in which the hand (i.e., fingertip) reached 10% of its peak speed [ 11 ], while the stop time was considered the first time in which hand speed fell below 5% of the peak speed, similar to previous work [ 42 , 44 ]. Reach duration was computed as the difference between these times. Reach accuracy was computed as the euclidean distance between the virtual fingertip and center of the virtual target at the reach stop time, as defined above.

The smoothed position and velocity data were trimmed to the reach duration and resampled at 100 evenly-spaced points between 0 and 2 seconds. For reaches lasting less than 2 s, trajectory data was padded with the final measured position and velocity data was padded with zeros. For each participant, the average position and speed (i.e., velocity magnitude) trajectories were computed for each condition. Overall average trajectories were then computed by averaging across participants. To calculate variability between trials for each condition, the variance of the x , y , and z positions were computed at each timestep during the reach. The total movement variance at each timestep was taken as the sum of the variance in each dimension. Overall variance profiles for each condition were computed by averaging across participants. All of the above measures were computed identically for real and simulated data.

We compute two metrics to quantify the fit of the simulated data. For each redirection condition and magnitude, we compute the MWD between the real and simulated position trajectory distributions of each participant, as described in Section 4.4.1 . Additionally, we compute the sum of squares error (SSE) between the average real and simulated position trajectories at each redirection magnitude. Since the real and simulated data are both resampled to 100 evenly spaced points in time, the SSE is given by:

This is intuitive, since participants could not anticipate the level of redirection prior to movement; once the conflict is perceived (either consciously or subconsciously), hand speed is adjusted to compensate for the movement scaling, resulting in the banding effect seen in Figure 5 . Comparatively, on average, simulated reach duration increased by 25% when scaling down, and decreased by 18% when scaling up.

5 STUDY 2: EFFECTS OF VISUAL ATTENTION

To gain more insight into the effects of sensory uncertainty on the effectiveness of hand redirection and further evaluate our SOFC simulation, we conducted a follow up study in which participants performed redirected reaches to the same target while visually focusing on different points in the virtual scene. As the focal point moves further from the reach target, visual feedback of the hand and target become noisier due to the reduced acuity of peripheral vision [ 45 ]. We selected this setup as it mimics the common real-world scenario of reaching to an object while visually attending elsewhere. To the best of our knowledge, the effects of peripheral viewing on hand redirection have not yet been explored.

5.1 Experiment

We recruited an additional 9 participants (5M, 3F, 1NB), ages 19-29 ( μ = 27, σ = 5.9) for this study; all had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no sensorimotor impairments. Participants received $ 15 for their involvement.

In this study, we tested only horizontal redirection with magnitudes of θ = 2°, 6°, and 12°. The study setup (task and apparatus) was identical to that of Study 1, with the additional task requirement of visually focusing on a separate specified target while reaching. The focal target was a 2 cm diameter yellow circle (shown in Figure 10 a), horizontally displaced from the reach target by an angle of β = 0°, 20°, 40°, 60°, or 80° nominally about the participant. To compensate for the limited field of view in the HMD, participants were instructed to rotate their head to a reference point halfway between the focal target and reach target (see Figure 10 a); this enabled us to test larger focal offsets than if participants directly faced the focal target (Figure 10 b). The angle β corresponds to the approximate retinal eccentricity of the reach target in the participants field of view. Trials were grouped into 5 blocks based on focal target displacement and ordered randomly. Within each block, each redirection magnitude was repeated 10 times and 60% of trials had no redirection. Including 10 initial trials with no redirection, this lead to a total of 10 + (3 redirection levels × 10 repetitions)/0.4 = 85 trials per block, presented in random order. Participant gaze data was recorded at 100 Hz for validation using the HTC Vive Pro Eye HMD and VIVE SRanipal SDK.

Prior to analysis, we identified and discarded trials in which the user shifted their fixation to the reach target from the focal target (excluding trials where β = 0°). If the reach target was within ± 10° of the user's gaze ray (i.e., the region of central macular vision [ 61 ]) for a dwell time of more than 200 ms (i.e., a typical fixation duration [ 4 ]), the trial was discarded.

5.2 Simulation

Our SOFC simulation models increase in visual noise using the scaling factor γ . We first fit SOFC model parameters (with γ =1) to each participant using data from non-redirected reaches with β = 0°. To simulate the effects of increasing focal offset, we then manually fit a γ value to a distribution of 100 redirected reaches (12°) uniformly sampled across participants for each β , visually comparing the shapes of simulated and real trajectories . For the conditions with β = 0°, we left γ =1. For the remaining β values, we found γ = 2, 5, 20, and 50 be suitable fits for β = 20°, 40°, 60°, and 80°, respectively. This is consistent with the fact that visual acuity decreases nonlinearly with increasing retinal eccentricity [ 45 ].

5.3 Results

Of the 3825 trials recorded, we identified and discarded 124 trials in which the user shifted their gaze away from the focal target, leaving 3701 trials for analysis.

The top row of Figure 11 shows the average redirected reach trajectories for each focal target offset β . Qualitatively, as β is increased there are clear trends suggesting redirection becomes less effective: that is, there is less corrective movement in the desired direction. Correspondingly, average simulated trajectories (Figure 11 , bottom row) also result in less corrective movement for larger values of γ . Under greater visual uncertainty, both real and simulated trajectories tend to follow a straight path toward the virtual target for the majority of the reach, with corrections (if any) occurring in the form of a slight "hook" toward the redirected target at the end of the reach (e.g., Figure 11 , last column). This highlights how participants rely largely on proprioception and forward models when reaching in the absence of clear visual feedback. Additionally, the hooking behavior may be an artifact of the state-dependency of visual noise – as the virtual hand approaches the target, uncertainty in judgements about their relative positions decreases (as suggested by Weber's Law [ 60 ]). This would lead to participants better perceiving, and thus correcting for, offsets in the virtual hand position as it nears the target.

Increasing visual uncertainty also tended to cause participants to overshoot the reach target. While, on average, simulated trajectories do not display the same degree of overshoot as real data for greater visual uncertainty, when examining individual trajectories (e.g., Figure 12 ) we see that SOFC produces comparable trajectory variance, which does lead to a portion of reaches over/undershooting the target. The individual real and simulated reach trajectories for all participants are provided as supplementary material.

To quantify the impacts of visual attention on the effectiveness of redirection, we analyzed reach duration and accuracy across focal offsets. Additionally, to gauge the effect on trajectory shape, for each redirection magnitude we computed the root mean square deviation (RMSD) between the measured trajectory and the straight line passing through the origin and the corresponding redirection target. We consider only the x and y components of position when computing RMSD.

Figure 13 plots these quantitative metrics for both real and simulated data, pooled by (a) redirection magnitude and (b) focal offset. For each metric, we fit linear mixed effects models to the real and simulated data with focal offset and redirection magnitude as fixed effects and participant as a random effect. We performed a type II analysis of deviance (ANODE) for each model. For both real (R) and simulated (S) reaches, increasing redirection magnitude significantly increased duration (R: χ 2 (1, N = 3701) = 48.67 , p <0.001; S: χ 2 (1, N = 18000) = 13128.66, p <0.001) and RMSD (R: χ 2 (1, N = 180) = 82.90 , p <0.001; S: ( χ 2 1, N = 180) = 6430.86, p<0.001), but did not significantly impact the endpoint error of real reaches (R: χ 2 (1, N = 3701) = 0.77 , p =0.38; S: χ 2 (1, N = 18000) = 515.37, p <0.001). The degree of focal offset was also found to significantly affect the duration (R: χ 2 (1, N = 3701) = 20.52 ; S: χ 2 (1, N = 18000) = 5357.26), endpoint error (R: χ 2 (3701) = 2692.17 ; S: χ 2 (1, N = 18000) = 4516.70), and RMSD (R: χ 2 (1, N = 180) = 60.17 ; S: χ 2 (1, N = 180) = 762.61) of both real and simulated reaches ( p <0.001 for all). For simulated reaches, larger focal offsets tended to reduce reach duration and endpoint error, while for real reaches endpoint error increased. For both real and simulated reaches, however, larger focal offsets increased RMSD in a similar way. Importantly, these findings suggest that indeed redirection became less effective for participants when the hand and target were viewed from peripheral vision. Furthermore, the SOFC model was able to reproduce the impacts of focal offsets on redirected reach trajectory shapes.

6 DISCUSSION

Overall, the findings from our two studies support the use of SOFC as a tool for simulating redirected reaching. The following sections discuss these findings and situate them within the larger scope of HCI modeling and simulation techniques.

6.1 SOFC as a General Redirection Model

Our model validation study demonstrated that simulated reaches generally matched the characteristic features of real redirected reaches in terms of trajectory, speed, and variance profiles, as visualized in Figure 5 . To the best of our knowledge, we are also the first to quantify the effects of redirection on reach duration in real users, shown in Figure 6 . Furthermore, the fact that infinite-horizon SOFC is able to reproduce these trends further supports its relevance for redirection simulation.

6.1.1 Gain Redirection as an Illustrative Example . The clearest effects of redirection on reach duration were in the case of gain redirection ( GR ) – here SOFC was able to reproduce durations within 10% for each redirection magnitude on average. The distinct reach speed profiles leading to this effect on duration were also clearly reproduced (Figure 5 , left column). Moreover, our comparison with non-redirected reaches of the same distance in condition GR* showed that this effect on duration is caused by more than just the distance the real hand must travel; for example, real reaches redirected to travel a physical distance of 40 cm when the visual target was located at 30 cm ( g = 0.75) lasted 35% longer on average than non-redirected reaches to a visual target 40 cm away ( g * = 0.75). The difference stems from the fact that under redirection, the user begins their movement expecting only to reach 30 cm (i.e., where they see the target), while in GR* the user is able to see the true physical target distance of 40 cm from the start, and plans their reach accordingly. This is clearly visualized by the fact that in GR speed profiles (Figure 5 , left column) initially all follow the same shape for different redirection magnitudes, while in GR* reach speed profiles to different targets have distinct shapes (e.g., peak speeds) from the start. Infinite-horizon SOFC captures this subtle difference by encoding the perceived distance from the target as a cost (scaled by q p ).

Unexpectedly, our simulation did not capture the same effect of target distance on reach duration in condition GR* , as we observed above in real reaches. We hypothesize that this is because we used a single model fit to reaches made to the default target distance of 30 cm. Unlike the other conditions, in GR* the user observed the virtual target at a range of different distances, leading them perhaps to adjust their motor plan. In simulation, this would correspond to an adjustment of model parameters. However, since the model parameters fixed for each user across study conditions, this behavior was not captured. From exploratory analysis, we observe that q p tends to have a strong influence on the duration of simulated reaches. While SOFC does a good job of replicating behavior of different redirected reaches to the same virtual target, future work should further investigate how to best capture the effects of reaching to different targets as well.

6.1.2 Generalizability Across Redirection Types . More generally, the fact that a single infinite-horizon SOFC model – with parameters fit only to non-redirected reaches – reproduced reach trajectories with comparable model accuracy across four different redirection conditions (overall median trajectory SSEs: GR = 0.022 m 2 , HR = 0.021 m 2 , VR = 0.019 m 2 , SR = 0.022 m 2 ) further supports SOFC as a suitable general model for redirected reaching.

6.1.3 Advantages Over Previous Redirection Models . It is important to consider how SOFC expands on existing models of redirected reaching – particularly the deterministic OFC model presented in [ 26 ]. While deterministic models can simulate average reach kinematics, SOFC models paint a broader picture, simulating the variability between repeated reaches observed in real users. This variability is visualized both by the variance profiles shown in Figure 5 as well as the length of the whiskers in the boxplots of Figures 6 - 9 indicating the spread of the data. Variability caused by proportional motor noise is one of the core characteristics of human movement, directly leading to important empirically observed phenomena such as Fitts’ Law [ 30 , 64 , 68 ]. We believe capturing this quality in a simulation is valuable for understanding how different redirection strategies impact users’ abilities to perform intended actions accurately. Beyond a lack of motor noise, the model in [ 26 ] did not account for sensory uncertainty or multisensory integration. In short, the simulated impacts of attention on redirected trajectories observed in Study 2 (Figure 11 ) could not be produced with the previous model. Furthermore, the model in [ 26 ] is finite horizon, so movement duration could not be predicted.

6.1.4 Considerations for Infinite vs. Finite-Horizon Models . In this work, we selected an infinite-horizon formulation of SOFC as it does not require a predetermined movement time and has been successfully used in previous reaching simulations [ 11 , 42 , 56 ]. However, it should be noted that this approach somewhat simplifies the problem, as it considers only the steady-state, rather than transient movement, during the optimization process. While this approximates many common movements (i.e., fast reaching), for more complex behaviors – such as targeting a particular duration or type of contact – it may be useful to consider a model that explicitly optimizes transient movement over a finite (or receding) horizon. Importantly, some of the current model's deviations from observed reality (e.g., reduced speed) may have resulted from the fact that users were targeting a certain reach duration. Recent work has explored how finite and infinite-horizon techniques could be used in conjunction through mixed-horizon SOFC [ 12 ], which may be an interesting direction for future work.

6.2 Visual Attention

Our study of the effects of visual attention on redirected reaching provides insight into how effective redirection may be in more ecologically valid interaction scenarios, such as when users are distracted or looking elsewhere in the virtual scene while reaching. These scenarios are common in the real world, so it is important to understand how they translate to the space of redirected interactions in VR. While Zenner et al. evaluated the effects of vibrotactile, auditory, and cognitive distractions on the detection thresholds of hand redirection [ 70 ], the effects of visual attention – specifically peripheral viewing angle – have not yet been explored to the best of our knowledge. Clearly when the virtual hand and target are outside the user's field of view, redirection is entirely ineffective. Our findings show that this degradation of redirection's "effectiveness" – measured by the RMSD of the virtual trajectory with respect to a straight-line path to the virtual target – is actually continuous, increasing RMSD as the reach target moves further into peripheral vision (Figure 13 b, right). Visually, this is evidenced by users’ reach trajectories making less corrections to compensate for redirection (Figure 11 , top row). At the individual reach level, this increases endpoint error and variability (e.g., Figure 12 , top right). Importantly, SOFC readily replicates these trends by adjusting a single parameter: the scale of visual noise (parameterized by γ ). Consistent with the fact that visual acuity increases nonlinearly with retinal eccentricity [ 45 ], the parameters γ = 1, 2, 5, 10, and 50 produced reasonable approximations for real reaches made with focal offsets of β = 0°, 20°, 40°, 60°, and 80°, respectively (e.g., Figure 12 ).

Importantly, visual uncertainty can also be explicitly leveraged to apply redirection in a less noticeable way. This is illustrated by the blink-suppressed hand redirection technique [ 71 ], where the virtual hand is shifted instantaneously during blinking (i.e., a momentary state of very high visual uncertainty) and as a result the range of undetectable redirection is increased. We believe similar results may be possible by exploiting moments where the virtual hand is in the user's peripheral vision, and, moreover, that an SOFC model would be valuable to optimize such a technique.

7 LIMITATIONS

The SOFC model we use has three key limitations. First, we do not consider the joint dynamics and constraints of the arm here. While this is a common assumption (e.g., [ 42 , 44 , 64 ]), to best model interactions at the limits of users’ reachable space these additional nonlinearities must be considered. One approach could be to build upon Li and Todorov's iterative linearization method for SOFC models with nonlinear dynamics [ 41 ].

Second, as mentioned in Section 3 , while a muscle delay of τ ≈ 0.05 s is more compatible with human biomechanics [ 64 , 67 ], in this work we used τ = 0.5 s. The reason for this choice is that typically in past work, recorded movements were made as fast as possible from one point to another [ 11 , 42 ], so the passive dynamics of the arm dominate the resulting motion. In this case, since redirection is thought to be more effective for slower reaches [ 7 , 13 , 70 ], we instructed users to reach with a frequency of approximately of 1 s per reach. As a result, reaches simulated with τ = 0.05 s were roughly twice as fast as those empirically observed. With the current form of the cost function, we were not able to produce slower reaches at τ = 0.05 s without significantly skewing the shape of the speed profile, which is not the behavior observed. To compensate for this, we increased the muscle time constant such that simulated speed profiles better matched real data without adjusting the form of the cost function. In the future, an additional goal capturing desired duration could be incorporated within the cost function to eliminate the need to adjust the modeled hand dynamics which in reality are known to be constant.

Third, in our simulation we also assumed proprioception is 100 times noisier than vision, which is significantly larger than previous work – typically a factor of 2.5-5 is used [ 16 , 42 ]. This value was required to ensure that simulation produced redirected reaches in which the virtual hand reached the target in all conditions. Since multisensory integration is modeled as an optimal Bayesian process, a single unified estimate is produced by weighing individual percepts inversely proportional to their noise level [ 16 , 18 , 38 ]. This is built on the assumption that vision and proprioception are completely integrated based on their true precision, which is the case for normal reaching, and likely the case of imperceptible sensory conflicts. For larger and more clearly noticable levels of redirection, however, users must place more emphasis on vision, as their goal is to touch the target with their virtual hand even if they do not feel that it is aligned with their true hand position . Along these lines, Limanowski recently presented a compelling review suggesting that humans may adjust their prior beliefs about the precision of vision and proprioception in order to enable a more flexible body representation during visuo-proprioceptive conflicts [ 43 ]. The artificial inflation of proprioceptive noise we use in this model is consistent with this theory of top-down precision control.

Additionally, our visual attention study prioritized ecological validity over precise experimental control. Because of this, we could not control the precise location of the visual target within the field of view – minor differences occur based on the participant's relative head position. In this way, our results should be viewed as an approximation of the general trends occurring as the reach target is viewed more and more from the periphery.

8 FUTURE WORK

Perhaps the most interesting future direction of this work would be to adjust the sensorimotor model to explicitly account for adaptation to redirection. This could follow the form of Braun et al. [ 11 ], where users’ belief about visuomotor rotation is incorporated into a nonlinear dynamical model and updated across different trials. Similarly, Ernst & Di Luca's approach of modeling sensorimotor remapping as learning sensory bias through Bayesian inference techniques could also be applicable. A recent longitudinal study by Kohm et al. found that users’ coordination and reach duration changed over time with repeated exposure to redirection [ 37 ]; it would be valuable to investigate whether a modified SOFC model could perhaps capture this adaptation as well.

While we have shown that infinite-horizon SOFC can capture core characteristics of sensorimotor behavior observed in real users, other models such as the recently proposed Intermittent Control (IC) model could be explored [ 46 ]. In IC, sensory feedback is sampled to adjust the control applied in open-loop (or refractory [ 62 ]) periods – larger discrepancies between observed and expected sensory feedback triggers more rapid sampling, similar to how large redirection offsets might draw users’ attention.

Furthermore, additional research is needed to connect the low-level kinematic aspects of redirection (e.g., trajectory generation) as reported in this work with high-level cognitive aspects, such as illusion detectability. From intuition, it seems logical that being aware of significant redirection may influence how users choose to reach. It would be both interesting and highly valuable to explore if it is possible to infer users’ high-level awareness of redirection from their movement data alone. From a modeling standpoint, this could be partially incorporated as users’ uncertainty in the modeled visuomotor mapping.

Finally, we believe that models of hand redirection such as the one proposed here will play an important role in enabling new redirection techniques and applications . We previously demonstrated this using a simpler model by developing a model predictive control framework for optimizing redirection on-the-fly [ 26 ]. Stochastic models could be particularly valuable to the development of future redirection techniques if built into a development toolkit (similar to HaRT [ 69 ]). Beyond generating plausible trajectories, researchers could simulate important characteristics of the user and environment that previous models could not capture. This includes simulating users with different motor abilities (via motor noise, as in [ 59 ]), and potentially capturing things like age effects. From a system standpoint, SOFC could also be useful for simulating the effects of tracking noise and visual latency on redirection and other VR interaction techniques. Beginning with the additional insights afforded by SOFC models (e.g., variability, duration, uncertainty), we envision a future where computational interaction techniques [ 53 ] can be used to better determine how and when to best apply redirection, incorporating factors such as visual attention, environmental constraints, and object affordances within a unified, context-aware redirection framework.

9 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we propose and validate an approach for simulating redirected reaching in VR using an infinite-horizon stochastic optimal feedback control (SOFC) model of the sensorimotor system. To our knowledge, this is the first redirection simulation which accounts for: (1) the natural variability in reach trajectory and duration within between both users and trials, (2) the integration of vision and proprioception, and (3) the effects of sensory uncertainty. We compared our simulation against redirected reaches made by real users in four distinct redirection conditions and found that, for each user, a single SOFC model fit to non-redirected reach data reproduced characteristic features of reaching for all tested redirection methods. We also presented the first evaluation of the effects of visual attention on redirected reaching. We found that as the hand and target are viewed more and more from the user's periphery, the effectiveness of redirection continually degrades. Importantly, we also found that the same SOFC simulation approach reproduces this effect as visual uncertainty is increased in the model. These results suggest that SOFC is a suitable general model for redirected reaching in many different contexts. We believe that stochastic sensorimotor models of redirected reaching are valuable for understanding and evaluating redirection methods, and moreover will be an important computational tool for the development of on-the-fly and contextually-aware redirection techniques.

\begin{align*} C_3 = \frac{\sigma _m}{4} * B\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1\\ 0 & 0 & 0\\ -1 & 0 & 0\\\end{bmatrix}, \hspace{20.0pt} C_4 = \frac{\sigma _m}{4} * B\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1\\ 0 & -1 & 0\\\end{bmatrix} \end{align*}

A.1.3 Additive Motor Noise. . The present model also supports additive motor noise, which is independent of control effort. This is typically small and less influential than the control-dependent motor noise [ 42 ]. The variance of this noise is determined by matrix F in Equation ( 4 ). Here we use the following additive noise matrix:

\begin{align*} F = I_3 \otimes \begin{bmatrix} 0.0001 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0.001 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\\end{bmatrix} \end{align*}
which results in additive position noise of 0.1 mm SD and additive velocity noise of 1 mm/s SD.

A.2 Sensory Measurement

Both vision and proprioception sample from noisy, delayed versions of the true hand state. We assume the state is fully observable by both senses, thus the observation matrices are given by:

\begin{align*} H_p = H_v = I_9 \end{align*}

A.2.1 State-dependent Sensory Noise. Following Li et al. [ 42 ], we model state-dependent sensory noise such that noise in position, velocity, and acceleration are proportional to the Weber fractions of visual perception in each of these categories [ 9 , 47 , 49 ]. In our model, we use:

\begin{align*} D_v = I_3 \otimes 0.004\begin{bmatrix} 0.03 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0.06 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0.3\\\end{bmatrix}, \hspace{20.0pt} D_p = 100D_v \end{align*}
where the scaling factor 0.004 was selected based on comparison with experimental data. The scaling factor of 100 was selected to match Equation ( 11 ).

A.2.2 Additive Sensory Noise. The relative magnitudes of elements in the additive sensory noise matrices were selected to correspond with previous work [ 42 ].

\begin{align*} G_v &= I_3 \otimes \gamma * \sigma _v \begin{bmatrix} 0.0002 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0.0004 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0.01\\\end{bmatrix},\\ G_p &= I_3 \otimes \sigma _p \begin{bmatrix} 0.0002 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0.0004 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0.01\\\end{bmatrix} \end{align*}
Here σ v and σ p are scaling factors for visual and proprioceptive noise in the system under normal sensory conditions. We fixed σ p = 100 σ v , and found σ v = 0.01 to be a suitable fit to experimental data. The parameter γ is an additional noise amplification factor used to simulate scenarios with greater visual uncertainty. Under normal sensory conditions, we set γ = 1.

A.3 Cost Function

In order to make the cost function amenable to traditional solution techniques for SOFC, it should be written in quadratic form. Rewriting Equation ( 10 ), we have:

\begin{equation} J = \lim _{t \rightarrow \infty } \mathbb {E}\left[{\bf x}^TQ{\bf x} + {\bf u}^TR{\bf u}\right] \end{equation}
(17)
where the state and control penalty matrices are defined as:
\begin{align*} Q = I_3 \otimes \begin{bmatrix} q_p & 0 & 0\\ 0 & q_v & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0\\\end{bmatrix}, \hspace{20.0pt} R = r*I_3 \end{align*}

A.4 Augmented System

As mentioned previously, to account for sensory delays we augment our state and measurement to be of the form:

\begin{align*} {\bf x}^* = [{\bf x};\hspace{3.00003pt} {\bf x}_v;\hspace{3.00003pt} {\bf x}_p], \hspace{20.0pt} \text{d}{\bf y}^* = [\text{d}{\bf y}_v;\hspace{3.00003pt} \text{d}{\bf y}_p] \end{align*}
where x v and x p are delayed copies of the original state x . Equations ( 4 ) and ( 5 ) are then updated to:
\begin{align} \text{d}{\bf x}^* &= (A^*{\bf x}^* + B^*{\bf u})\text{d}t + \sum _{i}{C^*_i{\bf u}}\text{d}\epsilon _i + F^*\text{d}\boldsymbol {\omega }^* \end{align}
(18)
\begin{align} \text{d}{\bf y}^* &= H^*{\bf x}^*\text{d}t + D^*{\bf x}^*\text{d}\upsilon _s + G^*\text{d}\boldsymbol {\xi }^* \end{align}
(19)
where the starred matrices are defined as follows, with 0 and 1 representing appropriately-sized zero and identity matrices, respectively:
\begin{align*} A^* \equiv \begin{bmatrix} A & 0 & 0\\ 1/\tau _v & -1/\tau _v & 0\\ 1/\tau _p & 0 & -1/\tau _p\\\end{bmatrix}, \hspace{5.0pt} B^* \equiv \begin{bmatrix} B \\ 0 \\ 0 \\\end{bmatrix}, \hspace{5.0pt} C_i^* \equiv \begin{bmatrix} C_i \\ 0 \\ 0 \\\end{bmatrix}, \hspace{5.0pt} F^* \equiv \begin{bmatrix} F\\ 0 \\ 0 \\\end{bmatrix} \end{align*}
\begin{align*} H^* \equiv \begin{bmatrix} 0 & H_v & 0\\ 0 & 0 & H_p\\\end{bmatrix}, \hspace{5.0pt} D^* \equiv \begin{bmatrix} 0 & D_v & 0\\ 0 & 0 & D_p\\\end{bmatrix}, \hspace{5.0pt} G^* \equiv \begin{bmatrix} G_v & 0 \\ 0 & G_p \\\end{bmatrix} \end{align*}
\begin{align*} \text{d}\boldsymbol {\omega }^* = \text{d}\boldsymbol {\omega }, \hspace{5.0pt} \text{d}\boldsymbol {\xi }^* \equiv \begin{bmatrix} \text{d}\boldsymbol {\xi }_v\\ \text{d}\boldsymbol {\xi }_p\\\end{bmatrix} \end{align*}

Additionally, the state cost matrix is updated such that the cost function can be written in terms of x * . That is, we can write:

\begin{equation} J = \lim _{t \rightarrow \infty } \mathbb {E}\left[{\bf x}^{*T}Q^*{\bf x}^* + {\bf u}^TR{\bf u}\right] \end{equation}
(20)
where the updated state cost matrix is defined as:
\begin{align*} Q^* \equiv \begin{bmatrix} Q & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0\\\end{bmatrix} \end{align*}

Given these augmented dynamics, measurement, and cost equations, the optimal Kalman-gain matrix K and control-gain matrix L can be found directly via the iterative algorithm presented in [ 42 ].
