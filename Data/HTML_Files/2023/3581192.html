Assisting with Fingertip Force Control by Active Bio-Acoustic Sensing and Electrical Muscle Stimulation

Arinobu Niijima , NTT Human Informatics Laboratories, NTT Corporation, Japan, arinobu.niijima.hw@hco.ntt.co.jp
Yuki Kubo , NTT Human Informatics Laboratories, NTT Corporation, Japan, yuki.kubo.sh@hco.ntt.co.jp

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581192
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Fingertip force control plays an important role in learning motor skills. Exoskeleton gloves have been developed to assist with fingertip force control, but having the equipment on the fingers interferes with finger motion control and tactile sensation. Thus, we present a system for assisting with voluntary fingertip force control that does not require any devices to be worn on the fingers. In this study, we focused particularly on lateral pinch force, which is grip force achieved with the pad of the thumb and the lateral surface of the index finger to grasp objects. We use active bio-acoustic sensing to estimate voluntary pinch force with piezo elements attached to the back of the hand and electrical muscle stimulation (EMS) to the forearm to control involuntary pinch force in a closed-loop system. We developed three prototypes and conducted user studies to investigate whether our system can assist with pinch force control under several target forces, from weak to strong. Our user studies showed that the combination of active bio-acoustic sensing and EMS can assist users in maintaining the pinch force closer to the target force.

1 INTRODUCTION

Electrical muscle stimulation (EMS) has been investigated since the 1960s–1970s in rehabilitation medicine [ 66 ], and human-computer interaction researchers have explored the uses of EMS for the learning of motor skills. EMS can steer the user's limbs [ 3 , 10 , 37 , 41 , 59 , 73 ]; thus, previous studies have proposed methods of helping users achieve good form and posture by using EMS [ 12 , 20 , 48 , 69 , 70 ]. Fingertip force control is also important for learning motor skills; sensitive force control is required for athletes in various sports such as golf [ 30 ], tennis [ 5 ], cricket [ 44 ], and kendo [ 23 ]. Surgeons require sensitive fingertip force control [ 7 , 21 , 64 , 74 ], and force feedback systems are in high demand to control the grip force for teleoperated medical interventions [ 1 , 18 ].

The aim of our study was to explore the use of EMS to support fingertip force control. As a first step, we focus on lateral pinch force, which is grip force achieved with the pad of the thumb and the lateral surface of the index finger to grasp objects [ 71 ]. There are two forearm muscles that are mainly relevant to pinch force: the flexor pollicis longus muscle and extensor pollicis longus muscle. Thus, the target muscles that we have to consider are only these two muscles.

To assist in controlling pinch force in a closed-loop system, it is necessary to measure the force with wearable or embedded sensors. For example, it is possible to measure fingertip force with sensors worn on the fingers [ 17 , 42 , 62 , 63 ]. Exoskeleton gloves [ 19 , 55 , 68 ] can also assist with fingertip force control. However, devices attached to the fingers would interfere with the user's finger manipulation and change the tactile sensation. By using electromyogram (EMG) sensors on the forearm, it is possible to estimate fingertip force with no devices on the fingers [ 4 , 16 , 72 ]. However, the EMS current would affect measured signals with EMG sensors. Therefore, it is necessary to consider how to reduce the noise when EMS and EMG sensors are used at the same time [ 11 , 49 ].

We present a wearable system that assists with fingertip force control by using EMS and active bio-acoustic sensing [ 32 ]. Active bio-acoustic sensing is based on the principle of active acoustic sensing [ 53 , 54 ] and can be used for micro-gesture recognition and discrimination of different levels of touch force. We use a sensing method for estimating fingertip force that uses a machine-learning regression model. The advantage of combining active bio-acoustic sensing and EMS is that the user's fingers will be free because the piezo elements for the sensing are attached to the back of the hand and the electrodes for EMS are attached to the forearm. It is also possible to use active bio-acoustic sensing while presenting EMS because the EMS current does not interfere with acoustic signals. Therefore, it is possible to control the intensity of EMS depending on the fingertip force estimated by active bio-acoustic sensing in a closed-loop system; our wearable system can assist the user in fingertip force control interactively. We developed three prototypes, as shown in Figure 1 , and conducted user studies to investigate whether our system can assist with fingertip force control under several target forces, from weak to strong.

Our main contribution is presenting a novel combination of active bio-acoustic sensing and EMS to develop a wearable system that assists with force control and does not require the wearing of any devices on the fingers; this initial study will lead to exploring the usability of EMS-based closed-loop systems for various users requiring sensitive fingertip force control. 1

2 RELATED WORK

Our approach builds on EMS research for assisting with the learning of motor skills, force feedback, and force control.

2.1 Electrical muscle stimulation for learning motor skills

Previous studies used EMS for the learning of motor skills, such as playing musical instruments [ 12 , 47 , 48 , 69 ] and sports [ 20 , 70 ]. Tamaki et al. [ 69 ] presented PossessedHand, which is worn on the forearm and manipulates users’ wrists and fingers by EMS to help beginners learn to play musical instruments. Ebisu et al. [ 12 ] also applied EMS to users’ arms and legs to reproduce generated rhythms, which was useful in helping beginners learn musical instruments. Niijima et al. presented EMS-based systems that support reducing muscle activity when playing a tremolo on the piano [ 48 ] and support learning muscle synergies when playing a scale on the piano [ 47 ]. Hassan et al. [ 20 ] presented FootStriker, which is a wearable running-assist system that uses EMS to control the foot angle before landing. Tatsuno et al. [ 70 ] developed a real-time training system that uses EMS to teach the correct rotation of the arms. These studies focused on the form and posture of motor skills. Our study focused on the force control of motor skills.

2.2 Electrical muscle stimulation for force feedback

EMS can be used to provide force feedback as well as steer the limbs. Lopes et al. proposed several methods for providing force feedback by applying EMS to the forearm for video games [ 35 ], virtual reality applications [ 36 , 39 ], mixed reality applications [ 40 ], and real objects [ 38 ]. Pfeiffer et al. also demonstrated force feedback by applying EMS to the forearm [ 61 ] and implemented it in a wearable toolkit [ 58 ] and e-textiles [ 60 ]. These studies revealed that it is possible to generate various types of force feedback by applying EMS to certain muscles.

2.3 Electrical muscle stimulation for force control

Previous studies presented force-control systems using EMG and EMS [ 11 , 45 , 49 ]. Nishida and Suzuki [ 49 ] developed a pair of wearable kinesthetic input-output devices, called bioSync. The devices can measure EMG and use EMS on the basis of EMG data with common electrodes by using a gate-switching mechanism and a mechanism for discharging residual potential. Duente et al. [ 11 ] presented MuscleIO, which measures EMG and presents EMS with common electrodes. By presenting EMS running at 25 Hz and 50-μs spikes, the EMG input signal can be measured in the gaps of the EMS output signal. Niijima et al. [ 45 ] proposed using facial electrical stimulation to control the maximum voluntary contraction of the upper limbs. Like the above studies, when EMG and EMS are used simultaneously, it is necessary to use a switching mechanism, measure EMG in gaps of EMS, or separate the body parts to which electrodes are attached. On the contrary, our approach explores the ability to estimate force while presenting EMS at the same time so that the system can interactively control the intensity of EMS on the basis of the estimated force.

3 IMPLEMENTATION

We use active bio-acoustic sensing to estimate voluntary pinch force with piezo elements attached to the back of the hand and EMS to the forearm to control involuntary pinch force in a closed-loop system, as shown in Figure 2 .

3.1 Active bio-acoustic sensing for pinch force estimation

3.1.1 Hardware. We implemented our active bio-acoustic sensing system by referring to the work of Kubo et al. [ 32 ]. The system consists of two piezo elements attached to the back of the hand, as shown in Figure 1 . The piezo elements are connected to an audio interface (Steinberg, UR22mkII). One is used for a speaker inducing a wave-pattern traveling through the user's hand, and the other is used for a microphone receiving the vibration response.

3.1.2 Software. The software consists of a sweep-signal generator, vibration-response analyzer, and force-estimation engine using a machine-learning regression model, as shown in Figure 2 . The sweep-signal generator emits sinusoidal sweep signals from 20–40 kHz, increasing linearly in intervals of 20 ms repeatedly. The frequency range is determined on the basis of the work of Ono et al. [ 53 ]. The sampling rate is 96 kHz. Audio signals passing through a Hann window are converted to the power spectrum by using a fast Fourier transform (FFT) with 8192 samples. From the power spectrum of 20–40 kHz that is rescaled to 0–1, it constructs a 400-element feature vector using a peak-detection algorithm and resampling to obtain valid values for the force-estimation engine. Using the feature vectors as input, we use machine-learning regression models, such as support vector regression (SVR) and Gaussian process regression (GPR), for the force estimation.

3.1.3 Principle. When a user changes the posture of the thumb or applies a pinch force, the shape and hardness of the back of the hand are changed due to the muscles and bones. Therefore, the boundary condition between the piezo elements and skin changes similar to different touch gestures in active acoustic sensing [ 53 ]. Figure 3 shows examples of feature vectors under different posture and force conditions; the feature vectors differ between each condition. Estimating the posture and force simultaneously with only two piezo elements attached to the back of the hand is an important advantage for active bio-acoustic sensing compared with other sensing methods, e.g., EMG cannot detect posture with no force because there are no signals under no-force conditions. We consider this advantage will be useful when we use our system for various sports training in which there are different forces and postures to grip [ 75 ].

3.2 Electrical muscle stimulation for involuntary force control

3.2.1 Hardware. We generate and use EMS by using a function generator (Nihon Kohden Corporation, SEN-8203) and isolators (Nihon Kohden Corporation, SS104-J) connected to pairs of disposable electrodes (Nihon Kohden Corporation, F-150M). The electrodes are placed on the flexor pollicis longus and extensor pollicis longus muscles of the right forearm. On the basis of a Japanese Industrial Standards standard (electric therapy apparatus for home use, JIS T 2003:2008) and design guideline [ 31 ], we set the maximum intensity of EMS; the pulse width is 200 μs, maximum frequency is 200 Hz, and maximum current is 20 mA. To prevent pain caused by EMS, we have to calibrate the applied current for each user before using the system. A microcomputer (Arduino, Arduino Uno) is connected to the function generator to emit a trigger to produce EMS.

3.2.2 Software. For involuntary pinch force control, we use proportional control or on-off control to change the EMS intensity. We use pulse frequency modulation (PFM) control to do proportional control; the higher the frequency is, the higher the intensity [ 9 ]. The microcomputer connected to the function generator is used for PFM control. The intensity-control method can be altered for pulse width modulation (PWM) control in principle, though the function generator in our system does not support PWM control. We apply EMS to the flexor and extensor pollicis longus muscles as follows. When the current pinch force is less than the target force, EMS is applied to the flexor pollicis longus muscle to increase the pinch force. When the current pinch force is higher than the target force, EMS is applied to the extensor pollicis longus muscle to decrease the pinch force.

We consider that sensitive control can be achieved when the user's voluntary pinch force control is combined with involuntary control by EMS.

4 PROTOTYPES

In this section, we describe the three prototypes shown in Figure 1 . All prototypes were designed on the basis of the common principles regarding active bio-acoustic sensing and EMS. The difference between the three prototypes is the means of attaching piezo elements to the back of the hand, but all can estimate fingertip force. Thus, we can select the form depending on the application; users can wear the piezo elements with double-sided tape, a wrist brace, or fingerless glove.

4.1 First prototype

We developed the first prototype, which started as a follow-up implementation of an existing system [ 32 ]. The system consists of two piezo elements (THRIVE, K2512BP1) attached to acrylic plates with hot glue. We use double-sided medical tape (3M, 2477 Double-Coated TPE Silicone Acrylate Medical Tape). The piezo elements are placed on the back of the hand; the locations are 10 mm from the metacarpal joints following the work of Kubo et al. [ 32 ]. We use an SVR model as a regression model for force estimation. We use the scikit-learn library (ver. 0.23.2) [ 57 ] to construct the SVR model. The parameters are set on the basis of grid search; “kernel” is “rbf”, “gamma” is 1, and “C” is 10.

4.2 Second prototype

We developed the second prototype that changes the position of the piezo elements to the thenar muscles. The system consists of two piezo contact microphones (Yibuy) put together with double-sided tape and attached to the thenar muscles on the back of the hand wrapped in a wrist brace. When a user changes the posture of the thumb or applies a pinch force, the thenar muscles bulge and push the piezo contact microphones. Therefore, the boundary condition between two piezo contact microphones changes. We use a GPR model as a regression model for force estimation because it was slightly better than the SVR model in a pilot study; we validated 2500 samples obtained from 5 participants, and the mean absolute error (MAE) was 1.49 N for the GPR model and 1.60 N for the SVR model. We use MATLAB to construct the GPR model.

4.3 Third prototype

We developed the third prototype consisting of two embedded piezo elements (THRIVE, K2512BP1) in a fingerless glove to be located on the back of the hand. This prototype is thinner and easy to wear than the previous two prototypes so that a user can wear it more comfortably. The software is the same as the second prototype regarding the regression model based on a pilot study; we validated 2500 samples obtained from 5 participants, and the MAE was 1.46 N for the GPR model and 1.49 N for the SVR model.

4.4 Suitable use of each prototype

The first prototype is the simplest. It is suitable for use only for a specific motor-skill-learning scenario. There is no need to consider the size of the user's hand because it can adjust the positions of the piezo elements. However, it is not suitable for daily use, because the double-sided tape needs to be replaced every time. The estimated force's accuracy also worsens when the user grips with strong force (detailed in Section 6.1.5). In contrast, the second and third prototypes are suitable for daily use because users simply need to wear the wrist brace or fingerless glove. When using the second prototype, the user can attach another sensor [ 34 ] or actuator [ 67 ] on the back of the hand because the piezo elements are on the thenar muscles. The third prototype can be used in various sporting situations in which players use gloves such as golf. The most important benefit of our system is that the form can be varied depending on the situation due to the robustness of the combination of active bio-acoustic sensing and EMS.

5 PILOT STUDIES

Before conducting the user studies, we conducted four pilot studies. We first investigated whether estimating the force using active bio-acoustic sensing with each prototype was possible. We then investigated the effect of the EMS current on the acoustic signals for active bio-acoustic sensing. Next, we investigated the effect of EMS in assisting with pinch force control. Finally, we investigated the accuracy of force estimation by using EMG sensors, a means of force estimation with no devices on the fingers, to compare it with active bio-acoustic sensing.

5.1 Force estimation by active bio-acoustic sensing

For each prototype, we compared the estimated force and force-sensor data. The accuracy of force estimation plays an important role in our system because EMS is applied on the basis of the difference between the estimated force and target force. Thus, we conducted this pilot study by recruiting more participants than those in the other pilot studies. We conducted each verification with one participant (male, 30s) for the first prototype, eight participants (three female, five male, 20s–50s) for the second prototype, and eight participants (two female, six male, 20s–50s) for the third prototype. Each participant wore the prototype on the back of the right hand. We measured the lateral pinch force using a force sensor (IMADA, ZTS-200N) and compared it with the force estimated by active bio-acoustic sensing. We first collected 1000 samples as training data to construct the regression model while the participant changed the pinch force in the range of 0–10 N. We then collected 500 samples for test data to compare the estimated force and force-sensor data while the participant changed the pinch force in the range of 0–10 N.

Figure 4 shows example results for each prototype comparing the estimated force and ground truth (force-sensor data). The MAE was 0.91 N for the first prototype, 1.26 N for the second prototype, and 1.38 N for the third prototype on average. Pearson's correlation coefficient was 0.93 for the first prototype, 0.86 for the second prototype, and 0.89 for the third prototype on average. The results suggest that our system can estimate pinch force by using active bio-acoustic sensing; the average error was under 1.5 N.

5.2 Effect of current on acoustic signals

We confirmed that the EMS current does not interfere with acoustic signals for active bio-acoustic sensing as follows. One participant (man, 30s) wore the first prototype on the back of his right hand. He also attached a pair of electrodes to the extensor pollicis longus muscle of the right forearm. We collected 1000 samples of the normalized power spectrum of the active bio-acoustic sensing under two conditions of the right-hand posture: opening and gripping. We first collected the data without EMS then collected the data with EMS with the current set at 10 mA. Under both posture conditions, the power spectrum with EMS was almost the same as that without EMS; the correlation coefficients were 1. The results indicate that the EMS current does not interfere with acoustic signals for active bio-acoustic sensing.

5.3 Pinch force control with electrical muscle stimulation

We conducted a pilot study to investigate the effectiveness of EMS in assisting with pinch force control. One participant (man, 30s) attached the pairs of electrodes to the flexor pollicis longus and extensor pollicis longus muscles of the right forearm. The EMS current was set at 8 mA for the flexor pollicis longus muscle and 11 mA for the extensor pollicis longus muscle. We then measured the pinch force by using the force sensor (IMADA, ZTS-200N) and applied EMS with proportional control depending on the current force measured with the force sensor. The target forces were set at 3, 6, and 9 N; the participant pinched the force sensor from 0 N to the target force. We also measured the pinch force without applying EMS as a control condition.

Figure 5 shows the results of pinch control with and without EMS when the target forces were 3, 6, and 9 N; the pinch forces were close to the target forces with EMS. These results indicate that EMS with proportional control can assist with maintaining pinch force at the target force.

5.4 Force estimation by electromyogram

We conducted a pilot study to investigate the accuracy of force estimation with EMG sensors to compare with that by active bio-acoustic sensing. On the basis of previous studies using EMG sensors for force estimation [ 4 , 16 , 33 , 51 ], two participants (20s and 50s, one woman, one man) wore three EMG sensors (Delsys, Trigno Wireless System) around the forearms. We measured the lateral pinch force by using a force sensor (IMADA, ZTS-200N) and compared it with the pinch force estimated by EMG. We first collected 1000 samples as training data for the SVR model while the participant changed the pinch force in the range of 1–10 N. We then collected 1000 samples for test data to compare the estimated force and ground truth of the force-sensor data while the participant changed the pinch force in the range of 1–10 N.

For analysis, we applied a bandpass filter (20–450 Hz) and notch filter (50 Hz) to remove motion artifacts and 50-Hz hum noise in the EMG data [ 8 ]. We calculated the root-mean-square of the filtered data over a 100-ms window, which is used for a feature vector. The MAE was 1.5 N, which is approximately the same as that in a previous study [ 4 ], 1.26 N. Comparing the MAE with that shown in the first pilot study using active bio-acoustic sensing, the accuracy of force estimation by active bio-acoustic sensing was comparable to that with EMG sensors.

6 USER STUDIES

We conducted three user studies to investigate the feasibility of our prototypes in assisting with pinch force control under various conditions. In the first study, we evaluated the effectiveness of our system in assisting with pinch force control by using active bio-acoustic sensing and EMS. In the second study, we determined the best condition of the sense of agency for force control, considering the combination of voluntary and involuntary force control. This is because we believe that the sense of agency plays a crucial role in EMS applications [ 24 , 25 , 43 ], as agency is significantly reduced when the user's muscles are moved by EMS. In the third study, we verified whether our system helps maintain a constant grip force in an interactive application such as golf. All user studies were approved by our institution's ethical review board.

6.1 User study 1: effectiveness of our system in assisting with pinch force control

The goal of this user study was to evaluate the effectiveness of our system in assisting with pinch force control by using active bio-acoustic sensing and EMS; whether the system could assist users in maintaining pinch force at the target force. To verify the basic principle of our system, we used the simplest prototype, i.e., the first prototype.

6.1.1 Participants. We recruited eight healthy participants (two identified as female, six as male, P1–P8, 20s–40s) from our institution. All signed a consent form before starting the experiment.

6.1.2 Calibration. It was necessary to calibrate the electrode positions on the flexor pollicis longus and extensor pollicis longus muscles and the amount of current for each participant. The calibration procedure was conducted as follows. The current was increased from 0 to 20 mA by the participants. The frequency of EMS was 200 Hz. If EMS did not flow to either of the muscles, we stopped EMS and slightly changed the electrode positions. The current could be increased up to 20 mA unless the participants felt pain. We asked them to increase the current until a visible contraction was observed, i.e., the thumbs moved by EMS. As a result, the EMS current to the flexor pollicis longus muscle was set at 7–8 mA, and that to the extensor pollicis longus muscle was set at 10–11 mA. The involuntary pinch force induced by EMS was about 1 N [ 69 ].

6.1.3 Tasks. The participants pinched the force sensor, as shown in Figure 6 . They were asked to maintain the target force for 10 s 5 times in 10–s intervals (total of 100 s for each trial). The timing was notified using an LED. There were three target forces: 3, 6, and 9 N.

6.1.4 Procedure. Each participant performed the following tasks. During training, participants looked at the display of the force sensor and pinched it while controlling the force from 0–12 N for about 100 s to collect training data from 1000 samples to construct the SVR model. During the first test, we covered the monitor of the force sensor so that the participants could not look at it. They then tried to maintain the target force for 10 s 5 times in 10–s intervals with or without EMS. These conditions regarding the target force and EMS were selected randomly. Under the EMS condition, EMS was applied depending on the difference in the target force and currently estimated force. During the second test, participants tried to maintain the target force for 10 s 5 times in 10–s intervals without or with EMS (the opposite condition from the former).

They repeated the above procedure for each target-force condition. The order of the selected target force and that of the EMS conditions differed for each participant; some participants conducted the tasks with EMS first and without EMS second, and others vice versa. During the tasks, they placed their hands on a force-sensor table and moved only their thumbs.

6.1.5 Results and discussion. Figure 7 shows example results under each target-force condition comparing the output force with EMS and without EMS. For quantitative evaluation, we calculated the median of the force-sensor value for each task. The number of pieces of data for each condition (e.g., a target force of 3 N, with EMS) was 40 (5 times × 8 participants). Figure 8 shows the distribution of the absolute error under each condition. When they tried to get the pinch force closer to each target force with EMS, the means of the absolute errors were 0.925, 1.31, and 3.18 N, respectively. When they tried to do so without EMS, the values were 1.73, 2.15, and 3.41 N, respectively. We conducted a Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Bonferroni correction to investigate whether there was a significant difference among them; the results indicate that there were significant differences at the target forces of 3 N ( Z = 3.07, p = 0.002, r = 0.49) and 6 N ( Z = 2.84, p = 0.005, r = 0.45), and no significant difference at the target force of 9 N ( Z = 0.03, p = 0.86, r = 0.03). The effect sizes [ 6 ] were medium effects at the target forces of 3 and 6 N and negligible effects at the target forces of 9 N.

The means of the absolute errors of force estimation while maintaining the pinch force were 1.76, 1.67, and 3.36 N, respectively. The force estimation in this user study was not sufficiently accurate and robust compared with that in the first pilot study; the boundary condition of piezo elements was changed during the tasks, affecting estimation accuracy [ 53 ]. This issue was resolved by tightening the piezo elements like in second and third prototypes.

For qualitative evaluation, we conducted a questionnaire survey to obtain feedback comments from the participants. The questions were as follows. [Q. 1] Did you feel pain from the EMS applied to the forearm? [Q. 2] Were there muscle twitches when the frequency of EMS was low? [Q. 3] How did you use the EMS feedback to maintain the target force? The responses were as follows. [A. 1] P1–P8 did not feel pain because they had calibrated the current themselves before the tasks. [A. 2] P1–P8 did not feel muscle twitches during the tasks because the intensity of EMS was very weak when the frequency of EMS was low. [A. 3] P1–P8 said that they followed the EMS feedback; when EMS was applied to the flexor muscle, they increased the pinch force, and when EMS was applied to the extensor muscle, they decreased the pinch force. They also used the change in the tactile sensation from the lowering of the frequency of EMS as a clue conveying that the current force was close to the target force.

The experimental results indicate that the prototype could estimate the pinch force by active bio-acoustic sensing while presenting EMS and assist users in reducing the absolute error under target forces through EMS feedback under the target forces of 3 and 6 N. However, under the target force of 9 N, the prototype failed to assist with pinch force control. From the qualitative evaluation, we believe this is because the participants followed the incorrect feedback based on poor force estimation under the target force of 9 N. To avoid following incorrect feedback, the sense of agency for force control is important, i.e., whether to follow EMS instructions. We investigated the best condition regarding the sense of agency in the second user study.

6.2 User study 2: best condition regarding sense of agency

The goal of this user study was to determine the best condition regarding the sense of agency considering the combination of voluntary and involuntary force control. To avoid estimation errors due to changes in the boundary condition, we used the second prototype, which tightens the piezo elements with the wrist brace.

6.2.1 Participants. We recruited 20 healthy participants (11 identified as female and 9 as male, 20s–60s) who were unfamiliar with EMS. All signed a consent form before starting the experiment.

6.2.2 Calibration. For each participant, we conducted calibration as in the first user study. The average EMS current to the flexor pollicis longus muscle was set at 6.2 mA and that to the extensor pollicis longus muscle was set at 7.6 mA.

6.2.3 Apparatus. We used a force-sensing resistor (FSR) (Interlink Electronics Inc., FSR402) attached to a pinch force sensor (SAKAI Medical Co.,Ltd., mobie) to transmit the pinch force to our EMS-based system via a microcomputer (Arduino Uno R3). The FSR was tied to a measuring resistor in a voltage divider configuration, and we measured the output voltage that changed depending on the applied force. The output voltage was 0-5 V and the resolution was 10 bits of the analog-to-digital converter of the microcomputer. By calibrating the FSR with the pinch force sensor, we derived the formula for converting voltage to force: F = 1.47 V 2 + 0.21 V , where F is force and V is voltage. We used a laptop (Apple Inc., Macbook Pro) to control our system.

6.2.4 Tasks. The participants pinched the FSR, as shown in Figure 9 . They were asked to maintain their pinch force close to the target forces that changed randomly every 3 s. The target forces were set at three levels: 1 V (set as a weak force: 2 N), 2 V (set as a medium force: 6 N), and 3 V (set as a strong force: 14 N) of the output voltage of the FSR.

6.2.5 Procedure. Each participant conducted the following tasks. During training, participants looked at the output voltage shown on the laptop's display and pinched the FSR. They were asked to learn the pinch force control to maintain the output voltage of the FSR at 1, 2, or 3 V. They practiced this for 120 s. We also collected feature vectors as training data to construct the GPR model. During this task, EMS was not applied.

During the test for 120 s, participants were asked to maintain their pinch force close to the target forces under the following three conditions.

  1. Without EMS: they look at the target forces shown on the display and apply pinch forces with only voluntary force control, i.e., EMS is not applied.
  2. With EMS: they look at the target forces and apply pinch forces with voluntary force control assisted with involuntary force control by EMS.
  3. Only EMS: they do not look at the target forces and apply pinch forces with only involuntary force control, i.e., they just follow the EMS instruction.

The threshold for applying EMS was set to 0.5 V; when the difference between the estimated force and target force was larger than 0.5 V, EMS was applied. They conducted the above procedure under each condition after 10 min of rest. The order of the conditions was selected randomly for each participant.

6.2.6 Results and discussion. For quantitative evaluation, we converted the voltage to force and calculated the MAE between the output force and target force for 120 s. Figure 10 shows example results under each condition comparing the output force and target force (P4’s data). Figure 11 (a) shows the box plot of the MAE under each condition. The medians of the MAEs were 4.1 N without EMS, 3.3 N with EMS, and 4.6 N with only EMS. We conducted a Friedman test and the results indicate that there was a significant difference ( χ 2 = 8.4, p = 0.015). Post-hoc comparison using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Bonferroni correction indicated that the MAE with EMS was significantly lower than that without EMS ( Z = 2.35, p = 0.01, r = 0.56) and that with only EMS ( Z = 3.36, p = 0.001, r = 0.73), and there was no significant difference between without EMS and only EMS ( Z = 0.67, p = 0.5, r = 0.15). We also extracted the MAE depending on each target-force condition: 2, 6, and 14 N. Figure 11 (b)–(d) shows the results; the MAE with EMS was lower than that without EMS and only EMS for each target-force condition.

The medians of force-estimation error with EMS and only EMS were 3.7 and 4.6 N, respectively. They included motion artifacts, i.e., participants changed the grip form during the procedure due to fatigue. When we extracted the first half of the test, the medians of force estimation with EMS and only EMS were 3.1 and 3.6 N, respectively. During that period, the medians of MAEs between the output voltage and target force were 3.3 and 4.7 N, respectively. These results indicate that the MAE with EMS was lower than that with only EMS even though the estimation errors were almost the same.

For qualitative evaluation, we conducted a questionnaire survey to obtain feedback comments from the participants. The questions were as follows. [Q. 1] Were the EMS instructions easy to follow? [Q. 2] How was your agency with EMS? The responses were as follows. [A. 1] All participants said that the EMS instructions were intuitive and easy to follow. [A. 2] There were two patterns regarding agency with EMS; P12, P15, P16, and P19 said that they relied on the EMS instructions and controlled their pinch forces accordingly. However, P13, P17, P18, and P20 said that they felt mixed agency and relied on their senses as well as the EMS instructions.

The most interesting finding in this user study was that the pinch force control with EMS was more sensitive than that without EMS and that with only EMS. This means that voluntary force control assisted with involuntary force control by EMS can achieve more sensitive force control even though involuntary force control cannot be superior to voluntary force control. In a previous study, GeniePutt [ 15 ], which combines EMS and user's actuation, provided the best results in a mini-golf game; this supports our results. We argue that agency with EMS was mixed between human and technology [ 43 ], which may be a key factor for human augmentation with a human-computer integration system.

6.3 User study 3: EMS force control for interactive golf application

The goal of this user study was to verify whether our system helps maintain a constant force in a sporting situation. We selected golf because it is a popular game and has been studied in previous work using EMS [ 15 ]. On the basis of kinematics and kinetics in golf [ 2 , 22 ], the aim with our system is for novices to maintain a constant grip force while putting; previous studies reported the variability of muscle activity for expert golfers was quite low. From the point of view of ease of wearing, we used the third prototype, which is similar to a golf glove. We evaluated the effectiveness of this prototype in maintaining the pinch force at the target force while gripping a golf club (putter).

6.3.1 Participants. We recruited ten healthy participants (four identified as female and six as male, 20s–50s) who were unfamiliar with EMS and novices in playing golf. All signed a consent form before starting the experiment.

6.3.2 Calibration. For each participant, we conducted a calibration as in the previous user studies. The EMS current was as set at 4–12 mA.

6.3.3 Apparatus. We used an FSR (Interlink Electronics Inc., FSR402) attached to a golf club to transmit the pinch force to our EMS-based system via a microcomputer (Arduino Uno R3), as shown in Figure 12 . Participants wore the third prototype on their left hands and gripped the golf club with two hands with the left thumb located on the FSR. The output voltage of the FSR was converted into force: F = 1.47 V 2 + 0.21 V . They were asked to fix the position of the thumb.

6.3.4 Evaluation of effects of motion artifacts on force estimation. We first evaluated the effect of motion artifacts on force estimation by measuring the MAE when participants take a bare swing with the golf club. Each participant conducted the following tasks. During the 30-s training, participants gripped the golf club by changing grip force from weak or strong following the instructions. They did this twice under two conditions: static and dynamic. Under the static condition, they did not move the club and changed only the grip force. Under the dynamic condition, they made a practice swing. During the 30-s test, they gripped the golf club in the same way as in the training; changing grip force from weak to strong following the instructions. They did this under both static and dynamic conditions.

The example results under both conditions are shown in Figures 13 (a) and (b), both obtained from the same participant. The MAE under each condition is shown in Figure 13 (c). The median of the MAE under the static condition was 3.6 N, and Pearson's correlation coefficient was 0.85. The median of the MAE under the dynamic condition was 4.8 N, and Pearson's correlation coefficient was 0.65. These results suggest that the errors for force estimation became larger due to motion artifacts though there was no significant difference ( p = 0.38) from a Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

6.3.5 Evaluation of maintaining grip force while putting. Next, we evaluated the effectiveness of the third prototype in maintaining the grip force at the target force while gripping a golf club (putter). Each participant conducted the following tasks. During the 30-s training, participants gripped the golf club by changing the grip force from weak or strong while making a practice swing. During the 30-s test, they were asked to grip the golf club with a constant grip force maintaining more than 14 N. They then puttered continuously for 30 s while maintaining the grip force. They did this under two conditions: without EMS and with EMS. With EMS, EMS was applied to the flexor pollicis longus muscle when the estimated grip force was less than 10 N.

Among the 10 participants, 4 could maintain the grip force at more than 14 N without EMS, but the other 6 participants could not, e.g., Figure 14 (a). Therefore, we compared the six participants’ average grip forces and the coefficient of variation (CV) during the test task under the two conditions. The medians of the mean grip force were 7.1 N without EMS and 12 N with EMS, as shown in Figure 14 (b). The medians of the CV were 0.66 without EMS and 0.40 with EMS, as shown in Figure 14 (c). We conducted a Wilcoxon signed-rank test indicating a significant difference between both conditions regarding the CV ( p = 0.03). These results indicate that our system could assist novices in maintaining grip force while putting.

7 DISCUSSION

Our key aim of this study was investigating the feasibility of the combination of active bio-acoustic sensing and EMS to develop a wearable system that assists with fingertip force control and does not require the wearing of any devices on the fingers by conducting four pilot studies and three user studies. The results indicate the potential of this combination. This section discusses the findings and applications.

7.1 Findings

First, we summarize the findings regarding active bio-acoustic sensing and EMS for pinch force control. Regarding active bio-acoustic sensing, we confirmed that the three prototypes with embedded piezo elements at different positions could estimate pinch force within 1.5 N error, as shown in pilot study 1. That is, the piezo elements, wherever on the back of the hand, can detect changes in the shape and hardness of the back of the hand due to applying a pinch force. This is unique for active bio-acoustic sensing compared with EMG sensors placed on the muscles. Therefore, our system can change the means of wearing depending on the application.

Regarding EMS control, we confirmed that the participants could control pinch force by using the induced force in all user studies, though the involuntary force induced by EMS was small at about 1 N. Our system worked as a human-computer integration system [ 43 ], thus it could achieve PID control even though the computer provided feedback on the basis of only proportional control or on-off control, i.e., the human added integral and derivative control based on the feedback. For a more personalized system, K p (proportional gain) should be calibrated for each user because the means of following EMS feedback differ among users, as mentioned in the qualitative evaluation of user study 2. We can also use the tactile sensation changed by the PFM control as a clue of EMS feedback for a user who does not feel any differences in EMS intensity.

7.2 Applications

We assume the target users are those who require sensitive fingertip force control and are sensitive to tactile sensation on the fingertips; the wearable system would assist with the fingertip force control interactively no matter what they grip. The target users are not limited to experts since it requires only the ability to grasp objects [ 27 , 28 ] such as eating and writing [ 13 , 14 , 65 ].

We also assume that our system can improve fingertip force control when playing musical instruments, e.g., the piano. Assisting in playing the piano is a common application in studies on human augmentation. Constant fingertip force control is important for playing the piano regarding the evenness of notes [ 29 , 56 ], and previous studies using exoskeletons presented assist devices [ 50 , 68 ]. However, fingertip force control is not easy when using EMS. Takahashi et al. proposed to manipulate each finger by EMS through the back of the hand [ 67 ], dextrEMS enables the independent actuation of each finger to achieve simple melodies [ 52 ], Niijima et al. proposed muscle synergies for learning scales evenly [ 47 ]; but these systems are not sufficient for controlling fingertip force directly. Our study on the combination of active bio-acoustic sensing and EMS would expand the use of EMS.

8 LIMITATIONS

Force estimation by active acoustic sensing is not yet sufficiently accurate and robust; estimation errors would increase when the grip form changes, i.e., motion artifacts, because the boundary condition changes depending on the grip form. Our system gave incorrect feedback to the users when the force estimation accuracy was low. The effectiveness of EMS assistance depends on the accuracy of force estimation; assistance will be more effective when such accuracy increases. From the first prototype to the second and third ones, we changed the regression model from SVR to GPR. As mentioned in Sections 4.2 and 4.3, the estimation error slightly decreased by 0.03–0.11 N. When we have more training data, we plan to construct deep-learning models.

It will also be necessary to re-collect training data whenever users change the gripped object or grip form because the vibration response of a sweep signal changes depending on the gripped objects and hand posture [ 32 , 53 ]. Therefore, users should collect more training data under several hand-posture conditions. We plan to validate our system's robustness when more training data are obtained. We will also use ultrasonic jelly to reduce acoustic impedance between the human skin and piezo elements for robustness.

Regarding hardware configuration, our system is not a completely wearable type system; we used a stationary apparatus for EMS and an audio interface for active bio-acoustic sensing. However, previous studies presented wearable types for EMS [ 58 , 60 ] and active bio-acoustic sensing [ 32 ]. Therefore, we plan for our system to be miniaturized as a wearable system based on those studies.

9 CONCLUSION

We presented a wearable system that estimates the user's fingertip force by active bio-acoustic sensing and provides involuntary fingertip force control by EMS in a closed-loop system. The system consists of piezo elements attached to the back of the hand and electrodes for EMS attached to the forearm; it requires no devices on the fingers and does not interfere with finger-motion control and tactile sensation on the fingertip. The EMS current does not interfere with the acoustic signals of active bio-acoustic sensing; it is possible to estimate force while presenting EMS and control the intensity of EMS interactively depending on the estimated force. We conducted various user studies to investigate the feasibility of the combination of active bio-acoustic sensing and EMS for pinch force control. The results indicate that this combination could significantly decrease the MAE between the pinch force and target force with EMS compared with that without EMS. Though there are limitations to force estimation, this initial study on the combination of active bio-acoustic sensing and EMS will lead to new wearable EMS-based systems to assist with sensitive fingertip force control. For future work, we will improve the accuracy, miniaturize the system, and explore other types of fingertip force control for various users who require sensitive fingertip force control.
