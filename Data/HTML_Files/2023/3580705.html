CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Secure development guidelines contribute to improving software security from the development stage by making developers aware of the risks to be assumed, the necessary security countermeasures, and how to implement them. In this study, we investigated the actual utilization of guidelines and their usability in the industry through a survey of software development professionals in the U.S. and Japan ( N =396 in the U.S. and N =474 in Japan). Our quantitative analysis revealed that “in-house” guidelines not examined in most existing studies are in fact widely utilized in the industry and also clarified how they are related to the use of public guidelines. In addition, we found that the practices for implementing guidelines recommended by existing studies are difficult for software development professionals with certain attributes, e.g., those who are working on small projects. The findings demonstrate the need for lightweight recommended practices taking into account organizational issues at industrial development sites that are easy for developers to implement.

1 INTRODUCTION

Security-by-design is widely recognized as a promising approach in software development to prevent and mitigate the cyber-attacks stemming from security problems in software, such as design flaws or implementation bugs. This approach considers and applies security measures from the early stage of software development. Recent studies on secure software development have focused on a human-centered approach—in other words, on the people who actually develop the software. These studies have identified the following issues pertaining to software development and proposed recommendations for overcoming them: the motivation of software development professionals to take adequate security measures (and the factors that inhibit them) [ 6 , 7 , 27 ] and the adoption, usability, and misuse of tools/libraries to support secure software development [ 21 , 32 , 36 , 51 ].

Research on software development has focused in recent years on the documents that are referenced to develop secure software, i.e., secure development guidelines [ 3 , 16 , 17 ]. Secure development guidelines systematically describe policies and design/implementation methods to improve the security of software under development. Examples include the OWASP Application Security Verification Standard [ 33 ] and SEI-CERT Coding Standard [ 11 ]. These guidelines help software developers make various decisions regarding security measures (e.g., which security measures to apply against certain risks) when developing secure software. Almost all the prior studies have focused on guidelines that are available on the web, and they typically consist of comparisons of the topics included in various guidelines [ 3 ] or surveys on the reasons to use or not use the guidelines [ 16 , 17 ]. These studies have provided insights into designing and using guidelines that are more effective for software developers. Nonetheless, we have identified three potential gaps between the assumptions in existing research and the actual industrial situation that could affect the survey results and recommendations.

The first gap is the difference in the nature of “public” and “in-house” secure development guidelines. Recent studies have conducted surveys focused on public guidelines that are available on the web [ 3 , 16 , 17 ], while a 2014 study featured interviews with software developers in the industry and reported that in-house guidelines established by each company contribute to the adoption of security tools in software development [ 52 ]. These in-house guidelines may differ from public ones in terms of the usage and usability from the perspective of software development professionals. The second gap is the actionability of operation of secure development guidelines. While existing studies have proposed various recommendations on how to implement the guidelines presented as a conclusion of their studies [ 16 , 17 ], these recommended practices have not been verified to determine whether they are sufficiently actionable in industrial settings from the perspective of software development professionals. Security has been reported to often be neglected in software development because it is regarded as a non-functional requirement [ 26 , 38 ], and that various characteristics of development projects affect the security awareness and behavior of software development professionals [ 27 ]. The third gap is the regional differences pertaining to the different residences of participants. Almost all prior studies have surveyed software developers in a single country/region (e.g., the U.S.), and even the few studies that have surveyed developers in multiple countries/regions have only analyzed overall trends [ 6 , 32 ]. One of the development characteristics that vary by country/region is the difference in the percentage of in-house and contracted developments, because contract development, in which the development is outsourced to other companies, is more prevalent in Asian countries than in Western countries [ 15 ]. Japan is one of the major countries in which contract development is prevalent [ 10 ]. According to a report published by the Japanese government [ 31 ], contracted development accounts for a higher percentage in Japan than in the U.S. In light of the above viewpoints, we focus on five research questions to clarify developers’ awareness of secure development guidelines and investigate how they implement them.

RQ1. Which do software development professionals utilize for development: public or in-house secure development guidelines?

RQ2. How are public/in-house secure development guidelines used by software development professionals in the industry?

RQ3. What are the reasons software development professionals do not use or have difficulty using secure development guidelines? Do they differ between public/in-house?

RQ4. Are the recommended practices on secure development guidelines mentioned in existing studies actionable?

RQ5. Are there any differences in the above results between Japan and the U.S.?

To answer these RQs, we conducted an online survey comprising 870 software development professionals that included 396 participants in the U.S. (227 developers and 169 managers) and 474 participants in Japan (234 developers and 240 managers), and then quantitatively and qualitatively analyzed the results. For RQ1, the results of our large-scale online survey revealed that in-house guidelines, which have not been investigated by most existing studies, are used by 85–87% of all participants, suggesting that they are prevalent in the industry. For RQ2, our statistical comparison revealed that, compliance with in-house guidelines is more often mandated by participants’ organizations than compliance with public guidelines. In addition, those who use public guidelines are more likely to refer to non-guideline information sources than those who use in-house guidelines. These results suggest that public guidelines are used by software development professionals at their own discretion compared with in-house guidelines, and that they actively supplement information that may be lacking from the guidelines alone with information from other sources. For RQ3, we observed that the perceived usability of guidelines is lower for participants with certain development characteristics (e.g., those who are working on small projects or contracted projects). In addition, we found that participants who used both public and in-house guidelines tended to perceive a higher usability of the guidelines than those who used only one or the other. These results suggest that public/in-house guidelines are complementary in terms of mitigating the usability problems of each. For RQ4, we found that recommended practices were less likely to be implemented by participants with certain attributes (e.g., those working on small development projects or contracted development projects, and those whose organizations lacked a chief security officer (CSO)/chief information security officer (CISO)). For RQ5, we found throughout the survey that in the U.S., participants with less development experience tended to give more optimistic answers (e.g., they indicated that guidelines have higher usability and that the practices recommended by existing research are easier to implement), while those with more experience tended to give more conservative answers. In contrast, no such correlation was observed in Japan. In addition, the practices recommended in existing research regarding the operation of the guidelines tended to be generally easy to implement for U.S. participants, but the actionability was lower for Japanese participants. These results suggest the existence of a gap in cognitive style between Japanese and U.S. software development professionals.

While the results of our quantitative survey revealed that in-house guidelines are widely used, further research through qualitative approaches such as interviews is needed to understand the more specific uses of in-house guidelines and their differences from the public ones. It is also necessary to examine how to operate guidelines in a lightweight and easy-to-implement manner, taking into account the characteristics of industrial development sites. In addition, researchers should be fully aware of differences in national/regional development characteristics/cognitive styles that could influence the results of a survey of software development professionals.

Software developers play a vital role in software security. Green and Smith [ 22 ] and Acar et al. [ 2 ] emphasized the need for research focusing on the human factors of software security. A key issue in such research is understanding the developers’ motivations, attitudes, and knowledge, and how the developers comply with best practices [ 2 , 42 ]. Researchers have tried to address it, especially by focusing on organizational software development processes [ 6 , 7 , 23 , 24 , 27 , 29 , 35 , 38 , 45 , 52 ]. For example, Assal and Chiasson showed that actual security practices differed markedly from the best practices identified in the literature and clarified the influential characteristics, which included division of labor, cost-benefit trade-offs, security knowledge, and company culture [ 6 ]. Assal and Chiasson subsequently conducted an online survey to explore developers’ strategies, motivators, and deterrents related to software security and found that the issues were caused by a lack of organizational or process support to handle security throughout development tasks [ 7 ]. Kanei et al. extended the above study by newly adding on the potentially influential development characteristics, such as the project's contractual relationship and user scope of the products and revealed that the lack of security-related decision-making authority or the difficulty in making decisions strongly affects software security [ 27 ]. In this study, we investigate whether those development characteristics also affect developers’ usage and the perceived usability of the secure development guidelines.

Another important goal of usable security research focused on developers is to establish secure and usable information sources [ 2 ]. While many security researchers have focused on Stack Overflow as a main information source of developers [ 1 , 8 , 19 , 54 , 55 ], we need to determine how developers comply with secure development guidelines and clarify the usability of such guidelines. Acar et al. reviewed security guidance websites and identified several significant areas of concern: fewer updates, lack of concrete examples or exercises, and lack of some critical topics (e.g., program analysis tools and logging) [ 3 ]. Xiao et al. [ 52 ] revealed through interviews that a company's security policies or guidelines related to secure coding directly influenced the developers’ use of security tools. The participants reported that their company's security policies or guidelines were informal and not sufficiently promoted. Motivated by this finding, we investigate developers’ usage and the usability of public and in-house guidelines.

Some studies have surveyed or interviewed software developers and recommended practices to effectively implement the guidelines [ 6 , 16 , 17 ]. These recommended practices range from those that developers can implement to those that are beyond the scope of developers, such as external pressure and upper management. We attempted to focus on recommended practices that could be implemented by developers, who are the users of the guidelines, and aggregated similar recommendations. As a result, we obtained nine summarized practices, as listed in Table 1 . While these practices are certainly valuable to the developers who use the guidelines, they have not yet been verified to actually be easy to implement in the industry. This study investigates the quantitative actionability of these recommended practices.

3 METHODOLOGY

We designed an online survey, which was based on a pilot interview/survey and existing literature, to obtain quantitative and qualitative data on how secure development guidelines are used to answer RQ1 – RQ5. Figure 1 gives an overview of our overall methodology. All questions mentioned in this paper (for screening and for the main survey) can be found in the supplementary materials.

3.1 Defining secure development guidelines

We define “secure development guideline" in this work as a document that systematically describes policies and design/implementation methods to improve the security of software under development , such as secure coding guides and security cheat sheets. Texts that are not systematically organized (e.g., articles on the web, information on forums and Q&A sites) are outside the scope of this work. This definition was determined on the basis of the guidelines focused in the existing studies [ 6 , 16 , 17 ] and the content of well-known documents for secure development [ 11 , 33 ].

While many existing studies [ 3 , 16 , 17 ] have focused on “public” guidelines to which anyone can refer on the web, in the pilot interviews we conducted to design the survey, many respondents reported that they refer to “in-house” guidelines that the companies have established on their own for their software development (e.g., rules, policies, and standards) in addition to public ones. Therefore, this work also includes in-house guidelines. There are likely differences between public and in-house guidelines in terms of the perception of software development professionals and their attitudes toward these guidelines. We therefore conducted a survey and analyzed the data to distinguish between these types of guidelines.

3.2 Survey design

Our survey contained questions about secure development guidelines and about development characteristics as well as basic demographic questions. The questions about guidelines included the type of secure development guidelines used (RQ1), usage of secure development guidelines (RQ2), usability of secure development guidelines (RQ3), and actionability of recommendations in existing studies (RQ4). We additionally asked about development characteristics (e.g., size of the development project and its contractual relationships) because the characteristics of the development project and the software being developed can influence the software development professional's awareness of the guidelines and how they are used. Furthermore, to analyze the differences between Japan and the U.S. (RQ5), we recruited participants from both Japan and the U.S. and compared their responses. We present descriptions of specific questions in Section 3.3 , participant recruitment in Section 3.4 , and the procedure of data analysis in Section 3.8 .

For designing the survey, we conducted a pilot interview with 19 software development professionals in Japan. To recruit participants, we used snowball sampling, that is, additional participants were referred by the few professional developers who could be contacted by the authors. The interview was conducted a total of eight times in a group format, and participants within each interview group belonged to the same company. We did not give monetary rewards to the participants, but shared the results of the survey conducted in this research as an incentive. Participants had software development experience of about 1–40 years, and their positions included general programmers, project managers, security assessment, etc. Table 8 in Appendix A shows the demographics of the pilot interview participants.

We repeatedly reviewed and modified our questionnaire to decrease participants’ cognitive load. We first created a prototype of our questionnaire on the basis of pilot interviews and a review of questionnaires in the existing studies [ 16 , 17 , 27 ]. Then, on the basis of a pilot survey of another two Japanese software development professionals on the prototype questionnaire, we clarified ambiguous wording, deleted duplicate question choices, and ensured that the questionnaire was of an appropriate volume. In this process, we also included simple attention check questions and a comprehension check question to help participants understand the definition of the guidelines on which this study is focused. We discuss our effort to ensure the quality of the response data in Section 3.6 .

All pilot studies (i.e., pilot interview and pilot survey) were conducted in Japanese. We first wrote our questionnaire in Japanese, and then it was translated into English by authors with ten or more years of experience writing research papers in English. Then, the translated questionnaire was proofread by a professional proofreader for clarity and additionally by a Japanese-English bilingual for semantic equivalence with the original questionnaire.

3.3 Questionnaire

3.3.1 Questions about secure development guidelines. RQ1. Type of secure development guidelines used. We explained to the participants the definition of secure development guidelines in this study and then asked whether such guidelines were used in their development projects. We then asked participants who were using the guidelines whether they were public, in-house, or both.

RQ2. Usage of secure development guidelines. We asked questions about how guidelines are implemented, including how organizations ensure participants comply with the guidelines and the percentage of security-related information sources that are complementarily referenced during software development. Regarding the enforcement of compliance with the guidelines, we asked participants whether compliance is mandated by the company or project, or whether it is left to the discretion of the developer. Xiao et al. reported that enforcement through a company's policies/standards to use security tools promoted the actual adoption of security tools in industrial development projects [ 52 ]. Similarly, the enforcement of compliance with guidelines may also affect their adoption and actual effectiveness, and in our pilot interviews, we observed cases where compliance with the guidelines was mandated by the company, as well as cases where it was left up to the developer. We therefore try to reveal the actual situation in industry regarding the enforcement of compliance with guidelines and whether it differs between public and in-house guidelines through the results of this question.

We also asked how often participants referred to various sources of information for secure development. Specifically, we asked how much they refer to public guidelines, in-house guidelines, security vendor white papers, web articles by experts (e.g., blogs), developer forums/Q&A sites (e.g., Reddit, Stack Overflow), vulnerability information databases (e.g., CVE), and other, and asked them to indicate the percentage of each source of information they refer to so that the total summed up to 100%.

RQ3. Usability of secure development guidelines. We asked questions to assess the usability of guidelines for each of the public/in-house guidelines used by the participants. Participants were asked to indicate the extent to which they agreed with 12 statements regarding usability on a 6-point Likert-scale ( strongly agree to strongly disagree ). These 12 items on usability were designed on the basis of the classification of developer documentation issues in existing studies [ 4 , 5 ] and feedback from software development professionals in pilot interviews. The full text of each item is provided in Table 2 . Note that if participants were using more than one guideline, they were asked to answer the questions assuming the one to which they most frequently refer. After the above questions on usability, we presented open-ended questions asking for any points that were poor or needed improvement with regard to each of the public/in-house guidelines.

RQ4. Actionability of recommendations in existing studies. We asked how easy it would be for participants to implement the recommendations on how to operate guidelines mentioned in existing studies. We used the representative recommended practices listed in Table 1 (summarized in Section 2 ). Participants were asked to rate the degree to which they found these nine recommendations easy or difficult to implement on a 6-point Likert-scale ( very easy to implement to very difficult to implement ). Participants who answered that it was difficult were also asked to clarify why in an open-ended answer.

RQ5. Differences between Japan and the U.S. We did not directly ask specific questions for RQ5, but we compared the responses to the questions for RQ1 – RQ4 between the U.S. and Japanese participants to answer RQ5.

3.3.2 Questions on development characteristics. We asked five questions regarding the development characteristics of participants’ organization/project. These development characteristics were selected on the basis of feedback from software development professionals during the survey design and on the findings of existing studies [ 7 , 17 , 27 , 35 , 39 , 44 , 45 , 51 , 52 ], and were chosen for their likely impact on the actual use of software development guidelines. Note that the questions asked in this survey do not cover all the characteristics that affect the actual use of guidelines (see the discussion in Section 5.4 ).

Development experience. We asked participants how many years of experience as software development professionals they had. Several existing studies reported that the length of development experience influences software development professionals’ behavior and awareness on secure development such as their ability to detect vulnerabilities and the adoption of security tools [ 44 , 51 ], suggesting that this characteristic may also affect the use/operation of secure development guidelines.

Size of organization. We asked questions about the number of employees in the companies to which the participants belonged, as well as the number of people in their development project. An existing study [ 7 ] reported that the size of the company to which the developer belongs affects the motivation to take security measures as well as the factors that hinder security. Similarly, it has been reported that the number of people in the development project affects the productivity of software development [ 39 ].

Security-related organizational structure. We asked participants whether their company has a CSO or CISO. Several existing studies have stated that support from the management is important for companies to adopt security in development projects [ 35 , 38 , 45 ]. On the basis of their findings, we assume that the presence or absence of security-related managers (i.e., CSO/CISO) in the participants’ company affects the security efforts, such as the use/operation of guidelines, conducted in their project.

User scope of developed software. We asked whether the software that the participants were developing was to be used by an unspecified number of users (e.g., consumer software) or by a limited number (e.g., in-house systems). Since the case of developing software that is used by an unspecified large number of users tends to have more security measures than the case of developing software for a limited number of users [ 27 ], this characteristic may affect the use/operation of guidelines.

Project contractual relationships. We asked whether the development project to which the participant belonged was in-house (developing software used in the company's own services) or contracted (developing software used in the services of others on a contractual basis). Existing research [ 27 ] has shown that the contractor-contractee relationship between projects influences the developer's decision-making on security: specifically, projects lower in the contract hierarchy (i.e., the contractor) may have difficulty implementing security measures due to requirements and restrictions from projects higher in the contract hierarchy (i.e., the contractee). Similar to the above, the use/operation of guidelines may be affected by the contractual relationship with the project.

3.4 Recruitment

We recruited participants through a paid service offered by a panel provider [ 30 ], which has a diverse participant pool in both the U.S. and Japan, in April 2022. This panel provider takes actions such as the following for quality control: detection/elimination of duplicate-registered participants, annual confirmation/update of participants’ attribute information, and elimination of participants who gave invalid answers in their periodic quality-check survey(s). This service has been used in multiple existing studies (e.g., Sawaya et al.’s study [ 40 ]).

Through the screening questions conducted prior to the main study, people who were aged 18 years or older and met the following criteria were invited to participate in the main survey 1 .

Screening condition 1: Developer or manager. There are gaps in security behavior and awareness between developers and managers in a software development project [ 27 ]. Assuming the possibility of similar gaps with respect to secure development guidelines, we divided the participants into groups of developers and managers. Specifically, we asked participants which of the two roles they had: developer (with development tasks such as design, implementation, test, debug, review) or manager (with management tasks such as scheduling, resource management, product lifecycle management). Those who did not fall into either role (e.g., marketing personnel) were excluded from participation.

Screening condition 2: Working in a team. Our study aims to understand the actual use of guidelines in industrial development projects. Therefore, we asked whether participants were professional developers/managers working on software development in a team of multiple people. Those who were not (e.g., students and individual developers) were excluded from our survey.

For this survey, we paid a total of approximately JP¥3.5M (approximately US $ 27K) to the panel provider 2 . This included recruitment and data collection fees (JP¥1.3k – JP¥3.5 per participant) and other administrative fees, in addition to the compensation for participants. All participants who passed the screening questions and completed the main survey were given a monetary reward worth US $ 8 3 . This amount is well above (about twice as much as) the minimum wages in participants’ residential regions calculated on the basis of the average survey completion time. Considering that developers are generally busy, we set the reward amount equivalent to or higher than that of recent representative studies that recruited developers for a quantitative online survey on a paid service [ 7 , 43 ].

3.5 Participant demographics

In this survey, we analyzed the responses from 396 participants in the U.S. (227 developers and 169 managers) and 474 participants in Japan (234 developers and 240 managers) after screening participants (i.e., developers or managers in a development project with multiple people) as described in Section 3.4 and excluding invalid answers by the attention check as described in Section 3.6 . The average survey completion time for participants with valid answers was 26.9 mins ( Md = 11.4). The participants’ ages, years of development experience, and size of their project and company varied, though ages or years of development experience tended to be higher in Japan, and the size of the project or company tended to be larger in the U.S. The summary of participants’ demographics is included in Table 7 in Appendix A .

3.6 Data quality

To ensure our participants were paying attention, we included two attention check questions in the screening questionnaire and the main questionnaire and one comprehension check question in the main questionnaire.

In the attention check question, the participants were instructed to choose a specific option in the question text. Participants who answered the attention check questions incorrectly were excluded from the analysis.

Prior to the questions about the secure development guidelines in this survey, the comprehension check question was asked to help participants understand the definition of the secure development guidelines so that they could correctly identify them in the survey. As described in Section 3.1 , the guidelines in this study are documents that systematically describe design and implementation methods and policies to improve the security of software under development. They include public documents to which anyone can refer, as well as rules/policies/standards that the company has established on its own. The comprehension check question gave a brief definition of “guidelines" and instructed participants to choose the correct explanation of the guidelines from a list of options, including documents and documents related to security. All participants, regardless of whether they answered the comprehension check correctly, were then re-explained the options that represent the guidelines being surveyed and proceeded to the next question.

3.7 Ethics

We conformed with the ethical principles detailed in the Menlo Report [ 9 ], specifically informed consent and privacy protection as follows. All participants gave their consent on the basis of the guidance text at the beginning of the questionnaire. We adhered to the privacy laws and regulations in the participants’ countries of residence. Our institutional review board conducted a privacy impact assessment and approved our study.

3.8 Data analysis

We analyzed the survey responses for the questions described in Section 3.3 from participants in the U.S. and Japan to answer RQ1–RQ4. Simultaneously, we compared the analysis results between the two countries to answer RQ5. The detailed procedures of each analysis are described in Section 4 .

We used SPSS Statistics v28 for quantitative data analysis. Significance level p <.05 was used for all statistical analyses, and Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons. When performing linear and logistic regression, we confirmed that the number of independent variables and sample size followed a suggestion by Peduzzi et al. [ 37 ] (ten samples per independent variable) or by Vittinghoff et al. [ 48 ] (5 – 9 samples per independent variable) to ensure our analysis was adequately powered. We also confirmed that each independent variable had no multicollinearity by calculating the variance inflation factor (VIF) score.

The responses for the open-ended questions were analyzed by inductive thematic analysis, which identifies, analyzes, and reports patterns (themes) within data. Two authors developed a codebook by separately coding the responses and then aggregated their codebooks in accordance with the definition of each theme. Once a reliable codebook was established, the two authors re-coded the responses and calculated the inter-coder reliability (Cohen's Kappa [ 13 ]). The final inter-coder reliability coefficients in this survey are 0.74-0.96, which are considered at least to be a substantial level of coding agreement [ 28 ]. The codebooks established in this study are shown in Tables 11 and 12 in Appendix E .

4 RESULTS

In this section, we preset the basic survey statistics and the results of analyses corresponding to each RQ 4 . Note that all questions in our survey were optional, meaning blank responses were included. We excluded these blank responses from the analysis, and the actual number of responses we included is indicated in each section.

4.1 RQ1: Type of secure development guidelines used

In our survey, the percentage of participants who used secure development guidelines in their project was 90.7% ( N = 359) in the U.S. and 60.1% ( N = 285) in Japan. Figure 2 shows the percentage of participants regarding the type of guidelines they used. In the U.S., the percentage of participants who used both public and in-house guidelines was the highest (50.1%, N = 180), followed by those who used only in-house ones (37.0%, N = 133) and those who used only public ones (12.8%, N = 46). In Japan, the percentage of participants who used only in-house guidelines was the highest (53.3%, N = 152), followed by those who used both public and in-house ones (32.6%, N = 93) and those who used only public ones (14.0%, N = 40). The percentage of participants who used in-house guidelines (i.e., the sum of participants who used only in-house guidelines and those who used both public and in-house ones) was 87.1% in the U.S. and 85.9% in Japan.

While most existing studies [ 3 , 16 , 17 ] have focused only on public guidelines, our results showed that a surprisingly large number of participants used in-house ones in their projects. In addition, while a prior study [ 52 ] conducted in 2014 reported that in-house secure development standards tended to be informal and not explicit, our results showed that in-house guidelines were widely used in the industry, suggesting that the number of companies that have in-house guidelines has increased over the past few years.

When we compare the type of guidelines that participants used (i.e., only public ones, only in-house ones, or both), the percentage of participants who were mandated to comply with the guidelines was the highest among those who used only in-house guidelines (U.S.: 85.4%, N = 111, Japan: 83.1%, N = 113), and the lowest among those who used only public guidelines (U.S.: 67.4%, N = 31, Japan: 67.6%, N = 25). We conducted chi-square tests to assess whether there is a statistically significant association between the type of guidelines and whether they were mandatory. We calculated effect size ( ϕ for 2x2 contingency tables and Cramer's V for others) as the measure of association of the two variables. We considered ϕ or Cramer's V >.10 as small effects, >.30 a medium effect, and >.50 a large effect [ 14 ]. For the U.S. participants, we found a statistically significant association between the type of guidelines and whether they were mandatory (chi-square test: χ 2 (2) = 7.295, p <.05, Cramer's V = .144, small effect size). Subsequent pairwise comparison with Bonferroni correction revealed that the number of U.S. participants who used only public guidelines and were mandated to comply with them was significantly lower than those who used only in-house guidelines and were mandated to comply with them ( χ 2 (1) = 7.058, p <.05, ϕ = .200, small effect size). Though the percentage of Japanese participants who were mandated to comply with the guidelines was similar to that of the U.S. participants, there was no significant association among the three groups of guideline type in Japan ( χ 2 (2) = 4.728, p = .094, Cramer's V = .135, small effect size).

To summarize the above, in the U.S., compliance with in-house guidelines tends to be more frequently mandated in organizations than compliance with public guidelines. In Japan, the percentage of participants who were mandated to comply with in-house guidelines was also higher than that of participants who used public guidelines, though the difference was not statistically significant. On the basis of an existing study that revealed that enforcement through a company's policies/standards contributed to the adoption of security tools [ 52 ], the above results suggest that in-house guidelines, which tend to be more frequently mandatory to comply with than public ones, are more effective for introducing necessary security measures into industrial development projects.

4.2.2 Information sources for secure development. We analyzed how much participants referred to various sources of information for secure development, and whether the results differed depending on the type of secure development guidelines that they used. Since we specifically focused on the difference between secure development guidelines and other sources, we summed up the percentage of references to other sources described in Section 3.3.1 (e.g., security vendor white papers, blogs, developer forums/Q&A sites, vulnerability information databases) by each participant and compared it with the percentage of references to public and in-house guidelines. As shown in Fig. 3 , regardless of the type of guideline that participants used, they referred to a certain percentage of information from sources other than the guidelines in both countries.

We performed Wilcoxon signed-rank testing to assess the differences in frequency of reference between guidelines and other sources. We calculate the effect size ( r ) as a measure of the size of the differences in frequency of reference between two information sources. We consider r >0.10 a small effect, >.30 a medium effect, and >.50 a large effect [ 14 ]. Focusing on the U.S. participants, the frequency of reference to other sources was significantly higher than the frequency of reference to public guidelines when the participants used only public guidelines ( T = 852, p <.001, r = –.688, large effect size). In Japan, we also found that the frequency of reference to other sources tended to be higher than that of public guidelines when participants used only public guidelines, though the correlation was not statistically significant. We assume that when participants used only public guidelines, they actively referred to various information sources to complement the information for secure development that could not be acquired sufficiently from the public guidelines.

As another observation, we found that the frequency of reference to other sources was significantly lower than that of in-house guidelines in Japan when participants used only in-house guidelines ( T = 1211.50, p <.001, r = –.604, large effect size), while this was not observed in the U.S. Though the reasons for this difference are unclear, future studies including the qualitative approach (e.g., interviewing the software development professionals who use in-house guidelines to ask the specific use case of their guidelines) may clarify this.

This correlation was less evident in Japan, as we found a significant effect only for the number of project members on F 2 JP (up-to-dateness) for in-house guidelines. We assume that these results stem from a lack of human resources, as mentioned in many existing studies [ 7 , 38 , 45 ].

We found that the participants who used both public and in-house guidelines perceived the guidelines as more usable compared with those who used either public or in-house ones. In particular, we observed significant effects on all four factors in the U.S. participants who used in-house guidelines, and on F 1 US (readability) and F 2 US (up-to-dateness) in those who used public ones. In Japan, significant effects were observed on F 1 JP (readability) and F 3 JP (easy to deploy/prioritize) in those who used public guidelines. Regarding the contractual relationship of development projects, participants in the projects for contracted development perceived in-house guidelines as less usable from the perspective of F 1 US (readability) in the U.S. and of F 2 JP (up-to-dateness) in Japan. Focusing on the public guidelines, the regression coefficients for all four factors were relatively low, though they were not statistically significant. Although we cannot claim a causal relationship between conducting contracted development and decreased usability of the guidelines, this may possibly be due to the constraints specific to the contracted development (e.g., requests/priority from client and contractee) mentioned in an existing study [ 27 ]. This assumption was supported by open-ended answers; for example, some Japanese participants who conducted contracted development stated “ the frequency of updates (of in-house guidelines) is low because they have been created based on the guidelines established by the parent company ” and “ (We) cannot keep (in-house guidelines) updated because clients frequently change specification. ”

4.4 RQ4. Actionability of recommendations in existing studies

We analyzed the responses to the question about the actionability of recommended practices for implementing secure development guidelines (RP1–RP9 listed in Table 1 ) to investigate whether they were actionable from the viewpoint of software development professionals in the industry. The percentage of participants who indicated that the recommended practices are actionable (i.e., the sum of participants who answered somewhat easy, easy, strongly easy) was 75–85% across RP1–RP9 in the U.S. and 39–51% in Japan. The full results of each Likert item for RP1–RP9 are included in Appendix C .

To investigate the factors that affect the actionability of recommended practices for guideline users, we performed logistic regression analysis on the results of RP1–RP9. For this analysis, we converted each Likert item for RP1–RP9 into binary answers (i.e., somewhat easy, easy, or very easy were deemed actionable, and somewhat difficult, difficult, or very difficult were deemed not actionable) and then utilized them as dependent variables. We used the answers for the following questions described in Section 3.3 as independent variables: years of experience as a software development professional (continuous), the number of people in participants’ development project (2–5, 6–10, 11-30, or 31 or over, ordinal scale), whether the participants’ company has a CSO/CISO (categorical), the contractual relationship of participants’ project (in-house development or contracted development, categorical), and the type of guidelines that participants used (public only, in-house only, or both, categorical). Similar to the analysis in Section 4.3.2 , we selected these independent variables with reference to the existing studies [ 7 , 27 , 35 , 38 , 39 , 44 , 45 , 51 ] and our pilot interviews.

The results of logistic regression for the U.S. and Japanese participants are shown in Table 6 . The listed values represent the odds ratio (OR) for each independent variable. In this analysis, an OR greater than 1 for a categorical independent variable means a recommended practice for guideline users is more actionable than the baseline condition, and an OR greater than 1 for a continuous or ordinal independent variable means there is a positive correlation between an independent variable and the actionability of a recommended practice.

Development experience. For the results of the U.S. participants, we observed that the years of development experience had a significant effect on the actionability of all recommended practices (RP1–RP9); experienced participants tended to regard each practice as not actionable. There was no significant effect on the years of development experience except for RP6 “Use SAST tools as a support” and RP7 “Monitor the quality of SAST tools” in Japan. Similar to the results in Section 4.3.2 , the implication here is that less experienced U.S. participants tended to be more optimistic (i.e., to regard each practice as actionable), whereas this did not apply to Japanese participants. We discuss this difference from a cross-cultural perspective in Section 5.3 .

Number of project members. We observed that the number of people in the participants’ project significantly affects the actionability of all recommended practices (RP1–RP9) except for RP3 “Form a community” for the U.S. participants; participants in the smaller projects found it difficult to implement these recommended practices. In Japan, the ORs for RP2, RP3, and RP4 were relatively higher than those for the other recommended practices, though only RP2 was statistically significant. Several existing studies [ 7 , 20 , 53 ] have shown that a lack of resources (i.e., time, people) is one of the major obstacles to security in development projects, and our results here seem to support their findings.

In fact, we observed many open-ended answers that claimed a lack of resources in their development project. For example, some U.S. participants belonging to the projects with fewer than five people stated the reason for the difficulty of implementing RP2 “Assign responsible person for guidelines” to be as follows: “ Our staff is small and it would be hard to have such a situation in our company ” and “ limited staff already over worked. ”

Presence of CSO/CISO. Whether the participants’ company had a CSO/CISO significantly affects the actionability of the recommended practice in the U.S. In particular, the actionability for the participants who had no CSO/CISO in their company tended to be low in the following recommended practices: “Include checks on compliance with guidelines,” “Assign a person responsible for guidelines,” “Hold training/hands-on workshops,” and “Use SAST tools as a support” (RP1, RP2, RP4 and RP6). These results suggest that these recommended practices are difficult to implement in companies that do not have well-established organizational security awareness.

Contractual relationships. Contractual relationships significantly affect the actionability of RP3 for the participants in Japan, meaning participants in a project for contracted development find it more difficult to form a community for secure development than those working on projects for in-house development. In the U.S., the ORs for RP3, RP4, PR5, and RP6 were relatively low, though they were not statistically significant. An existing study mentioned that software development professionals in projects for contracted development cannot make security efforts at their own discretion, possibly due to requests or priorities made from their contractor [ 27 ]. Our results imply that the constraints inherent in contracted development also hinder the establishment of a secure development community in the industry, and that this problem is more severe in Japan, where contracted development is more prevalent.

Guideline type. The type of guidelines that participants used significantly affected the actionability of RP1 “Check compliance with guidelines in the quality assurance” and RP2 “Assign responsible person for guidelines” in the U.S. Our findings showed that U.S. participants who used both public and in-house guidelines found it easier to implement RP1 than those who used only public guidelines. Note that a significant effect was also observed if the baseline condition of the guideline type was changed to participants who used only in-house guidelines, meaning that participants who used both public and in-house guidelines also found it easier to implement RP1 than those who used only in-house guidelines. This means that participants can more easily check the compliance with the secure development guidelines in their quality assurance if they use both public and in-house guidelines than when using only one or the other. We also found that U.S. participants who used only public guidelines found it more difficult to implement RP2 than those who used only in-house guidelines and those who used both public and in-house guidelines.

In addition to the above findings, we observed several key differences in the results of the regression analyses between the U.S. and Japan. For example, unlike the results for the U.S., the type of guidelines significantly affects the actionability of RP7 in Japan but not that of RP1 or RP2. Also, the presence of a CSO/CISO significantly affects the actionability of several recommended practices in the U.S., but we cannot confirm significant effects in Japan. In future work, we intend to clarify what causes these differences between the U.S. and Japan through qualitative analysis.

5 DISCUSSIONS

5.1 Understanding in-house software development guidelines

Almost all existing studies on secure development guidelines have been based on “public” guideline surveys and do not explicitly consider “in-house” guidelines. While the results of interviews conducted by Xiao et al. in 2014 indicated that many participants felt their in-house security standards were informal and verbal best practices that were not enforceable [ 52 ], our survey in 2022 found a surprisingly large number of participants using in-house guidelines. We believe that over the past approximately ten years, more and more companies have established their own in-house guidelines.

In our survey, we identified the following interesting findings regarding in-house guidelines. (i) Among participants who used both public and in-house guidelines, those who were in the U.S. considered RP1 (“Include checks on compliance with guidelines”) and RP2 (“Assign a person responsible for the guidelines”) to be more actionable compared with those who used only public guidelines (Section 4.4 ). (ii) Many participants referred to in-house guidelines that are mandatory to comply with (Section 4.2.1 ). (iii) Participants who used both public and in-house guidelines perceived the guidelines to be more usable compared with those who used either one public or the other (Section 4.3.2 ).

Although we did not ask participants directly about the details of the guidelines to avoid the complexity of the questionnaire as mentioned in Section 5.4 , we obtained the following responses obtained in the main survey (open-ended answers) and the pilot interviews: (i) in-house guidelines are used to complement public guidelines, and (ii) in-house guidelines are created by customizing public guidelines for the company's own use. Given these observations, we can infer a difference in the role of public and in-house guidelines; public guidelines contain generalized information, while in-house guidelines contain company-specific information for secure development that is used to address operational issues that arise in public guidelines. Further qualitative research is required to clarify exactly how in-house guidelines are developed and used and which aspects differentiate them from public guidelines. Understanding in-house guidelines more fully is vital because it will contribute to improving public guidelines by identifying areas where they are potentially lacking.

5.2 Improving actionability of practices

Much research has investigated secure development guidelines for presenting recommendations. However, no previous study verified the actionability of these recommended practices in the industry, and our study is the first to do so quantitatively. While the majority of participants perceived high actionability for each recommended practice, we found that participants with certain attributes perceived low actionability. Briefly, many recommended practices were perceived as easy to implement in organizations with large-scale, pervasive security awareness, or in-house development. Conversely, organizations with the opposite characteristics (small-scale or featuring contracted development) tended to have some difficulty in implementing the practices. For example, participants in the U.S. whose development projects were small perceived almost all recommended practices (PR1–9, excluding RP3) as unactionable, participants in the U.S. whose company did not have a CSO/CISO perceived “Include checks on compliance with guidelines,” “Assign a person responsible for the guidelines,” “Hold training/hands-on workshops,” and “Use SAST tools as a supporting tool” (RP1, RP2, RP4, and RP6) as unactionable, and participants in Japan whose projects involved contracted development perceived “Form a community” (RP3) as unactionable. These results suggest that there are additional challenges in how to implement the recommendations, and more lightweight and easy-to-implement practices are required for addressing project- and organization-specific issues. Some existing studies have investigated lightweight practices in secure software development through their qualitative analysis, i.e., interviews, ethnographic studies, and workshops [ 35 , 45 , 46 , 50 ]. These studies advocated for the importance of the following efforts: security champions who are members of software development project and serve as important liaisons between developers and security experts, a co-creation model where developers and security experts closely collaborate, a situated learning framework that can be instrumental in creating positive changes in secure development, and that workshop sessions facilitated by managers appear more effective than those facilitated by developers or security experts. It is worth exploring to what extent the above-mentioned organizations that had difficulty implementing recommended practices could make these efforts easily. OWASP has recently published the Software Assurance Maturity Model (SAMM) v2, which provides measurable and actionable security practices for organizations to improve software security posture [ 34 ], but at a much higher level (technology and process agnostic) than the guidelines targeted in this paper.

5.3 Cross-regional study

The results of our quantitative analysis revealed various differences in the perceptions of guidelines’ usability and recommended practices’ actionability between participants in the U.S. and Japan. Almost no existing studies on secure development guidelines have focused on cross-regional comparisons [ 6 , 16 , 17 ]. However, in reality, there are several key differences when it comes to the model, method, and customs of software development depending on the country and the region; for example, a contracted development is a major development model used in Japan. We found that several existing recommended practices were perceived as difficult to implement in organizations that perform such contract development.

There were also significant differences in terms of the years of software development experience regarding the perceived usability of guidelines or actionability of existing recommended practices. Specifically, while U.S. participants with less experience tended to be optimistic, those with more experience were relatively conservative. This suggests that software development professionals with a variety of experience may have a better grasp of what is missing from the guidelines and what might be difficult when implementing recommended practices. This is similar to the result of Ukrop and Matyas [ 47 ], who found the perceived usability of command line OpenSSL is lower for the users with more experience. In contrast, there was no significant difference in software development experience in Japan, as participants with both little and more extensive experience tended to express conservative opinions. Further investigation is needed here to clarify whether these differences between the two countries are due to differences in cognitive style.

We should point out here that having conservative and critical participants was beneficial in terms of understanding people's perceptions because we were able to extract a greater variety of opinions from such participants than from optimistic ones. In our survey, U.S. participants expressed an average of 70 opinions ( N = 396) for each recommended practice, while Japanese participants expressed an average of 150 opinions ( N = 474).

Regional and cultural differences are known to significantly influence survey results [ 41 ], and surveys on secure software development are no exception. Comparative analyses need to be conducted between countries/regions with different software development situations and participants with different cognitive styles.

5.4 Limitations

Potential bias. A survey on security and privacy may include potential biases—such as social desirability bias, where participants over-report their security awareness and practice to seem more favorable in the security context. At the beginning of the survey, we explained that the survey was anonymous and allowed empty responses for questions that were difficult to answer due to participants’ affiliation or position. These instructions probably reduced false responses to such difficult questions.

Coverage of development characteristics. Our questionnaire did not cover all the characteristics of development projects. We carefully selected which development characteristics to focus on by referring to our pilot interviews and questionnaires in the existing studies. We statistically analyzed all selected development characteristics and reported statistically significant differences.

Individual secure development guideline details. We did not ask the specific name of public guidelines to which participants referred in their software development projects. There are numerous guidelines on the web, and if we were to precisely identify each one, we would have to ask not only for the name of the guidelines, but also for details such as URL, publishing organization, and date of publication. To avoid increasing the burden on participants, we decided to leave comparison with individual guidelines outside the scope of this study.

6 CONCLUSION

In this study, we conducted a large-scale online survey of software development professionals in the U.S. and Japan to quantitatively analyze the actual utilization of secure development guidelines in the industry. The results of our analysis revealed that, in addition to the public secure development guidelines that have been studied in existing research, in-house secure guidelines established by each company are widely utilized. We also found that the practices for operating guidelines recommended by existing studies are difficult to implement in development projects with certain characteristics, e.g., small projects or projects featuring contracted development. Future studies should include conducting a more detailed analysis of in-house secure development guidelines by means of qualitative approaches (such as interviews) to better understand specific purpose/usage trends, and investigating lightweight strategies for effectively operating secure development guidelines while taking the organizational issues in industrial development projects into account.

D DETAILED RESULTS OF FACTOR ANALYSIS

We performed an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) on the results of the questions about the usability of secure development guidelines (U1–U12) listed in Table 2 . The questions with factor loading greater than 0.4 [ 18 ] for the common factors were grouped together, and the others were excluded from the subsequent analysis. We calculated Cronbach's alpha for each group to validate the results of the question grouping. EFA was conducted using principal axis factoring and promax rotation. We used promax rotation, which is an oblique rotation method allowing for correlated factors, because we assume that individual factors related to the usability of secure development guidelines are likely to correlate with each other.

Since the population of the survey respondents differs between the U.S. and Japan, EFA was performed separately for each country as recommended by Child and Watkin [ 12 , 49 ]. Note that Bartlett's test of sphericity and the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure [ 25 ] showed that our data from both countries are suitable for EFA (Bartlett's test of sphericity: p <.001 for both the U.S. and Japan, KMO US = .939, KMO JP = .910).

Tables 9 and 10 show the detailed results of factor analysis for the responses from U.S. and Japanese participants. We grouped 10 of the 12 questions into four factors for the results from U.S. participants, and grouped nine of the 12 questions into three factors for the results from Japanese participants. Two questions for the U.S. and three questions for Japan did not belong to any factor. We found that the results of EFA for both countries were similar: U1, U2, and U3 belonged to both F 1 US and F 1 JP . We consider these factors to describe the extent to which the guidelines are easy to read (readability). F 2 US and F 2 JP conformed to the same questions (U10, U11). We consider these factors to describe how frequently/appropriately the guidelines are updated (up-to-dateness). We also consider F 3 US to describe how easy it is to deploy guidelines in the development project (easy to deploy) and F 4 US to describe how easy it is to prioritize the necessary security measures by the guidelines (easy to prioritize). F 3 JP conformed to several questions belonging to F 3 US and F 4 US , so we consider this factor to describe a combination of F 3 US and F 4 US (easy to deploy/prioritize).
