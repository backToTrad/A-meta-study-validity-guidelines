Emoband: Investigating the Affective Perception towards On-wrist Stroking and Squeezing Feedback mediated by Different Textile Materials

Xingyu Yang , School of Creative Media, City University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong, xingyyang8-c@my.cityu.edu.hk
Kening Zhu , School of Creative Media, City University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong, keninzhu@cityu.edu.hk

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3580769
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

This paper investigates how different textile materials on the wrist bands may mediate the affective experience of two forms of tactile icons, namely stroking and squeezing. To this end, we designed Emoband, a wrist-worn textile-based tactile display that can stroke or squeeze on wearers’ wrists through the moving fabrics. We conducted two studies on the participants’ valence and arousal ratings towards the stroking and squeezing feedback from Emoband, respectively. Results showed that the valence ratings were significantly affected by the stroking or squeezing parameters and the wristband's material, interactively. The movement-related parameters dominated the arousal rating. Meanwhile, the valence ratings demonstrated different tendencies between the two tactile feedback, with the valence increasing and decreasing along with the strength of stroking and squeezing, respectively. Besides, the valence ratings of the stroking stimuli demonstrated varied distributions across different materials, indicating the materials’ varying affective expressiveness in the wrist-worn haptic devices.

Introduction

Human communicate emotion/affect through various facets, including vocal factors [ 56 , 76 , 77 ], facial expressions [ 25 , 89 ], body movements [ 4 , 6 ], tactile [ 39 ] and thermal sensations [ 90 ]. The affect communication however could be debilitated by circumstantial settings (e.g., remote communication) [ 50 ] or the wreck of sensational and perceptual abilities (e.g., visual or hearing impairment) [ 49 , 85 ]. Besides common vision- and audio-based affective interfaces, HCI research has expanded the perceptual modalities of emotional cues by adopting tactile stimuli, including vibration [ 11 , 57 , 65 , 78 , 95 ], on-skin stretching/stroking and squeezing [ 10 , 73 , 86 ], as well as thermal feedback [ 26 , 37 , 74 , 84 ].

While the tactile stimuli for conveying affect were mostly conveyed through the on-body wearable devices [ 10 , 11 , 26 , 57 , 84 ], they were usually applied to the users directly from rigid mechanical actuators (e.g., vibrating devices or motors [ 11 , 18 , 57 , 78 , 95 ], Peltier panels [ 26 , 37 , 74 , 84 ], and moving plastic rods [ 73 , 80 ]). The rigid medium might limit the comfort of contacting the devices, hinder users’ acceptance towards the devices for daily usage [ 51 , 59 ]. To this end, textile-based human-computer interfaces, blending the digital and the physical properties into users’ intimate accompany [ 21 ], have attracted a lot of research interest. Several existing work revealed the emotion-evoking effect of touching or manipulating different textured materials [ 24 , 47 , 60 , 71 ]. Other researchers also studied the hedonic response to robot-driven moving fabrics through the users’ self report [ 30 , 31 ] or analysing the participants’ brain-wave changes [ 10 , 86 ]. However, they did not specifically look into the tactile icons through the digitally interactive textiles, and some of their device setup was neuroscience-oriented without concerning wearability [ 30 , 31 ]. Here we adopted the definition of tactile icons, or named “Tactons”, as structured short tactile stimuli associated with abstract meanings [ 13 ]. Existing HCI research found that the affective experience could be evoked by the parameterized active haptics (e.g., vibration [ 11 , 52 , 78 , 95 ], thermal stimuli [ 26 , 37 , 73 , 84 ], stretching stimuli [ 74 ]) and the mediated materials [ 24 , 47 , 60 , 71 ]. Yet, the combined effect of the active haptic stimuli and the mediated materials on users’ affective understanding still need in-depth investigation.

In this paper, we investigate how the textile materials may influence users’ affective perception towards the on-wrist stroking and squeezing tactile icons which could be considered as short and parameterized tactile stimuli [ 13 ]. Here the stroking movement is defined as the textile sliding on the users’ skin while the fabric contacts the skin closely with no compression. In our study, the tactile icons were rendered through Emoband, an on-wrist textile-based tactile display with a belt attached to two servo motors that drive the textured band to stroke or squeeze over users’ wrists. We conducted two user-perception experiments with Emoband. Study 1 measured the valence and the arousal ratings yielded from the wrist-stroking stimuli by Emoband with five different textile materials, while Study 2 investigated the corresponding affective ratings of on-wrist squeezing stimuli. Our results showed that:

  • The users’ valence rating for both stroking and squeezing stimuli was significantly affected by both the wristband's material and the movement-related factors (e.g., velocity, duration, direction for stroking, and pressure, duration for squeezing). Meanwhile, the arousal rating for stroking was dominated by the velocity and the duration, while that for squeezing was significantly affected by the pressure and the duration.
  • For the stroking stimulation, the effect of the movement factors on the valence rating was mediated by the wristband material, leading to varying distributions of valence ratings across different materials. In contrast, the squeezing stimulation's effect on the valence rating was mostly consistent across materials, leading to similar distributions of valence ratings for all the tested materials.

Related Work

Haptic-related research in HCI revealed that emotion is an embodied experience highly related to haptic sensation [ 41 , 80 ], and designed various haptic devices for affective expression. Meanwhile, textile-related researchers have shown how textured materials could evoke affect. Our work was built upon these two streams of research, investigating how textile materials may influence the perception of affect through the active haptic signals from a wrist-worn device.

Affect Communication through Stroking- and Squeezing-like Haptics

The tactile affective device usually involves a range of cutaneous experience, such as cutaneous force and movement (e.g., stretching, squeezing, pressing, etc.), vibrotactile and thermal stimulation. There has been extensive research on conveying affect through vibration, including rendering realistic touch behaviors [ 11 , 57 ], and representing the abstract emotional meanings of tactile icons with different vibration properties [ 78 , 91 , 95 , 95 ]. Besides vibrotactile, thermal sensation has been proven to connect to emotional experience inherently [ 90 ]. HCI community has been using thermal stimulation to induce affect [ 74 , 75 , 92 , 93 ], or to augment the affective experience of digital media [ 3 , 26 , 37 , 58 , 84 ].

As our main focus, cutaneous force devices mostly present on-skin motions (e.g., stretching/stroking and squeezing) through the mechanical hardware. In line with Zeagler's recommended body sites for passive touch [ 97 ], the existing devices mainly focused on the on-wrist/finger design. Most works adopted servo mechanical or pneumatic configurations to drive the belt to contract or move over users’ skins, thus rendering the sensation of pressure and tangential stretch for remote communication [ 20 , 82 , 87 ] or notifications [ 22 , 28 , 38 , 69 ], myoelectric prostheses [ 5 ], surgical training [ 14 ], robotic manipulators [ 2 , 17 , 61 ], and VR applications [ 66 , 67 ]. Besides these belt-driven cutaneous force feedback, some other researchers constructed the mechanical stimulators on the back of the watch face and communicated tactile shapes [ 46 ], and augmented animations [ 44 ]. Comparing parameterized wrist-worn squeezing, vibration, and thermal stimuli, Song et al. [ 81 ] found that squeezing cues resulted in better-recognized notifications than thermal stimuli.

For the cutaneous force devices, emotion communication was rarely concerned until Salminen et al., who investigated affect perceived via a horizontally rotating plastic fingertip stimulator [ 73 ]. They found that continuous forward-backward rotating stimuli were perceived as significantly more unpleasant, arousing, avoidable, and dominating than other stimuli (e.g., discontinuous forward rotation). Later, Bianchi et al. developed a wearable system based on an elastic superbiflex band for simulating a human caress via the on-arm movement [ 10 , 86 ]. Their results revealed that the perceived valence and arousal correlated to the velocity and the strength of the caress-like stretching, respectively. Other work also investigated affective squeezing feedback. ServoSqueeze is a rubber wristband that intends to emulate the sensation of a hand squeezing the user's wrist [ 7 ]. The authors rendered attention-getting expressions with ServoSqueeze and found that the on-wrist squeezing could evoke the impression of an animal or human, which was generally felt positively by the subjects. Wang et al. constructed a squeezable armband to augment the experience of listening to emotive stories [ 87 ]. Their results showed that touch-accompanied storytelling conveyed a higher sense of connectedness between the listener and the storyteller and a more positive affective communication tendency. Suhonen et al. studied the users’ experience and expectations of applying squeezing stimuli in interpersonal communication [ 82 ]. The squeeze feedback in their experiment was generally weak and perceived as conveying a positive sense. Their focus group results indicated that the squeezing feedback could be used as notifications and remote affective communication (e.g., comforting the other, caring for the other). Additionally, the subjects preferred receiving haptic stimuli to their hand area via a mobile phone or a wristband.

Among the aforementioned affective haptic devices, we focus on the on-skin stroking and squeezing design for investigating the influence of textile materials on users’ affective perception of haptic stimuli. The choice is because embedding vibrotactile and thermotactile components in the textiles may affect the original materials’ touch feelings (e.g., softness and roughness) [ 83 , 88 ] while stroking and squeezing could be directly applied through the raw textile materials.

Emotions Evoked by Textiles

Fabric can be defined by its yarns and the weaving techniques [ 27 ]. Varying the material of yarns could change the fabric's physical properties (e.g., elasticity and roughness) and yield different tactile sensations. For example, textiles composed of silk are usually perceived as smoother and thinner than wool fabric. Researchers from psychology and neuroscience have investigated how different tactile attributes of the fabrics could influence affect.

As an early work, Major collected pleasantness ratings of actively manipulating 51 textured fabric materials by hand from three subjects [ 60 ]. The results showed that the soft and smooth materials were reported as pleasant, while those that were stiff or rough were rated as unpleasant. Similarly, Ripin et al. compared the hedonic response to a series of silk and rayon fabrics with 100 blindfolded women [ 71 ]. The results echoed Major's conclusion and suggested that participants preferred materials with “relaxing” attributes, which referred to a range of tactile properties, including smoothness and softness. While these two early research laid their lens mainly on subjective pleasantness, most later works adopted the two-dimensional Valence-Arousal (V-A) model [ 54 , 72 ] or discrete emotion models (e.g., the wheel model for eight types of emotions [ 9 ], and the differential emotion theory with ten fundamental emotions [ 48 ]), to study the emotion evoked by materials. For instance, Ebe et al. [ 24 ] collected the subjects’ V-A ratings and choices of emotion labels for 82 samples of textures (including fabrics). Their results showed that warm, smooth, and soft textures conveyed the most positive emotions, with bumpy, wet, and sticky textures expressing the highest arousal. Iosifyan and Korolkova evaluated 21 textured samples on six basic emotions with 108 participants [ 47 ]. They found that people associated touching different textures with distinct emotions.

The aforementioned research presented the material samples as static objects waiting for the users’ active touching. Scholars also looked into the affective effects of actively moving/actuated textiles. Similar to the still materials, actuated soft and smooth materials are pleasant, whereas the stiff and rough textures are unpleasant [ 16 , 29 , 30 , 32 ]. In Essick et al.’s early work [ 29 ], the subjects were stroked by three textured end effectors (velvet, cotton, and plastic mesh) of a motor-driven device under three velocities. Their results showed that increasing the velocity of stroking decreased the variance of hedonic ratings between velvet and cotton and enlarged their superior pleasantness over the rough plastic mesh. Cascio et al. studied the hedonic response to tactile stimuli applied by a brush of different materials [ 16 ]. When the manually controlled brush stroked the skin with increasing force, the smooth stimuli maintained the pleasantness, unlike rough stimuli that caused a more unpleasant feeling. The results were confirmed by the later work of Essick et al., which presented a systematic study on the interactions between materials, body sites, velocities, forces, and gender [ 30 ].

Despite focusing on dynamic materials, most works framed their question on subjects’ hedonic response to the stimuli [ 16 , 29 , 30 , 32 , 60 , 71 ]. How users interpret the emotional meanings was not addressed. Besides, the design of such devices barely considered wearability, with the moving fabrics being placed on the grounded rotating rods or restrained as filament [ 16 , 30 ]. Recently, Valenza et al. studied the brain-wave patterns evoked by the pleasant (silk) and the unpleasant (burlap) fabrics with the care-like squeezing feedback through a wrist-worn device [ 86 ]. They demonstrated that the caresses delivered by the unpleasant and pleasant fabrics contribute to brain activation differently. Similar to our work, this work evaluated the subjects’ valence and arousal ratings towards different combinations of squeezing parameters (e.g., velocity and force). However, our work with Emoband is different in the following aspects: 1) We focused on the short tactile icons lasting for 1 and 2 seconds; 2) Our experiments on the on-wrist stroking feedback revealed a different pattern of emotion ratings compared to the squeezing feedback; 3) We studied more types of fabric materials.

Design of Emoband

To investigate the potential effect of the textile materials on users’ perception of affect through the wearable interface, we designed Emoband (Fig. 1 ), a wrist-worn haptic device to provide the stroking and squeezing stimuli. The design of Emoband mainly considered the choice of on-body site, the mechanical design, and the materials for skin contact.

On-body Site

To design an on-body haptic device, we first considered that the body site needs to be tactilely sensitive and socially acceptable. We referred to Zeagler's wearability guidelines [ 97 ], which present 13 body maps with each illustrating appropriate body locations concerning the use of wearable technology and the affordances provided by different body sites. We overlapped the two maps (i.e., sensitivity to touch and social acceptability) relevant to our design considerations, which led to a new map indicating the face and the upper-body limb (from forearm to fingers) as the potential body sites. We then inspected the emotional sensitivity across different body parts concerning the affect representation. Nummenmaa et al. [ 64 ] suggested that the body trunk (from face to stomach) and the upper limbs (from forearm to fingers) are the more effective locations that link to the emotionally elevated activities, which is likely to be corresponding to the changes in heart rate, breathing, and social touch [ 39 , 79 ]. Considering these aspects above along with the rise of smartwatches and smart wristbands [ 62 ], we chose wrist as the body site to investigate the perception of affect through textile-mediated tactile stimuli.

Emoband Prototype

The design of Emoband (Figure 2 A) was inspired by the existing research on on-wrist tactile feedback [ 2 , 10 , 17 , 61 ]. The prototype consists of five units: 1) the wristband (36 mm × 340 mm ) which supports the easy replacement of different textile materials ( a, b in Fig. 2 B); 2) the actuating unit including two servo motors ( c in Fig. 2 B), two cylindrical cores ( e in Fig. 2 B) and their relevant connectors ( d, f in Fig. 2 B); 3) the 3D printed two-layer frame ( h, i, j, k, l in Fig. 2 B) for mounting the servo motors; 4) the pressure-sensing unit with four load cells for the close-loop stimuli control ( g in Fig. 2 B); 5) two elastic polyester bands that fasten the device onto users’ wrists ( m in Fig. 2 B). The textile material connects to the actuating unit by sheathing two steel sticks, each fixed inside one of the two cylindrical cores coaxially. Each cylindrical core connects to a servo motor (Model No.: Dynamixel AX-12A, torque: 0 - 1.5N.m, speed: 0 - 113.6rpm). The motor drives the connected cylindrical core to make a coaxial and synchronized rotation, thus leading to the fabric's on-skin movement. When the two cylinders rotate in the same or opposite directions, the fabric stroke or squeeze the wrist respectively as shown in Fig. 3 A. The microcontroller controls the two motors through the USB connection to the desktop computer and receives the feedback signals via two octal buffers (Model No.: SN74LS241N). The motors are mounted on two holding slices respectively by a set of screws. Each motor-mounting slice is then inserted into the neck of the top frame layer. The position of the slide can be adjusted and anchored to fit the different wrist sizes.

While designing the squeezing stimuli, modulating the pressure applied to the skin is essential. We argue that it is also important to maintain the pressure level close to zero during the stroking stimuli, to minimize the bias created by the potential concurrent squeezing movement. To this end, we designed a real-time pressure-sensing unit for close-loop pressure control. Specifically, four strain gauge load cells (30 mm × 30 mm ) were inserted between the two frame layers as shown in the right illustration of Fig. 2 A. When the two motors rotate in different directions for the squeezing stimuli, the fabric will be contracted, leading the two frame layers to move toward each other and press the load cells. The four load sensors are connected to an HX711 Load-Cell Amplifier and capture the real-time pressure data between the two frame layers. To maintain the tension on the wristband, the band segment connecting the two frame layers needs to sustain a fixed orientation. Therefore, we placed four linking rods in the two-layer structure (two rods on each layer) to stabilize the belt movement's direction. By passing through the gap between the rods on the two sides, the fabric band goes across the two frame layers with its orientation parallel to the compression direction between the two layers (Fig. 3 B). Additionally, this structure allows a large contact area between the skin and textile, achieving a full distribution of pressure on users’ wrists and simulating the condition of wearing a watch or on-wrist accessory.

To investigate the stroking and the squeezing tactile feedback from different materials, we considered the easy replacement of the fabric bands. As shown in Fig. 4 , the cylindrical core can be detached from the clasps screwed on the motor. Thereafter, the steel stick with the fabric attached can be taken out. The fabric band can be disassembled and replaced by another material. The internal stopping structure in the cylindrical core aims to minimize the positional offset that may occur during the material replacement. To stabilize the device under motor rotation, we fixed the prototype on the table via a 3D-printed resin base during the experiment.

Textile Materials

For the fabric materials to be studied in the experiments, we considered two main criteria: 1) The materials should be commonly adopted in daily usage (e.g., clothes, accessories and wearable devices); 2) The physical characteristics of the textile should support a persistent effect of stroking and squeezing when the motors move at different velocities. To this end, we looked into five natural materials, including cotton, silk, flax, wool and leather, considering their popularity in the local community. Furthermore, these materials (excluding leather) were chosen as non-elastic woven textiles due to their superior ability to maintain structure over knitted fabric [ 70 ]. Each material was cut into a rectangular strip (36 mm × 340 mm ) while its two ends were bent and sewed into two sheaths for easy connection to the actuating unit. Table 1 presents the specifications and physical parameters of the five materials we measured in the laboratory.

We also investigated people's tactile sensation towards different textured materials being still on the wrist. Based on previous studies on tactile properties [ 19 , 24 , 42 , 43 ], we adopted “softness”, “slipperiness”, “smoothness”, “warmth” as the four dimensions to describe the tactile sensation of wearing a textured wristband. 15 participants (8 males, and 7 females) averagely aging 27.3 years old (SD = 2.55) were invited to try on Emoband with the five materials in our lab, with the order of the materials being counterbalanced. All the participants are right-handed, with their wrist sizes ranging from 125mm to 172mm (M = 149, SD = 1.32). For each material, the participant put on the wristband, moved his/her wrist slightly to feel the tactile contact, and ranked the five materials by each aforementioned property, respectively. For data analysis purposes, we assigned materials an ordinal value from 1 to 5 according to their position on the ordinal scale of a particular tactile dimension (e.g., 1 - the least soft, 5 - the softest for “softness”). Concerning the ordinal nature of our measurements, we conducted the non-parametric Friedman tests for the ratings of the four tactile properties across the five materials. Friedman test revealed a significant effect of the material type on all the tactile properties (softness: χ 2 (4) = 40, p < 0.01; slipperiness: χ 2 (4) = 37, p < 0.01; smoothness: χ 2 (4) = 52, p < 0.01; warmth: χ 2 (4) = 47, p < 0.01). Table 2 presents the detailed pair-wise comparisons of the five materials by tactile property. While silk and leather are relatively slippery, smooth and cool, they are also the softest and the hardest material, respectively. On the other hand, wool is the most sticky, rough and warm material.

Tactile Parameters

Stroking

The stroking stimuli usually come along with the tangential movement between the textile and the skin. We investigated the velocity, duration, and direction choices for the textile-stroking movement. While Bianchi et al. suggested a set of “velocities of the caress” (9.4mm/s, 37mm/s, 65mm/s) for on-wrist fabric tactile display [ 10 ], we empirically found that these velocities were too subtle to drive the on-skin movement of certain fabric materials, such as wool, due to its rough surface. Besides, we intended to thoroughly study the on-wrist fabric-based tactile icons with short duration instead of the caress-like touch, which usually lasts longer (4-30s), as shown in the related work. Therefore, we tuned the movement velocity faster (from 78mm/s to 206mm/s) to ensure the noticeable offset between the fabric band and skin for the five chosen materials. For the duration of the stimuli, Wilson et al. [ 91 ] and Seifi et al. [ 78 ] suggested a duration ranging from 50 ms to 2000ms for the perceivable affective tactile feedback. To this end, we set 3 levels of velocity (78mm/s, 142mm/s, 206mm/s) × 2 durations (1s, 2s) × 2 directions (clockwise, anticlockwise) = 12 stroking stimuli. We also aimed to control the pressure close to zero, which means the fabric band rested on the skin lightly and minimized normal force (i.e., pressure) on the wrist as much as possible. Therefore, we adjusted the band segment wrapping a subject's wrist to fit with his/her wristline.

Squeezing

We also looked into the pressure and the duration of squeezing which mainly presents the pressing force in the normal direction around the wrist. Here we defined the duration of squeezing as the period of the fabric band maintaining the desired pressure on the wrist. Taking Gupta et al.’s previous work on wrist squeezing [ 36 ] as the reference, we adopted the force range of 0 - 15N for the squeezing stimuli, and chose 5N, 10N, and 15N as the interval target levels for affective evaluation. In our testing, the activation took approximately 900ms, 1300ms and 1500ms to reach the low, medium and high pressure levels, respectively and it took around 700ms to deactivate. While the pressure maintained at the three set points, the average pressure was 4.91N (SD = 0.11), 10.01N (SD = 0.17) and 15.26N (SD = 0.20), respectively. When determining the duration, we had a similar consideration of the perceivable affective tactile feedback as we did for stroking stimuli. We also took into account that the time of sensing the lasting pressure should surpass the pressure-changing phase. Thus, we referred to the maximum tactile duration from the previous study [ 78 ] as the long duration level (2s) and halved the value as the short duration level (1s).

Experiment One: On-wrist Stroking Feedback

After determining the tactile parameters, we first conducted user-perception experiments to inspect how people perceive the affect conveyed by a textile band stroking on their wrists.

Participants

Through social media and word of mouth, we recruited 15 participants (8 males, and 7 females) aged 24 to 32 (M = 24.6, SD = 2.67). All the participants are right-handed, with wrist sizes ranging from 125mm to 162mm (M = 148.7, SD = 1.13).

Study Design

Experiment one was a 5 × 3 × 2 × 2 within-subject design with four independent variables: 5 types of materials for the wristband: cotton, silk, flax, wool, leather; 3 levels of stroking velocity: slow - 78mm/s, medium - 142mm/s, fast - 206mm/s; 2 stroking duration: 1s, 2s; and 2 stroking directions: CW, ACW. For the experiment, we measured the following dependant variables: 1) the participants’ valence and arousal ratings on wearing a still wristband of specific material through a 7-point emotion rating Likert scale [ 12 ]; 2) the participants’ valence and arousal ratings on the parameterized stroking stimulation through the same emotion rating Likert scale. We also collected the participants’ subjective feedback concerning their emotion perception towards the stroking stimuli as well as the potential applications of Emoband with different textured materials.

Experiment Setup and Procedure

The experiment was conducted in an air-conditioned room with the average temperature controlled at 23°C (M = 22.8, SD = 0.7) throughout the study. Fig. 5 shows the experiment setup. The Emoband device was attached to the base and grounded on the table. Participants sat in front of a laptop connected to the Arduino Mega board via USB. The laptop screen showed the user interface that guided the participants to go through the experiment. We hung a black curtain between the Emoband and the laptop, to block the participants’ vision from the wristband during the study. The participants also wore earplugs to block the noise made by the rotating motors.

Upon arrival, the participant was greeted and guided to read and sign the informed consent form. We then measured the participant's wristline using a tapeline. Afterward, the researcher helped the participant wear the Emoband and adjusted the wristband length to achieve a no-pressure contact state between the skin and the textile. After wearing the Emoband, the participant was informed of the experiment procedure through a demonstration in which he/she got familiar with the experiment interface and the stroking stimuli.

After the demonstration, the participant put on the earplugs and started the experiment sessions. All the stimuli were presented through five sessions corresponding to the five textile materials chosen for our studies. The sequence of the sessions was counterbalanced in Latin square. At the beginning of each session, the participant rated the perceived valence and arousal of wearing the still wristband made of a specific material via a 7-point SAM Likert-scale [ 12 ] presented on the desktop user interface (Valence: 1-“very negative” to 7-“very positive”, and Arousal: 1-“very calm” to 7-“very excited”). After rating the affect of the material being still, the participant experienced and rated all the 36 trials (3 levels of velocity × 2 durations × 2 directions × 3 repetitions) in the random order. Each stimulus consisted of a three-second countdown and the actual stroking stimulus with a specific configuration of speed, duration, and direction. After each stimulus, the participant rated the valence and the arousal perceived through the tactile feedback using the 7-point SAM Likert scales shown on the user interface, and clicked the “Next” button for confirmation and motor reset. When rating the affect for the moving material, participants were reminded to infer the emotional meaning conveyed by the stroking wristband (e.g., the stimulus notifies a message from his/her friend), rather than their hedonic response to the material. This question framing aims to inspect the tactile feedback's effect in the context of daily communication.

The interface reminded the participant to take a one-minute break every 6 trials by text. During the break, the participant could take the wristband off and relax his/her wrist. When a session was finished, the researcher changed the textile band with another material while the participant could take a 5-minute break.

The whole experiment ended with a short semi-structured interview, during which the participant was encouraged to recall the experience, discuss his/her emotional perception under different conditions and the potential applications of the Emoband stroking feedback with different textile materials. The experiments were video- and audio-recorded with the participants’ consent, lasting around one hour and forty minutes per person.

Results

We considered the effects of the four independent factors on the ratings of valence and arousal towards the tactile stimuli. Additionally, we collected the affect ratings of wearing the still wristband across the five materials. Shapiro-Wilk normality tests showed that the participants’ valence ratings towards the stroking stimuli for two materials (Cotton: p < 0.05; Silk: p < 0.001), all the levels of velocity (all at p < 0.001), both durations (both at p < 0.001), and both directions (ACW: p < 0.05, CW: p < 0.001) are not normally distributed. The arousal ratings for all the materials (all at p < 0.001), all the velocities (all at p < 0.05), both durations (both at p < 0.05), and both directions (both at p < 0.05) are not normally distributed. Regarding the affect ratings for the still materials, Shapiro-Wilk normality tests showed that participants’ valence (Flax: p < 0.001; Else: p < 0.05) and arousal (all at p < 0.001) ratings for four materials (except for wool) are not normally distributed. Therefore, we conducted the non-parametric Friedman tests for the affect ratings for the still wristband. Considering the interaction effects between the materials and the movement parameters, we adopted the aligned rank transform (ART) [ 94 ] followed by the repeated-measures ANOVA, to assess the effects of the materials and the movement parameters on the participants’ valence and arousal ratings.

For the still materials, Friedman Test revealed a significant effect of the material type on the valence ratings ( χ 2 (4) = 11.0, p = 0.02), but not on arousal. Post-hoc Wilcoxon signed-rank tests showed significant differences between wool and three materials on the valence ratings (wool-silk: p = 0.01; wool-flax: p = 0.02; wool-leather: p = 0.02). Most participants (12/15) claimed that the tactile feel of the materials affected their valence ratings. They pointed out that the uncomfortable materials could cause negative feelings and more negative overall valence ratings compared to the comfortable ones. Five participants mentioned that wool was rough and felt uncomfortable. In contrast, soft materials like silk brought a positive (P5) and easygoing (P13) impression. The difference in the heat capacities of different textile materials could cause different thermal feelings. For instance, silk could feel cooler than cotton. The four subjects (P3, P4, P6, P13) mentioned that the thermal sensation also influenced their affective impression. However, their interpretation of the materials’ thermal impact was different. Two subjects (P3, P4) perceived the cooler materials (i.e., silk) as the medium with negative meanings, while the others (P6, P13) felt the cool sensation comfortable and considered it a positive signal. Two participants (P4, P11) also mentioned that highly textured materials like wool stimulated more striking feelings than smooth and soft materials (e.g., silk, flax) and made more vital emotional impressions.

Most participants agreed that the stroking velocity affected their rated affect (13/15). Half of the participants (8/15) mentioned that the slow stroking conveyed a calm but somehow negative sense and described the feeling as a depressed emotional state (P4, P8, P9, P11, P13, P14). In contrast, the fast stimuli were perceived as happy (P8, P9, P13), outgoing (P2), and exciting (P11), representing a positive and active emotional state. Six participants (P2, P5, P6, P13, P14, P15) also pointed out that the wristband material mediated the velocity's impact on their affect ratings. For the comfortable materials (e.g., silk, leather), the participants agreed that increasing the stroking velocity caused a more positive perceived valence. They reflected that the wool wristband was uncomfortable and caused negative impressions. Three (P2, P5, P13) said that increasing the velocity enlarged the negative feelings. In contrast, the other three participants (P6, P14, P15) felt the high-speed stroking decreased the uncomfortable feeling and made the stimuli more positive. The varying valence perception for wool-based on-wrist stroking echoes the even distribution of valence ratings under high-velocity conditions, as shown in Fig. 10 (e).

As for the stroking duration, all the participants found that more prolonged stimuli brought more intense emotional experience. Five subjects (P3, P4, P5, P6, P9) mentioned that the longer stroking felt like a clear and strong expression. Four subjects (P1, P7, P11, P15) considered the duration of stroking to represent the intensity of the expressed emotion. That is, “ The longer the stroking is, the stronger emotion I felt ”. Two participants also mentioned that the longer duration enabled them to thoroughly experience the stimuli and catch more emotional cues (P2, P13).

P1 commented on the effect of stroking direction on his valence ratings, saying that he tended to map the two stroking directions to the two polarities of the perceived valence. Rotating clockwise represented the positive signals, while the opposite direction (i.e., counterclockwise) meant the negative cues. P15 mentioned the different tactile intensities between the two directions’ stroking, claiming that the wristband moved more smoothly when rotating clockwise, potentially due to the unsymmetrical shape of the human wrist, thus leveling up the urgency of the information.

Summary

Our first experiment revealed that the textile-based on-wrist stroking could represent affective information which could be impacted by the material type and the stroking parameters, including the velocity, the duration, and the direction.

Generally, in our first experiment, the mild stroking stimuli with low speed and short duration tended to cause a calm and negative impression regardless of the wristband's material. Increasing the stroking velocity or prolonging the duration would cause more intense feelings and increase arousal. For valence, speeding up the stroking stimulation would cause varying effects depending on the materials. In our experiments, the faster stroking movements tended to cause a more positive impression for most materials while having no significant impact on wool. This part of the results, which concerns the effect of the stroking velocity, is different from the findings from previous work of mimicking care-like touch with textile stripes [ 10 , 86 ], which reported that the valence ratings could be negatively correlated to the stroking velocity. Our first experiment showed that the participants’ valence ratings tended to go positively along with the stroking velocity. One possible reason might be the different stroking parameters and fabric materials of the on-wrist stimulation. Their care-like touch via an elastic Superbiflex lasts more than 4s when the fabric continuously rotates with a constant pressure (2N or 6N). In contrast, our stimulation experimenting with varying textured material is short (1s or 2s) as tactile icons [ 95 ] with no compression on the wrist.

Previous studies on tactile sensations revealed that speeding up the passive touching would increase the perceived roughness for the rough materials (e.g., vinyl) other than the smoother materials (e.g., silk) [ 96 ]. The increasing roughness could cause dominating unpleasant feelings [ 23 ]. We observed that for wool which was perceived as the roughest, there is no significant correlation between the velocity level and the rated valence (R = 0.04, p = 0.59), while the valence ratings for the other smoother materials were positively correlated to the stroking velocity (Cotton: R = 0.26; Silk: R = 0.29; Flax: R = 0.26; Leather: R = 0.41, all at p < 0.001). To this end, the participants’ emotional perception might also depend on tactile sensations (e.g., warmth, stickiness, on-skin locomotion) other than roughness. For example, one participant mentioned that the fast stroking felt negative through the fuggy wool but positive and active through the cool materials like silk. Besides, Gabrielsson [ 33 ] stated that people's perception of an emotional expression is a perceptual-cognitive process rather than their emotional response to the sensory stimuli. In our case, many participants said they would consider the semantic meanings of the wristband's movement (e.g., the fast stroking was described as “exciting”, “active” and “optimistic”) when interpreting the embedded emotions. The factors discussed above might jointly affect our participants’ appraisal process [ 68 ] when perceiving the affect delivered by the stroking feedback. While the faster stroking tends to be perceived semantically positive in general, it would cause unpleasant feelings tactilely with wool (e.g., the enlarged roughness brings the dominating unpleasantness [ 23 ]). The conflicting affective cues might cause mixed feelings [ 45 , 55 ] and enlarge the between-participants variation of affective interpretation for wool. In contrast, speeding up the stroking stimulation holds a more consistent effect on valence interpretation with the tactile sensations from the smoother materials whose perceived roughness may not be largely influenced by the stroking velocity [ 23 ], thus contributing to the increasing effect of the stroking velocity on valence.

Materials also affect how the participants perceived the emotional pleasantness of the on-wrist stroking stimuli. Within the five studied materials in our first experiment, wool was found to convey the least pleasant information, while the on-wrist stroking stimuli delivered through silk, flax and leather were rated more positive than the stimuli from wool and cotton. Our post-study interview suggested that the tendentiousness on the valence ratings might be interfered with by the wearing comfort of the material, which could be related to the material's physical characteristics (e.g., softness, roughness). This echoed the previous research on hedonic response to moving materials, in which the soft and the smooth materials yielded more pleasant response [ 16 , 29 , 30 , 32 , 34 ]. While the on-wrist stroking stimuli caused similar arousal ranges across the five studied materials, intensifying the stimuli with higher velocities could affect the perceived valence differently depending on the material. With the materials causing less discomfort (e.g., silk, leather), the rated valence would be increased to the positive side, thus creating an affect space spanning diagonally from the “Calm-unpleasant” quadrant to the “Exciting-pleasant” quadrant on the V-A map. However, increasing the stroking velocity and duration did not affect the valence ratings for wool, which was considered uncomfortable by most of our participants. We also observed that the valence ratings tended to be diverse across the participants when the stroking stimuli reached the “high” velocity level, especially for wool. The slope of the V-A rating change varies among materials and therefore yielded different distributions of valence expression. Comparing the distribution to affective stimulation of other forms (vibrotactile, thermal and visual feedback) [ 91 ], we found that in our experiment, the on-wrist textile-based stroking feedback created a different affect distribution since the other modalities tended to express emotions spanning from the “Calm-pleasant” quadrant to the “Exciting-unpleasant” quadrant. The difference could potentially help designers to expand the expression space of affective feedback.

Experiment Two: On-wrist Squeezing Feedback

Following the first experiment, we conducted the second user-perception experiment to inspect how people may perceive affect conveyed by a textile band squeezing on their wrists.

Participants

Following the same method used for the first experiment, we recruited 15 participants (5 males, and 10 females) aged 23 to 32 (M = 24.6, SD = 2.67) who didn't participate in the first experiment. All the participants are right-handed with wrist sizes ranging from 136mm to 159mm (M = 146.0, SD = 0.72).

Study Design

In the second experiment, we adopted a 5 × 3 × 2 within-subject design with three independent variables: 5 types of wristband materials: cotton, silk, flax, wool, leather; 3 squeezing pressure: low - 5N, medium - 10N, high - 15N; and 2 squeezing durations: 1s, 2s. Similar to the first experiment, we measured: 1) the participants’ valence and arousal ratings on wearing the still wristband of a specific material through the 7-point emotion rating Likert scale [ 12 ]; 2) the participants’ valence and arousal ratings on the parameterized squeezing stimulation through the same emotion rating Likert scale. We also collected the participants’ subjective feedback concerning their emotion perception towards the squeezing stimuli and the potential applications of Emoband with different textured materials in the post-experiment sessions.

Experiment setup and procedure

The second experiment was conducted in the same air-conditioned room where the temperature was controlled at 23°C (M = 23.3, SD = 0.4). The setup and the procedure followed the same manner as experiment one, except that the participant rated 18 randomized trials (3 pressure levels × 2 durations × 3 times) for each material session for which the order was counterbalanced across the five materials. After each stimulus, the participant rated the valence and the arousal perceived through the squeezing feedback. The study also ended with a semi-structured interview, during which the participant recalled the experience, and shared his/her emotional perception and the potential applications of Emoband squeezing feedback with different textured materials. The experiment took around one hour and twenty minutes per person.

We considered the effects of the three independent factors (i.e., the type of material, the pressure and the duration of squeezing) on the ratings of valence and arousal towards the squeezing stimuli. Similar to experiment one, we also collected the affect ratings of wearing the still wristband. Besides, the arousal ratings for all the materials (all at p <= 0.01), all the pressures (all at p <= 0.01), and both directions (all at p <= 0.01) are not normally distributed. Therefore, we conducted the non-parametric Friedman tests for the affect ratings for the still wristband. Summary

Our second experiment revealed that the textured squeezing wristband could be felt emotionally, which could be affected by the material type and the squeezing parameters, including the pressure and duration.

In general, the mild squeezing stimuli with low pressure and short duration in our second experiment tended to make a calm and positive impression regardless of the wristband's material, occupying the calm and pleasant quadrant on the V-A map. Increasing the squeezing pressure or prolonging the duration would cause more negative valence ratings but higher emotional activation and increased arousal. The most salient feedback with high pressure and long duration tended to convey a highly aroused negative state, supporting squeezing stimulation's ability to encode anger as reported in previous research [ 39 , 40 ]. Besides, the affective perception towards the varied-level squeezing feedback is similar to that towards vibrotactile, thermal, and visual stimuli [ 91 ]. The stimulation with intensified parameters would increase the emotional activation but cause more negative valence.

Materials also affected how the participants rated the emotional pleasantness (i.e., valence) of the on-wrist squeezing stimuli. Within the five studied materials in the second experiment, comfortable materials like silk were found to convey more pleasant meanings, with the V-A distribution more positive-sided on the V-A map. The participants also mentioned that physical characteristics like “smoothness” and “softness” mediated the perceived comfort, which aligned with the previous study on hedonic response to moving materials [ 16 , 29 , 30 , 32 ]. While the material type significantly affected the pleasantness perceived from the squeezing stimulation, they did not interact with the movement factors for emotional expression. The slopes of the V-A distributions for all the studied materials are similar, spanning diagonally from the “Calm-pleasant” quadrant to the “Exciting-unpleasant” quadrant. Additionally, we observed that for all the materials, the valence ratings tended to be diverse across the participants when the squeezing stimuli reached the “high” pressure level.

General Discussion

The Affective Expression Space of On-Wrist Textile Motion

After the two experiments, we overlapped the V-A maps of stroking and squeezing stimulation for each material, which led to the space of deliverable affective states from both forms of on-wrist tactile icons per material (Fig. 16 ). In the combined V-A map, the solid-colored squares indicate the average affect ratings of stroking stimulation, while the blank-colored ones represent the average ratings of squeezing stimuli.

According to the maps, most materials (cotton, silk, flax, and leather) could evoke the affective on-wrist stroking and squeezing stimuli to cover all the quadrants on the V-A space. Yet, the communicated emotional meanings from these four materials mainly lie on three quadrants of the V-A map with only one V-A pair located in the “Excited-Unpleasant” space. For wool, the yielded affective states cover three quadrants (Calm-Unpleasant, Excited-Unpleasant, Calm-Pleasant). Among the five materials, silk and flax mostly posit their affective ratings on the positive side along the valence axis. On the other hand, wool tended to yield less pleasant ratings, with most of its average valence ratings lying on the negative side. The average affective ratings for cotton and leather are more balanced on both valence polarities.

Besides, potentially due to our participants’ higher consistency of valence perception towards the stroking leather, the extent of the yielded pleasantness from leather tends to broaden along with the increasing of arousal. In contrast, the average affective ratings for wool mostly sit on a small range of “negative” positions on the valence axis, regardless of the variations in stroking conditions. Considering the universal movement parameters for both materials, We speculate that the variation of the between-subjects consistency of valence perception might owe to the differences in the tactile sensations between the materials. To this end, we first computed the linear-regression slope in the V-A space for each material and each participant, to reflect the participant's V-A rating tendency towards different stroking parameters. We then calculated the standard deviation (SD) of the linear regression slopes for each material, with the smaller SD indicating the higher rating consistency across the participants. The Spearman's Correlation Test showed that the between-subjects rating consistency was positively correlated to the perceived smoothness (R = 0.97, p < 0.001), slipperiness (R = 0.69, p < 0.001) and softness (R = 0.37, p < 0.01) of the still material, but negatively correlated to the warmth (R = -0.76, p < 0.001). That is, smoother, slipperier, and cooler materials like leather and silk tend to elicit more consistent valence perception than rough, sticky, and warm materials like wool.

For all the materials, the average valence ratings span narrower ranges than arousal. The thin and tall V-A distribution is due to the aforementioned between-subjects difference of the valence ratings on the fast stroking or high-pressure squeezing, which resulted in the average valence ratings lying on the more neutral area on the valence axis. This variation of valence ratings also turned up for affective vibrations [ 78 ]. In contrast, our participants showed high consistency in rating the arousal aspects, with faster (93.33% of participants), stronger (93.33% of participants), and longer (all participants) stimuli being rated with higher arousal values. Our narrower valence spanning compared to arousal echoed with the results in the previous works on on-wrist caress stroking stimuli whose duration is longer than our tactile icons [ 35 , 63 ], potentially indicating the general characteristic of such textured on-wrist tactile affective stimuli. On the other hand, the different consistencies of valence ratings across our five materials might suggest that the textured media would provide another dimension for affect interpretation other than the movement parameters. Moreover, the consistency between the two dimensions when delivering affective cues might affect the between-participants difference in emotion perception. In our case, the smooth and cool materials (e.g., silk, leather) might contribute to a more consistent affect interpretation than the rough and warm ones (e.g., wool).

Design Considerations for Textile-Mediated On-Wrist Stroking and Squeezing

According to the results from the two studies, we formulated several design considerations for wrist-worn stroking and squeezing feedback mediated by different textile materials.

V-A Maps as Affective Design References. The V-A distribution maps presented in this paper could serve as the design references for designing wrist-worn textile devices to communicate affect. The distribution indicates the possible affective states that the users could potentially perceive through a wristband fabricated with certain types of textile material. While we mainly studied the affect space of five common natural materials at the current stage, other natural (e.g., hemp, velvet) and synthetical materials (e.g., nylon, silicon rubber) could be investigated with a similar process. Given a textured belt, the corresponding distribution map shows how to tune the movement parameters to present a desired emotional meaning within the deliverable affective space.

The Yielded Pleasantness vs The Perceived Comfort. Our experiments showed that the rated valence of the stimulation tended to be correlated with the comfort of the textile material commented by the participants. For both the stroking and the squeezing stimulation, comfortable materials like silk tended to cause more pleasant impressions than uncomfortable ones (e.g., wool). According to the V-A maps, silk and flax tended to convey positive meanings, while wool would yield more negative ratings. Leather and cotton behaved better at expressing more balanced emotional meanings compared to the other three materials.

Materials for the Manifestation of Stroking Feedback. Materials could mediate the emotional expression of stroking stimuli. For example, when the stroking stimuli changed from mild to prominent, the leather-based wristband behaved best at manifesting the stimulation's enhancing effect on the perceived valence, enabling a relatively wide range of valence expression. In contrast, the participants’ affective perception towards the tactile feedback tended to be inconsistent with the wristband made of rough, sticky, hard and warm materials (e.g., wool, cotton), leading to no clear patterns of valence change along with the variations in tactile parameters.

Individual Differences of Affective Ratings. We observed between-participants variance in the emotional understandings towards the on-wrist stroking and squeezing stimuli in certain combinations of parameters. For example, materials like cotton and wool tend to cause varying valence interpretations while the stroking feedback speeds up. To provide reliable affective feedback, it is advised to consider individual perception differences through a calibration phase. As suggested by Wilson et al. [ 91 ], the system could present example stimulation to the user and record his/her responses. Therefore, the users’ perception tendency could be captured by the system to offer tailored affective feedback.

Movement Factors Dominated Emotional Activation. In our experiments, the activation of emotional meaning (i.e., the arousal level) was found to be dominated by movement-related factors for both stroking and squeezing stimulation. The more salient tactile feedback with higher velocity/pressure or the longer duration would convey more activated emotional states. If the wristband is used to communicate hierarchical information regardless of the valence meaning (e.g., the urgency of the incoming message), designers could forego the concern of materials’ effect on users’ perceived pleasantness and choose materials based on other considerations like the visual appearance, durability, etc.

Limitations and Future Work

During the study, we identified a few limitations that could be solved and improved in future work. First, we investigated five textile materials due to their popularity in the local community. However, it could still be challenging to generalize the results to other textiles or the same materials with different fabrications. The affective effects found for our materials are limited to the textiles with the given specifications (Table 1 ). Considering that a certain type of fabric could be defined by its yarns and the methods that construct the yarns together [ 27 ], researchers and designers could create textile samples using specific fibers and fabrication techniques as Ku did [ 53 ]. Thereafter, future research could control both the materials and the weaving manners of the textile materials by adopting industrial technologies (e.g., the programmable weaving machine), for thoroughly studying the subjects’ affective response towards textile-mediated tactile stimuli.

Second, we studied the emotional meanings of purely material-mediated tactile icons, which revealed people's interpretation of tactile stimulation's abstract meanings. While visual, audio or multi-modal facets usually communicate daily information, our results may lead to the application of pure material-based affective tactile icons in eyes/ears-free scenarios. We admit that combining with other feedback modalities (e.g., visual, audio, vibration, and thermal) may enrich the affective expressiveness of pure material-based on-wrist tactile icons. HCI scholars have explored how vibro- and thermo-tactile feedback could be combined with visual feedback for a wide range of affective representation [ 91 ], and augment the emotional expression of multimedia contents, including text messages [ 84 ], voice messages [ 26 ] or images [ 58 ]. Our results of the squeezing icons showed a similar distribution as vibrations [ 91 ]. This could be because both short pressure and high-frequency vibration (i.e.,  100Hz) tend to stimulate the same group of skin tactile receptors, the Pacinian corpuscles [ 1 ]. Thus we could hypothesize that the squeezing stimuli may potentially create similar V-A distribution as vibrations when paired with other modalities/media, which needs further validation. In addition, it is unknown how users may perceive the affect of the textile-based on-wrist tactile icons under various environmental contexts, such as in/outdoor, still/move, and so on, which also needs in-depth investigation.

Third, our current Emoband prototype was fixed on the table during the experiments and made noise when actuating, which may not support the portable requirement for daily communication. We argue the necessity of this configuration due to the requirement of precise tactile control for the two experiments. The current prototype could be further improved by minimising the device's hardware settings (e.g., using tinnier motors, analysing the relationship between the device's input power and the squeezing pressure and removing the load cells). With the refined device, we would investigate the participants’ affective perception of the on-wrist tactile stimuli in mobile situations, such as walking, jogging, and taking turbulent transportation, both indoors and outdoors.

Last but not least, our results showed that the type of textile material could yield different affective responses through the on-wrist stroking and squeezing stimuli for our participants. This indicates that different application scenarios and personal preferences could adopt different materials. On the other hand, one may not frequently change the material of his/her wristband, even in different usage scenarios. This places an interesting research problem and challenges on dynamically changing the wearable materials to trigger different affective perceptions. The change could be potentially achieved by either specific mechanical structures to switch among different materials or smart textiles that could change its internal chemistry properties with external triggers.

Conclusion

In this work, we investigated the participants’ affective ratings (i.e., valence and arousal) towards on-wrist stroking and squeezing icons mediated by different textile materials, through a wrist-worn device called Emoband. Our results showed that the valence ratings of both stroking and squeezing were significantly affected by the movement-related parameters and the wristband's material interactively. The arousal rating was dominated by the stroking or squeezing parameters, such as speed, direction, duration, and pressure (for squeezing). Meanwhile, the valence ratings demonstrated different tendencies between the stroking and the squeezing feedback, with the valence going positively and negatively along with the strength of stroking and squeezing, respectively. Besides, the valence ratings of the stroking stimuli demonstrated varied distributions across different materials, indicating that the different types of fabrics may have different expressiveness for evoking emotional experiences in the wrist-worn haptic devices. Our findings can provide references for designing wrist-worn affective feedback, which can be applied to a series of communication applications like tactile emoticons or hierarchical reminders of a smart watch.
