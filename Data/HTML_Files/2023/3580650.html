Tips, Tricks, and Training: Supporting Anti-Phishing Awareness among Mid-Career Office Workers Based on Employees’ Current Practices

CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Preventing workplace phishing depends on the actions of every employee, regardless of cybersecurity expertise. Based on 24 semi-structured interviews with mid-career office workers (70.8% women, averaging 44 years old) at two U.S. universities, we found that less than 21% of our participants had any formal anti-phishing training. Much of what our participants know about phishing comes from informal sources that emphasize “tips” and "tricks" like those found in conversations with friends, news stories, newsletters, social media, and podcasts. These informal channels provide opportunities for IT professionals wishing to enhance employees’ anti-phishing awareness by better aligning the delivery of expert advice with employees’ current practices and desires. We provide four recommendations designed to embrace "guerrilla learning" by distributing anti-phishing educational resources across the workplace and workday in part to encourage the delivery of more accurate information in more informal and incidental ways, and greater dialogue between anti-phishing training instructors and learners.

1 INTRODUCTION

Adult Americans typically spend a significant amount of their waking hours online; a 2021 Pew research study found that 85% of American adults say they go online on a daily basis and 31% claim to be online “almost constantly” [ 77 ]. Unfortunately, despite this high level of connectivity and reliance on online computer-mediated interaction, these same adults are likely to have little to no formal computer education–including cybersecurity training–given its absence in U.S. K-12 and secondary education [ 16 , 48 ]. With high computer usage but little pre-work training, one might wonder how our workforce actually does learn about cybersecurity–and whether answers to this question might help workplace organizations strengthen cybersecurity efforts.

Of course, online cybersecurity threats present an ongoing risk to users and institutions whether or not individuals have been educated on how to recognize, avoid, and recover from such attacks. Of these cybersecurity threats, phishing attacks—semantic [ 40 ] technology-mediated social engineering attacks in which recipients are defrauded into revealing sensitive information by individuals purporting to be legitimate contacts [ 44 , 95 , 100 ]—are of particular concern. In 2016, for example, an employee at the Austrian aerospace company FACC transferred an estimated 54 million euros to fraudulent accounts outside of the company's control after responding to a counterfeit email purporting to be from the company's CEO. [ 70 , 71 ]. Global cybercrime damages are predicted to cost $ 10.5 trillion annually by 2025 [ 66 ], and as many as 91% of successful cyberattacks begin with a phishing message [ 17 ]. The frequency [ 38 ] and the costs [ 56 ] of phishing have more than doubled in the last 5 years. In fact, the Anti-Phishing Working Group (APWG) found that phishing reports doubled in frequency in 2020 [ 38 ], and July 2021 saw the highest number of phishing attacks since APWG began collecting reports [ 37 ]. Phishing is of particular concern in the cybersecurity world, then, because it is a huge and growing threat to organizations across the private and public sector.

One reason for the current increase in phishing attacks is that a successful attack depends on a single click from a single, unknowing computer user—which, as we have just described, may very well define the majority of the organization's membership. Phishing attacks may appear in a variety of ways [ 4 , 82 , 88 , 97 ], but they are all communications directed toward individuals and are designed to trick the user into providing sensitive information such as system access credentials. This may happen either directly, in which the user types and provides their credentials in response to the scammer's request, or, for instance, by clicking on a link or attachment, which will then install software that will gather this information, usually without the user's knowledge.

Because phishing is such a large and growing problem for organizations, and because it targets individual users wherever they might be logged in, we believe anti-phishing solutions need to be approached at both the level of the organization and from the users’ perspectives. While numerous studies have focused on organizational responses such as the creation of automated filters, regular software security updates, and scanning for the presence of malware [e.g., 68 , 78 , 88 , 5 , 75 ], too few phishing studies actually take a user-centered approach to understand how individuals make sense of phishing threats and countermeasures and how they learned to think about such threats in these ways [ 20 ].

For this reason, we were inspired to ask, systematically, what do non-cybersecurity-expert computing users think they know about phishing, how do they come to learn it, and how confident are they in what they know? Based on interviews with 24 university office workers in the United States, we find that mid-career professionals do, in fact, learn about phishing in largely informal ways through a collection of workplace and non-workplace sources (e.g., social media, podcasts, YouTube, and the news), and that overall, they would like more training in this area. Based on what we learned from our participants, we also propose some ideas for the kinds of ways that organizations might offer new opportunities for them—and others like them—to learn more about phishing and how to thwart these attacks. We suggest meeting participants where they are and in this case, adopting an approach of “distributed learning opportunities”—comprised of both more systematic and accessible formal training and more unexpected, diverse and informal moments of learning distributed throughout the workplace environment. The rapidly, massively increasing problem of phishing attacks on organizations suggests that whatever training is currently in use in today's workplaces, it is insufficient to stop the growing success of these attacks. While better employee education may not be the answer to the entirety of this problem, it is one tool in the cybersecurity toolkit that should perhaps be more finely honed than is currently the case. We therefore conclude with four specific and actionable recommendations to help organizations better realize this goal of distributing informal opportunities across different environments and contexts to better supplement formal anti-phishing workplace training.

We believe three primary contributions of this work to the broader usable privacy and security research community are 1) a focus on the organizational security habits of a participant pool of mid-career employees that is largely comprised of middle-aged (mean age=43.92) women (70.83%), 2) a detailed assessment of these computer users’ informal phishing information-seeking and learning, and 3) our targeted recommendations to reshape current anti-phishing training approaches and to supplement formal training through informal learning opportunities.

2 RELATED WORK

Anti-phishing training has taken a number of forms as researchers and organizations try to understand what helps educational content stick in learners’ minds. For years, those like Kumaraguru et al. have advocated for an "embedded training" approach by which imitation phishing attacks are used to test individuals’ phishing awareness. A user who clicks through on one of these imitation phishing attempts is immediately given feedback about how they might avoid it in the future [ 46 , 54 ]. Other researchers have explored not just the form by which training is delivered, but also the emphasis of training content. Jensen et al., for example, find that training approaches that emphasize mindfulness over specific rules may be more effective in helping individuals avoid clicking on phishing links [ 49 ].

Despite these efforts, attempts to design a one-size-fits-all training standard often fall short as training outcomes vary greatly based on a learner's previous experience with phishing, the learner's overall computing technical expertise, and social and environmental factors in learning and testing settings [ 12 , 18 , 26 , 28 , 36 , 45 , 46 , 83 ]. Moreover, commonly-used anti-phishing training approaches often fail to account for critical factors such as the age of the users, the quality of the materials included in the training, and prior experiences users may have had with phishing [ 46 ].

Traditional anti-phishing education can be further impeded by a lack of motivation among would-be learners [ 46 , 47 ]. Not only do computer users report feeling unincentivized to better their individual or organizational security, but anti-phishing educational material is often so lacking in entertainment and engagement that content is never learned, much less retained [ 55 ]. Phishing experts have responded to this latter problem by trying to make training materials more entertaining by presenting material in the form of comics [ 55 ] or by the gamification of learning through a challenge-reward structure [ 7 , 29 , 64 , 92 , 105 ], to varying success [ 46 ].

In order to assist organizations and individuals looking to improve their cybersecurity, a great number of tools have been created to help automate phishing detection, including web browser extensions and toolbars [ 84 , 91 ] and email filtration systems [ 39 ]. None of these tools are able to totally prevent phishing and all still rely on the end user to intervene if a phishing attack gets through [ 43 , 102 ]. As helpful as anti-phishing scripts and other tools can be in live encounters with phishing, the warnings and nudges they provide do not amount to a robust anti-phishing education, as they are rarely designed with education as their primary goal or effect [ 32 ].

While both security experts and non-experts fall for phishing and spoofed websites [ 51 ], and both rely on the alignment of expectations and reality in deciding what to do in response to a message [ 102 ], the greater susceptibility of non-experts in falling for attacks may be related to their sources of anti-phishing knowledge [ 23 , 102 ]. For example, many non-security experts only begin to learn about cybersecurity threats after their employer or they, themselves, become victims [ 8 , 85 ]. Non-experts’ understanding of cybersecurity matters is the focus of Rick Wash, Emilee Rader, and their collaborators, whose research on the importance of informal and incidental learning for how users respond to phishing attempts has helped inspire our own approach to understanding what users know about phishing and how they have come to know it [ 80 , 81 , 99 , 101 , 103 ]. Throughout their work, these researchers explore these questions at least partly by asking users to explain how they think about cybersecurity threats, share stories about cybersecurity incidents, and, through experiments, show what is more effective as anti-phishing training: "facts-and-advice" by an expert versus "stories" from a peer. These researchers confirm that much of what non-security experts actually know about cybersecurity appears to come from sources outside of formal training or educational settings [ 21 , 73 , 83 , 106 ]. For example, storytelling among friends, family, and colleagues can help teach others about the consequences of computer fraud, viruses, hacking, and phishing [ 30 , 79 , 81 ]. However, because this information is often unvetted or is repeated and changed with every retelling, the so-called "common wisdom" or folk models among non-security experts can differ greatly from professionals’ understandings and recommended practice [ 99 ] or may emphasize parts of the story that are less helpful in educating about how phishing works more generally [ 80 ].

The exact sources of non-experts’ phishing knowledge, including that of employees, remains unclear. Exactly where did the information come from that informed these participants’ stories and mental models of how cybersecurity works? If, as Dourish et al argue, "we proceed from the observation that security is an issue that people already deal with in their electronic workplaces," and that "managing security is an everyday, practical problem ," then "effective solutions will not come solely from repairing the usability problems associated with existing technologies, because the very nature of those technologies—the ways in which they conceive of the problems of security—is a source of trouble....Effective security will require that we examine the conceptual models on which our systems are built" [ 24 , pp.22-23]. For us, this begins with our conceptual models of the users, and of the ways they conceive of and manage security as an everyday, practical problem.

The challenge is two-fold. First, few researchers have systematically asked study participants what they know about phishing, and what sources participants rely on for their knowledge. There is plenty of research within the domain of anti-phishing training, for instance, but it largely focuses on how participants respond in an experimental setting to an author's new training tool or program [e.g., 105 , 14 , 53 , 93 , 6 , 63 ] or how participants comparatively respond to multiple training tools or events, sometimes over time [e.g., 72 , 101 , 49 ].

Second, the participants in these studies often remain a mystery, and when their attributes are reported—in the most general terms—the findings appear to be based on the responses of university students. Few phishing researchers report the demographics of their research participants [ 20 ]. We can't know if there are meaningful variations between different kinds of users’ knowledge and sources of phishing information if researchers don't report demographic information about their study participants. This information is not only fundamental to the research process—or should be—but it is also vital if we wish to systematically tailor new, potentially more effective educational opportunities for different kinds of users.

In sum, the current literature does not tell us the importance of phishing training relative to other sources of an experimental subject's knowledge, and it does not tell us the importance of formal workplace training—much less a specific type of training—among all the sources of an employee's phishing knowledge. In this paper, we address this gap in current cybersecurity research, taking an explicitly feminist scholarly approach [ 22 , 35 , 86 , 96 ] to understanding cybersecurity threats and possible ways to lessen them. This is an approach typified by work that questions and finds value in the everyday, ordinary, and most taken-for-granted aspects of an area of inquiry, which often go unexplored—including the possibly informal, incidental, and hodgepodge-y ways that non-expert and under-studied social actors do something, like learn about phishing.

3 METHOD

Following the grounded theory-based method [ 15 ] used in the CHI and HCI communities [ 13 , 62 ] and as recommended by others in interview-based research [e.g., 9 , 10 , 61 ], we sought to identify and contextualize a wide range of phishing-related user opinions and behaviors. Our in-depth [ 67 , 89 ], semi-structured [ 34 , 58 ] interview questionnaire included a similarly broad range of questions inviting stories about participants’ experiences with phishing, including who participants go to talk to about phishing, participants’ perceptions of any anti-phishing training they may have received, and stories participants had relating to their experiences with phishing.

Given our interests, we decided to talk to the kinds of people we have interacted with every day in our own workplaces—university staff members. These employees seem to be constantly on their computers, constantly getting email from a list of correspondents both familiar and unknown, and regularly availing themselves of the same kinds of IT support as do faculty and students. They are good candidates, in other words, when seeking individuals with phishing experiences and who may not have any formal computer education. Indeed, university administrative staff may have been out the classroom for many years and entirely reliant on informal, incidental sources of computing information, including that related to cybersecurity. We also thought that, being in the education industry yet peripheral to the classroom, university staff members might be willing to talk with us on this topic for research purposes and with more readily-granted approval by their supervisors, particularly at a time when security by obscurity is still so prevalent [ 65 ].

Accordingly, over the course of November 2019 through April 2020, with approval from our university Institutional Review Board, we interviewed 24 participants about their experiences with online scams and suspicious messages and their knowledge about and ways of responding to these things. Although the majority of our interviews were conducted face-to-face in November and early December 2019, scheduling conflicts and local safety concerns due to the global COVID-19 pandemic led us to conduct the remainder of our interviews over video-conferencing software. The resulting 24 interviews ranged from 24 to 100 minutes long, with an average time of 57.4 minutes. All interviews were conducted in English with fluent English-speakers.

We initially recruited participants from the researchers’ social networks as well as with flyers and email announcements shared on and around the campuses of two public universities in the United States. Due to the significant gaps in phishing-related user studies demographics and reporting [ 20 ], we focused our recruitment invitations on individual office groups within the universities as a whole, thereby affording us the opportunity to hear about certain phishing experiences from different perspectives. Through this approach, we sought individuals and offices that allowed us to capture the behaviors and opinions of a wide range of ages [ 31 ] and highlight individuals who are part of the everyday workplace but not necessarily captured by many phishing studies, those individuals being mid-career women professionals. Following a snowball sampling approach, we asked participants at the conclusion of their interviews whether they would be able to recommend any other friends, colleagues, or family members for participation in the study. All potential interviewees were asked to affirm that they were 18 years of age or older and that they were interested in discussing with us their experiences with “unusual or unsafe” email, SMS messages, or other targeted computer-mediated messages.

Interviews were initially transcribed using the online, automated transcription service Temi. Using an iterative, open, and axial coding process [ 11 , 15 ], we used an initial set of 4 transcripts to identify emergent themes and their relatedness. From these codes, we developed a draft codebook and applied this codebook to all 24 interview transcripts using the MAXQDA qualitative analysis software. New and emergent themes were added as appropriate to the codebook until thematic saturation was reached, at which point no new themes were identified and all interviews were re-coded as necessary. All final coding was done by the same researcher on all transcripts with spot checks from co-authors to confirm that codes were applied according to their intended meaning.

4 PARTICIPANTS

In this section, we provide an overview of our participants, limiting our discussion to their demographic profiles as well as their computer use and expertise. Table 1 includes this summary information as well as sources of participants’ anti-phishing knowledge. Our participants are largely employees in positions of responsibility such as management and with access to higher levels of information and who are heavily dependent on computers for getting their work done. Their ages ranged from 26 to 67 (mean=43.92, s.d.=12.95), and all reported attaining a bachelor's degree or higher. Of our 24 participants, 11 said they also held a master's degree (n=7) or doctorate (n=4), a bias likely resulting from our focus on mid-career professionals working in university administrative offices. We consider three of our participants (P10, P11, and P12) to be security experts, as these are the only three who reported both formal training on phishing and computer-mediated scams and IT- or security-related careers.

The vast majority of our participants (n=20) identified as White or Caucasian with the exceptions of P05, P07, and P09, who identified as Black, and P10, who chose not to disclose their race or ethnicity. When asked their gender at the time of interview, 17 of our participants answered “female” and 7 “male.” 1

In response to the question, “About how many hours a day do you spend interacting with computers and smartphones?” (i.e., “Screen Hours” on Table 1 ), our participants reported spending most of their waking hours using computers and smartphones. Some, like P19, even suggested they had difficulty identifying a number because they spend “almost every hour” on their devices. Conservatively, our participants estimated that they spend a mean average of at least 11.61 hours (SD=4.15) on their computers and smartphones, encompassing use at home, work, and out in town when doing things like shopping for groceries.

Perhaps in part because our participants spend so much of their time on computing devices, many reported being sought out for computing assistance by others in their social networks. When asked, ‘How strongly would you agree or disagree with the statement, ‘Others often ask me for help with the computer,’—Strongly disagree, disagree, neutral, agree, or strongly agree?” (i.e., “Helps Others” column on Table 1 ), 15 responded positively, to varying degrees. Of those participants who answered positively, some have embraced being—in the words of P14—“the point person for people to ask” their computer questions, incorporating it into their self-identity:

P14: A lot of people [in the office] will send me an email saying, “Is this something? Is this a scam? Is this something we should even, you know, pay attention to?” And it's like, “Yeah, ignore this one.”

In fact, P14 is motivated to seek out anti-phishing tips online in part because others come to him for advice:

I do try to seek out some of the things, um, especially when it affects me personally from the work side. It's usually—you'll see those things come across. Or other people will surface it to me and it's like, okay, now we're seeing a rise of—I need to talk to our friends over in IT, find out what's going on from them.

When our participants were asked to respond to the statement, “How strongly would you agree or disagree with the statement, ‘I often ask others for help with the computer.’—Strongly disagree, disagree, neutral, agree, or strongly agree?” (i.e., “Seeks help” column on Table 1 ), answers were once again mixed: 5 participants strongly disagreed, 7 disagreed, 2 were neutral, and 10 responded positively. Although fewer than half of our 24 participants affirmed that they often seek computing advice, 9 of the participants who did emphasized in their interviews that conversations with those in their social networks were a key source of their anti-phishing knowledge, particularly conversations in the workplace (n=4), with friends (n=1), and spouses (n=1).

5 FINDINGS

We asked our participants about the sources of their phishing knowledge, focusing especially on participants’ answers to a subset of questions from our questionnaire. First we asked, “Have you ever had any kind of formal training or education about email scams or how to spot a fake email, or what to do if you get an email attachment from someone you don't know? Did you ever learn about this in school or at work, maybe?” Second, we also asked, “Sometimes people learn about these things less formally, too. They might listen to technology podcasts, or read about it somewhere, maybe in the paper or from a magazine. Friends and family and coworkers can be important teachers about computers and good practices, too. Have you learned anything about email scams from any of these sources?”

Definitions of formal learning vary [e.g., 59 , 87 , 90 ], but Eraut identifies five features commonly ascribed to formal workplace learning: the presence of an integrated learning framework, the training is packaged or otherwise organized as a specific event, an identified expert or trainer is present, the awarding of certification or other qualifications at the training's completion, and explicit specification of learning outcomes [ 27 ]. For the purposes of this paper, we join Rader et al. in using "formal" sources of phishing and cybersecurity knowledge to mean "classes and training seminars about computer security" [ 80 , 81 ]

Note that although we used the language of “formal training” in the first question and distinguished it from “less formal learning” in our second question, we were (and are) not interested in our participants’ classification of these sources. Instead, our goal was simply to elicit as complete a list of phishing knowledge sources as possible through a paired set of questions in which the follow up was intended to encourage recall of additional sources. We left it up to our participants to decide how to classify whichever sources of learning they shared with us, and what source they mentioned in response to which of these questions. Thus, we have combined and also presented summaries of participants’ answers to these two questions in Table 1 , under the column header, “anti-phishing information sources.”

A third of our participants (n=8) told us about formal training they had received in the workplace or school as vocational preparation. For non-expert participants, this took the form of workplace presentations and workshops. Our expert participants (n=3), however, described formal phishing learning in the form of coursework and academic reading, security industry conferences, and anti-phishing training certification programs. In addition, all of our participants mentioned learning about phishing outside of the workplace, often incidentally while going about their daily activities. Our participants mentioned learning from diverse media such as YouTube videos, podcasts, and social media as well as more technology-oriented news and entertainment sites. In the sections below, we explore these varied sources of phishing learning in greater detail.

5.1 Workplace Sources of Phishing Learning

During the course of our interviews, we were interested to learn what anti-phishing education our participants had received in the workplace or as part of their vocational learning. Only 3 of our 24 participants (P11, P12, P23) had ever taken a course on computer security, 2 of whom (P11, P12) were in computer security or IT-related careers at the time of interview. However, as P11 pointed out, phishing education is not necessarily included in computer security courses:

I studied security through [university name]. Um, you know, in terms of phishing training, we didn't really necessarily get any. And it's surprising...it's surprising given how common phishing is that security professionals aren't necessarily trained on how to spot phishing....I just don't think there is enough education about scamming...and how to report them.

All other structured anti-phishing or anti-scam education mentioned by our participants was carried out in fulfillment of our participants’ work responsibilities. Our expert participants (P10, P11, P12) highlighted several computer security data/consulting/training companies, including Cofense (formerly PhishMe), TechTarget, and FireEye, whose resources greatly informed what our participants know about phishing. They also highlighted the importance of regularly attending industry conferences to remain up-to-date.

Both P11 and P13 spoke positively about the anti-phishing training that they had received in the workplace, suggesting that it made them more effective at identifying and responding to phishing threats.

P11: It was like an escape room kind of scenario. You have to use all these different tools, whether it'd be emails, pick up on certain things that, you know, they're trying to train you on: the from address, the cert if it's signed, the person who claims to be from all these different things. They're clues in a larger game to determine, you know, who the attacker is. And you know, it's a fun game. It's gamification of phishing training.

P13: We actually have the IT people come in about once a year to our big staff meeting and they will go over that, you know, they will go over [things like] “Why does [the university] have you change your password every 90 days?” “Things that you should not put in your email when responding.” You know, I mean, they're really good and [the department] is really good about doing that as well.

Of our 5 non-expert participants who affirmed participation in anti-phishing presentations or workshops, 3 described the training as “beneficial,” “informative,” and “helpful.” P05 and P23, however, expressed frustrations with the repetitive content and unengaging formats of the formal anti-phishing education they had received.

P05: I feel like I have [received formal anti-phishing training], but I don't remember. I know I've seen videos on YouTube about this, but formal training? I can't remember.

P23: My employer provides—I can't remember if it's annual or every other year or what—a little PowerPoint thing I have to click through about some of this stuff.... Honestly, most of them [anti-phishing trainings] are pretty obvious. Kind of useless.... It feels a little bit like, “Oh, something bad happened. We need a training here. We'll put together a 15 minute training video and make everybody watch it.” Except once you've got like 40 of those, people kind of tune out.

On the whole, the workplace anti-phishing learning described by our expert participants appears to be very different from that of the majority of our non-expert participants. The anti-phishing educational material that non-expert participants received could be characterized, in the words of P08, as “tips more than training.” Like others we interviewed, P03 and P04 said they had never received official anti-phishing workplace training at all:

P03: No. Formal as it's a seminar that's even a half hour? No, just guidelines given to us by our [unit's] IT department and/or [the university].

P04: No, nothing formal. I mean, I think [the university] tries to educate us by sending out emails to say, “Hey, look for these things.”... [The IT department] sends out, um, once a month, a [computing newsletter] thing that kind of tries to educate people.

In addition to workshops, retreats, and other invited speakers mentioned by four of our participants, our conversants suggested they also learned about phishing in the workplace from newsletter columns and “guidelines” put out by IT departments (n=11), emails advertising these resources (n=2), and emails warning about specific phishing threats (n=5).

P14: It's more of, you know, “here's an email newsletter” and within it, “here's some tips.” “Here, this is what a phishing scam would look like.” Or you know, “It's a start of a semester, we're going to see a rise [in phishing attempts.]” That shows up around here.

In addition, participants described learning more about how phishing works from tools that may or may not have been designed with education in mind, such as a phishing reporting button in the Outlook email handler (n=1) and labels indicating an email was not sent from an enterprise email address (n=1). Similarly, P02, P03, and P04 described learning about phishing from “gotcha” phishing vulnerability assessment tests in which organizations intentionally send phishing emails to employees to assess how many people mistake these illegitimate emails for legitimate ones and click through.

P02: You know, I've fallen for a couple of those. And it's like, it looked like it came from [the university]—which it did. Looked like it came from [the university], and it was something I was interested in.... I clicked on that audit and then it tells me I've just been [scammed].... Not a good feeling for sure.

One participant (P06) even mentioned anti-phishing bus wraps (serving as a mobile billboard) advertising the university phishing reporting link as a source of (perhaps unintentional) learning, rounding out the variety of sources identified as related to the workplace.

5.2 Non-Workplace Sources of Phishing Learning

Given that the vast majority of our participants were unable to describe any formal anti-phishing education—within or outside of the workplace—we were especially interested to hear where else our participants learn about phishing. These are employees in positions of responsibility, often in management with access to higher levels of information for a broader social network than those who report to them, and heavily dependent on computers for getting their work done. If they are not learning about phishing from formal training, where are they learning about it?

We begin by noting that identifying specific educational resources proved challenging for some participants:

P23: I listened to the radio and I read the newspaper, and I'm sure I picked some of this stuff up in those places too. But I don't know that I can point to specific, you know, articles or, or anything.

Our non-expert participants emphasized that they generally do not seek out anti-phishing information, which may in part explain the difficulty of identifying specific learning sources. These participants frequently learn about phishing incidentally in fulfillment of their work or as something that they encounter more generally over the course of their everyday lives.

P15: It's more passive. It's more sort of an organic thing. Um, the podcasts I remembered listening to [earlier in the interview], it was a podcast I already listened to, and they just happened to have a segment on this. NPR, I just had it on in my car, so I'm not, not particularly seeking this out.

A small subset of our participants seek out media like technology podcasts and technology news/entertainment sites, places in which discussion of phishing may be more common. However, none of our non-expert participants, even those who expressed a clear interest in technology, mentioned any phishing- or scam-specific information sources.

Instead, many of our participants discussed the importance of conversations with friends and family as opportunities to learn about phishing. Even among our limited participant pool, they show how information—whether correct or not—spreads within a social network, and in this case, fills in a relative vacuum about what phishing is and what an individual should and should not do about it. P21, for instance, said they often consult their spouse on computer-related questions. In turn, their spouse consults their workplace's IT department when necessary—including people like our expert participants. Moreover, being around other individuals who are more aware of phishing and computer-mediated scams may help those like P01 and P03 to become more aware themselves:

P01: [After a story of a colleague who was successfully phished:] I thought, “God, that's never going to happen to me.” So I think, you know, if you know somebody that it has happened to, you're much more wary.

P03: I think it might be closer to the edge of my subconscious to be aware of these things than maybe someone who works in a nontechnical school environment. So I feel like I'm relatively more aware than the general public? Not because I'm a special snowflake, but because it is something important where I work.

In addition to learning about phishing from conversations with others in their social networks, our participants also relied on stories featured in the news. In fact, news stories about phishing—be they in radio, television, papers, online or other forms—were the most frequently cited (n=14) source of phishing information outside of the workplace. Several participants mentioned podcasts (n=6) and radio (n=4), specifically the Reply All and Throughline programs and National Public Radio (NPR) more generally. Finally, participants also discussed learning about phishing from social media such as Twitter (n=2), Reddit (n=1), and Facebook (n=1).

Though we cannot know the quality of anti-phishing information in these stories shared over these channels, most of the specifically technology-oriented news and media sites named by participants (Wired, CNET, Ars Technica, ZDNet, and Dark Reading) were mentioned by P10 and P11, who worked in IT or computing security. Of the non-expert participants, only P22 specifically mentioned reading the technology news publications Wired and Ars Technica, a habit they partially attributed to having friends and family working in technology fields.

6 DISCUSSION

Our participants expressed a desire to learn more about phishing so they would be faster and more effective at identifying and resolving phishing threats. Unfortunately, the information sources our participants say they rely on for anti-phishing learning—Twitter, colleagues, podcasts, and more—are likely to vary in the quality of information they convey. Recommendations from these sources may be out-of-date and no longer reflecting current practice, details from phishing stories may get twisted as stories filter through multiple non-expert retellings, and information may include specious explanations of what actually happened in a particular attack.

As part of their efforts to better educate computer users on the extant phishing threat, cybersecurity professionals and researchers might reconsider the delivery mechanisms they use to convey anti-phishing concepts to users by meeting their users where they are, wherever they are, providing the most current and accurate information possible via the channels through which users already seek anti-phishing guidance. A variety of approaches to learning about phishing may work very well with our participants, but if we at least add techniques that are already familiar to them, and around which they have already built habits, then we should make it even more likely that they will increase their knowledge and act in ways that better thwart these attacks.

In the sections below, we propose a two-pronged approach for learning about phishing, using both structured, formal training as well as incidental, informal learning opportunities (much like those already identified by our participants). Based on our findings, however, we believe this approach would not only be tailored to the existing practices and needs of our participants, but it is also more likely to embrace the full diversity of users found in a variety of workplaces. This approach would also allow cybersecurity professionals to seed a wide variety of learning opportunities with quality anti-phishing recommendations.

As part of this two-pronged approach, we look at education and cognitive science to better understand how cybersecurity professionals might better leverage learning opportunities. Lisa Weems proposes an approach to teaching called “guerilla pedagogy,” “a form of engagement that makes use of a wide range of strategies, tactics, and missives toward the aim of re-territorializing both the academy and what counts as knowledge production” [ 104 ]. As with “guerilla marketing,” this approach to teaching asks educators to reconsider the roles of performativity, surprise, and responsibility in order to create novel and impactful modes of instruction [ 104 ]. We suggest that would-be instructors looking to enhance anti-phishing education as well as their trainees might find inspiration and joy from approaching the task from this perspective, which includes three important guidelines.

First, when considering the roles performativity and performance play in education, instructors might reconsider who and what are useful teaching resources and which non-traditional as well as traditional learning spaces can be leveraged for anti-phishing education. As we have already heard from our participants, the majority of their knowledge about phishing was gained through incidental and distributed sources. Rather than seeking out this information specifically, our participants pick up small pieces of knowledge (often in the form of “tips”) over the course of their everyday lives. Distributing learning across various spaces and contexts creates new opportunities for exactly these kinds of bite-sized packets of anti-phishing education and awareness.

Second, unexpected sources of anti-phishing knowledge can be powerful pedagogical tools. Surprise refocuses attention and invites deeper reflection on the subject matter [ 104 ], which helps maintain interest in and retention of learning material [ 1 , 57 , 69 ]. The anti-phishing bus wraps mentioned by P06, for instance, cannot convey detailed or nuanced information about how to avoid phishing attacks, but they can serve as nudges to be more aware of phishing attempts the next time a user checks their email or social media. Much like guerilla marketing, these unexpected reminders to be on the lookout for phishing and what to do if you think you detect it help disrupt the desensitization to one's environment that comes with familiarity with an unchanging space.

But placing phishing education resources in unexpected locations is not enough. When considering responsibility in education, educators should remember that although they serve as advocates for a certain perspective, they must also create opportunities for the educational material to be interrogated and challenged. In traditional anti-phishing training modules or even informal learning from the newspaper or radio, how often are would-be anti-phishing learners afforded the opportunity to discuss with security professionals what they're being exposed to, perhaps by bringing up their own experiences? According to Weems, a guerilla pedagogical approach “dislodges the teacher's interpretive authority while re-positioning him or her as open to surprise, learning, collective responsibility, and transformation” [ 104 ]. Just like those attending their trainings, anti-phishing educators should design experiences that allow themselves to learn, too, to transform and become more aware of the concerns and responsibilities of the collective they serve. In whatever combination of formal and informal learning used to educate everyday, non-security experts on phishing, there should always be an opportunity for conversation so that learners can critically engage with the information presented and educators can better come to understand the multiplicity of user perspectives in the user-driven phenomenon that is phishing.

This novel, “guerilla” approach to learning encourages organizations to 1) distribute educational actors and resources so they might be found even outside traditional educational spaces 2), leverage the element of surprise in learning to disrupt desensitization and encourage more meaningful reflection, and 3) encourage dialogue so that concepts are fully understood within the context of learners’ lived experiences. With this in mind, we now turn to what this might mean for improving opportunities for people like our participants to learn more about phishing–and be better prepared to respond to it.

6.1 Redesigning Formal Training

Significant research attention has already been paid to improving formal anti-phishing training [ 3 , 32 , 46 ]. But formal training is not the only way by which we learn. Whereas formal learning is often characterized as being guided, structured, and repeated [ 50 , 60 , 98 ], informal learning is often described as being unplanned, integrated into existing routines, and without pre-defined outcomes [ 60 ]. Previous research suggests that people may learn about phishing in the course of their everyday lives in just this way, through personal experience with computer security threats [ 81 ], watching television [ 33 ], or holding conversations and storytelling with others [ 74 , 81 ]. Informal learning opportunities that replicate employees’ current ways of learning about phishing present promising—if neglected—ways for organizations to better achieve anti-phishing outcomes. These informal learning sources become especially significant for individuals who never encountered formal anti-phishing education over the course of their academic or professional careers.

Despite spending an average of at least 11 hours on computers and smartphones each day, only one of our non-expert participants (P23) had ever encountered computer security education while in school. Our participants were even less likely to have encountered anti-phishing education specifically. According to a 2021 report published by the Code.org Advocacy Coalition, 23 states in the United States will require high schools to offer computer science courses to students by the 2023-2025 school year; of these states, however, an average of only 58% of high schools currently offer computer science [ 41 ]. Of course, the proportion of students in these states who will choose to take such required course offerings, much less the content of these courses, remains to be seen. A “computer science” course at this level may mean anything from an introduction to Microsoft Office software packages to making a personal website with WordPress or even HTML. It may have nothing to do with computer security, how computers work, or basic computer literacy—including a variety of fraudulent online activities like phishing. And as P11 reminds us, even computing security students at the university level may not receive any education specifically in phishing. It is therefore up to workplaces to make up the difference if organizations want employees to receive any kind of formal training in avoiding cyber-attacks.

Such is the context for our non-expert participants’ unstructured and incidental processes of phishing learning that helped get them where they are today. They may not be in the habit of seeking out additional anti-phishing information, but they do integrate pieces of what they encounter into their sum phishing knowledge. Often, our participants would only seek out additional anti-phishing educational resources when they had a specific question about a specific threat. Unfortunately, self-guided learning is time-consuming and necessitates not only resources that are accessible and comprehensible as well but also the knowledge that such resources exist and where they can be found. Moreover, what people believe they know about phishing varies in accuracy as information becomes outdated—or was never very accurate to begin with [ 42 ]. Even anti-phishing training materials are rarely vetted for accuracy [ 46 ].

Based on our participants’ responses, we see several opportunities to make anti-phishing education more robust. Training those who have not been trained before, such as by requiring computer literacy courses that teach online safety at the K-8, secondary, and post-secondary levels, is an important first step. Fraudulent schemes constantly evolve, of course, so workplace training will probably always need updating and refreshing, too. However, we can go further. What our participants remembered about phishing, they often attributed to audio content such as podcast and radio and case studies and featured stories like those found in the news or in conversations with friends. Rather than presenting a slide deck for those like P23 to click through on their own, what might formal anti-phishing learning look like if it was intentionally designed to be more enjoyable, more like the media and conversations with which our everyday users already, voluntarily engage? As Alan Rogers reminds us, “All education is learning, but not all learning is education” [ 87 ].

6.2 Adding “Tips and Tricks”

For many of our participants, phishing knowledge is amassed over time as they unsystematically encounter tips and snippets of information over the course of their daily lives. Thinking like a guerilla marketer, how else can we embed tips and formal learning into users’ everyday environments in a way that is still impactful?

Below, we describe our recommendations for IT professionals and other anti-phishing educators in the workplace to 1) create opportunities for surprise by balancing phishing education in the predictable and the unexpected, 2) send nudges about phishing threats timed to coincide with likely attempts and time of need, 3) diversify the formats of anti-phishing learning resources to better mimic how computing users already learn about phishing, and 4) cultivate basic anti-phishing expertise among non-expert users so they can recognize and to seek out anti-phishing learning opportunities in their everyday environments.

  • Information distribution should be balanced between the surprising and the predictable. Bus wraps may disrupt the environment and grab attention, encouraging phishing awareness and curiosity about learning more. Or imagine, for instance, an art installation in a high traffic lobby with light projections representing the number of aggregated reports made to a university's phishing reporting link at any given moment with different colors representing reports made about emails originating from within and without the organization's email system. Or the IT department engaging in the simple act of attaching a printed ad with anti-phishing tips to any new or repaired technology delivered to employees. Of course, in anticipation of instances when someone has specific questions or concerns about a phishing threat, there also need to be familiar and reliable resources that educate and/or direct people where to go for useful information. Organizations might consider a dedicated anti-phishing informational webpage for employees to access or a banner on top of the email client page. For those who prefer conversations, an IT Phishing Czar might be a good solution as long as they are approachable, accessible, and known to all employees.
  • Nudges to avoid phishing attempts can be made more effective by timing reminders to coincide with likely attempts and points of need. This past holiday shopping season, the United States’ Cybersecurity & Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) and the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) published a warning to consumers regarding an increase in cybercrime, including phishing, around the Thanksgiving holiday [ 2 ]. Offices and schools can share similar warnings—perhaps even by newsletter or landing pages—to direct community members’ attention to the issue of phishing when threats are more likely. Our participants describe receiving similar notices when a specific phishing scam is affecting their workplace, yet they also say that they wished they were given information about what to do if they also received a query like this.
  • Formats of current anti-phishing educational materials can be diversified to better reflect the diversity of materials mentioned by our participants - and the diversity of our participants, themselves. Podcast listeners, for example, frequently listen to shows while engaging in other activities [ 76 ]. Organizations can look into putting out their own podcasts for employees or create playlists and media packets of news programs, podcast episodes, and YouTube videos to recommend to employees. These could even be played/displayed in office and lobby spaces. Similarly, rather than mimicking an instructor teaching from the lectern, IT staff looking to train units on phishing can shift to be more conversation-based through the adoption of workshops or guided “coffee hour” discussions of phishing case studies. Our participants seem to gravitate to stories, to cases, when they think about phishing, and seem eager to discuss them. Why not embrace that as a training model?
  • The frequency illusion (also known as the Baader–Meinhof phenomenon) describes a cognitive bias in which, shortly after learning new information about a particular topic or thing, individuals are more likely to see references to that thing in the course of their daily lives [ 19 ]. What if an experience described by the frequency illusion was the explicit goal of an anti-phishing educational campaign? The goal is to first sensitize users enough that they understand what phishing is, what its consequences can be for the organization and the individual, and provide some simple guidelines for how to respond to phishing attempts. The goal then becomes one of trying to ensure that users repeatedly touch on the matter, over time. Opportunities to recognize and revisit small bites of earlier knowledge and interwoven with opportunities to notice and add new nuggets of helpful information that build on the earlier ones. Rather than a goal of providing a single, block training and then testing to see if it lasts, the goal is more like sensitize, with baseline information, then incentivize, with the pleasure that comes from recognizing and accumulating even more expertise. As employees increasingly access their work from different locations and different devices (e.g., enterprise desktops, personal laptops, and smartphones), it becomes especially important for anti-phishing education strategies to not assume that an IT or security expert will always be on hand.

The combination of tips and tricks along with more structured, formal trainings that are designed to make users feel comfortable, conversational, and open to learning something new may go a long way toward better equipping individuals to meet today's phishing challenge. According to fraud researcher Martina Dove, fraud warnings often “concentrate on ‘dos’ and ‘don'ts” rather than the complexities underlying actual instances of fraud [ 25 ]. Instead, she writes, “we could look deeper and consider the psychology behind fraud, and make warnings more people friendly, more personal. People often want to know more; they want to know how to protect themselves and understand the intricacies that make them vulnerable to fraudulent attacks, but often this information is just not there” [ 25 ]. Or, if it is, it's not provided in a user-friendly kind of way. Our participants certainly want to know more. Deeper, more formal and sustained anti-phishing training in combination with cleverly delivered tips—“dos and don'ts”—could go a long way in helping achieve this goal.

It is equally important, however, to remember what training is not. Like formal learning, training is similarly focused on planned objectives and often relies on repetition in order for the information being taught to be recalled and applied. However, anti-phishing tips in office newsletters and penetration tests such as “gotcha”-style phishing vulnerability assessments are only transformed into training when an intentional and systematic effort is made to teach [ 60 , 94 ]. Even small changes, such as creating a schedule to send these tips and reminders on a semi-regular basis and a plan for where readers might be directed to more contextualized or nuanced content if they want to learn more, can transform the incidentally helpful into an intentional piece in a larger anti-phishing training plan. Similarly, although our participants cited “gotcha” assessment tests as informative , they were not found to be helpful . Individuals who “pass” these assessment tests by not clicking through may not have made the choice of whether to click intentionally, and often those who “pass” are never informed that a test took place to begin with. As a result, successes are unmarked and cannot be reinforced. Individuals who click through and “fail” are often given little more information than the fact that they failed this unexpected test, leaving them possibly frustrated and without any helpful lessons to take away.

7 LIMITATIONS

Because other user-centered phishing studies have failed to report the attributes of their participant pools [ 20 ], it is impossible to say how our participants’ knowledge and practices align with that of other kinds of users. We have found a great deal of overlap with user patterns reported in others’ research. However, even the few researchers in this area who have reported on their participants’ characteristics tend to have done so in fairly general terms, e.g., undergraduate "students across five different...sections" of an unnamed course [ 80 ]. It would require a sea change in the research community's practices for us to make any meaningful comparisons between our study participants’ views and others’.

Most importantly, the findings of semi-structured interviews with two dozen people who worked in university administration and were willing to talk with us would likely not include the full range of responses we would receive to our questions if we conducted a survey of many more people, informed by what we learned here. Ideally, our research community would benefit from a wide-scale survey that could reach a large number of people more systematically, such as a stratified survey examining users’ scope of knowledge and confidence, competency, and investment in organizations’ prescribed anti-phishing policies and approaches. We would be similarly interested in a study that queries our findings across different organizational settings. The more we hear from users, the better organizations can understand where flexibility is needed in anti-phishing approaches.

Based on additional small studies or larger, more generalizable survey results, future work might also explore the feasibility and efficacy of tailoring anti-phishing messages to different audiences. Where does a person normally get their information from and how do they like it? What channels do they prefer to access, in order to learn more, in a given way? We would be very interested in how experts and non-experts learn about phishing differently in these contexts, too.

8 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we discuss the importance of designing anti-phishing learning opportunities to be more distributed throughout everyday life in order to cultivate both anti-phishing awareness and competency. We report the planned and the incidental sources of anti-phishing learning identified by our middle-career office worker participant pool. Most of our participants do not intentionally seek out anti-phishing learning materials, but they nevertheless learn from the news websites, podcasts, videos, and people already in their lives.

We synthesized these findings into an anti-phishing education strategy that recognizes a need for both formal and informal learning opportunities, embracing three guiding principles of 1) distributing educational resources, 2) creating opportunities for surprise in learning, and 3) encouraging greater dialogue between instructors and learners. To that end, we make four specific recommendations for universities and other organizations looking to improve anti-phishing competency among non-expert users, including suggestions that would-be anti-phishing educators 1) balance phishing education in the predictable and the unexpected, 2) send timely nudges about phishing threats to coincide with likely attempts and time of need, 3) diversify the formats of anti-phishing learning resources to better mimic the informal learning sources non-expert computing users already consume, and 4) cultivate basic anti-phishing expertise among non-expert users so they may be primed to recognize and motivated to seek out anti-phishing learning opportunities in their everyday environments. Though neither the human nor the machine can identify phishing threats with perfect accuracy, stacking different imperfect solutions can still have a successful cumulative effect [ 102 ]. We believe our recommendations will help organizational phishing experts to choose educational opportunities that fit the needs of a wider range of users.
