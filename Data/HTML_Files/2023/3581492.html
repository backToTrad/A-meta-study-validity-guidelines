Personalised But Impersonal: Listeners' Experiences of Algorithmic Curation on Music Streaming Services

Martin Gibbs , Human Computer Interaction Group, The University of Melbourne, Australia, martin.gibbs@unimelb.edu.au

CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

The consumption of music is increasingly reliant on the personalisation, recommendation, and automated curation features of music streaming services. Using algorithm experience (AX) as a lens, we investigated the user experience of the algorithmic recommendation and automated curation features of several popular music streaming services. We conducted interviews and participant-observation with 15 daily users of music streaming services, followed by a design workshop. We found that despite the utility of increasingly algorithmic personalisation, listeners experienced these algorithmic and recommendation features as impersonal in determining their background listening, music discovery, and playlist curation. While listener desire for more control over recommendation settings is not new, we offer a number of novel insights about music listening to nuance this understanding, particularly through the notion of vibe .

1 INTRODUCTION

Offering access to vast catalogues of music, streaming services such as Spotify or Apple Music provide a platform experience for listeners, in which personalisation, curation and recommendation are foregrounded as crucial design elements [ 34 ]. Users of music streaming services increasingly rely on algorithmic personalisation and recommendation features to navigate these platforms, as well as to discover and curate music [ 70 ].These services have for many years been shaping listening behaviours and modes of music discovery, by fundamentally shifting the promotion, distribution, consumption and revenue models of the music industries [ 55 ]. Johansson et al. [ 29 ] posit that through this disruption, “streaming has altered listening modes and meanings of music in everyday life”. Given the primacy and power of such algorithmic systems in determining music recommendations and music listening, users are increasingly aware of their effects [ 62 ]. Music recommender systems as “socio-technical cultural intermediaries” command expertise and authority over listeners [ 70 ]. Despite their widespread adoption and utility, many issues such as transparency, control and bias [ 19 ] have been noted with recommender systems, making music recommender services an interesting object for investigation. Drawing on Alvarado & Waern's notion of algorithmic experience (AX), we approach recommendation and automated curation on music streaming services as ‘algorithms worthy of experience analysis’ [ 1 ]. Alvarado and Waern propose Algorithm Experience (AX) as an analytic tool for HCI to take a user centred approach to algorithmic systems, “making the interaction with and experience of algorithms explicit”[1]. Using Facebook as a case study, they undertook a semiotic analysis, and two design workshops to explore the ‘salient features for AX’ on social media. They offer five categories for improvement for AX: algorithmic profiling transparency, algorithmic profiling management, algorithmic user control, selective algorithmic memory and algorithm awareness. Though they focused on the social media context, a number of these aspects of AX are relevant to music streaming, in particular algorithmic user control and transparency which will be explored in our discussion. Given its potential as an analytic tool to elucidate the user perspective on algorithmic systems, in this study we use AX as a lens to explore the music streaming context.

Music holds a powerful place in many people's daily lives [ 16 ]. It is used for emotion regulation [ 72 ], and has powerful affective qualities associated with nostalgia & memory [ 51 ]. Listening to music is a source of fun, as well as a means of expressing one's identity [ 7 ]. So, within this context, music streaming platforms attempt to create a holistic listening experience for their users, albeit a commercial one [ 18 ]. Subscription music platforms like Spotify or Apple Music offer their customers a suite of personalisation and recommendation features, as well as discovery features like Discover Weekly (Spotify) or For You (Apple Music), that come together in a commodified or branded listening experience. Many algorithmic features encourage users to engage with music within certain contexts, or for particular activities. Spotify has a number of contextual offerings such as “At the Gym”, emotion or mood-based playlists (“ Happy Pop ” or “ Mood Booster ”), playlists for discovery of new music ( Release Radar, Discover Weekly ), and playlists to create a background soundtrack ( Daily Mix ). Streaming services offer a major shift away from personal digital collections, to subscription-based, on-demand music listening [ 15 ]. Rather than building their own digital or analogue collections, on streaming services listeners rent access to seemingly unlimited catalogues of music and become increasingly reliant on automated curation and recommendation features to help them discover and listen to music personalised for them.

Liikkanen & Åman's earlier research on music streaming proposed two interaction paradigms [ 42 ]. The first was the Curated paradigm of listening, in which playlists were not created by a listener, but rather programmed algorithmically or by someone else. The second paradigm was the On-Demand paradigm whereby listeners ‘actively program the playlist for listening’. Now, 10 years later, there have been many changes within contemporary music streaming services, with increased personalisation, and automated curation reshaping the experience of music listening [ 54 ]. For instance, in 2016 Spotify launched Discover Weekly , a highly-personalised algorithmically compiled playlist for discovery; and as of 2021 Spotify refers to algotorial playlists [ 63 ], which are blurred creations of human and algorithm [ 67 ]. The increasing influence of algorithmic recommendation features and editorial curation on music streaming platforms allows for what is characterised as a ‘lean-back’ experience [ 60 ] whereby music is ‘seamlessly’ personalised and tailored to individual listeners. In this study, we build on HCI research on music recommendation, and in particular the work of Liikkanen & Åman, and their of notion of music interaction paradigms as being shaped by music technologies [ 42 ]. This paper contributes to existing research on music interaction, by examining the Algorithm Experience (AX) of commercial music streaming platforms and the emerging modes of interaction they encourage and enable. Exploring the user experience of recommendation and personalisation in the context of contemporary music streaming services, our research updates and extends their work on music listening paradigms by investigating the nuances of three related but distinct modes of listening: background, discovery , and curation on commercial music streaming sites. These normative modes of listening are encouraged and enabled through the features of these platforms. We argue that although there is a trend towards lean-back listening experiences, this does not fully encapsulate the experience of contemporary music streaming. We found that some users are still engaged in more active lean-forward activities on music streaming service in efforts to improve the personalisation of their listening.

Our research also builds on the work of Lukoff et al. [ 44 ] who explored user agency and how different features of YouTube's recommender caused users to feel less in control, especially when they had a specific intention for their viewing session. In the contexts of music interaction, we examined how algorithmically-led interactions influences listeners’ experience of autonomy, control and agency. We employed algorithmic experience (AX) as a lens by investigating the user experience of recommendation and automated curation features on commercial music streaming services. Drawing on two stages of qualitative research, we explore how participants use or rely on personalised, curated content and features and unpack how different modes of interaction operate across a spectrum of algorithmically-led and user-led behaviours. Our findings reveal that despite the perceived utility of increasing algorithmic personalisation on music streaming services, this was often experienced as deeply impersonal by the participants in our research. Workshop participants expressed a desire for more control and customisation of algorithmic settings and transparency, as they wished to understand and operationalise algorithmic personalisation as a more fluid, context, and intention dependent process. Though user control is not in itself a novel observation, our findings highlight nuances to the music streaming context through the qualities of personalisation sought in such user control. We offer original insights into the user experience of impersonalisation operating within commercial systems designed and branded as personalised . In turn, we offer novel user-centred insights about ways to reshape algorithmic recommendation to support greater personalisation, particularly by incorporating notions of vibe in listening experiences to meet user desires for more control and transparency around algorithmic settings on music streaming services.

2 RELATED WORK

Our research concerns the experience of music recommender systems in music commercial streaming platforms. We will next unpack related work in the areas of music recommendation, everyday listening, music interactions and algorithm experience.

2.1 Music Recommendation

There is a wealth of research into user-modelling and personalisation for music recommendation, as well as improving music recommendation techniques and music information retrieval (MIR). This includes research into various factors such as personality traits [ 20 ]; personas [ 37 ]; user context and mood [ 2 ]; preferences for explanation [ 47 ] or for exploration [ 40 ]; even user levels of musical sophistication [ 21 ]. Scholarship on music recommendation often seeks to increase the accuracy of recommendations, such as through intent or session-aware recommendations [ 71 ], as well as improving models of user preferences for recommendation. Other studies look to specific aspects of music interactions with recommenders, such as how recommenders can assist with playlisting [ 31 ]. Another focus within music interaction research has been the social aspects of music such as sharing or collaborative playlisting [ 56 ], and music creation [ 28 ].

This research has tended to exclude the algorithmic contexts of music recommendation or platform experiences of listeners on current commercial music streaming services like Spotify and Apple Music [ 65 , 69 ]. Notable exceptions within HCI literature include Wadley et al. [ 69 ] who explored mood regulation by students using streaming services, and Liikkanen [ 41 ] who conducted a comparative study between YouTube and Spotify by looking at streaming rates, popularity of hit songs, and temporal factors in listening. Many studies have presented novel interfaces for music interaction or listening, such as Mood Play [ 3 ], Taste Weights [ 8 ], Auralist [ 73 ], which explore alternate ways of interacting with music recommendation. Knees, Shedl & Goto [ 34 ] present a useful overview of intelligent music interfaces over the last two decades, highlighting the prevalence of automated discovery and personalisation in contemporary music streaming (see also [ 15 ]). Thus, whilst music recommendation research has focused on user preferences across factors such as context or mood, there is a need for more concentrated attention on listeners’ platform experiences within the algorithmically-dominated environments of a limited number of commercial music streaming services.

2.2 Everyday listening and user interaction studies

Everyday music listening is a well-researched multi-disciplinary area. A lot of attention has been given to understanding the role of music through exploring individual's goals and intentions for listening [ 60 ]. Scholarship into listening modes come from a variety of fields such as Music Psychology and Musicology, HCI and Music Information Retrieval (MIR). For instance, research into listening modes within the field of music psychology often explores music in the context of meaning-making, attention, and perception or embodied cognition [ 65 ]. Likewise, previous user experience research has looked into music listening modes, for example, sequential vs shuffle listening on private collection via iPods, as well as the experience of serendipity [ 39 ]. Though there is research into device choice and selection [ 35 ], there is much less focus on the impacts of recommender systems within commercial streaming platforms on everyday listening. Music interaction paradigms [ 42 ] are highly dynamic, and shift alongside changes in playback technologies [ 15 ].

Within the broader focus on music interactions, user studies have explored people's music listening preferences and decision-making. Studies have analysed why people listen to music, such as listeners’ preferences for active or passive engagement with music, depending on their session or intention [ 28 ]. Others have recognised this dynamism for intention, acknowledging use-level idiosyncracies [ 46 ], and changing interaction signals, depending on user intent or goals [ 24 ]. Similarly Passino et al. [ 58 ] used Spotify as a case study, to explore how user preferences for recommendation and personalisation change over time. We build upon this work by also including a focus on discovery – along with enjoyment and engagement with the music itself – as key factors in the experience of listening on music streaming services. Looking to how people use and interact with music, in a qualitative study Hagen [ 26 ] explored playlisting behaviours on music streaming services. Others have looked at listener relationships between users and music recommenders on commercial streaming services, finding that listeners in that study often had imagined conversations with the recommender system [ 22 ]. Lee & Price [ 37 ] proposed a number of different user personas for interacting with music information retrieval systems. Personas included the ‘Active Curator’, ‘Music epicurean’, ‘Guided listener’, ‘Music recluse’, ‘Wanderer’, ‘Addict’, and ‘Non-believer’. These personas were later validated with a larger sample by [ 23 ]. We refer to a number of these personas in relation to our findings. Kamalzadeh [ 30 ] surveyed 590 listeners on their desires for music interaction and control. Focusing on music listened to during attention activities, they discussed different preferences for interaction across various user types, but overall found a general desire for increased control, with a small amount of interaction. Notably, this survey data is from 2012, which is relatively early in commercial music streaming history.

Our research builds on previous research that has identified several music interaction paradigms (and interfaces) for content organisation on music streaming platforms that has, for example, compared the user experiences of shuffle versus seqential listening [ 39 ]. However, music streaming platforms and the algorithmic organisation of content on these commercial services has evolved markedly since this previous research was conducted. Our work updates this prior research through our focus on contemporary streaming platforms and the influence of now dominant commercial algorithmic recommendation and curation features on listener's user experience. Our own use of the term music interaction modes is distinct, as we look at the music listening experience in the context of contemporary music streaming services and their algorithmic recommender systems. Our research extends work on music interactions, recommendation, and everyday listening experiences by exploring the complexities of the user experience of algorithmic recommendations on major commercial music streaming platforms. We further develop music interaction paradigms [ 42 ] engendered by music streaming services by identifying a tension between personalisation and impersonalisation in the design of algorithmically-led modes of discovery, curation and background listening . Further, our work nuances research on the role that recommendation and automated curation plays in the user experience by introducing the novel but significant quality of vibe in music listener experience.

3 METHOD

This research aimed to analyse the use of music recommender systems in individual music seeking behaviours, and to gain further insight into the user experience of recommendation and automated curation on commercial music streaming services. Focused on the everyday use of music recommender systems and online music streaming platforms, this research asked two questions: RQ 1: How do participants use music streaming services and experience their personalisation and recommendation features? And RQ 2: How do recommendation and automated curation features affect listening behaviours on music streaming services?

The opacity of ‘black box’ commercial environments such as on music streaming sites, presents challenges to research [ 6 ]. As such, this study employed a user-centric, qualitative method as this allowed for a detailed, focused in-depth analysis into the human elements [ 27 ] of participants’ experiences. Like Park & Kaneshiro [ 52 ] an exploratory study method was chosen to gather more ‘in-depth stories’ and background knowledge and experiences of participants. We used a participatory and exploratory workshop design similar to Wirfs-Brock et al. [ 72 ], who worked with users’ own Spotify data to explore and imagine the future of voice assistants and music streaming, though our workshop focused on user desires and needs for the role of music recommenders.

3.1 Stage 1: Semi-structured interviews & participant observation

Semi-structured interviews were chosen as they allow for participants to discuss and narrate their experiences, attitudes, and habits on music streaming platforms [ 36 ]. By conducting individual interviews, we were able to focus on users of online music streaming platform that have lived through their development and rise in popularity. Interviews were conducted with 15 participants between the ages of 18-36, which is in line with the most common sample size at CHI identified by Caine [ 12 ] and with Guest et al. [ 25 ] recommendations for data saturation. This project had ethics approval from The University of Melbourne (1954459). Participants, who were daily users of a music streaming platform, were recruited through online channels and on campus flyers. Spotify was the dominant music streaming service among participants (12 participants used Spotify daily, and 3 used Apple Music daily). It's important to note that many participants used more than one music service, but our focus was on the platform used most frequently. All participants provided informed consent, and participants received a small supermarket voucher as compensation for their time. The interview questions focused on music recommendation and discovery across platforms, prompting interviewees to share their experiences of interacting with music recommender systems. The interviews were 45-60 minute in duration, and designed to extract rich, empirical qualitative data [ 71 ]. While participants were avid music listeners, they were not expert or power users of the technology. Knowledge and expertise of the streaming service varied greatly among participants, with some not knowing about the existence of certain features at all.

Additionally, drawing from Robards & Lincoln's [ 57 ] scroll-back elicitation method, participants were invited to show and share their curated and personalised playlists on the streaming platform interface on their own devices. For example, they showed their Discover Weekly , a personalised, algorithmically curated playlist on Spotify, and while scrolling they discussed their reactions to the curated offerings. This allowed for participants “to fill in blanks and provide context [and] the deeper meanings” of their profiles and experiences on the app [ 57 ].The interview and elicitation data were analysed thematically using the 6 phases of reflexive thematic analysis proposed by Braun & Clarke [ 9 ]. The interviews and observations demonstrated a large number of activities and behaviours. These were coded and arranged into themes iteratively [ 64 ]. Results from this initial stage of research are presented in section 4.

3.2 Stage 2: Workshop Design Activities

Building from the findings from the first stage of research, we invited participants to return to explore these themes further in a design workshop. By inviting users into the design process, we were able to further explore their desires, motivations and potential future needs for music recommendation and curation. We ran the workshop in our User Experience (UX) lab, after an initial internal pilot with colleagues through which we refined the activities. Participants were seated around a large table, in pairs. Each pair had in front of them large sheets of paper, coloured markers, pens and post-Its for brainstorming and documenting their ideas. Other workshop materials are discussed in detail below, and in Figure 1 , 2 , 3 , and 4 . We ran one design workshop, with 6 participants from the first stage of research.

The workshop consisted of one short warm-up activity, and three design activities. The design workshop activities were each designed to pick up the findings from Stage 1, that there were three dominant listening modes encouraged by commercial streaming services (Figure 1 ) with listening interactions within these modes operating on a spectrum of algorithmically-led to user-led. Extending the work of Freeman et al. [ 22 ] who found that users were imagining personas and dialogues for the music recommender systems they interacted with, we used the workshop to investigate whether music recommenders might have their own personalities, as well as how users might have a conversation with them.

The three listening modes were written up as scenarios and printed on cards to be used as prompts throughout the workshop. These scenarios were described by participants in the initial study and can be seen in Figure 1 . An overview of the workshop activities can be seen in Table 1 .

Workshop participants were introduced to the themes from the earlier research, as well as the design activities and goals, emphasising their responses were not restricted to current technologies and commercial systems, but to use their imagination as this was exploratory research. The workshop began with a quick, warm-up activity which invited participants to open their most used music streaming service and spend 3 minutes taking note of the recommendation and personalisation they encountered. Participants reported back to the group. This activity provided a quick reminder of the current state of music streaming and its personalisation and recommendation features. At this stage some participants said that some of their favourite features or playlists were “hidden” down the home screen, and they wished they had more control over what features they saw upon opening the app. During the activities, the researcher was quietly present, answering questions and prompting occasionally with “how would this work?” etc.

The first design activity was a Persona Template activity . Participants were given a persona template (see Figure 2 , also available in supplementary material), and in pairs invited to fill out the template according to their imagined ideal music recommender. In this design activity, we approached the use of personas from a novel perspective, the imagined persona of the recommender system rather than the user. We aimed to explore how users wanted to imagine and relate to the music recommender system, by asking them to describe its persona. Participants then reported back, sharing their design with the other groups. Other groups asked questions, and there was a lively discussion among participants about each of the persona designs.

The next activity was a card-based dialogue mapping ( See Figure 3 ). This was adapted from Wirfs-Brock et al. [ 72 ], who explored the use of personal data & voice assistants in music recommendation. In this activity participants mapped out an interaction with their imagined recommender, using the dominant listening modes as prompts. After filling out and arranging the cards, each group reported back and presented their designs. This was chosen as way to explore the finding from Freeman et al. [ 22 ] that listeners on music streaming sites were having imagined conversations with the music recommender system. We aimed to explore how these dialogues would be designed across the three listening modes using the scenarios as prompts.

In the final design activity, Wireframes (see Figure 4 ) were used to design how these elements would come together on an interface. Again, participants reported back and shared designs.

At the end of the workshop, participants were led in a short discussion about their designs and the workshop. All workshop materials can be found in the supplementary material. The workshop was video recorded, and audio was transcribed. We conducted a thematic analysis of the transcribed discussion [ 64 ], as well as analysis of the design artefacts. Discussion of the workshop findings can be found in section 4.2. All participants are referred to by pseudonym.

4 FINDINGS

4.1 Interview findings – Listening Modes

During interviews, participants discussed distinct modes of use on their chosen music streaming platform. These modes were related to their intention for a listening session, as well as being driven by the features they interacted with. For example, users might be seeking music to listen to in the background of an activity, or they might be seeking to actively discover new music. The focus in this study was on individual listening (though sometimes others were present), as such these modes take place around directed, normative, daily listening rather than listening or hearing music in public spaces (for more on social listening see [ 52 ]). Our definition (see Table 2 . below) identifies three normative modes of interaction with a music streaming service: background mode, curation mode and discovery mode. User reliance on, and satisfaction with recommendation and automated curation varied across these modes of interaction. Participants discussed the existence of both user-led and algorithmically-led behaviours (as discussed by [ 44 ]). In the algorithmically-led (hereafter algo-led) modes, participants described interacting with, relying on, or deferring to algorithmically powered features such as recommendation and automated curation in their listening sessions. This mode is associated with the trend towards lean-back listening experiences. On the other hand, user-led behaviours were controlled, initiated, or directed by the user themselves, which is associated with more “lean-in” types of listening [ 10 ] or what Liikkanen & Åman [ 42 ] called “on-demand” listening. The main modes of interaction on music streaming service described by participants are as follows.

Background mode was very common among participants. In this mode music was often used as an ambient soundtrack to accompany activities like work, study, cooking, or to provide a background to match or create a mood, sound or beat appropriate to an activity such as exercising or dining. As curation is a ‘fuzzy concept’ and is done differently by various actors in different spaces [ 28 ], we define curation here as the act of listeners putting together, arranging music, or playlisting generally for a specific or intended purpose. This purpose could be for a mood (or ‘vibe’ as participants called it), for a future context or activity, for sharing, for play (fun), for a profession (radio, music industry, for workplace listening). Discovery mode meant actively seeking out new music, or sometimes re-discovering content that participants had forgotten about. This could be through active search, or use of automated discovery features such as Discover Weekly (Spotify) or For You (Apple Music ) , maybe starting with inspiration from their life around them or a seed song or “jump off point” as Johnny described it. Participants described how they readily switched between these modes, so for example, a background session could become a discovery session through a serendipitous encounter with a song, artist or genre. Likewise, though some activities were more clearly either algo or user-led, others demonstrated a hybrid of the two. For instance, some participants would make discoveries from outside of their music platform, such as from a TV show or a friend, and then would using the music platform as a tool for further discovery via algorithmic or automated features. Similarly, Roy said he liked to create a ‘flow’ like the algo-led background listening playlists he used at work, so when walking he liked to be curating at the time of listening (rather than curating for a later date). Many of the features on streaming platforms operated as modes of interaction and listening, which demonstrates the significance of platform features within contemporary commercial streaming sites for reinforcing, driving, or catalysing new behaviours.

Across these three main listening modes on music streaming services, we found that they operate on a spectrum of algorithmically-led and user-led interactions. Algo-led personalisation for some users allowed them to “lean-back” [ 60 ] whereby, by providing a (mostly) relevant experience. However, for others the endless, and often repetitive ‘personalisation’ was “annoying”, as it served them content they had listened to many times before and did not aid in their efforts to curate or discover music. It was common among participants to feel like recommended content ‘missed the mark’ and didn't seem tailored to the participants. Thus, many participants perceived some of the personalisation features to be impersonal. Overall, participants expressed desired for more control and feedback across the listening modes. Within these prototypical high-level categories of music interaction on streaming services, a number of compelling themes arose, explored in the next section.

4.2 ‘Vibe’

The notion of vibe was a consistent and novel theme that was generated from the interviews, and later the workshop. Vibe was mentioned by almost all of the participants across the two stages of research. Vibe was used as a verb, as in “ I'm vibing with something ” (Nina), meaning that they were enjoying or ‘feeling’ the suggested music, or was also used “ I don't vibe with them ” (Maria), as in 'I don't get along with that person’. It was also used to encapsulate the participants’ intention and musical, aesthetic choices for a playlist they were curating as in, “ I put in 3 songs to set up the vibe of the playlist ” (Lewis), then they would use automated playlist continuation (APC) features to recommend similar content for that vibe . Similarly, Hiroshi said they regularly used the radio feature while at work, choosing a particular artist to get algorithmically-led content that they deemed to be in line with the vibe they were trying to cultivate (or match). Hiroshi explained, “ you can put on an LCD Soundsystem Radio at work and be pretty safe and not have your vibe interrupted ”.

Vibe also expressed the context of the listener (i.e., the vibe in a room, or time of day), or their mood as in “ what vibe I was feeling at the time of day ” (Emily). Vibe was useful for participants when articulating their reactions to suggested or recommended music that was close but didn't quite “fit” with their intention or knowledge of music. When evaluating recommended content, Eric said that the offerings were from the same genre but did not have the same vibe. Similarly, Roy said: “That checks out." But it's like, just missing the mark. It's like a B-grade version of what I want to hear ”. Though perceived as 'accurate’ in terms of similarity, these suggestions were experienced as impersonal as they were not the right vibe .

‘Vibey’ was also used to mean that songs or playlists were perceived as good or used to describe an ideal characteristic in a recommender. Vibe captures musical or aesthetic qualities, as well as the mood, context, and intention of the listener. For instance, “my vibe” could mean the more immediate contextual or mood of the listener, but this also sat within broader discussions of the participant's vibe in general (i.e., their music tastes and preferences). Notions of vibe also extended to social interactions. In both the interviews and workshop, it was expressed that it was essential to “vibe with someone” [Liz] in order to trust their music recommendations. This manifested in increasing trust through explanation, as well as the persona of a recommender system.

4.3 Mnemonics

We use mnemonics here to mean pertaining to or eliciting memories, such as subjective or affective associations of music with personal memories. Re-discoveries of ‘forgotten’ music were particularly interesting, as they could evoke for participants the same level of excitement or satisfaction as new discoveries, but for different reasons. Some participants mentioned being glad to have found or been recommended a song again simply for its musical qualities, as well as for it being ‘vibey’. For others, the intrinsic link between music and memory was the cause of this affect or feeling, with appreciation for the mnemonic association music prompted. For example, Will described the associations with particular live concerts they had attended that evoked memories when they were recommended that artist or song, “ Remembering that [live] experience, that always becomes tagged to the listening experience because I remember exactly where we were at that time, listening to those songs” (Will) .

Contradictions arose here, as some relished the opportunity to remember the times they associated with the music, while others described this as “forced nostalgia” (Jerome). Maria said that their Spotify ‘Throwback’ playlist had offered them songs that reminded them of a time when they were going through a breakup, which was jarring for them. This demonstrates the strong link for participants between certain life phases, memories, and algo-led music recommendation.

Despite the proliferation of algorithmically generated ‘throwback’ playlists such as Repeat Rewind or Spotify Wrapped , listeners still had a desire to archive music themselves, which often mapped onto specific time-periods or life phases and moments, such as breakups, live concerts, overseas trips and so on. Archival work was also done by many participants as an effort to save songs from being ‘lost’ or forgotten. Given the high volume of content that listeners interact with and are suggested, it was a common fear among participants that they would forget songs even after they were discovered. Many had an ‘archive’ or ‘dump’ playlist that they would return to, to keep discoveries safe, while others used the Liked songs feature. They would return and move the songs to “an appropriate playlist after” (Lewis). Ari described this as ‘telephone book’ for future reference. Many participants mentioned that often after a re/discovery, they would listen to that song on repeat, which was referred to as ‘rinsing’ a song. Maria mentioned that they would listen to the song all week, and then not want to hear it again for months or years This was a source of frustration when these songs were recommended to them in this time or phase. Overall, in the interviews, several themes emerged that offered insights into the nuances of music listening, such as algo-led user-led listening modes, vibe and mnemonics. These themes were further explored in the design workshops.

4.4 Workshop findings: Sliders and dials

All three final design artefacts featured sliders or dials heavily. Though these sliders or dials represented different things for each group, the sliders were a clear mechanism to increase customization and control over each participant's listening experience and recommended content. Across the groups, dials or sliders were designed for various parameters such as vibe, musical attributes, preferences for recommendations, saveable profiles based on activity or mood, as well to choose the level of interaction they wished to have with the recommender system.

4.4.1 Slider & dials for musical vibe. Some sliders and dials were related to musical attributes, such as tempo, era, music label, producers and so on. For some groups, sliders related to more arcane or deeper musical attributes, while for others they indicated musical vibe. For instance, some participants expressed a desire to search for and see connections between songs that goes beyond genre, era, or artists, using more vibe related attributes such as intensity, BPM or tempo, producer, ghost-writers or song writers, particular instruments, samples, country of origin, or music label. For example, Eric expressed a frustration that he couldn't search for individual instruments, such as the Roland TR-808 drum machine: “ If I can put ‘Euonymus celebrates Christmas’ into an AI image generator, then why can't I type 808 drum machine into a music app?” Eric, Group 2.

4.4.2 Customising profiles for different moods or modes. Other sliders and dials related the user's own taste or ‘ own vibe’ . All designs featured some version of a sliding scale or dial to choose between a preference for obscure and familiar content. This was dependent on their mood, or situation, or vibe, and allowed for the participants to “set the tone” or direct the recommender towards the kind of content they were looking for. This was an extension of them of being in a ‘mood to discover’. Liz suggested a scale of how diverse the content was (from the familiar to the unfamiliar), using an investment portfolio as a metaphor: “I've kind of got like an investment portfolio, like the risk how much you are willing to listen to new weird stuff, maybe it's really high or maybe it's really low” Liz, Group 1.

4.4.3 Customising the recommender. Customisation of the appearance and personality of the recommender, as well as an ability to change how often recommendations were offered was a common element among the designs. Most groups chose an avatar for their recommender system, though one group chose “a hologram of Dolly Parton” on a portable holographic disc. One group used dials to customise the personality of their avatar for a distinct session. Hiroshi explained: “I like the idea of a recommender having multiple personalities. A personality mode that matches to listening modes – you can set up your recommender to do that. One end is give me what I like, and the other end is throw something new at me” Hirsohi, Group 3.

Like the use of sliders and dials for their own moods, participants also incorporated saveable profiles and personalities for their ideal recommender system into their designs. One group designed their ideal recommender with the option to change the personality to suit their mood or context: “We said that we wanted it to be customisable personality wise and looks wise, so if you had it set to bubbly happy Dolly Parton, if I'd just got dumped, I wouldn't want to listen to bubbly happy Dolly at that time ” Eric, Group 2. There was a discussion around choosing the personality and avatar creating more trust – i.e., ‘an avatar like me’. Participants suggested that having control over their recommender's avatar or ‘persona’ would allow them to vibe with it more, and thus they would trust and accept the recommendations more than they currently do.

1.1 4.5 Workshop findings: Relating to and interacting with the recommender system,

Across the 3 activities, groups discussed their desire for feedback to the recommender system.

4.5.1 Choosing level of interaction. In discussions, and in the designs, participants wanted to include options to give feedback in various forms. For instance, Group 3 developed a screen that would pop-up after a song skip. They suggested that after a skip happened, users would be shown the tags associated with the skipped song, and they can choose which tags to remove or include. Participants agreed that they wanted to opt-in and out of this feedback, depending on their mood, or intention for the session. Sometimes they would want to interact and give feedback (while in discovery mode), and other times (background listening) that they would not want to be. Liz explained:

“I like the idea of setting the tone before a session – so like I'm just here to vibe to sad music. I feel like sometimes I'm in a mood and I'm only here to listen to new music and find new artists, or here to make a playlist like these listening modes – like setting the tone before you start. And maybe if you're in discovery mode, that's when a little person comes up and recommends you stuff” , Liz Group 1

Some designs also incorporated a mechanism to turn recommendations off, or to mute the recommender.

4.5.2 Trust and transparency. As well as giving feedback, participants also wanted more transparency from their recommender system, as they asked, “why these recommendations?”. For example, Daniel said:

“We thought the most important characteristics was trustworthiness as well as transparency around how decisions are being made, because there is so much going on under the hood with apps like this... it's like how or who decided that this was good for me? Was it someone who had a commercial interest in peddling something to me, because if that is the case then I don't really trust”? Daniel, Group 2

There was a discussion around conversational recommendations, which some participants related to in person or human recommendations that they trusted or ‘vibed with’, such as ‘the person at a record store’, or trusted curator or DJ, or a friend who knows them: ‘make it more exciting than ‘here's a thing you'll like’, be quirky or weird!” Johnny, Group 1

Overall, the designs spoke to a desire for more control, and more customisation over the musical content or vibes, the recommender itself, interaction styles and level of feedback.

1 5 DISCUSSION

The following discussion brings together findings from the two stages of research into three overarching themes, before unpacking how this paper contributes to understanding music interactions on music streaming.

5.1 Tension between personalisation and user control over customisation

Through the interviews, participant observation and design workshops a theme was generated around tensions between personalisation and control over customisation. Though control over recommendation is not a new observation, there are nuances to the music streaming context which are worthy of analysis. For users, personalisation was a fluid, and dynamic process, dependent on intention, listening mode, mood, and context, and vibe . We saw that interactions on music streaming services operated across a spectrum of algorithmically-led, and user-led activities. For many participants, personalisation could become impersonal with little control or feedback with the system. Inaccurate recommendations, curation that ‘missed the mark’, especially in relation to vibe or mnemonic association added to this sense of being impersonal. As such participants’ designs demonstrated a desire for more control and customisation in some listening modes, while also expressing desires for more exploration and diversity in others.

Using algorithm experience (AX) as a lens, we sought to explore the user experience of commercial music streaming services, paying particular attention to participants’ reliance on and interaction with algorithmic recommendation and automated curation features. Alvarado & Waern's notion of algorithmic experience is presented as a useful “analytic tool” for HCI to explore the user perspective [ 1 ]. Using Facebook as a case study, their framework relates to social media, but there are areas of overlap to the context of music recommender systems and music streaming. These similarities include user profiling into musical taste profiles, recommended and personalised content, and platform experiences. The framework category most relevant to our study was that of algorithmic user control . Participants desired the ability to limit or turn off the influence of algorithms (through turning off or muting the recommender), as well as adjusting parameters for search, discovery and recommended content through sliders and dials that facilitated more personalised vibes or mnemonic music listening experiences. They articulated a need to provide negative feedback for faulty prediction, but also focused on explicit feedback for positive experiences. Notably, participants desired to be able to control the presentation of the recommender itself, through personas, avatars, and adjustable interaction settings.

The desire for more user control in the music streaming context was expressed in two key ways: control over the musical content itself (musical attributes, styles and so on), and the influence of the recommender (level of interaction, interaction mode). These preferences for user control were then also dependent on the situated mood, context, or activity [ 71 ]. Participants designed customisable, saveable profiles for themselves based on their chosen listening mode, context, mood or activity so they didn't have to do this for each listening session, thus decreasing their effort. In this way, users could choose their level of interaction between ‘active’ with lots of customisation and control, or more ‘passive’ and ‘lean back’ interactions [ 64 ]. This echoes Kamelzdeh et al. [ 30 ] who found that surveyed listeners desired more control over recommendations, and yet they often wanted minimal interactivity. In our design workshop, participants addressed this by using dials or explicit feedback to “set the tone” before a session and included options to turn off or mute recommendations. Sliders and dials were used by participants to allow for explicit feedback and for customisation of their experience with music recommendation, automated curation and music streaming. The designs of the dials were in many ways an homage to earlier, analogue musical technologies, such as turning physical dials on amplifiers. Exploring the dynamics between user-led and algo-led modes, we found that participants desired more feedback for improving algo-led modes, and more control for user-led modes of interaction. The desire for more control was dependent on the participants’ moods and intentions. So, while there were some examples that confirm the trend towards ‘lean-back’ experiences on music streaming services, such as background listening, or algorithmically-led modes, there were also examples of distinctly user-led behaviours and practices, as well as hybrid work across both. Though characterised as lean-back, this in some ways may be the push from the platforms themselves but not necessarily what users are always doing while listening on them.

5.2 Transparent feedback and the significance of vibes and mnemonics in music recommendations

Other salient aspects of the AX for music streaming were transparent feedback and diversity. Though these issues are similar to other recommendation contexts, the music streaming context is unique in several ways, and it is important to pay attention to these differences. Participants were seeking more explanation about the content served to them, asking questions like “why did you give me these recommendations?”. They also sought more transparency around which playlists are editorial (made by a human curator) or algorithmic, as well as explanation around how those decisions were made [ 67 ]. Participants not only wanted to understand how recommendations were made in order to adjust their settings, or change their behaviours to influence future recommendations, but also discussed how this increasing transparency would increase trust. These insights build on the growing field of explainable AI (XAI), [ 17 ], as well as work on user agency [ 44 ], and trust in algorithmic systems [ 33 ]. Another means to create trust was through the ‘persona’ or avatar for participants’ ideal recommenders. Participants emphasised the importance of trust by saying they would only take recommendations from people or recommenders that they ‘vibe with’. This was expressed through creating avatars “like themselves”, or similar people in their own circle that they would trust. Participants wanted an avatar or persona they could relate to, which they felt would make the recommendations more trustworthy. This speaks to cultural capital and role of music recommenders as potential tastemakers [ 48 ] and gatekeepers [74]. Understanding the importance of trust, persona, and cultural capital as specific to the music streaming context presents opportunities for the design of recommender systems. Diversity of content and of listening was particularly important to participants. They discussed the importance of not getting stuck in algorithmic feedback loops and addressed this in their designs through sliders or dials for adjusting for familiarity or diversity of content. Overall participants desired for more participation in processes of personalisation on music streaming services, even in more lean-back modes of interaction, as they found the current state of recommendation and personalisation to often be impersonal.

The use of the term vibe was an elegant encapsulation of user preferences for music and interaction modes, as well as the language used to articulate their satisfaction with the recommended content. Vibe presents a more intimate way to approach research on music interaction, as it reveals insights into how users think about and experience music. Vibe demonstrates how mood, affect, and memory are crucial, related dynamics within music interaction and can affect the potential success of a suggestion or recommendation. Vibe is part of the vernacular, especially on social media [ 32 ], and is even employed as an aesthetic by Spotify in their branded playlist “ Chill Vibes ”, which, given hybrid curation of editorial playlists [ 5 ], most likely responds to users own use of the term in naming their own playlists. However, to our knowledge the notion of vibe has not been the subject of scholarship in the music interaction context. We suggest that the notion of vibe is a salient aspect of the AX of music streaming services. Though a vague term, vibe may account for some of the tensions between personalisation and control outlined above. For instance, recommended content was perceived as ‘accurate’ in terms of similarity but was not deemed to be the ‘same vibe’ by participants. Depending on ‘their vibe’ participants might have contradictory desires to explore more diverse content, or to have more control over personalisation or their listening experience. Though vibe presents challenges to recommender system design, this concept opens up avenues for further research into music interaction, and into understanding user satisfaction with music recommendation.

Other important idiosyncrasies of music listening were the mnemonic or affective memory associations to music. The links between music, affect and memory have been investigated in music psychology and musicology fields, providing useful insights [ 4 , 67 ]. However, here our focus was on the memories evoked by recommended content. The strong connection between songs, and life phases and memories were a source of contradiction. For some participants, increasing personalisation meant that reminders of previous listening habits and life phases was jarring, like a ‘forced nostalgia’. Personalisation here was experienced as ‘jarring’ because it evoked a personal memory. For others, this personalisation allowed them to recover lost music, which was especially useful given their high volume of music on streaming platforms [ 18 ]. Nuances such as this add to our understanding to the idiosyncrasies of music listening and AX of music streaming.

5.3 Implications of user-led research for algorithmic recommendation on commercial music streaming platforms

Music streaming platform features have the power to encourage or constrain certain listening behaviours, and modes of discovery and consumption [74]. In this study, we discussed three normative modes of listening on streaming platforms: background, discovery, curation. Understanding how commercial recommender systems encourage or engender new behaviours and interactions with music compliments the wealth of research that has come before. As Krause et al. [ 35 ], note there are many variables to music preferences, and this study builds on this by exploring the attitudes towards, and effects of automated curation, discovery and personalisation in the context of commercial music recommender systems. Participant preferences for the different interaction modes echoed Lee & Price's [ 37 ] work on personas in music listening and recommendation, as listeners often moved between ‘archivist’, ‘guided listener’, ‘active curator’ personas and so on. This study confirms earlier research that listeners use music for emotional regulation [ 69 ] and that music recommender preferences are contextually and mood dependent [ 2 ].

This study also responds to the call from Schedl et al. [ 61 ] to explore the complexities of music seeking behaviours with a more holistic approach, and to focus on platform experiences and platform effects. Studying platform experiences comes with many challenges [ 11 ], but is important to do so as this is how most users are listening to music, and the features of these platforms do have an effect on their use [ 69 ]. Liikkanen & Åman argued that a music interaction paradigm is shaped by the interface designs and features available to listeners. Since much has changed in the music technology space since this 2015 study, namely the proliferation of automated curation, recommendation and personalisation on streaming services [ 13 , 57 ], and the blurred lines between editorial and algorithmic curation and personalisation, we have explored music interactions that are enabled and encouraged by contemporary music streaming services. We chose a user-centred approach to explore the needs, desires, frustrations, perception, and experiences of listeners on commercial music streaming sites. This study also reveals novel data about the user experience of personalisation on music streaming sites which was often experienced as impersonal. This empirical study contributes to knowledge of music interaction paradigms by focusing on listening behaviours on commercial music streaming services, and the impacts of automated personalisation and curation features. Given the power and effects of commercial streaming platforms, as well as acknowledged issues associated with recommender systems such as control, transparency, fairness, bias [ 19 ] it is crucial to consider the impacts on users and how interactions with these services may influence music seeking behaviours, and everyday listening. The algorithmic curation and personalisation features on music streaming services are certainly powerful tools for listening and discovery, though we argue that a focus on AX could empower users through incorporating more transparent feedback, explanation, user control and customisation over personalisation in response to user desire for music experiences organised by vibe or mnemonic association with the past.

6 LIMITATIONS & FUTURE WORK

This research employed qualitative methods to gather rich data about users’ intentions, and use of music streaming platforms. However, given the small number of participants, future research could look to broader samples, as well as more task-based observation to further elaborate and build on the research reported here. As the focus of this study was on daily users this could have an effect on their preferences for interface, for control, and for how they interact with and rely on algorithmic features. This group of participants were avid music listeners, however it is important to make clear that they were not expert or ‘power’ users of the technology. In fact, many participants were not aware of entire features or how to navigate the system. This is an important distinction, and again opens areas for further discussion and analysis in future work around ‘expertise’ in the music streaming context. This work opens up questions and areas worthy of further analysis, such as the perspectives of designers of music recommender systems and interfaces or a comparison of other music streaming platforms. Given the complexities of music interactions on music streaming sites, the boundaries of these categorisations of interaction modes are fuzzy. Other studies should look to validate and expand these categories of interaction modes on music streaming platforms, as well as the notion of vibe. Looking at how factors such as age, musical interest, or whether users are regular or casual listeners, may affect preferences for interaction modes would also be valuable avenues for future research.

7 CONCLUSION

This study aimed to understand the way listeners interact with and use contemporary commercial music streaming services, by focusing on their interactions with algorithmic recommendation and automated curation features. Across the interviews, participant observation and design workshops, we found that despite its utility in some sessions, participants often found increasing algorithmic personalisation to be fundamentally impersonal. We found that participant's reported listening behaviours could be seen to be operating on a spectrum of algorithmically led, or user-led interactions, rather than simply being categorised as lean-back experiences. This is an important delineation to explore in the context of music streaming platforms or platform experiences, as it is crucial to understand the influence of algorithmic recommender systems in order to more fully understand the user experience of music recommendation. Understanding preferences for algorithmically-led or user-led recommendation, or a hybrid of the two, provides opportunities not only to understand this experience, but provides opportunities to enhance it. In order to support good user experience and satisfaction of music recommendation, it is critical to understand the complex dynamic and shifting music interactions between users and recommender systems on commercial music streaming services. This paper contributes to understanding the user experience of recommendation on music streaming services by offering original insights into experience of ‘impersonalisation’ on commerical services increasingly labelled as ‘personalised’, as well as user-centred insights about ways to reshape algorithmic recommendation to support greater personalisation for listeners. The mnemonic role of music listening as well as notion of vibe is especially compelling, and though it raises more questions than it answers at this stage, we suggest that attending to the experience of vibe could present new and worthwhile challenges to designers of music streaming and recommender systems.
