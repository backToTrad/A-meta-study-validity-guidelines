“Should I Follow the Human, or Follow the Robot?” — Robots in Power Can Have More Influence Than Humans on Decision-Making

Yoyo Tsung-Yu Hou , Information Science, Cornell University, United States, th588@cornell.edu
Wen-Ying Lee , Mechanical Engineering, Cornell University, United States, wl593@cornell.edu
Malte Jung , Information Science, Cornell University, United States, mfj28@cornell.edu

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581066
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Artificially intelligent (AI) agents such as robots are increasingly delegated power in work settings, yet it remains unclear how power functions in interactions with both humans and robots, especially when they directly compete for influence. Here we present an experiment where every participant was matched with one human and one robot to perform decision-making tasks. By manipulating who has power, we created three conditions: human as leader, robot as leader, and a no-power-difference control. The results showed that the participants were significantly more influenced by the leader, regardless of whether the leader was a human or a robot. However, they generally held a more positive attitude toward the human than the robot, although they considered whichever was in power as more competent. This study illustrates the importance of power for future Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) and Human-AI Interaction (HAI) research, as it addresses pressing concerns of society about AI-powered intelligent agents.

1 INTRODUCTION

Artificially intelligent (AI) agents such as robots are increasingly delegated power. Uber algorithms directly make decisions on the fare for the ride [ 5 ], while some others act as decision support systems to aid human decision-making, such as resume screening systems that suggest which applicants have more potential [ 7 ]. During the pandemic, physical robots were also delegated power to ask people to wear masks and some even worked as airport security guards [ 10 , 51 ]. This trend has led to a surge of new research interest in the concept of power, often broadly defined as the capacity to influence others and so omnipresent and fundamental in human social interaction, and its derivatives, such as authority and compliance, in Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) and Human-AI Interaction (HAI). For example, Agrawal and Williams [ 1 ] studied whether people comply with an authoritative robot, Saunderson and Nejat [ 42 ] investigated whether authority makes a robot more persuasive, and Haring et al. [ 18 ] compared humans and robots to reveal the effects of human-likeness on compliance.

These studies, however, mostly focused on the interactions between a single agent and a single person, overlooking the fact that power is most relevant in social structures that have more than two members. Many fundamental mechanisms of power only exist in such larger structures or groups and not in dyads [ 38 , 45 ]. Examples include coalition formation (forming subgroup in a group to gain power) and relational demography (if some people has demographic characteristics like the majority of group members, they are likely to feel and possess more power than the minority). As a result, when researchers study power, it is crucial to go beyond dyads. It is more so for studying power in HRI, because the default variable of research interest in this field, agent type (human vs. robot), will inevitably come into play in these fundamental mechanisms of power.

As intelligent agents are increasingly interwoven in human-robot mixed work settings with multiple members [ 50 ], it is becoming likely that a human member has to weigh a robot's power against that of another human. Yet it remains an open question how the power relationships in such mixed contexts affect people's behavior, especially when humans and robots compete directly for influence. We therefore would like to initiate an endeavor into this topic with a “minimum viable structure” of such mixed contexts: a group of three, where a person is facing two competing influences from a robot vs. another human.

In the current study, we investigated how power affected interaction in such a human-robot mixed group through an online experiment. We matched each participant with one robot teammate and one human teammate to form a three-member team to perform a set of decision-making tasks. We then manipulated power following French and Raven's Bases of Power framework [ 12 ], giving the powerful the title as the leader (to gain legitimate power, the power given by social structures) and the right to allocate monetary bonus (to gain reward power, the power to control desirable resources). By controlling who had these two kinds of power in the team, we created three conditions: the robot teammate as the leader (r-lead), the human teammate as the leader (h-lead), and a control condition with no power difference (control), see Fig. 1. Across the conditions, however, all “teammates” were actually computer-generated responses and were identical except for their labels. This allowed us to study people's behavior in a minimum human-robot mixed context that was well-controlled and required only one participant at a time.

By measuring how a participant's decision changes after seeing their teammates’ answers, we were able to quantify the effects of power on decision-making. We found that people were more influenced by their leaders than their peers, even if the leader was a robot and the other team member was a human. Power also influenced whether the robot or the human would be perceived as more competent, even though the human teammate was perceived as more intelligent, warmer, and causing less discomfort.

There are three main contributions of this study. First, we found that power is one deciding factor that influences people's behavior in human-robot mixed contexts. People can be more influenced by the powerful, regardless of whether it is a human or an intelligent agent. Second, we created a single-participant experiment paradigm that affords a direct comparison between the influence of humans vs. robots in a human-robot mixed context. Third, more generally, we hope to call the attention of our community to the importance of power in HRI and HAI, as power is at the core of many concerns of society on AI-powered intelligent agents.

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 What is power?

Power is arguably the most fundamental element in social interaction. It decides whom we interact with and how we interact, as well as how our behavior shapes the behavior of each other, often in an asymmetric way. In almost all social structures [ 9 ], power dictates how the social hierarchy looks like and who controls the resource, is held responsible, and has the final say in critical decision-making processes. In other words, power defines the structure and context of the social world we are in. At personal levels, power also affects how people think and behave, including their cognition processes, self-perception, social perception, resistance, performance and behavior, motivation and evaluation, and physiological effects [ 14 ]. It is so omnipresent that Bertrand Russell has even stated that “The fundamental concept in social science is Power in the same sense in which Energy is the fundamental concept in physics.” [ 41 ]

Though embedded deeply in almost every aspect of social behavior, power has long been known as a concept that is hard to define [ 34 , 44 ]. In the history of social science, its definition has evolved into several forms, and some of them even seem unrelated to others. In the current study, we will adopt one common view in social psychology to define power as potential influence or the capacity to influence [ 13 , 54 ]. We should notice from the “capacity” in this definition that power can exist even if there is no perceivable influence. This aligns with our daily experience that some powerful people have the capability to influence others when they want to, but they do not always do so. Power also has some special characteristics that makes it different from other common variables. For example, as Cartwright stated, “If O has the capability of inଂuencing P, we say that O has power over P” [ 3 ]. We should note from the O and P here that power does not exist in a single person. Instead, it is contingent on the relationship between two or more parties. In this kind of relationships, those with more power are usually referred to as the high-power, and the others as the low-power [ 13 ].

This definition, though better capturing our sense of what power is, is hard to apply operationally. Capacity is a concept that is hard to manipulate directly, and we don't know how people can gain it or lose it. As a result, it remains crucial for us to develop a theory on sources or bases of power. In one of the most cited papers on power to date, French and Raven [ 12 ] suggested that there are ଁve basic bases of power: Reward power, Coercive power, Legitimate power, Expert power, and Referent power. People gain Reward power by controlling how much reward others can get, and they gain Coercive power when they can punish others who do not obey. Legitimate power is the authority and legitimacy given by social norms or social structures, such as the hierarchy in an organization. Referent power, closely related to personal charisma, is held by people whose personality and personal traits attract admiration and identiଁcation from others. Expert power is a result of being more knowledgeable and knowing what a better action is to take.

This theory of different power bases provides us with a systemic way to operationally manipulate power, as well as to analyze behavior through the lens of power. Following this framework, our current study will use two of these power bases (legitimate power and reward power) to manipulate an agent's power, and then we will measure the influence caused by the agent as a measurement of its power. We chose these two types of power because they are less entangled with agent type. While legitimate power and reward power can be manipulated more independently of agent type, the expert power and referent power might confound more with whether an agent is a robot or a human — for example, people might find a human naturally more charismatic than a robot (or vice versa in some contexts). While these interactions are interesting, we decided to keep it simple and chose manipulations that can be cleaner for the current study.

2.2 Power in HRI and HAI

2.2.1 Power. Despite occupying such a critical position in social interaction, power is less discussed in social interaction between human and artificial agents, including robots and AI agents. Many CHI studies conducted in-depth investigations in topics such as algorithmic management [ 31 ], which was highly related to power, but most of them did not directly address the role power played in the interaction. Still, some HRI studies have noticed the importance of power. Ju [ 29 ] stated that power is deeply embedded in the definition of robots. The word “robot” comes from the word “robata” in Czech, which means “slave”, hinting that it is given a low-power position from the start. She also discussed how people are afraid of losing control of robots, how this becomes more pressing as technology develops, and how, informed by her own research, people usually do not prefer robots that exhibit power explicitly, such as being dominant [ 32 ] or proactive [ 37 ]. She concluded that social structure, social class, and power in human-human interaction would also affect human-robot interaction. This view, built on the ground of the paradigm of Computers Are Social Actors (CASA) [ 39 ] that people applied social norms naturally in their interaction with computerized systems, inspired our current study.

A series of studies by Hashemian and her colleagues [ 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 ] are also among the few HRI works that addressed power directly. In these studies, they also followed the framework of French and Raven's bases of power and manipulated power according to the power bases (in particular, reward power, coercive power, and expert power). They then measured the robot's persuasiveness as the effect of power (influence). Their results generally indicated that robots could be persuasive when having power from these power bases [ 24 ], and these bases also influence how people perceived the robots [ 24 , 25 ]. However, it is still not clear whether some power bases are more effective than the other bases [ 21 , 25 ]. Our current study followed the same structure as their studies: we manipulated power according to the bases of power framework, and then we measured how people were influenced as the measurement for power.

2.2.2 Authority. Closely related to power, authority is the concept that has been studied more in human-agent interaction. It is so closely related to power that sometimes authority is simply referred to as “accepted power” in sociology [ 17 ]. In social psychology, it is usually considered a type of legitimate power [ 48 ]. When people have authority, others often choose to follow them because they are perceived to have socially approved legitimacy. What distinguishes authority from power is that, for power, the capacity to influence can sometimes result from sheer force, such as punishment, while for authority, the follower's volition is emphasized. Authority makes people follow because they are internally motivated to, while power is the broader term, which includes both voluntary and involuntary influences. As a result, we use power as the core concept in the current study, as it is broader and more thoroughly studied in human-human interaction such as fields of social psychology and organizational behavior.

In terms of authority studies in HRI and HAI, Young and his colleagues have created a series of works on robots as authoritative figures and people's obedience [ 6 , 15 , 16 , 52 , 53 ]. In their studies, they followed the Milgram paradigm, manipulating a robot's power by assigning it as the experimenter. Instead of asking the participants to execute electric shock, which Milgram did [ 36 ] and is considered unethical now, the robots in the study asked the participants to do boring and repetitive tasks and asked them to continue when they stopped or protested. The results showed that, although the obedience to a robot experimenter was lower compared to a human experimenter, there were still 46% of participants who did the task until the end after the robot requested. Young and his colleagues suggested that robots might take over simple management tasks in the future [ 53 ], and we should be aware of the danger that robots can be used to manipulate people [ 52 ]. Along this line, Haring et al. [ 18 ], and Agrawal and Williams [ 1 ] have both studied robots in authoritative roles such as security guard or coach, and whether factors such as human-like appearance and aggressive behavior affected people's compliance.

With a research paradigm similar to that of Hashemian and her colleagues, Saunderson and Nejat [ 42 ] investigated the effects of formal authority (roughly corresponding to legitimate power in French and Raven's framework) and real authority (roughly corresponding to reward power and coercive power) on a robot's persuasiveness. They found out that, contrary to previous knowledge in human-human interactions and in Hashemian and her colleagues’ studies, people were surprisingly less influenced by robots in an authoritative role. They also observed a generally negative attitude toward an authoritative persuading robot. This study interested us with its contrasting results with previous human-human research, which called for a direct comparison between human-human and human-robot interactions.

Sembroski et al. [ 43 ] also investigated the effect of a human experimenter's authority on the participant's compliance in their study, where the experimenter and an assistant robot gave different instructions. They found out that participants usually obeyed to the human, but they followed the robot instead when the robot was considered an in-group member and the human had low authority. Like our current study, the human and the robot in this study were competing for influence. However, they only manipulated the authority of the human, and the power relationship between humans and robots remain ambiguous.

These studies on authority highlighted that power has long been studied in the fields of HRI and HAI, although they were not under the name of power. As previously mentioned, authority is commonly viewed as a specific subset of power, closely related to legitimate power and sometimes even referred to as “accepted power”. In the current study, we decided to use the overarching term, power, as the core concept instead of authority to be more inclusive and better connected to the existing literature of other academic fields we referenced. We recognized that these two terms can be complementary or even interchangeable in many contexts, and we viewed the current study as part of the unremitting endeavor to understand both power and authority.

In general, these studies on power and authority highlight the importance of power in human social interaction. While power is thoroughly studied in human-human interaction, its study is in a nascent phase in the field of HRI and HAI with many mixed results. It remains unclear whether power enables robots to cast more influence, whether this influence by a robot differs from the influence by a human in a mixed context, and whether power shapes how people perceive humans vs. robots differently in such contexts. In a world where robots are increasingly placed in positions traditionally held by human workers and are working alongside people, these questions are pressing, and we try to address these in the current study.

3 CURRENT STUDY

Informed by the aforementioned studies, we would like to investigate the effect of power on people's perception and behavior in situations where they interact with humans and robots simultaneously. Specifically, we would like to test the following hypotheses and research questions.

First, the paradigm of Computers Are Social Actors (CASA) [ 39 ] implies that people often interact with computerized systems as if they are social actors and therefore apply social norms and exhibit social behaviors toward these systems. As a result, we assume that power will work similarly in HRI as in Human-Human Interaction. In addition, most studies in HRI have shown that power or authority also made a robot more influential [ 20 , 42 ], which aligns with the definition of power. We therefore think it will likely be the same in human-robot mixed contexts.

  • In human-robot mixed contexts, the high-power agent will be more influential than the low-power agent.

Second, as agent type (human vs. robot) is inevitably a variable of interest in human-robot mixed contexts, we would like to compare the influence of humans vs. robots in such contexts. Since many previous HRI studies have shown that people prefer humans over robots in such mixed contexts [ 11 , 43 ], we believe it will still hold true in the current study.

  • In human-robot mixed contexts, the human will be more influential than the robot.

Third, given the hypotheses of H1 and H2, we would like to ask: which one has a larger effect? Since every agent in such contexts will simultaneously have its type (human vs. robot) and a level of power (higher vs. lower), these two forces will work together. When these forces compete, it is crucial to know which one is more critical because influence can only be one way or another. Given the fact that power is often the deciding factor in most social structures, we speculate that power might have a stronger effect. However, this question has not been investigated before, and there are no theories on the relationship between agent type and power to support our speculation. We therefore formulated this as a research question.

  • When comparing the effects of power and agent type, which one will be larger?

Lastly, while the above are all group-level questions, we would also like to ask whether power will change the interaction at the individual level, i.e., between one person and another human vs. between one person and another robot. Previous studies have investigated how power affected relationships between a single person and a single agent. However, they did not do so in a multi-agent mixed context, so direct comparison between different levels of power and different agent types was not possible. We therefore would like to ask:

  • How does power change people's perceptions and attitudes toward humans vs. robots in human-robot mixed contexts? Specifically,
  • (1) How do the perceptions and attitudes differ between high-power and low-power agents?
  • (2) How do the perceptions and attitudes of robots differ from those of humans?

Because the “perceptions and attitudes” in RQ2 are quite broad, we limited our scope by using only three of the most common concepts and surveys in HRI, which included perceived intelligence, perceived competence, and The Robotic Social Attributes Scale (RoSAS). [ 2 ]

To address these hypotheses and research questions, we designed and conducted an empirical online experiment.

4 METHODS

4.1 Participants

After approval by the Cornell University Institutional Review Board (IRB) with Protocol ID 1808008209, we recruited 150 participants on Amazon's Mechanical Turk (72 males, 77 females, 1 prefer not to say) with an average age of 36.99 ( SD = 10.47). The three criteria were: Previous HIT approval rate > 99%; Location is in the USA; Have not done any of our previous studies with similar methodology. We decided on the number of participants following the rule of thumb of 25 per condition [ 27 ], and we timed the number by two to accommodate the high percentage of unusable data that is common on Mturk. All participants were paid $ 7.50, including the base value $ 5.50 plus a $ 2.00 bonus payment, which was for power manipulation and will be explained in the following section.

4.2 Design

Our study leveraged a three-condition between-subject study. In all conditions, every participant was paired with two teammates, one human and one robot, to form a three-member team to work on a series of decision-making tasks. To manipulate power in such teams, we manipulate who (or none) had power in the team, creating three conditions.

In the two experimental conditions, one of the two teammates was chosen to be the team leader, forming two conditions: the human as the leader (h-lead), and the robot as the leader (r-lead). The leader's power was manipulated by two sources of French and Raven's power framework: legitimate power and reward power. The participants were informed that the leaders were chosen as the team leaders, they decided the final answer for the teams, and they also decided how to split the bonus payment after the game.

The third condition was a control condition (control), where no power-related information was provided, and thus there were no power differences. The participants worked equally with the other two teammates as peers to complete the tasks, and they were informed that the team's final answer would be based on aggregating all the team members’ answers. They were also told in advance that they would decide on bonus allocation together.

This study design therefore can be analyzed at two levels: at the group level, we had three between-subject conditions of different power configurations that enabled us to see whether people were more influenced toward the human vs. robot, or toward the high-power vs. the low-power. At the individual level, we can analyze a participant's reaction to two teammates with two underlying variables: agent type (robot or human) and power (high or low).

Note that in all conditions, both the human and the robot “teammates” were in fact computer-generated responses, and despite the labels, their behavior was identical. However, this was not revealed to the participants until the debriefing session.

4.3 Materials and Procedure

The whole experiment and data collection happened on the Qualtrics system. Participants who agreed to take part in this study were guided from Amazon's Mechanical Turk to our experiment on Qualtrics, where they role-played as a member of a consulting team. They had to collaborate with teammates to make ten suggestions to their “clients”, and then they answered questions about the whole process.

The experiment contained three parts:

4.3.1 Consent, Instruction, and Teaming. Participants consented to participate in this study and read through the instructions. They learned that for the main task, they had to role-play to join a consulting team with two other teammates. They were also told that they had the chance to win a bonus payment base on their team performance: the more their team answer was like professional consultants’ answer, the more they could win. Then they were matched with two teammates, one human and one robot. A delay of several seconds was intentionally created in the matching process to make participants believe that they were being matched with real participants online.

For h-lead and r-lead, the participants were also informed about who (the robot or the human) was selected as the leader, as well as the team leader's responsibility (deciding the team answer and the bonus allocation), and thus were exposed to the manipulation of power. To prevent possible biases, we did not let the participants know how the leader was chosen. If we said it was according to personal abilities such as leadership, the manipulation might confound with another type of power, expert power. On the other hand, if we said it was done randomly, it might nullify the effect of legitimate power. Therefore, we decided not to disclose any information, while it was in fact done randomly.

4.3.2 Main Task: Decision-making Teamwork. In this part, the participants worked with their teammates to provide suggestions to their clients’ questions. There were ten questions in total. For each of them, participants had to suggest an optimal ratio between two options. These questions were created by our research team for this study. We first created 12 questions and then selected the best ten from a pilot test with the standard that these questions must seem to have a correct answer while no one really knew it.

For example, participants were asked to provide an answer for an orange juice brand:

  • Your client, which is a famous beverage brand, is coming out with a new kind of orange juice which is lower in calories but still tastes great. They want to promote the new product but do not know where to show their ads. How would you suggest splitting the budget between these two? (A) Through the internet. (B) Through in-store promotions.

Every participant then went through 2 steps of suggestions, see Fig. 2.

  • Initial Suggestion: Firstly, participants used a slide bar to indicate a ratio that they thought was the best answer between options A and B.
  • Final Suggestion: After submitting the first suggestion, participants were shown all three suggestions from themselves and their teammates. Participants then gave out their final suggestion for this question.

This method originated in the judge–advisor system paradigm [ 46 ], with which the influence can be quantified by comparing participants’ initial response and their final responses. However, we adapted this method for comparing two competitive influences. Here, the suggestions from the teammates were actually based on the participants’ initial suggestions. If a participant's initial suggestion was x, the teammates’ answers would be x+17 and x-17 (whether the robot or the human was higher was randomized for every question). This symmetry afforded a direct quantitative measure of the influence. We could therefore judge which teammate had a larger influence (by looking at whether participants changed their answer toward the human or the robot), and the amount of the influence (by looking at how much their answer changed). To prevent participants from giving extreme answers as their initial suggestions and made the symmetry impossible, we let participants know beforehand that “None of the correct answers from the professional consultants are extreme. So submitting a very low or very high answer as your suggestion is probably not a good idea to win the bonus payment.”

To make the experiment more realistic, 4 out of 10 questions (1st, 3rd, 6th, 9th) were random questions, where the two teammates’ suggestions were random numbers instead of ±17. As a result, only the data of the other 6 actual questions (2nd, 4th, 5th, 7th, 8th, 10th) were used for analysis. The order of the ten questions was identical for all participants because we did not expect a strong order effect for this kind of tasks and for a between-subject study. This design was informed by our pilot test results. We found that if all the questions were given ±17 suggestions, many participants would spot that the system was suspicious. However, starting with random suggestions (instead of ±17) can make the users believe that the system is legit and much less likely to doubt why many of the suggestions were symmetric.

As part of the power manipulation, the instruction for answering the final suggestion differed slightly between the three conditions. In h-lead and r-lead, the instruction was, “After seeing all the initial suggestions, please submit your final suggestion below for your leader's reference.” In the control condition, the instruction was, “All three suggestions from you and your teammates will be aggregated to form the team answer. The bonus payment will be based on how good this team answer is.” This difference was introduced to enhance the power of the leader. It also reflected the common practice in most teamwork.

Like the previous part, some delays were introduced to make believe the reality of this experiment. Participants experienced delays of 0-14 seconds after submitting initial suggestions, as if their teammates were still answering. For h-lead and r-lead, a longer delay (16-41 seconds) happened after the submission of the final suggestions, as if the leader was making the final decision. However, the leaders’ "final answers" were not shown to the participants to prevent influencing their attitudes.

These delays, together with the use of mixing random and ±17 questions, made this experiment realistic and believable. One participant even said, “You got me!” and that he had done so many robot/AI studies on Mturk that he already knew most of them were unreal, but our intricate design made him believe it was true this time.

4.3.3 Post-task Survey, Personal Information, Attention Check and Debriefing. After the main task, the participants were asked how they would distribute the bonus. For the r-lead and the h-lead, the question was:

  • You have answered all of your clients’ questions. Although we know that you are not the team leader, we are still interested in your opinion. What do you think is a reasonable split of the bonus payment between you three? (Total must sum to 100) (This will NOT be shown to your teammates, so please just follow your true thoughts and feelings.)

For the control condition, the question was:

  • You have answered all of your clients’ questions. We are interested in your opinion in this collaboration process. What do you think is a reasonable split of the bonus payment between you three? (Total must sum to 100) (This will NOT be shown to your teammates, so please just follow your true thoughts and feelings.)

After the submission, they were told that the leader has made the allocation but they will not see the results until the end of this study. We designed the experiment this way so that their opinions about their teammates will not be influenced by this (actually non-existing) allocation. The results of their proposed allocation, however, were just part of the manipulation and out of the main focus of this paper, so we decided not to include them in our analysis and focused on just the influence factor.

After the allocation, the participants answered questions about their experience in the process in this part. The first half included questions for perceived intelligence and competence, and they needed to rate the robot and the human side-by-side. The second half was The Robotic Social Attributes Scale (RoSAS) survey. The survey was asked twice, once for the robot and once for the human. These measures will be explained in detail in the measures section. For the perceived intelligence, the perceived competence, and the RoSAS, the orders of human/robot were all randomized.

Lastly, participants provided general personal information such as gender, age, and cultural background. For the cultural background, we used a standard (but highly simplified and thus less inclusive) question which asked the participants to choose one of the followings: American (White), American (African American), Hispanic, Asian, American Indian or Alaska Native, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, European, Mixed (please specify), and Other (please specify). This question was asked just to make sure each participant batch did not differ significantly, and they were not included in the main analysis. Attention check questions asked about which companies were (or were not) their clients. Given the varied quality of responses on Amazon MTurk, we decided beforehand that we only took responses that passed the attention check questions.

At the end of this study, the participants were given the debriefing that there were actually no other teammates, and they would get the maximum possible $ 2.00 bonus payment. They then gave consent to data usage and were given a code to report in Mechanical Turk to receive their payment.

4.4 Measures

4.4.1 Influence Factor. The main dependent variable for the study was the influence factor, which was measured by the change in participants’ opinions. For each participant, the influence was calculated in three steps. First, the influence of the 6 actual questions (2nd, 4th, 5th, 7th, 8th and 10th, where teammates’ answers were participants’ answers plus or minus 17) was calculated by subtracting a participant's initial suggestion from the final suggestion. Second, we transformed this change into a directed percentage. A change toward the robot was denoted as positive and one toward the human was denoted as negative. Therefore, a 0% means that there was no change between their initial answer and final answer, a +100% means that participants’ answer fully aligned the robot's suggestion (which was plus or minus 17), and -11.8% means that their final answer was, compared to their initial answer, closer toward the humans’ answer by 2. Third, we averaged the number of all six questions, which we called the influence factor. We used this as the measure for influence, as it summarized how much each participant was influenced by the teammates.

4.4.2 Perceived Intelligence. A single question: “How intelligent do you think your teammates are?” on a 0 (Not intelligent at all) - 7 (Extremely intelligent) Likert scale was used for measuring perceived intelligence. The participants were asked to rate the robot and the human separately.

4.4.3 Perceived Competence. Similarly, a single question: “How competent do you think your teammates are?” on a 0 (Not competent at all) - 7 (Extremely competent) Likert scale was used. The participants also needed to rate the robot and the human separately.

4.4.4 RoSAS. The Robotic Social Attributes Scale, is an 18-item scale that measures people's attitude toward a specific robot in a certain context[ 2 ]. The scale includes three dimensions: warmth, competence, and discomfort. Each dimension has six sets of two opposite adjectives. This is one of the most popular scales to measure people's social perceptions and judgments toward robots.

5 RESULTS

5.1 Preliminaries

We collected 50 participants for every condition. Among the 150 participants we recruited, 30 did not pass the attention check question (7 from h-lead, 5 from r-lead, and 18 from the control condition). Due to the survey design, h-lead and r-lead were identical except for who was the leader, and therefore these two conditions can be collected simultaneously with the same Qualtrics survey (only one random variable was introduced to decide who was the leader). However, the control condition differed in many ways and require a different survey design, thus it had to be collected later at a different time (to prevent participants from enrolling in both surveys), which might result in the different pass rate. However, the demographic data of the participants did not differ significantly between different conditions. In the end, we have a total of 120 participants whose data were included in the analysis (56 males, 63 females, 1 prefer not to say, Average age = 37.42).

5.2 Group-level

5.2.1 Influence Factor.

  • In human-robot mixed contexts, the high-power agent will be more influential than the low-power agent.

H1 was supported. We found a significant effect of power on the influence factor. The participants were more influenced by the leader in both h-lead and r-lead. The influence factor of h-lead was more toward humans, which we defined as minus ( M = -18.79, SD = 27.71). On the other hand, the influence factor of r-lead was more toward the robot, which we defined as positive ( M = 12.33, SD = 25.06), see Fig. 3.

More specifically, we first ran a normality test for the influence factor and then did a one-way ANOVA.

A Shapiro-Wilk test was performed to the influence factor and did not show evidence of non-normality (W = 0.99, p = 0.60). If tested separately according to the condition, there was still no evidence of non-normality for h-lead (W = 0.96, p = 0.13), r-lead (W = 0.97, p = 0.23), or the control condition (W = 0.97, p = 0.39). Based on this outcome, and after the visual examination of the histogram and the QQ plot, we decided to use a parametric test.

Then, a one-way ANOVA analysis of the influence factor indicated a significant difference between the three conditions, F (2, 117) = 17.66, p < .001, η 2 = 0.23. A Tukey post hoc test revealed that the influence was statistically significantly different between all three pairs: between control ( M = -1.34, SD =18.70) and r-lead, p = 0.046, 95% CI = [0.18, 27.17]; between control and h-lead, p = 0.0082, 95% CI = [3.82, 31.07]; and between h-lead and r-lead, p < .001, 95% CI = [18.67, 43.57]. These results showed that power does have a significant effect on how participants were influenced in human-robot mixed teams.

We also conducted three separate one-sample two-tailed t-tests to validate H1 further. The null hypothesis is that the influence factor = 0, which means that participants were not more influenced by either the human teammate or the robot teammate. For the control condition, t(31) = -0.41, p = 0.69, 95% CI = [-8.09, 5.40], showing that the participants did not lean significantly toward the robot or the human. However, for both h-lead and r-lead, the null hypothesis was rejected. In h-lead, t(42) = -4.45, p < .001, 95% CI = [-27.31, -10.26]; in r-lead, t(44) = 3.30, p = .0019, 95% CI = [4.80, 19.86], indicating that participants were more influenced by the leader in both conditions. H1 was supported.

  • In human-robot mixed contexts, the human will be more influential than the robot.

H2 was not supported, as we can see that:

(1) in both h-lead and r-lead, the human did not always have a larger influence, as the high-power robot was more influential than the low-power human.

(2) In the control condition, although the mean was negative, showing that participants were leaning toward human influence a bit, the t-test demonstrated that it was not significantly different from zero, t(31) = -0.41, p = 0.69, 95% CI = [-8.09, 5.40].

(3) To further test H2, we investigated the main effect of agent type by “flattening” the data: We combined all three conditions, eliminating the factor of power configuration (since the number of participants was similar in the two experimental conditions, we anticipated that the effect of power would cancel out each other when we flattened the conditions) and looking at only whether the influence was toward the human vs. the robot. Again, the result was not significant, t(119) = -0.97, p = 0.33, 95% CI = [-7.49, 2.56]. Therefore, by all three analyses, H2 was not supported.

  • When comparing the effects of power and agent type, which one will be larger?

RQ1 can be answered simply by the combination of the acceptance of H1 and the rejection of H2. Since the effect of power was significant but the effect of agent type was not, we can conclude that power had a larger effect than agent type in the current study.

Originally, if both H1 and H2 were supported, we planned to answer RQ1 by flattening the data by power or by agent type, which would yield two main effects, and we can then compare their effect sizes. However, since only H1 was supported, we can answer RQ1 without running this complicated statistical test.

5.3 Individual-level

  • How does power change people's perceptions and attitudes toward humans vs. robots in human-robot mixed contexts? Specifically,
  • (1) How do the perceptions and attitudes differ between high-power and low-power agents?
  • (2) How do the perceptions and attitudes of robots differ from those of humans?

To answer RQ2, we will compare the participants’ reactions to the high-power robot, high-power human, low-power robot, and low-power human, in a 2 (power: high vs. low) x 2 (agent type: human or robot) framework. Because the control condition did not have a power difference, we will only include h-lead and r-lead in this section. All tests were two-way ANOVA tests, and the two independent variables were power and agent type.

It should be noted that, this 2x2 analyzing framework can not be applied to influence factor, which was why we distinguished between Group-level and Individual-level sections. We can use this framework to analyze perceived intelligence, perceived competence, and RoSAS because every participant rated the robot and the human individually and separately on these scales. However, the influence factor was the collective net effect of both the robot and the human trying to influence the participants. Therefore, it was not possible to separate the effect of the robot and the human, and we could only analyze the influence factor at the group level.

5.3.1 Perceived Intelligence. We found a main effect of agent type, that humans were perceived as more intelligent than robots. There was not a main effect of power, and there was no interaction, either.

Specifically, a two-way ANOVA showed a statistically significant difference in perceived intelligence by agent type, F(1) = 8.54, p = 0.0039, η 2 = 0.05. The main effect of power was not significant, F(1) = 1.53, p = 0.22, η 2 = 0.0083, and the interaction was also not significant, F(1,172) = 1.31, p = 0.25, η 2 = 0.0072.

No Tukey post hoc test was needed because there was only a main effect of agent type, and it had only two values. Human teammates were perceived to be more intelligent than robot teammates, see Fig. 4.

5.3.2 Perceived Competence. Unlike perceived intelligence, here we found a main effect of power. High-power agents were perceived as more competent than low-power agents.

Specifically, with a two-way ANOVA, we found a statistically significant difference in perceived competence by power, F(1) = 5.60, p = 0.019, η 2 = 0.03. The effect of agent type was not significant, F(1) = 3.68, p = 0.057, η 2 = 0.02. The interaction was also not significant, F(1,172) = 1.78, p = 0.18, η 2 = 0.0097. Again, no post hoc test was needed, see Fig. 5.

5.3.3 RoSAS. We analyzed the RoSAS results by its three dimensions: competence, warmth, and discomfort. Overall, the RoSAS results showed that humans were perceived to be more competent, warmer, and cause less discomfort compared to robots. The effects of power were not significant.

Specifically, for competence, there was a significant effect of agent type, F(1) = 9.54, p = 0.0024, η 2 = 0.05. But the effect of power, F(1) = 0.32, p = 0.57, η 2 = 0.0018, and the interaction, F(1,172) = 0.018, p = 0.89, η 2 < 0.001, were both insignificant. The humans were perceived as more competent ( M = 23.5, SD = 4.78) than the robots ( M = 21.1, SD = 5.50).

For warmth, there was also a significant effect of agent type, F(1) = 160.35, p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.48. The effect of power, F(1) = 1.844, p = 0.18, η 2 = 0.0055, and the interaction, F(1,172) = 1.69, p = 0.20, η 2 = 0.0050, were both insignificant. The humans were perceived as warmer ( M = 21.8, SD = 4.16) than the robots ( M = 13.5, SD = 4.60).

Similarly, the effect of agent type on discomfort was also significant, F(1) = 18.28, p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.10, while the effect of power, F(1) = 0.54, p = 0.46, η 2 = 0.0028, and the interaction, F(1,172) = 0.005, p = 0.94, η 2 < 0.001, were both insignificant. The humans were perceived as causing less discomfort ( M = 7.90, SD = 4.07) than the robots ( M = 10.4, SD = 3.68).

6 DISCUSSION

6.1 Main Findings

In this study, we aim to investigate the effects of agent type (human vs. robot) and power (high vs. low) on people's decision-making in a human-robot mixed team.

The results showed that power was the deciding factor. At the group level, H1 was supported. We found that people were indeed influenced more by the high-power agents in such a context. No matter the leader was a robot or a human, the participants were significantly more influenced by the leader. On the other hand, when there was no power difference, the participants were not more influenced by either teammate.

On the other hand, H2 was not supported. Participants were not always more influenced by the other human. When the robot was high-power and the human was low-power, the participants were more influenced by the robot instead. This result was further validated by two tests. First, in the control condition, the mean of the influence factor was not significantly different from zero. This demonstrated that, when there was no power difference, participants were not more influenced by the human or the robot. Also, if we “flatten” all three conditions and only analyze the main effect of agent type, the difference was still not significant.

The combination of H1 and H2 led to the answer of RQ1, that the effect of power is larger than that of agent type since in all three conditions, power is a better predictor of the direction of influence than agent type. A robot in power was more influential than a human peer, and a human in power was more influential than a robot peer.

At the individual level, we investigate RQ2: How does power change people's perceptions and attitudes toward humans vs. robots in human-robot mixed contexts? The results showed that agent type was more crucial here: The participants were generally more positive toward humans than robots. Humans were perceived as more intelligent, warmer, and causing less discomfort compared to robots. However, power also seemed to have one important main effect: the high-power agent was perceived as more competent than the low-power. Because the measuring method was the same for perceived intelligence and perceived competence (rating on a scale of 1-7), this created an interesting comparison: agent type affected intelligence but not competence, while power affected competence but not intelligence. RQ2 revealed that previous findings in HRI mostly hold true in human-robot mixed contexts. People still preferred humans over robots in such contexts, with an interesting exception of competence. (It should be noted, though, these results of RQ2 have to be taken with a grain of salt for methodological reasons and thus might not be generalizable to other human-robot mixed contexts. We will discuss these in the limitation section.)

The findings that people generally preferred humans over robots also proved that our manipulation of agent type was successful, and the finding of H2 (that people did not lean significantly toward humans or robots) was not because of weak manipulation of agent type. Instead, this means the two agents actually had a similar amount of influence. In general, we showed that power was an important factor in such contexts. We will discuss all these in the following sections.

6.2 Theoretical Implications

The findings of the current study demonstrate that power is indeed an important factor in human-robot interaction. Previous studies have shown that power (or its related concept, authority) makes a robot more influential or persuasive [ 20 , 42 ], and our current study provides further support to their findings that these findings also seem to stand true in human-robot mixed contexts when robots directly compete with humans. Furthermore, we believe that our study can inform not only human-robot mixed contexts, but also human-AI agent mixed contexts. The “robot” used in this study was essentially a virtual AI agent which gave suggestions like an AI-driven decision-support system. In previous research, there have been studies investigating social power in decision-support systems where the AI was presented as an agent and compared against humans [ 28 ]. Due to the similarities of the AI agents in those studies and the robot in the current study, we think leveraging those AI studies of decision-support systems can give us important insights to our current discussion. As a result, even though HRI and HAI are two distinct fields, under the context of this study we will bring in relevant research from both fields to the following discussions of theoretical and design implications.

A unique contribution of the current study is that we were able to compare the effects of agent type and power directly. Our findings suggested that power can have a larger effect than agent type. In the control condition, the mean influence was negative, showing that the general trend was somehow toward the human, although it was not statistically significantly different from zero. However, the effect of power was much larger, which caused a clear preference for the leader's answer in h-lead and r-lead, showing that power over-weighted agent type in the context of our study. We would like to note, however, that this just denotes the possibility that the effect of power can be stronger than agent type. It does not mean that it will always be stronger . That is because, by the definition of power, the influence is caused by the capacity to influence, and people can always choose not to use their full capacity. Even so, the fact that the effect of power can be larger than agent type has important implications.

Here we would like to point out that this result seem to echo some previous HRI studies. For example, Fraune et al. [ 11 ] investigated people's aggressive behavior toward others in a competition where teams included both humans and robots. They found that people generally preferred humans over robots, and they preferred teammates to their opponents. However, when both factors came into play, the organizational factor, ingroup or outgroup, had a larger effect. That is, they preferred ingroup robots over outgroup humans. Combined with our results, this indicates that factors about social structures (ingroup-outgroup in their study and power in ours) seem to have a larger effect than agent type in a human-robot mixed context. We will discuss the design implications of this finding in the next section.

In this study, H2 was not supported, meaning that people did not prefer humans to robots in our context. This was not because of manipulation failure, as results in RQ2 indicated that people did react significantly differently to humans vs. robots. A possible explanation was that the two agents, human and robot, actually had roughly equal influence in this context, and therefore the participants’ answers did not lean toward either side significantly. Previous HAI studies have shown that people can prefer AI over humans (algorithm appreciation) [ 33 ] as well as prefer humans over AI (algorithm aversion) [ 8 ]. Subsequent studies showed that this preference was not binary but a point on a spectrum that can be nudged by task type [ 4 ] or perceived expert power [ 28 ]. Even if some previous research indicated that people generally prefer humans to robots [ 11 ], it was possible that the scenario of this study, consulting agency, indicated that these questions had objectively correct answers and therefore were perceived as “robot tasks” instead of “human tasks”, which might sometimes balance out people's original preference over humans.

This speculation might also explain the inconsistent findings in our two competence measurements. For the perceived competence (rate an agent's competence on a 1-7 scale), the participants rated whichever agent in power as more competent, and the effect of agent type was not significant. However, for the competence scale in RoSAS (calculated from people's general attitude on six adjectives: capable, interactive, responsive, reliable, competent, knowledgeable), people still preferred humans over robots. We believe that these two contradicting results suggested that people's general attitude still leaned toward humans (as shown by RoSAS), yet the competence rating right after the main decision-making tasks was influenced simultaneously by power, agent type, and task type. These factors might all come into play that made robots to be perceived as competent as humans in r-lead.

Finally, although the current study resonated with most previous research showing that power promoted influence, it went against Saunderson and Nejat's work, where they indicated that persuasive robots should avoid authority. In their work, robots in authoritative roles were less persuasive than robots as peers. We think that what caused the difference was the persuading mechanisms. In their studies, the robot deliberately tried to persuade participants to change their decision. This might cause the robot to be perceived as more dominant and threatening to the participants’ autonomy [ 42 ], which has been shown to decrease a robot's influence [ 32 ]. In contrast, with our study design, the robot teammate's opinion was only presented to the participants, and they could choose freely to adjust their answers or not. We believe that this study design allowed us to harness the influential effect of power without its adverse effects. This echoes with power research in human-human interaction, where Kelman [ 30 ] proposed three types of processes through which powerful people can influence others: instrumental compliance, internalization, and personal identification. While instrumental compliance involves explicit persuasive or controlling actions, the latter two can occur even without the high-power expressing their will. By internalization, the low-power adopt the high-power's values and beliefs as their own, and thus their behavior gradually aligns with the expectations of the high-power. Personal identification differs slightly in that the low-power identify with the high-power themselves instead of their ideas. Therefore, the low-power often begin to mirror the high-power's behavior or seek a relationship with them. Studies also suggest that in work settings, the latter two mechanisms, internalization and personal identification, can yield longer-lasting and more positive effects than instrumental compliance [ 40 ], which might explain the difference between our vs. Saunderson and Nejat's studies. Of course, other factors were also different between our studies. They used embodied robots, while ours used online virtual robots. They examined the interaction between one participant and one robot, while we investigated three-member human-robot mixed teams. We also manipulated different power bases. For example, their robots used punishment, but we did not. These factors might all cause the inconsistent findings. The relationships behind these factors and power remain unknown, and they call for in-depth future investigations.

6.3 Design Implications

This study brings about important design implications for future robot designs as well as designs for AI systems. Specifically, it highlights the importance of incorporating social structural factors such as power into the design of AI agents. Previously, the design of AI agents mostly focused on their appearance, capability, and behavior, and fewer robot designers focused on the social structure which the agent will be in. Our results showed that, even if people generally had more positive attitudes toward humans, power might be able to compensate for the agent's inferiority. As our study demonstrated, a robot given power can be perceived as more competent. We believe this suggests a new way that we can design for an agent's influence by designing for its power. For example, if an AI agent is known to perform well yet users are reluctant to adopt its suggestions for reasons such as algorithm aversion, we might consider increasing its power by giving the AI an expert framing, a higher organizational position, or the power to reward users. By doing so, we might be able to increase its perceived competence and thus its influence on users.

This is especially interesting because power seems to be able to form a self-reinforcing loop. A previous study has shown that expert framing given to an AI system can lead to increased perceived power and influence [ 28 ]. In addition to that, our current study shows that power can in turn increase perceived competence, which is equivalent to expert power, forming the self-reinforcing loop. This might suggest that a small tweak in an agent's design of power might lead to a huge effect, causing the robot to be overly powerful. This is especially alerting when the robot's suggestions needed to be compared against that of a human's, such as the scenario in our study. Because the effect of power can be larger than agent type, the design of power should be carefully controlled in relation to the human's power in order to achieve an adequate level of influence: not too little that waste the AI's capability, yet not too much that may cause over-reliance on AI agents over humans.

Finally, this study highlights that it is possible to test in advance innovative yet potentially socially dangerous ideas — such as robot leaders, robot managers, or even robot bosses. Many public media, movies, and novels have expressed their concerns about scenarios such as robot takeovers or robots that rule the world [ 35 ]. However, although there were theoretical discussions [ 47 , 49 ], fewer empirical academic works have directly investigated these concerns. Our current study showed that it is possible to study these possible future scenarios by simulating those situations and then observing people's reactions and psychological mechanisms. By showing that a robot in power may be more influential than a human in a mixed context, we demonstrated that in future society — where robots may work alongside humans — it is not impossible that robots may be placed in positions of power and become more influential than some humans. We believe that power lies at the core of this kind of social concern, and it is crucial to study such cases in advance to understand possible interaction patterns and associated risks. With this study, we hope to call the attention of our community to address these social concerns and potential ethical issues. We can and we should generate research that can guide the development of future designs and technologies that might otherwise drastically change our society.

6.4 Limitations and Future Works

There are many limitations in our current work. First, while power permeates into almost every aspect of our social life, the context of our study was quite limited. It only featured the scenario of a consulting team, which was a very specific case. Also, there was only one robot teammate and one human teammate, resulting in a very simple power structure, only slightly more complicated than the human-robot dyads in previous research. These limit the generalizability of our conclusion: we do not know if these results can be applied to contexts other than small work teams. We also do not know what it will be if it involves multiple humans and multiple robots, or if the power structure becomes more complicated, i.e., if the participants have both leaders and subordinates. With that said, our current study chose this minimum viable structure for a reason: the interaction in a multi-agent group is very complicated and hard to analyze. There are variables at the individual level, variables at the level of relationships between any of the two group members (the dyad level), variables at the whole-group level, as well as any combinations of members that happens at the sub-group level. Not just that. Every relationship between any two members can be a moderator for the relationship between another two (as shown in the quote, “the enemy of my enemy is my friend.”) Adding to this complex web of relationships is the factors of agent type and power structure that we investigated in the current study, which further muddle the situation. To study larger groups empirically, we not only need careful experiment design but probably also new analytical methodologies.

Second, the analysis of the current study already suffered a bit from such methodological limits. To study the effects of agent type and power in human-robot mixed contexts, we must include one robot and one human as the minimum social structure. However, while the manipulation of agent type was simple, the manipulation of power was relational — the power between the participant and the robot vs. the power between the participant and the human can not be manipulated independently. For example, if both the robot and the human were high-power, none of them was really high-power anymore — they now had equal power. As a result, our independent variables in the analysis of RQ2 were not really independent. While we analyzed the individual-level variables (perceived intelligence, perceived competence, and RoSAS) with ANOVA, as if it were an independently manipulated 2 (human vs. robot) x 2 (high- vs. low-power) study, we would like to point out that it was actually not so. We only had two conditions: high-power human vs. low-power robot and high-power robot vs. low-power human. Due to the relative nature of power, the condition of high-power robot vs. high-power human was hard to manipulate (it will be very similar to low-power robot vs. low-power human, which was the control condition). Therefore, the current results in the individual-level variables in RQ2 should be taken with a grain of salt, as the variable of power can not be manipulated independently.

Third, this study is an online study, and there were no real embodied robots. Although the individual-level results indicated that people did perceive the robots and the humans differently, we did not know whether this would still hold true if we had used physical robots. Also, the interaction was minimal in this study. To make the conditions comparable and to minimize confounding variables, the participants did not chat or communicate with their “teammates” at all. All they can see is a profile picture and their teammates’ suggestions, and the behavior of the humans and the robots was identical. Therefore, the difference found in the current study might be reflecting people's general attitudes and stereotypes toward robots instead of their actual behavior when interacting with real robots. It will be good if a similar study can be repeated in situ, where participants can really interact with wizard-of-oz robots, and we can observe more nuances in the interaction.

Fourth, the fact that, due to technical issues, the control condition was collected after the r-lead and h-lead and therefore had a lower pass rate might have potential confound. Although the demographic data (age, gender, cultural backgrounds) did not differ significantly between conditions, they might differ in other aspects that we did not measure. It may be possible that these aspects affected whether people actually leaned toward robots or humans in our experiment setup, although it is unlikely that this will lead to significantly different conclusions, given the hypotheses and the data we have. Still, if this study is to be repeated, we would use an augmented Qualtrics survey where data of all three conditions can be collected simultaneously.

Lastly, this study only serves as an initial endeavor into the realm of power in human-agent interaction, and more in-depth investigation is needed. Not only the context was highly specific, but the power manipulation was also simplified. We manipulated only two types of power: legitimate power and reward power because we speculated that the other three types — expert power, coercive power, and expert power — might interact with agent type. It will be interesting to look at how these power bases entangle with agent type and how all these are related to some of the most studied concepts in HRI, such as human-likeness and trust.

We also did not look into specific power mechanisms. As mentioned in the introduction, power mechanisms such as coalition formation (forming subgroups) and relational demography (the majority will feel more powered) are important in groups of more than two members. They might have played a role in our study, but we only examined their overall results (influence) and did not examine these mechanisms closely. That said, when we reviewed the experiment data, we saw some hints of these mechanisms that we can almost be sure would lead to interesting future research. We had an open question at the end of this study that asked, “Any thoughts on this study? How do you feel when working in a human-robot mixed team? Any comment is highly appreciated!” This was originally added to collect technical issues such as bugs, but we saw some interesting responses. While the responses in the control condition were more neutral, like, “After the first client, I kind of forgot that I was working with a robot since we functioned together as a team” or “I enjoyed working with a human-robot mixed team”, the responses in the r-lead and h-lead were more intriguing. Participants in the r-lead had responses like, “Robot seems to be my most favored teammate, more than the human.” or “I actually felt more comfortable thinking that the leader was a robot and would likely be making decisions based only on known facts.” However, participants in h-lead said otherwise, “I rather work with humans who think and act like me.” or simply, “I am not a fan of working with robots.” Were these participants trying to form a coalition with the leader? Is the force or power stronger than the humans vs. robots demographic distinctions? These intriguing and “political” responses made us eager for the next power research in HRI and HAI.

We believe that there is a whole new field to explore around these empowered intelligent agents. Will people trust their judgment more? Will people think they are more unbiased and fairer than humans in interpersonal or ethical issues? What are the social risks associated with them? Will their invention threaten people's autonomy and self-esteem? These are all questions that are not just interesting but also address many concerns of society about AI-powered intelligent agents, and we hope more researchers will join us to answer them.
