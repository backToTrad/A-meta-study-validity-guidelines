Tuning Endpoint-variability Parameters by Observed Error Rates to Obtain Better Prediction Accuracy of Pointing Misses

Shota Yamanaka , Yahoo Japan Corporation, Japan, syamanak@yahoo-corp.jp
Hiroki Usuba , Yahoo Japan Corporation, Japan, c-hiusuba@yahoo-corp.jp

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3580746
CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

Error rates ( ER s) in target-pointing tasks are typically modelled in two steps: predicting the click-point variability ( σ ) based on target sizes and then computing the probability that a click falls outside a target. This is an indirect approach if the researcher's purpose is to achieve the accurate prediction of ERs because the model coefficients are optimized to predict σ accurately in the first step. We compared the prediction accuracies of this method with a more direct technique in which the coefficients used for σ are determined in such a way as to optimize the closeness between observed and predicted ERs . Our re-analysis of eight datasets from mouse- and touch-based pointing studies showed that the latter approach consistently outperforms the conventional one if the starting values for the parameter search are appropriate (which can be achieved by hyperparameter optimization), thus enabling the interface configuration on the basis of accurately predicted ERs .

1 INTRODUCTION

Target pointing is one of the most fundamental operations on desktops and touchscreens. However, users sometimes miss the desired target, particularly when the target is small. In the HCI field, where deriving quantitative models is a core topic, researchers have proposed various models to predict how frequently users miss a target, i.e., the error rate ER [ 13 , 49 , 67 , 75 ].

Accurate ER models are beneficial for helping designers determine the target sizes on applications and websites so that, for example, all buttons on a webpage can be clicked with an $\mathit {ER} < 5\%$ . In addition, accurate quantitative models form the theoretical basis of user interface (UI) research. These include models for estimating the upper limit of typing speed based on Fitts’ law [ 6 , 55 ] and optimizing UI layouts based on the ant-colony algorithm [ 5 , 20 ]. Developing accurate ER models would be helpful for such research by providing an additional factor on how correctly users operate the UI. These are the background issues that motivated us to conduct this work.

We investigate the prediction accuracy improvement by tuning the parameters for click- or touch-point (i.e., endpoint) variability regressions using the observed ER s. Conventionally, predicting ER s has required two steps: (1) regressing the endpoint variability (standard deviation σ ) against target sizes, and (2) computing the probability that a click falls outside the target. We call this the “ SD -based method.” With this method, even for a new target size, the σ can be accurately predicted. However, the prediction accuracy of ER s is out-of-focus in the coefficient-determination process of the first step.

As an alternative, we compute the coefficients for σ to optimize the prediction accuracy of ER s: we call this the “ ER -based method.” Although this method has been utilized in prior studies, its benefits and issues have not yet been adequately compared to those of the SD -based method. For example, since the ER -based method utilizes a nonlinear regression, giving inappropriate starting values of the parameters to be searched would result in poor prediction accuracy [ 31 , 54 ]. Fortunately, tools developed in the machine-learning field can resolve this issue, which enables us to investigate the potential of the ER -based method and discuss how the two methods perform differently.

Our key contribution is thus empirical comparisons derived by re-analyzing eight datasets; not proposing new models or methods. The results showed that the ER -based method always outperforms the SD -based one. However, this conclusion is limited to when the starting values are appropriately given. To enable this, we utilized a hyperparameter optimization (HPO), which turned out to be critical in ensuring that the ER -based method could outperform the SD -based one. Using the ER -based method with HPO, researchers can robustly predict an ER in a new (untested) target condition with better accuracy than the SD -based one.

The references of the datasets are shown in Figure 1 . While these studies have reported several experiments, we only re-analyzed those with more than ten target conditions so as to avoid noise due to small numbers of fitting points. If these numbers are too small, when we run a leave-one-out cross-validation, an extremely inaccurately predicted ER would affect the resultant prediction-accuracy score (e.g., R 2 is very sensitive to outlier data points [ 53 ]). In addition, the ER s should be sufficiently precise for re-analysis, and thus we used only the data whose raw ER values for each target condition are available to us. For example, Bi et al. reported the ER s for six target conditions (2 distances × 3 sizes ) and rounded the results to one place, e.g., “ $\mathit {ER} = 6\%$ ,” which does not meet the above-mentioned criteria. For each experiment in Sections 4 – 11 , we report the quantitative results, and then provide a comprehensive discussion in Section 12 .

We assume that the ER -based method is applicable to other tasks, such as moving-target pointing [ 29 , 30 ] and temporal pointing [ 37 , 38 , 39 ], but examining these paradigms is outside the scope of a single paper. Instead, to strengthen our claim that the ER -based method robustly outperforms the SD -based one within the context of the most general case of pointing (static-target acquisition), we examined various conditions including target shapes (1D bar, rectangle, and circle), movement directions (left-right, downward, etc.), input methods (mouse and touch), environments (laboratory and crowdsourcing), and sample sizes (ranging from N = 12 to 391).

2 RELATED WORK

2.1 Error-rate Prediction Models

For target-pointing tasks, modeling the movement time MT has been widely conducted, e.g., by using Fitts’ law [ 21 ] and its variations. Modeling ER s has also been studied as a metric of task outcome, starting with Meyer et al.’s work [ 46 ], and has been more frequently conducted in the HCI field after Wobbrock et al.’s work [ 67 , 68 ].

For static targets without additional factors (e.g., time limit [ 67 ]), modeling ER s is typically implemented in two steps, as mentioned in Section 1 . As the simplest case, we here explain the model used for Experiment 1 (Figure 1 a): horizontal movements for 1D bar-shaped targets where the size is defined by the width W . The models for the remaining experiments are explained in Sections 5 – 11 and summarized in Table 3 in the Appendix. While the eight experiments in the original papers used different regressions on σ , we do not compare the various formulations, as our purpose is simply to compare the SD - and ER -based methods.

An endpoint on the x-axis is a random variable X assumed to be distributed normally over the target [ 13 , 19 , 44 , 67 ]: $X \sim \mathcal {N}(\mu _x,\,\sigma _x^2)$ . The mean position is located close to the target center ( μ x ≈ 0), and the variability σ x increases with W . σ x is proportionally related to W , or it needs a small intercept:

\begin{equation} \sigma _x = a + b\cdot {}W. \end{equation}
(1)
In this paper, italic letters a – h refer to empirical coefficients (free parameters). The target distance (or amplitude A ) does not affect μ x and σ x as long as users have sufficient time to aim for the target [ 13 , 19 , 72 , 75 ].

The second step is to compute ER based on σ x . By using the Gauss error function erf(·), the ER is predicted as

\begin{equation} \mathit {ER} = 1 - \text{erf} \left(\frac{W}{2\sqrt {2} \sigma _x} \right). \end{equation}
(2)
To predict ER for a new target condition, we first predict σ x by using Equation 1 and then obtain ER on the basis of the new W and predicted σ x by using Equation 2 .

The coefficients a and b are optimized by a linear regression for Equation 1 to minimize the residual sum of squares on σ x . Thus, for a new target, we can predict σ x accurately, and then, if Equation 2 holds true, the ER can also be predicted accurately. However, we should point out that the predicted ER is a by-product of the σ x -prediction process, as a and b are not considered in the prediction for ER .

While there are other formulations to express σ , these two steps have been used as the standard methodology for mouse- [ 72 ] and touch-based pointing [ 13 , 59 , 75 ], ray-casting pointing in the virtual-reality world [ 78 ], moving-target pointing [ 29 , 30 , 79 ], and moving-target crossing [ 28 ]. Wobbrock et al.’s model [ 67 , 68 ] is an exception in that it predicts ER s when changing the given MT , A , and W , but it cannot be used with off-screen-start pointing when the initial finger positions are undefined. This applicability difference is presumably why the SD -based method has been used more commonly in recent studies.

2.2 Error-rate Prediction by Parameter Optimization

The ER -based method for Experiment 1 runs a nonlinear regression for the combination of Equations 1 and 2 :

\begin{equation} ER = 1 - \text{erf}\left[ \frac{W}{2 \sqrt {2} (a + b\cdot {}W)} \right], \end{equation}
(3)
where ER is the observed value. The ER -based method uses no extra parameters to improve the model fit compared with the SD -based one. The point of difference from the SD -based method is that a and b are optimized for the closeness between observed ER s and the right-side values, rather than the prediction accuracy for σ x .

Determining the coefficients to optimize ER prediction has been investigated in several studies. Huang et al. [ 27 ] and Park et al. [ 49 ] derived ER models for moving-target pointing. For example, in [ 49 ], they empirically obtained one coefficient value (duration of the final submovement) by segmenting the cursor-speed profiles, and then determined the remaining coefficients (e.g., encoding precision of the user's internal clock) by a nonlinear regression. Because the latter set of coefficients cannot be calculated from the measured data, running a nonlinear regression is a necessary step. Similarly, in Yamanaka et al.’s path-steering ER model, two coefficients on submovement endpoint variability are calculated by parameter optimization [ 77 ], and these cannot be measured from the data.

Parameter optimization has also been used when coefficients can be directly measured. For Fitts’ law, calibrating finger-tremor variability σ a can improve the MT prediction [ 10 , 16 , 34 , 66 ]. A re-analysis showed that a direct measurement of σ a sometimes outperformed the parameter optimization in terms of prediction accuracy [ 76 ].

In summary, parameter optimization for both ER and MT prediction models has been used. However, the potential benefits and challenges have not been shared in the HCI community, presumably for two reasons. First, cases in which the parameter optimization did not work well (thus resulting in poor prediction models) are not likely to be published. Still, this can be partially resolved by HPO, as we show in the current paper. Second, HCI researchers have emphasized the importance of understanding human behavior by interpreting model coefficients, which is sometimes difficult with coefficients optimized for predicting ER s. Thus, we assume that even when researchers have examined the ER -based method and obtained a better prediction accuracy than the SD -based one, they have not explicitly reported the performance difference according to our survey. The second point is discussed in more detail in Section 2.3 .

2.3 Aiming for High Prediction Accuracy or Explainable Parameter Values

Because the parameters are balanced in such a way as to minimize the residual sum of squares in nonlinear regressions of the ER -based method, sometimes an obtained parameter value might not be meaningful for researchers wanting to understand human motor behavior. In the machine-learning research field, there are similar discussions on the inherent pros and cons of aiming only for high prediction accuracy by parameter tuning, e.g., explainable AI [ 1 ] and interpretable machine learning [ 47 ]. In particular for HCI, parameter values play an important role in understanding human behavior. For example, in Park et al.’s work on modeling ER s [ 49 ], they emphasized that “each parameter of the [proposed] model has a clear cognitive meaning” and then discussed how well each parameter value matched previous reports on human cognitive models.

We are not trying to discuss which is the more correct approach: obtaining interpretable parameter values or achieving high prediction accuracy. Rather, we would like to emphasize that focusing on the high accuracy of prediction models is an important goal for a broad range of HCI research. Examples include gesture recognition on touchscreens [ 40 , 61 ] and in-air interactions [ 48 , 52 ], sensing and classifying daily activities such as washing dishes and opening doors by using smartwatch accelerometers [ 36 ] and smart speaker microphones [ 69 ], and detecting a command that a user wants to select based on eye-gaze [ 32 , 33 , 62 , 63 ].

In these studies, while some parameter values are not interpretable, implementing a recognition system with high prediction accuracy is a substantial basis of the research contribution. For example, in the gaze-based command selection system that predicts a user's intent reported in [ 33 ], numerous factors can affect the prediction result, including eye-gaze coordinates, changes in the eye-gaze coordinates during a given time window (2000 ms), durations of saccades, and durations of fixations. The 127 parameters in this system were optimized to enable the best prediction accuracy. It was thus difficult (or unfruitful) to discuss how appropriate each of the 127 parameter values was. 1

Another example is swipe-based typing on touchscreens [ 35 ]. While Quinn et al.’s model to predict a finger path for inputting a word was based on human kinematics [ 50 ] (e.g., the minimum-jerk model [ 64 ]), Mehra et al. later showed that a generative adversarial network (GAN) could predict the finger path more accurately [ 45 ]. Since Mehra et al.’s system simply optimizes GAN parameters so that a generated path resembles the data produced by real users, we cannot explain why humans make such a stroke when they try to input a word, but achieving higher prediction accuracy will contribute to optimizing the keyboard layout [ 11 , 51 ] and predicting a text-entry time [ 7 , 56 ].

Similarly, typical operation-time predictions for menu selection [ 4 , 18 ] have been based on human cognitive models such as Hick's law [ 15 ], but Li et al. demonstrated that a deep learning-based method was better able to predict the time [ 41 ]. Note that Li et al. also examined the explainability, such as whether the generated model could appropriately mimic a human user who memorizes and forgets menu items over time.

To sum up, achieving high prediction accuracy with less of a focus on the explainability of parameter values occupies a certain place in a broad range of HCI subfields outside the context of target pointing. While we acknowledge the importance of understanding human behavior by interpreting model coefficients, this paper is positioned more on the accuracy-improvement side.

3 DATA ANALYSIS PROCESS AND EXPECTED ADVANTAGE OF THE ER-BASED METHOD

3.1 Criteria for Prediction Accuracy

We utilized three criteria to evaluate the prediction accuracy: R 2 , mean absolute error ( MAE ), and root mean square error ( RMSE ). R 2 indicates the correlation between the observed and predicted ER s, but it is independent of the closeness between them (an easy-to-understand result is shown in Figure 6 b). Thus, we also used MAE and RMSE ; both are widely utilized and the latter is more sensitively affected when there are poorly fit data points. A higher R 2 is better, while lower MAE and RMSE are better. Note that there are no definite thresholds to judge poor/good for these metrics, so we will discuss the results in a relative manner to compare the candidate prediction methods.

In addition to using all target-condition data points (e.g., 24 fitting points when we use three A s and eight W s in Experiment 1), the prediction accuracy should be judged for a new (unknown) target condition. For this purpose, we utilized leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV), which has been commonly used in prior studies (e.g., [ 34 , 65 , 76 ]). In Experiment 1, for example, we predicted the ER of one data point by using the parameters computed from the remaining 23 points, and repeated this process for all the 24 points. In the end, we computed the R 2 , MAE , and RMSE values by comparing the 24 observed and predicted ER s. Figures to show these results on LOOCV do not have error bars because this process was performed only once, which is different from some other cross-validations (e.g., k-fold).

3.2 Parameter Optimization

In nonlinear regression for the ER -based method, we used the curve_fit function in the scipy.optimize library of Python. For this function, if starting values are not given, they are all 1 by default.

Since a nonlinear regression finishes the parameter search when the prediction improvement becomes less than a pre-determined value (e.g., 10 − 8 ), there is a risk of obtaining meaningless coefficients with poor fits or large prediction errors (cf. locally optimal solution) [ 31 , 54 ]. Researchers therefore need to examine a number of starting-value patterns by (e.g.) grid search, random search, or manual search [ 8 , 9 ], all of which require considerable effort [ 54 ].

This issue in determining the appropriate starting values can be overcome by means of HPO. We used the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator [ 8 ], a kind of Bayesian optimization method implemented in Optuna [ 2 ]. This tool iteratively examines the next starting values on the basis of the previous fitting history. The number of iterations was set to a maximum of 2,000.

For HPO, we used multi-objective optimization. Specifically, when Optuna determines the next starting values, it concurrently tries to maximize R 2 and minimize MAE . Because the coefficients showing a smaller MAE would also exhibit a smaller RMSE , we used only R 2 and MAE for computational simplicity, but it is possible to use additional metrics.

Through the multi-objective optimization, we might obtain several optimal solutions (known as Pareto front ): a set of optimal coefficients could give a better R 2 and poorer MAE (e.g., 0.951 and $1.7\%$ , respectively), while another set could have a poorer R 2 and better MAE (e.g., 0.949 and $1.6\%$ ). We therefore used a set that had a smaller MAE , as we want to predict ER s that are close to observed values. In a fortunate case, we might obtain the best R 2 and MAE at the same time.

3.3 Parameter Range

For the ER -based method with HPO, we have to define the search ranges for each coefficient. In the case of Experiment 1, for example, a in Equation 1 ( σ x = a + b · W ) could be either positive or negative, and b is assumed to be positive [ 57 , 72 ]. Because the largest W in Experiment 1 is 78 pixels, if a = 0 and b = 10, the resultant σ x is 780 pixels. This means that, for normally distributed endpoints, $32\%$ of clicks fall outside the ± 1 σ x = 1560-pixel area around the target center, which is obviously incorrect considering the experiment system's window size (1200 pixels wide). Thus, b needs to be smaller, but this conclusion changes depending on a . In addition, it is difficult to determine the appropriate ranges for models using a higher number of free parameters.

In fact, we do not need to be too careful about the parameter ranges at this stage, since the Bayesian optimization will find more appropriate coefficients as the iterations progress. Hence, extremely wide ranges suffice. We limited a to [ − 10, 10] and b to [10 − 8 , 10] in Experiment 1. The ranges used for each dataset are noted in their sections.

3.4 Candidate Fitting Methods

We compared three methods: SD -based, ER -based without HPO, and ER -based with HPO. For the second case, we examined three variations: (1) default setting, i.e., without specifying the coefficient ranges or starting values, (2) giving the same ranges as HPO, and (3) giving the same ranges and applying the coefficients obtained in the σ regression to the starting values. Because the ranges used for HPO were, as mentioned, meaninglessly large, it might seem unfair for the cases of (2) and (3) above, but iteratively tuning the starting values from wide search ranges is what the HPO aims for. The fitting results are explained in Sections 4 – 11 and summarized in Table 4 in the Appendix.

3.5 Sensitivity of Predicted ERs Affected by σ

Even if the SD -based method predicts σ s accurately, a slight prediction error might notably affect a prediction accuracy for ER s. To investigate this phenomenon, we here introduce the result of Experiment 1: namely, Equation 1 could predict σ x s with R 2 = 0.9966 and MAE = 0.2097 pixels. Even for the worst prediction-error case, the LOOCV showed that σ x was predicted as 2.656 pixels, while the observed σ x was 2.269 pixels; a difference of just 0.3867 pixels.

While this error might be trivial, the predicted vs. observed ER s were more clearly different: $13.20\%$ vs. $8.394\%$ (≈ $4.8\%$ difference). As a check, if we do not use the predicted σ x but rather the observed σ x of 2.269 pixels, the ER could be predicted more accurately as $7.790\%$ (only ≈ $0.6\%$ difference). Hence, a more accurate prediction of σ x is needed for accurate ER prediction, as the predicted ER is sensitively affected by σ x .

In contrast, the ER -based method predicted the ER as $8.914\%$ (≈ $0.5\%$ difference), which is more accurate than using the observed σ x . This result demonstrates the effectiveness of precisely tuning the endpoint-variability parameters to optimize the closeness between observed and predicted ER s. This advantage of the ER -based method was consistently observed for the remaining datasets, as described below.

12 DISCUSSION

12.1 Prediction Accuracy Achieved by the ER-based Method

Over the past decade, a number of research groups have used the two-step approach, i.e., the SD -based method. However, the results of our re-analysis consistently showed that the direct approach of the ER -based method is more effective to predict the ER of a new target condition as long as the starting values are appropriately given.

The ER -based method was not particularly robust when using many coefficients. Specifically, in Experiments 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 8, the default ER -based method gave meaningless results, including extremely large or almost zero ER s, a negative slope for regression, and N/A results for cross-validation. Defining coefficient ranges and applying observed coefficients to the starting values occasionally resolved this issue, but not completely; e.g., worse fits than when using HPO were obtained in Experiments 5, 6, and 8.

These issues could be resolved by using the ER -based method with HPO, which consistently outperformed the SD -based method for all eight datasets. Thus, we empirically demonstrated that the ER -based method with HPO is the most robust approach among all the candidate methods we examined, and there were no limitations on target shapes, input devices, environments, etc.

The discussion here also applies to which of the SD - and ER -based methods researchers should use, and when. Our results indicate that using the ER -based method is better when researchers want to focus on the prediction accuracy. In contrast, using the SD -based one is preferable if the intent is to evaluate the model validity of the two steps independently. For example, in Bi et al.’s ER prediction model [ 13 ], they first verified whether σ fits for Equation 15 ( $\sigma _x^2 = a+b\cdot {}W^2\ \mathrm{and}\ \sigma _y^2 = c+d\cdot {}W^2$ ) by confirming if there were high R 2 values, after which they computed the predicted ER s. If the prediction accuracy of the SD -based method is poor, it is possible to isolate the cause by first verifying whether the results of the σ fitting are sufficient.

In contrast, the ER -based method regresses the ER data in a single process that combines the first and second steps. Therefore, if a resultant prediction accuracy is poor, researchers cannot distinguish whether the cause was a fault in the model formulation of the first or second step (or both). Using the ER -based method is thus a safe choice if the model has already been validated with the SD -based method and if a much higher prediction accuracy is necessary. For example, when researchers want to further refine the model formulations (e.g., taking the finger-movement angle into account for predicting σ [ 43 ]), the new model should be first verified with the SD -based method. In addition, as discussed in Section 2.3 , using the SD -based method is a more straightforward approach if the researcher's goal is to understand human behavior by interpreting the obtained coefficient values.

12.2 Necessity to Use Appropriate Endpoint-variability and ER Models

The σ models should be appropriately formulated: e.g., we should use the complete version of σ x = a + b · W + c · H + d · ( W / H ) in Experiment 6. For example, when we used a simplified version ( σ x = a + b · W and σ y = c + d · H ), the ER -based method with HPO gave R 2 = 0.8039, $\mathit {MAE} = 0.7350\%$ , and $\mathit {RMSE} = 0.9861\%$ for the LOOCV, all of which were worse than when using the complete version ( R 2 = 0.9612, $\mathit {MAE} = 0.3395\%$ , and $\mathit {RMSE} = 0.4397\%$ ).

The question then is how to determine the appropriate σ models. In previous studies, researchers have recorded endpoints and examined the statistical significance of the resultant σ models. For example, in the original study of Experiment 6, ANOVA results revealed significant main effects of W and H , as well as their interaction, on σ x , and then the model of σ x = a + b · W + c · H + d · ( W / H ) showed that all parameters were significant contributors ( p < 0.001).

However, for better utility of the ER -based method, using only observed ER s and not recording endpoints would be desirable. To do so, we can compare several candidate models by cross-validation, as the possible combinations of independent variables are limited and previous studies have provided several candidate formulations.

To develop accurate σ models, screening outlier data is preferable and has been done for the datasets we used, e.g., endpoints that were > 15 mm away from the target center were removed [ 75 ]. This process would require each endpoint to be recorded, but removing such outlier data with a pre-determined threshold is also possible when measuring ER s. Therefore, recording each endpoint gives the information for making appropriate σ models, but this is not necessary if researchers have the knowledge and resources to examine candidate models.

If the σ and ER models are ideally developed, the prediction accuracies of the SD - and ER -based methods should be matched. However, this assumption did not hold to our datasets, probably because the σ and ER models use several simplifications. Future improvement points, which are also the potential reasons behind the different performances between the SD - and ER -based methods, are as follows.

  • Lack of necessary variables. For example, in Experiment 8, A is not used to predict σ , but in actuality, A slightly affects σ [ 75 ]. We also simplified the mean of endpoints being on the target center ( μ = 0), but it actually has a slight offset for touch pointing [ 3 , 22 , 26 ].
  • Merged directions. The datasets in Experiments 5, 6, and 8 merge various directions even though movement directions affect endpoint distributions and ER s [ 23 , 43 ].
  • Assuming normal endpoint distribution. The Gauss error function works well if this assumption holds, but endpoints are not normally distributed in some target conditions [ 75 ].

12.3 Possible Issues in Parameter Optimization for ER-based Method

Regarding the explainability of models, in our ER -based method, coefficients such as those for “ $\sigma _x^2 = a+ b\cdot {}W^2$ ” in Experiment 3 keep the same meanings as the original: namely, a is the absolute finger-touch precision of a user, and b is the relative accuracy that is affected by task conditions and the user's speed-accuracy bias [ 13 ]. However, the default ER -based method gave a = −1.84 pixels 2 as the optimum, which is inappropriate since the original meaning of a is a variance (i.e., non-negative). Using HPO helped resolve this issue. See Table 1 for a summary of the coefficients.

We can predict σ s on the basis of these obtained coefficients. Unfortunately, the ER -based method sometimes does not predict the σ s accurately, but given the purpose of this method, the optimized parameters need not necessarily reflect the actual σ s. For example, in Experiment 7, by using a and b obtained by the ER -based method (Table 1 ), we predicted $\sigma _y^2$ s for 16 target conditions. The R 2 for the observed vs. predicted $\sigma _y^2$ s was 0.861, which is relatively low compared to the results of the other experiments (Table 2 ).

Other questionable results include b = 1.00 − 8 in Experiment 3 and d = 1.01 − 8 in Experiment 8, which seem too small. For example, the latter result means that $\sigma _y^2 = c + d\cdot {}W^2$ was almost constant ( $\sigma _y^2 \approx c$ ), and thus the ER was affected mainly by the x-axis variability ( $\sigma _x^2 = a + b\cdot {}W^2$ ). Because HPO tries to ensure a good prediction accuracy of ER by balancing the four coefficient values in this case, this low prediction accuracy for σ is acceptable unless the goal is to understand human motor behavior.

From the scientific perspective, the ER -based method might give inappropriate parameter values for researchers whose goal is to understand human motor behavior. Accurate prediction of σ enables researchers to develop pointing-facilitation techniques (e.g., the Bayesian Touch [ 12 ] and the Rotational Dual Gaussian Model [ 43 ]), but using the ER -based method does not have such an enhancement. On the other hand, from the viewpoint of UI design, focusing on the accurate prediction of ER for an unknown condition has certain advantages as long as it does not overfit known data. For example, Bi et al.’s model [ 13 ] has been used by UI designers at Yahoo Japan after confirming its prediction accuracy to unknown data [ 73 ]. Simply increasing the number of free parameters may not be effective in some cases as it can cause overfitting, while the ER -based method did not overfit to the known data, at least for the eight datasets we examined.

12.4 Limitations and Future Work

We previously mentioned limitations such as the use of several simplifications and the difficulty in understanding human behavior. In addition, while the results of the eight datasets we re-analyzed support the utility of the ER -based method, the applicability to conditions other than static-target pointing requires further empirical evidence. Examples include pointing in virtual reality [ 78 ], input with latency [ 42 ] or jitter [ 58 ], moving targets [ 29 , 49 ], temporal targets [ 38 , 39 ], and pointing with distractors [ 14 , 70 ].

Bayesian optimization for hyperparameter tuning can identify good starting values as the iterations progress, but this method sometimes searches unfruitful values repeatedly, which increases the time cost for parameter computation. In the machine-learning field, reducing the hyperparameter search space has been attempted, e.g., by using sparse recovery methods [ 17 ] or interactively re-defining the search space by reviewing the optimization history [ 24 ]. Research on performance modeling like ours will be accelerated by these novel approaches.

Still, to exploit the full potential of the ER -based method, using HPO is necessary. In Experiments 1 and 7, whether or not HPO was used did not affect the results, which eases the burden of researchers to develop a program that runs HPO and saves on the time cost. However, it is unclear for researchers whether the ER -based method without HPO has already achieved the optimized prediction accuracy or if using HPO could further improve the accuracy. This lack of clarity forces researchers to always use HPO if their goal is to achieve as high a prediction accuracy as possible.

13 CONCLUSION

We investigated the prediction accuracy of ER in static-target pointing by tuning the endpoint-variability coefficients on the basis of observed ER s. The ER -based method has been used previously [ 39 , 49 , 77 ], but prediction-accuracy comparisons with the conventional SD -based method have not been performed in detail because directly measuring the coefficients was impossible. Our study is the first to empirically demonstrate that the ER -based method consistently outperforms the SD -based one if the starting values of nonlinear regressions are appropriately given, which is easily accomplished by using HPO. We hope this approach will be effective for other types of target selection tasks as well.
