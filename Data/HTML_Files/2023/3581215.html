“I Won't Go Speechless”: Design Exploration on a Real-Time Text-To-Speech Speaking Tool for Videoconferencing

CHI '23: Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems , Hamburg, Germany, April 2023

The COVID-19 pandemic has shifted many business activities to non-face-to-face activities, and videoconferencing has become a new paradigm. However, conference spaces isolated from surrounding interferences are not always readily available. People frequently participate in public places with unexpected crowds or acquaintances, such as cafés, living rooms, and shared offices. These environments have surrounding limitations that potentially cause challenges in speaking up during videoconferencing. To alleviate these issues and support the users in speaking-restrained spatial contexts, we propose a text-to-speech (TTS) speaking tool as a new speaking method to support active videoconferencing participation. We derived the possibility of a TTS speaking tool and investigated the empirical challenges and user expectations of a TTS speaking tool using a technology probe and participatory design methodology. Based on our findings, we discuss the need for a TTS speaking tool and suggest design considerations for its application in videoconferencing.

1 INTRODUCTION

Videoconferencing has continuously evolved over the years as the hyperconnected society has progressed. In particular, the COVID-19 pandemic has sparked a demand for non-face-to-face communication [ 15 ] and has become a turning point in remotely bringing traditional business activities [ 1 ]. Businesses and organizations have rushed to adopt videoconferencing, and productivity tools have quickly updated their remote collaboration capabilities. Videoconferencing services such as Zoom, Teams, and Webex support the ability to communicate while viewing one another's faces and shared data. Collaborative tools such as Figma and Miro provide voice or video communication during real-time multiplayer editing. Consequently, online voice communication with others while looking at a monitor has become a new norm for work [ 87 , 102 ].

However, suitable spaces for videoconferencing, such as conference rooms and private spaces, are not always available. During the pandemic, many people have participated in videoconferencing in communal living spaces or public places, and the spatial context or insufficient user preparation has restricted or interfered with their meeting experiences [ 60 , 75 ]. This problem persists even in the post-COVID era. Because non-face-to-face communication has already become a new paradigm in various fields such as healthcare [ 58 ] and education [ 103 ], people will continue to practice it [ 57 , 60 ]. Moreover, people are more likely to engage in videoconferencing in various contexts than ever because some people return to their previous communal workspace, whereas others keep remote working. Therefore, to provide a sustainable videoconferencing experience, we should continue to investigate the user's videoconferencing experience in various surrounding contexts even in the post-COVID era.

The surrounding context in which a user participates in videoconferencing could impose a psychological strain on the user to turn on the microphone and speak. For example, users may be concerned about turning on a microphone if unexpected noise constantly occurs (Figure 1a). Because speaking in a public place can annoy other people unrelated to the speech [ 53 ], users may also be concerned about speaking in videoconferencing when they are in public places. Wells et al. [ 106 ] informed that it's important for videoconferencing participants to have an isolated and private space considering privacy issues. Freely talking is generally discouraged when a family sleeps or when users are in a quiet café or library (Figure 1b). Even if the microphone is broken or unprepared, users will not be able to speak by voice during videoconferencing.

Typically, text chats can be used as alternatives. However, communicating via text chats in videoconferencing can distract the attention of other interlocutors and interfere with audiovisual interaction [ 29 , 52 ]. Weisz et al. [ 105 ] noted that text chat could interfere with the viewing experience. Because being unable to concentrate for long periods due to potential distractions is a major problem in videoconferencing [ 77 ], it is problematic to continue texting while others communicate by voice. Wang et al. [ 104 ] reported that chatting was less effective than verbal communication when performing tasks that required visual judgment. In multi-party videoconferencing, where multiple users edit or share some material, audiovisual interaction is highly important. Thus, chatting alone in multi-party videoconferencing may not be the recommended method of communication.

Considering the accessibility and practicality of most videoconferencing users, we propose text-to-speech (TTS) [ 31 ]-based augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) [ 8 ] tools as a new communication channel, and conducted two probe studies in laboratory settings. In the first study, we created a simple technology probe to observe a user's multiparty videoconferencing experience by means of speaking. Based on the first study, we identified the problems of the text chat system and the possibilities of a TTS speaking tool. We then conducted a second probe study to better understand implicit user needs and communication experiences. The participants reported various empirical problems with using the TTS speaking tool in videoconferencing but, at the same time, found its effectiveness and presented various possible use cases.

This paper makes four contributions. First, we explored the multiparty videoconferencing experience of users in a speaking-restrained spatial context. Accordingly, we found that only text chatting alone was insufficient to engage in multiparty videoconferencing, where voice conversations were dominant. Second, we suggested the possibility and usefulness of the TTS speaking tool even for users without speech impairments [ 8 ] based on the results of the two probe studies [ 49 ]. Our results suggested that TTS-based AAC could be utilized in online voice communications, as even people without speech impairments may experience contextual speech restrictions in online communications. Third, we introduce the empirical problems, user expectations, and needs that may appear when using TTS speaking tools for real-time voice conversations. Finally, we suggested design considerations for a TTS speaking tool to preserve users’ videoconferencing experiences.

2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORKS

2.1 Studies for a better videoconferencing experience

Videoconferencing has been mainly dealt with in the human–computer interaction (HCI) and computer-supported cooperative work (CSCW) fields based on its advantages of being able to participate in the space of your choice, saving money, and being easy to manage recorded data [ 44 , 50 ]. To provide a better videoconferencing experience, researchers have reported various empirical problems in videoconferencing, such as the lack of eye contact [ 100 ], lack of presence [ 9 , 47 ], and turn-taking issues [ 51 ], and have suggested many solutions. Chen et al. [ 16 ] studied an algorithm to solve the out-of-sync problem between audios and videos. Nguyen et al. [ 76 ] proposed a system for preserving nonverbal cues in multiparty videoconferencing. However, these studies have focused on the communication between interlocutors and the internal videoconferencing experience. To prevent the exposure of information around the user to other interlocutors, researchers have studied various techniques such as noise-cancellation [ 56 ], background blurring [ 12 , 112 ], and virtual background [ 55 ]. However, these techniques only prevent the external context from reaching the other interlocutors. Nonetheless, they did not consider preserving the user experience in a space where contextual challenges exist. Neustaedter et al. [ 75 ] stated that the videoconferencing background processing system could not be a fundamental solution to maintain users’ privacy, and even a single failure can greatly impair the experience, so additional protection is needed.

In response to the sudden spread of videoconferencing due to the pandemic, many researchers have recently reviewed refreshed videoconferencing experiences. Rice et al. [ 86 ] discussed the phenomenon of being overly concerned with the appearance of oneself and others during videoconferencing. Elsden et al. [ 34 ] proposed strategies to protect users from potential surveillance for a sustainable videoconferencing experience. Lee et al. [ 60 ] presented an understanding of remote working experiences via videoconferencing and provided a unified framework for distracting factors. Neate et al. [ 74 ] described barriers to people with an aphasia and their unique ways of communicating in videoconferencing. Researchers also addressed the unequal participation of remote users in hybrid meetings, where only some of the conversation partners are participating remotely. Saatçi et al. [ 88 ] highlighted the need for design solutions that support the democratic participation of remote participants in hybrid meetings. Grønbæk et al. [ 45 ] explored video-mediated collaboration solutions that enable remote participants to collaborate well, highlighting their alienation in hybrid meetings. However, even these studies focused only on the internal experience of videoconferencing. Despite the rapid popularity of videoconferencing, there is insufficient discussion to conserve user experiences from interference with the external context. In particular, measures to support videoconferencing users who are challenged to speak because of the surrounding context have not been discussed. Under the rise of remote work, we intend to explore in depth the experiences of those users.

2.2 Augmentative and alternative communication

AAC systems are defined as systems that help individuals with complex communicational needs [ 8 ]. Voice output communication aid (VOCA), also known as a speech-generating devices (SGDs), is a type of AAC system that provides natural speech production [ 10 ]. Research on AAC and VOCA has mainly been conducted in people with disabilities or diseases (e.g., aphasia, Parkinson's/dementia, and autism) [ 23 , 39 ]. However, Beukelman et al. [ 8 ] described AAC as a system that supports effective communication among all people with temporary or permanent communication difficulties. Pino et al. [ 81 ] claimed that AAC could support communication among people of all ages, cultures, and languages using the “Design for All” approach. In other words, people without physical disabilities can also be targeted users of AAC. Hence, in a world where online communication has become a new norm, alternative communication tools are needed to respond to contextual and physical difficulties.

AAC can be produced in several ways, one of which is the silent speech interface (SSI). The SSI has been discussed as a communication system for silence-requiring or noisy environments [ 27 ] or as an advanced AAC for people with speech impairments [ 27 , 42 ]. Research on SSI has aimed to develop an interface that can communicate verbally without voice input through technical approaches using electroencephalography, electromyography, ultrasound, or video imaging [ 36 ]. However, these approaches require heavy mechanical equipment or a long time for calculations and need several additional discussions are needed [ 27 , 36 ]. Curtis et al. [ 23 ] summarized various problems with AAC utilizing complex technologies, such as inconvenience, slowness, unreliability, expensiveness, and unhandy to carry. These kinds of AAC are not suitable for our research. We consider AAC that are readily available at a low cost and accessible to videoconferencing users without additional equipment.

A low-cost AAC with a touch screen, such as “Proloquo2Go” [ 5 ], has been created so that users can composes a sentence by selecting graphic symbols or short messages arranged in a grid layout [ 33 , 39 ]. Another low-cost AAC with keyboard input, such as “PredictableTM” [ 11 ] and “Proloquo4Text” [ 6 ], was designed so that users can directly type to speak, and the system then synthesizes it into speech. We believe that utilizing text input would be most suitable for our study because it does not require much training for most videoconferencing users. However, it is unclear whether existing tools are suitable for users without speech impairments, as these tools have been designed only for users with speech impairments. The evaluation criteria for the communication experience of people who speak freely daily differ from those of people with speech impairments. For instance, people with aphasia have a unique way of communicating in videoconferencing and have particular difficulty manipulating videoconferencing UIs [ 74 ]. Therefore, it is necessary to newly explore the needs and experience of using AAC for those who are accustomed to voice communication with their voice, which have not been discussed so far. In online communication, anyone can face contextual interference toward speaking.

2.3 Text-to-speech

TTS, also known as speech synthesis, is a technique for converting text into natural human-like speech [ 31 ]. Recently, with the evolution of deep neural networks such as WaveNet [ 78 ], natural-sounding voices can be synthesized in near real-time [ 96 ]. Studies have been performed on fast and high-quality synthesis [ 73 , 85 ]. Research on improving the expressiveness of synthetic voices, such as expressing emotions, has also been conducted [ 59 , 62 , 89 ]. Tan et al. [ 95 ] pointed out that existing TTS systems are still not as natural as humans and studied more natural speech synthesis systems. The field of adaptive TTS, which uses a user's real utterances, is also being actively studied [ 4 , 17 , 96 ]. Recently, considering the use of TTS for conversation, a few researchers have conducted studies on spontaneous conversational speech synthesis [ 22 , 46 , 71 , 94 ] that expresses spontaneous behaviors such as hesitation and breathing [ 22 ].

However, most studies on TTS have focused on one-way audio content (e.g., automatic dubbing or screen reading) [ 2 , 32 ], machine–machine dialogue (e.g., conversational podcast) [ 94 ], or machine–human dialogue (e.g., conversational agent) [ 46 , 71 ]. Although TTS is utilized for real-time human–human communications in the AAC field [ 6 , 11 , 37 ], there is not enough discussion on a suitable speech synthesis system configuration considering AAC users. Fiannaca et al. [ 37 ] noted that various recent TTS technologies have not yet been applied to AAC. Discord, a communication app for gamers, provides a TTS feature, but it is just a TTS bot reading the message as it is when a user types “/tts” [ 64 ]. There are no other interfaces or features. In this work, we explore user needs for TTS-based AAC in videoconferencing through a user-oriented approach and discuss design considerations for speech synthesis systems.

3 STUDY 1: METHOD

The goal of Study 1 is to investigate the current videoconferencing experience in a situation where users feel a contextual challenge in speaking and to determine whether the TTS speaking tool could support this situation. We observed multiparty videoconferencing using the technology probe methodology [ 49 ] to confirm the possibility of the TTS tool for videoconferencing. We organized four teams of four people and conducted a user test consisting of three meetings per team to observe their experience according to the speaking channel.

We designated one participant per team as PX (participant X) and controlled PX's experimental conditions in the three meetings as follows:

  • First meeting: PX was in a public library where speaking is undesirable, and the TTS tool was not provided.
  • Second meeting: The TTS speaking tool was provided in the same space as the first meeting.
  • Third meeting: The TTS speaking tool was available, and the PX could freely speak in a private conference room.

To minimize the differences in the proceeding style from affecting each meeting experience, we asked each team to elect a leader among the participants, except for PX. Therefore, each team consisted of one PX, one leader, and two participants (e.g., Team 1: PX1 [participants under environmental control], PA1 [team leader], PB1, and PC1).

3.1 Probe design

A simple TTS speaking tool was developed as a web application for a technology probe (Figure 2 ). If users type a message on the “Input Page” and send it using the send button or “Enter” key, all other interlocutors who have turned on the “Output Page” can hear the synthesized voice. We designed the input area to be large enough to increase user accessibility to the tool. Considering that actual speech utterances are composed in real-time, and the following utterance contents can change even while speaking, we created a “Speak word by word” feature (shown as “Speak immediately” in Figure 2a) that can be used to deliver a synthesized speech in more real-time. With this mode activated, the typed message is queued whenever the user presses the “Spacebar.” If no synthesized speech is being played, all queued texts are synthesized and played at once. Although the primary function of the “Output Page” was to play the synthesized speech, the transmitted text was designed to be displayed to prevent failures due to not pronouncing the text properly. We thought it might help for some participants perceive the conversation more easily. Because the main purpose of this study was to identify the challenges of the current experience and the possibility of using TTS in real-time online conversation, further specific features were not considered at this point.

3.2 Implementation

The probe was implemented as a web application using HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. Naver's Clova Speech Synthesis API [ 82 ] was used for speech synthesis. The synthesized voice and typed text were sent to other interlocutors through the Firebase Realtime Database [ 38 ]. The Firebase Realtime Database delivered the synthesized voice instantly, but synthesizing the voice using the Clova API took about a second.

3.3 Participants

Experimenting with groups that already have regular meetings can lead to meeting in a way that has already been negotiated, and social relationships can influence the content of the meeting. However, we thought it would be hard to consider each experience of three consecutive meetings as similar conditions if this study was conducted with people who did not know each other. In particular, since this study had to produce a situation in which the PX inevitably participated in a meeting in a public library and found it hard to speak with a voice, proceeding with proper ice-breaking is almost impossible. Therefore, to compare meetings under similar conditions as much as possible, we conducted this study with a group that knew each other and had meeting experience.

We distributed a preliminary screener survey to online university communities and company communities in South Korea. 35 respondents answered that they had ever did not fully convey what they intended to speak because they felt challenged to speak due to the surrounding contexts of having other people around them. They responded that they partially conveyed or did not speak in those contexts. Because we wanted to inspect situations in which PXs actually felt challenged to speak, we selected PXs among those 35 respondents. We finally recruited 4 PXs, who could participate in this study along with 3 acquaintances who had regular meeting experience together (Table 1 ). All 16 participants had experience in multiparty videoconferencing, in which two-way communication continued, and answered that they had no difficulty using Zoom and typing text. All participants were Koreans and provided informed consent for this study.

3.4 Procedure

Before the user test, only PXs were given a tutorial on using the TTS speaking tool and were informed the overall procedure. The PXs were also informed that they have to participate in a quiet library prepared in advance. We explained to the PXs that difficulties could arise due to technical issues and that they could ask for help or discontinue the study at any time without panelty. Other participants were unaware that the TTS speaking tool was being used in this study until the beginning of the second meeting. We also instructed the PXs to inform other participants that they were in an environment where it was undesirable to speak with voice during the first and second meetings. We asked PXs to hand over the leader to someone else if they were elected as the leader. We provided PXs with a sufficient practice time to become accustomed to the TTS tool. Other participants wore earphones and participated in quiet, private spaces.

The study consisted of an introduction, three meetings (up to 30 min each), and a group interview (30 min). The entire process was performed using Zoom [ 115 ]. At the first meeting, the PXs were instructed not to speak with their voices because they were in a quiet library (Figure 3a). We secretly hired someone to study in advance to make PXs feel a psychological challenge to speak. A researcher stayed near the place so that we could respond immediately if PXs encountered difficulties unrelated to the study. After the first meeting, we explained the probe to all the participants and allowed PXs to use it. All participants gave their consent to proceed with the study. The participants followed a brief tutorial at this point to familiarize themselves with the probe. After the second meeting, the PX was moved to a private conference room (Figure 3b) for the final meeting.

To prevent distinction in the participants’ prior knowledge and interests from affecting the meeting experience, we provided three topics dealing with the group to which all team members belonged for three meetings. The three topics covered different causal areas to minimize the reuse of the discussion points. The topics were presented in a random order to reduce the presentation order effect.

  • Planning an event for department students who had not met in person for a while due to COVID-19
  • Planning a new space in the department building for students who stay there for an extended period
  • Planning a departmental program to support students who live in the suburbs and want more academic inspiration

We asked for simple results to observe the overall process. Work forms and examples were provided by Google Slides. The form requirements were as follows: 1) title of the idea, 2) background, 3) summary, and 4) details. Examples of the results were provided in advance to help participants understand. We determined that the reviewed contribution and participation score of each participant could help interpret the overall experience. Therefore, we conducted peer reviews at the end of each meeting using a 7-points Likert scale. All participants responded about each participant's contribution to the meeting outcome and each participant's participation level regardless of the outcome. After the three meetings, a group interview was conducted to understand the causal relationship between the experiences. The interview was semi-structured [ 7 ], and we presented topics such as overall impressions, impressive moments for each meeting, uncomfortable points at each meeting, each other's presence, sense of belonging in the meeting, and quality of the conversation. We encouraged participants to express their opinions, empathize, and discuss freely.

3.5 Data Collection and Analysis

Three types of data were collected: recorded videos of the entire process, peer reviews of each meeting, and qualitative interview data about the overall experience. Two types of screens were recorded as videos: Zoom screens and participants’ working slide screens. All meeting contents and group interviews were transcribed, including audio output from the TTS probe, and the non-verbal data were refined and reorganized from recorded videos.

Two researchers qualitatively analyzed the collected data with a thematic coding approach [ 13 ]. To derive the characteristics and challenges of text chat and the TTS tool, we repeatedly reviewed the recorded non-verbal data and the transcribed scripts to extract the initial code. Data from the third meeting was considered an ideal videoconferencing situation and was mainly used as references to understand the contents of the first and second meetings. It was useful to understand the participants’ established meeting style. Because the peer review data lacked the amount for statistical analysis, it was used as supplementary data to interpret the qualitative data. We went through reflective and flexible progress until a full consensus was reached on all data, with a focus on reflecting different perspectives of the same data on the code. The relationships of the participants were also taken into account for iterative code modifications. During analysis, we found that although there were differences in how meetings proceeded between teams, each meeting within a team proceeded similarly regarding decision-making, turn-taking strategy, and speech manner.

4 STUDY 1: RESULTS AND FINDINGS

4.1 Results of the peer evaluation

The peer evaluation results showed that differences in the PXs’ scores between the three meetings were more noticeable than those of the non-PX participants (Figure 4 ). Assuming that the condition of the third meeting, all uttered by voice, is ideal, the scoreboard could mean that the condition of the second meeting, where PXs used the TTS speaking tool, supported a more equitable participation than of the first meeting, where PXs used text chats. In the interviews, some participants mentioned that speaking via text chats did not seem like participating in a meeting. PA3, the leader of Team 3, said that PX3 seemed almost nonexistent at the first meeting. Unlike other PXs, Team 1’s PX scored very well in all meetings, whereas the score of PA1, who was the leader of Team 1, gradually decreased. Regarding this outcome, PC1 explained that as PX1 became progressively more liberated from joining the conversation, he showed a strong leadership growth, which resulted in decreased participation and contribution from PA1 (the team leader).

4.2 Characteristics and challenges of speaking through text chats during videoconferencing

All PXs delivered their message through texts in the first meeting. They used the Zoom chat or texted out what they wanted to say on Google Slides. This indicates that current videoconferencing users in speaking-restrained contexts could use text chats as the only alternative communication method. Therefore, we analyzed the characteristics and challenges of speaking through text chat to understand the empirical issues that current videoconferencing users could face.

4.2.1 Fast understanding. The participants said that expressing what to say in the written text tended to entail refining the content. Therefore, messages conveyed as texts are likely to be more structured than those conveyed as spoken speech. Moreover, reading the same amount of messages is faster than listening. Therefore, text chats can be understood more quickly than voice utterances.

4.2.2 Asynchronous delivery of speech. When PXs used Zoom chats or composed text messages in Google Slides, text utterances often lost effectiveness because others did not capture PXs’ messages on time. When no one noticed the text messages of the PXs, the messages were frequently unintentionally ignored.

PA2 : ( speaking in voice ) Sorry, I just saw your chat.

“It was very frustrating when people couldn't figure it out. I wanted to be passionate, but I felt I couldn't participate well.” (PX4)

We observed situations where only some interlocutors checked the text, whereas others did not. When PC1 read PX1’s idea proposed by the text chat, the leader (PA1) misunderstood it as PC1’s idea and proceeded with the discussion. In other words, the PXs did not receive other people's attention when they wanted.

4.2.3 Tiresome checking process. When delivering utterances in the text, the other participants had to switch windows or shift their gaze to a narrow chat window to check them. The three PXs gave up using Zoom chat and created text boxes on Google Slides to deliver their messages. They did this because people generally do not check the Zoom chat very well and think that it is tiresome. The participants expressed the annoyance of checking text chats when the PXs used the Zoom chat.

PB3 : ( speaking in voice ) Then, could you text on Google Slides instead of Zoom? We can't see it because we're looking at the slide.

4.2.4 No feedback on who checked it. The PXs had no way of knowing who had read their text chats. PX1 requested verbal feedback several times to confirm this finding. The other participants had to either directly tell them that they had captured the texts or read them as they were. Responding to text messages without these processes made it difficult for others to understand the flow of the conversation. Mainly, the leaders briefed the PXs’ text messages, which they said interfered with leading the meetings.

“I had no choice but to wait for someone to read others my chat.” (PX2)

PX1 : ( speaking through text chat ) When you see this, say oh yeah!

4.2.5 No turn-taking process. In the second and third meetings, the PXs refrained from transmitting utterances while the others were speaking. Contrarily, in the first meeting, the PXs freely sent texts even while others were speaking. This indicates that the users do not consider the conversation turns when speaking in the text. PA1 stated that it is selfish to continue texting while another user speaks.

“Texting a lot of utterances continuosly is not a problem for text chat users. But, in a conversation, we have to be considerate of others. Sometimes, we hand over the turn to someone else and wait even if we want to say something. Chat users don't seem to do that.” (PA1)

Texting utterances formed a new conversation flow independent of voice conversations. When only some of the interlocutors checked the text chat and answered them by text chats, a new conversation thread was formed. The new conversation thread generated by the text chat sometimes reached a different consensus from the voice discussion.

4.2.6 Weak presence. The participants said that what was communicated via chats was not perceived as very important because of its weak presence. They said that the presence of PXs speaking with text was weak and unequal. Some stated that participating via text chats alone seems insincere.

4.3 Possibilities of the TTS speaking tool

Current videoconferencing users were able to deliver messages as texts in a speech-restricted context. However, we identified that participating only in text chats has many challenges in multiparty videoconferencing, where voice conversations mainly occur. Because the text chats were delivered asynchronously to the interlocutors, an additional synchronization process was required for the discussion. The process of others reading the entire text unnecessarily lengthened the meeting time. Otherwise, miscommunication occurs repeatedly. As a result, text chat users became dependent on other speakers and passively engaged in voice conversations. When the PXs actively wanted to state something, they were disappointed with the text chats as they could not attract attention. 3 of 4 PXs reported low a sense of belonging in the first meeting.

“It seemed like I wasn't participating in the first meeting. At the second and third meetings, I felt I'm in there.” (PX3)

The TTS speaking tool has solved most of the challenges of conversing through text chats. During the second meeting, all the PXs preferred to use the TTS tool rather than text chats. The PXs evaluated that the biggest difference was that it was clear whether or not a message was delivered when using the TTS tool. The PXs described that its clarity of the delivery had huge leverage on their meeting experience. Because the TTS speaking tool promptly plays the message in a synthesized voice, all interlocutors can check the message simultaneously without any additional process. The TTS speaking tool users tried to take conversation turns while considering others. They were able to actively decide the delivery time for each utterance. Overall, it can support the active participation of users in speaking-restrained contexts.

“It was great when I couldn't speak. Text chat has no way of knowing whether my chat was delivered. But, TTS undoubtedly delivered my message by voice.” (PX2)

However, we also observed various empirical failures of the TTS speaking tool because of the fidelity of our probe. Our probe system, which only transmitted text into speech, forcibly took others’ communication turns when its synthesized voice overlapped with others. The participants said that frequent voice overlapping and low expressiveness lowered their experience. They stated that the TTS speaking tool should address these issues. Therefore, we conducted the following study to explore considerations for designing a TTS speaking tool that can support a better videoconferencing experience.

5 STUDY 2: METHOD

Based on Study 1, we confirmed the limitations of text chatting and the potential of the TTS speaking tools when users feel restrained in speaking due to the surrounding contexts. However, Study 1 was insufficient to understand in-depth considerations for designing an effective TTS speaking tool. This is mainly because our probe's insufficient controllability has been an obstacle to the communication experience. Therefore, we prepared a new viable probe to stimulate the users’ feelings and perceptions for Study 2.

The goal of Study 2 is that investigate users’ experience speaking through TTS in videoconferencing and listening to others’ speeches played through TTS. We thus did not compare experiences according to the means of speaking in Study 2. Instead, we attempted to identify empirical challenges and user needs using the minimum viable product [ 28 , 30 ] approach. Study 2 consisted of experiencing videoconferencing similar to Study 1’s second meeting (four people participating and one speaking via the TTS speaking tool) and sketching ideas about the TTS speaking tool. We encouraged the participants to freely share and discuss each other's ideas because we wanted to deeply understand their perception of the probe and each idea's meaning rather than obtain possible solutions. We obtained university IRB approval.

5.1 Probe design

We modified the probe to meet the minimum viable level based on factors that significantly interfered with communication in Study 1 (Figure 5 ). The previous probe had no means of dealing with voice overlapping, so the non-PX speakers had to unconditionally yield the communication turn to the TTS tool user (PX). Because this was critical to all interlocutors’ experiences, we added features to stop or pause speech to the improved probe. Holding the “Tab” key pauses the speech being played and pressing the “Esc” key completely cancels the remaining and queued speech (keyboard or upper right buttons of Figure 5a). We believe these features will help the user quickly control the utterance. In a previous study, the meeting experience was also disturbed when the speech rate of the synthesized voice was too slow compared to that of other interlocutors. Therefore, we added a slide to control the utterance speed of the synthesized voice (bottom right of Figure 5a). A volume controller was added to the listeners because the fixed volume of the previous probe was a major hindrance to their listening experience (bottom of Figure 5b). To observe the user's expectations in the voice selection, we added four voice options (upper left of Figure 5a) and a pitch adjustment slider (lower right of Figure 5a). We felt that the previous study did not sufficiently gather users’ perceptions of the ”Speak word by word” feature and showing text log, so we kept these features.

Study 2 was performed not to evaluate the improved probe's usability but to understand users’ general feelings and perceptions of using the TTS speaking tool. Therefore, our probe is designed to be faithful to the features supported by the current TTS APIs and the features were introduced in the most basic form, similar to the TTS demo interfaces provided by Google (Figure 6a) and Microsoft (Figure 6b). Considering that there is a lack of discussion on the TTS interface for real-time online conversation and that there are only basic few actual examples, we believed our probe could work as a stimulus to show users a new experience.

5.2 Implementation

The probe for Study 2 was implemented as a web application using HTML, CSS, TypeScript, and React. During the implementation period, as the Naver Clova API was renewed as Clova voice [ 20 ], we encountered several technical issues. Considering our study schedule, we used the Google Text-to-Speech API [ 19 ] as an alternative. The Google Text-to-Speech API supports the speech synthesis markup language (SSML) [ 97 ] and supports four WaveNet [ 78 ] voices for Korean, so we judged it is suitable for our study. As with previous probes, all the data was transmitted through the Firebase Realtime Database [ 38 ]. The time taken from the user's text transmission to voice output took an average of 0.973 seconds (SD = 0.096) when measured in a laboratory environment.

5.3 Participants

To recruit participants, we distributed a screener survey to online university communities. The survey asked about the type and duration of videoconferencing experienced and the spaces where they participated. In Study 2, since all participants were given time to use the probe, screening was conducted for volunteers who answered that they had been unwilling to speak because of the surrounding context. We recruited 20 participants with different types of videoconferencing experience to understand the diverse perspectives on using TTS tools during videoconferencing (Table 2 ). All the participants answered they had experienced participating in multiparty videoconferencing, in which two-way communication continued, in public spaces (e.g., cafés, public transportation, communal living spaces, and communal workspaces). All the participants were members of the same university or graduate school.

As Study 2 aimed to understand the user-centered perspective on the TTS speaking tool, unlike in Study 1, the teams were formed without grasping the acquaintance relationship between participants. We tried to organize each team to include diverse types of videoconferencing experiences because we wanted to understand overall perception and feeling of using the probe. All participants were Koreans between the ages of 21 and 31 and provided informed consent for this study. None of them had participated in Study 1.

5.4 Procedure

The participatory design workshop consisted of four sessions: (1) introduction and ice-breaking, (2) exploring the TTS speaking tool experience, (3) individual brainstorming, and (4) group discussion. All the sessions were conducted as face-to-face meetings in a prepared conference room, except for (2) exploring the TTS speaking tool experience. All the participants were informed that they could discontinue study participation at the anytime without penalty.

Session 1. Introduction and ice-breaking . General information about the experiment was provided, including goals and specific procedures. During the ice-breaking, each team shared their videoconferencing experience and elected a leader for session 2. Basic information about the TTS technology and tutorials on how to use the TTS speaking tool were provided. Each participant had time to become accustomed to the probe. After this session, all participants moved to each prepared private conference room and participated in a Zoom videoconferencing room to conduct the next session.

Session 2. Exploring the TTS speaking tool experience . In this session, each participant used the TTS speaking tool in a context where it was burdensome to speak during videoconferencing. Each team chose one of three casual meeting topics: (1) designing local slogans and tour guides, (2) planning pop-up booths for the school festival, and (3) planning to open a café for school students. With the selected topic, they had a Zoom videoconferencing collaborating on shared Google Slides documents. Each participant, one by one, took part in the meeting using the TTS speaking tool for approximately 15 min in a quiet public space (Figure 3a, same location as the library in Study 1), whereas other participants joined the meeting in the private conference room. After one participant's “Exploring turn of using the TTS tool” ended, the next participant moved to the quiet public space to use the TTS tool, and the previous user moved to the private conference room to resume the meeting. This process was repeated four times to allow all participants to use the TTS tool for 15 min. We provided memo pads and pens for the participants to jot down any ideas regarding their needs, difficulties, and expectations during the meeting. After four cycles of the experience session, the participants gathered back in the conference room where Session 1 was held and shared their experiences and thoughts on using the given tool.

Session 3. Individual brainstorming . An individual brainstorming session (Figure 7a) was conducted for 25 min. We provided a brainstorming template consisting of three parts: background situations or challenging points, requirements and reasons, and ideas (Figure 7b). The participants were asked to provide as many ideas as possible.

Session 4. Group discussion . A group discussion was conducted for 45 min to share ideas with other participants. We encouraged the participants to share ideas that emerged during conversations and opinions on others’ ideas. Excessive criticism of others’ ideas and opinions was restrained.

5.5 Data Collection and Analysis

Throughout the study, we collected several types of data: videos of “Exploring the TTS speaking tool experience” session, audio recordings of the group discussion, individual brainstorming results, and idea memos. In the session “Exploring the TTS speaking tool experience,” three types of videos were collected: the TTS tool users’ behavior from over the shoulder, a zoom screen, and the slide screen. We transcribed the recorded videos and selected a thematic coding approach [ 13 ] as a qualitative data analysis methodology for this study. The lead author transcribed and reorganized the raw data. Idea memos and brainstorming results were reorganized with group discussion scripts referencing them. Ideas not mentioned are listed separately. The lead author repeatedly reviewed every situation in the recorded video and extracted the primary code for each raw data. Subsequently, three researchers reviewed and refined the entire code and selected 24 main themes about “Using strategies,” “Perception of the synthesized voice,” “Physical challenges of users,” “Psychological challenges of users,” “Challenges of interlocutors communicating with the synthesized voice,” “Positive opinions and potential,” and “Expectations and needs.” We varied the researchers’ engagement levels in Study 2 to minimize the bias that researchers may have when analyzing the data. The lead author engaged in the entire process, whereas the second author participated in Study 2 and the analysis. The third author participated only in the analysis. As a result, we could actively discuss the meaning of each code based on the different levels of understanding of the study data and reflect various perspectives on the same data. We strictly matched the consensus on the meaning of the data, applied our themes to recorded videos of the “Exploring the TTS speaking tool experience” session again, and performed an iteration of adding, deleting, and merging the extracted themes through repeated face-to-face discussions.

6 STUDY 2: RESULTS AND FINDINGS

In order to explore design considerations for a TTS speaking tool, Study 2 observed a multi-party videoconferencing experience using a probe designed in the most elementary form. Our participants discovered the effectiveness and potential of the TTS speaking tool and suggested various ideas implying empirical issues and expectations. In this section, we first introduce seven empirical problems that can be experienced when videoconferencing with TTS speaking tool users. We also present seven themes on user expectations and potential demands derived from the participants’ ideas and discussion contents. Then we introduce the positive feedback and possible use cases mentioned by participants.

6.1 Empirical challenges in videoconferencing with TTS speaking tool users

6.1.1 Not agile turn-taking. Participants’ typing speed affected their turn-taking experience when using the TTS tool. Speakers with their voices could take a turn immediately by uttering their voice, but TTS tool users should complete typing to take a turn. We provided a “Speak word by word” feature to help users take turns faster, but that feature was rarely used because participants preferred to write a text corpus and send the corpus at once as using text chat. P15, who had a remarkably fast typing speed, rated the overall experience very positively, but more than half of the participants cited difficulties with turn-taking. Six participants said that they occasionally missed the chance to speak because the conversation topic had changed during typing. The participants said that this problem was exacerbated when they had long content to speak or when the overall tempo of the meeting was fast. They attempted to minimize the turn-taking issue by composing utterances concisely, such as speaking using only words.

Dialogue in “Exploring the TTS speaking tool experience” session.

P9 : ( speaking in voice ) Does anyone have an idea for the name or concept?

P12 : ( speaking through TTS ) goose cafe.

The time required for synthesizing also made agile turn-taking difficult. The latest TTS system, based on neural networks, converts text data into speech immediately upon transmission, but it still takes a little time. The synthesis time was short, but it was sufficient for someone else to take a turn and start to speak, so it caused frequent voice overlapping. Due to the slow turn-taking performance of the probe, participants found it particularly difficult to intervene while others were speaking.

“There was a slight delay in playing the voice, so it was difficult to restrict the other's utterance at the desired time.” (P9, team leader)

6.1.2 Creating silence when repeatedly transmitting utterances over TTS. When the TTS tool users sent texts continuously instead of delivering all desired utterences at once, there was silence between played speeches because of the typing. The participants with slower typing speeds created longer silence durations. Therefore, other interlocutors had to wait in silence for the next speech to be typed by the TTS users. During the silence, some interlocutors misunderstood that the TTS tool users had finished their speech, which caused voice overlapping.

“I often made other people wait while typing, which was a bit embarrassing.” (P6)

6.1.3 Insufficient expressiveness for text chat-styled nuances. As long as the TTS technology is used, typing text is an unavoidable activity. This type of input makes users express what they would say similarly to when typing a text chat. The TTS tool users naturally composed utterances in text style.

“I have a slightly different way of thinking and making speeches at chatting and speaking. But when I used the TTS tool, I made my speeches just like chatting.” (P7)

P15 : ( speaking through TTS ) Hi :) [Hi colon closing parenthesis] The contents in “ [] ” are words spoken by the TTS speaking tool.

However, the TTS system did not adequately express the nuances of typed text. The participants added punctuation marks to properly convey desired nuances such as “...”, “ ∼,” and “!”, but the Google Cloud TTS used in our probe did not reflect these well. It always synthesized the speech in the same way, even if the user added multiple “!.” In Korean, there are numerous nuance variations of “네” (i.e., yes), such as “넵,” “넹,” and “넴,” and the nuance difference is greater than the difference between “Yes,” “Yeah,” “Yup,” and “Yep.” However, the TTS did not capture the subtle nuances of Korean. The participants said it was awkward for the chat-styled text to be synthesized without depicting nuances.

“‘넹’ should be said more charmingly. If I put ‘...’ at the end, it would be better to say it a little timidly.” (P5)

“I used a lot of expressions like‘Ah ∼ ’ and‘Um ∼,’ but it came out a bit strange. It wasn't what I expected.” (P17)

This issue was also the case when the user wanted to express a laughing sound. Current TTS services provided by Google, Amazon, and Microsoft does not have the ability to synthesize a laughing voice. Most of the users typed “ㅋㅋㅋㅋㅋ” (i.e., lollollol) to express the sound of laughter, but the TTS service played that gently by pronouncing “Kikikikikieok” in a dull tone. The participants said that they felt artificial and awkward.

“It's odd to just pronounce it as‘Kikikikiki’.” (P4)

6.1.4 Psychological burden on permanently displayed text messages. We designed the text displayed on the “Output Page” with the expectation that it would be helpful to users from time to time. Participants mostly agreed on the need for the original text to be subtitled, but P1 and P3 expressed strong negative opinions about having it logged permanently. P1 said that logging only his messages into text sometimes made him hesitate to speak freely. P3 said he was ashamed of displaying his grammatical errors permanently. P3 argues that if the tool record users’ text, it should also record other interlocutors’ utterances using Speech-to-Text to reduce users’ psychological burden. P11 and P18 wrote the idea of staking other interlocutors's utterances together in the “Output Page”. P10, P16, and P17 presented the idea that text would be displayed around that user's camera screen only while the synthesized voice was playing.

“Verbal conversation has a volatile nature, but TTS has logs. In a formal meeting, it would be nice to be recorded, but in a casual meeting, it was a bit burdensome for me to logging all my text.” (P1)

6.1.5 Siri-like ear-catching synthesized voice. There was no noise or reverberation phenomenon in the synthesized voice. Six participants said that it was awkward to have casual conversations with flawless synthesized voices. The probe was applied with Google Cloud WaveNet TTS, which emphasizes a natural speech synthesis technique, but the participants rated it as an unnatural and artificial voice, similar to Siri.

“It was a bit confusing when it felt like I was talking with Siri.” (P2)

Because of the noise-free and clear voice of synthesized speech, eight out of the 20 participants said that they recognized synthesized voices as having a higher priority than human voices. They said that it sometimes drew more attention than necessary. P6, P11, and P14 said they refrained from unimportant conversations and spoke only necessary words.

“When I used it, I got too much attention. So, I only said what I really wanted to say.” (P11)

6.1.6 Difficulty in distinguishing TTS speaking tool users. Unless a model trained with a real user voice is used, the synthesized voice is not identical to the user's voice. Seven participants said it was confusing to identify who was using the TTS tool. The participants noted that it would be more difficult to identify the speaker if multiple participants used the TTS tool simultaneously.

“It was confused about who was speaking through TTS.” (P12)

6.1.7 Sense of disparity in hearing third-party voices. P3 and P13 said that when they used the TTS speaking tool, they felt that the virtual agent was reading their utterances for them. P2, P5, and P11 stated that it was awkward for their utterances to not come out with their real voices. Six participants (P1, P8, P13, P15, P16, and P19) said that listening to the speech they typed in a third-party voice did not feel weird, but other interlocutors still felt a sense of disparity when the tool users spoke by unbecoming voices to them. P1 and P13 used voices that differed from their gender, and the other participants said it was very awkward because the voice was very different from what they had expected. Most participants wished for TTS users to use voices similar to their own.

“It was uncanny because the voice didn't sound like mine.” (P5)

“It felt more like an assistant was speaking for me. It wasn't awkward.” (P13)

“Its voices didn't match the real users’ voices, so it felt unnatural.” (P20)

6.1.8 Difficulty in distinguishing between network issues and just silence. Because TTS tool users do not move their lips and users’ manipulations are transmitted through the network, typing is almost no different from freezing owing to network issues. One group (P13–P16) experienced a network issue due to the heavy rain during the workshop. They said that it was difficult to distinguish between network issues and intentional speech manipulations. The participants also misunderstood pausing with the “Tab” key as a network error.

“We couldn't tell whether there was a network latency or the user didn't type anything.” (P15)

6.2 User expectations and needs on the TTS speaking tool for videoconferencing

6.2.1 Typing aids. The typing time was the major cause of several empirical problems. The participants with lower typing speeds had challenges in taking communication turns at the desired time and frequently created silence. Therefore, typing time-saving features were required. The participants suggested ideas for providing frequently used sentences as presets or registering them to shortcut keys (Table 3 ). They said that these presets and shortcuts would reduce unnecessary conversation delays and enrich interactivity. In particular, more than half of the participants aksed for interfaces to express empathizing and response tokens quickly. Some participants expected features to store and reload typed texts because they occasionally wanted to say something else first while typing some texts. Type correction and autocompletion were also suggested.

6.2.2 User status indicators. When participating in videoconferencing with the TTS speaking tool, there are fewer clues about the beginning and end of speaking than with a voice. P12 said that the probe had no clue to convey a willingness to say while the voice speakers could hint at it through non-lexical interjections such as “Um....” Therefore, it was difficult for the participants to know whether the TTS user had something to say or had finished speaking. These issues make it difficult for TTS tool users to start speech during others are speaking and make it easy for other interlocutors to interrupt TTS tool users’ speech. Most commercial TTS services synthesize received text into completed speech utterances, which made it more difficult to notice the end of a speech. When the TTS tool user did not send what they were going to say at once, each utterance was expressed as a complete sentence, and other interlocutors often misunderstood that the user finished speech.

“I finished speaking and did nothing, but people kept looking at me, thinking I didn't finish yet.” (P5)

Our participants thought that showing typing indicators such as “…” on the videoconferencing screen could solve these problems. Some participants suggested the idea of playing typed sounds, white noise, or filler words during typing.

“It would be nice to automatically fill the silence between each speech, such as‘Um...’ and‘Uh...’ while I'm typing.” (P8)

The P15 and P20 stated that the status of the network issue and the user operations should also be displayed to distinguish them.

6.2.3 Turn-taking aids. Most participants thought that showing the TTS tool users’ status would facilitate turn-taking, but three participants requested a more advanced turn-taking aid. P16 proposed a feature to hold synthesized speech automatically while another person was speaking. P14 presented a system that takes turns only when users set the importance of speech as high and automatically hands over turns to others in other cases. P8 suggested a way to prevent the loss of a turn during synthesis by playing the “Um...” sound as soon as users transmit text.

6.2.4 Paralanguage expressions. Paralanguage is a communication component, including voice qualities and vocalization, used to coordinate the meaning of verbal communication or convey emotions [ 99 ]. It includes pausing speech; manipulation of prosodies such as voice speed, rhythm, pitch, and stress of speech; and various non-lexical interjections such as “Mhm” and “Huh.” The participants emphasized the need for features related to speech stress control, emotional expression, and backchanneling in addition to the features provided by our probe.

Some participants said that pausing and the pitch and speech rate control features were useful, and others said they wanted to use them well. The participants mainly wanted to deliver utterances at a pitch and speech rate that matched their emotions. Eight participants stated that they needed additional means to control the volume or stress of their speech in following situations:

  • When users want to express confidence level in the speech contents
  • When users want to express importance level in the speech contents
  • When users want to adjust the intensity of non-lexical responses (in usually weaker than other utterances)
  • At the beginning of utterances (fade-in for a smooth initiation)
  • At the end of utterances (fade-out for blurring the end)

More than half of the participants wanted interfaces to express their positive emotions in their utterances. By providing various emoji buttons, a feature that synthesizes utterances according to the moods of the emoji was proposed. A system of playing sounds of laughter and “Umm...” was also proposed.

“It would be nice to have emoji-to-speech as well as text-to-speech.” (P8)

6.2.5 Intuitive interfaces and automation features. The participants said that they wanted to control the pitch and speech rate but had limited time to control them because of the slow typing speed and refining process. In particular, they emphasized that small sliders are unsuitable for real-time adjustment, and intuitive interfaces for quick operation are required.

“Sliders were so inconvenient. It was difficult to drag, and its values were not intuitive. Multiple buttons might be better.” (P9)

Some participants suggested that the system should automatically set them up for the situation. P1 showed a system that automatically adjusts the pitch and speech rate by recognizing the user's face through a camera, and P5, P18, and P20 presented auto-adjusting features recognizing the speaking style or conversation context of other speakers. P14 mentioned an automated feature that adjusts the mood of speech based on the typed texts.

“We don't think consciously about the speed and pitch when speaking, so it is odd to adjust them manually.” (P18)

6.2.6 Various voice options and customizable voices. The participants wanted more diverse voices. There were expectations of selecting the preferred voice among the many options. However, the more dominant idea was that various voices should be provided to easily distinguish TTS tool users. Some participants said that tags describing voices should be available to easily find the proper voices. Other participants suggested systems to quickly find voices similar to themselves using various options. Six participants came up with the idea of using a speech synthesis model trained with real user voices. In particular, P3 raised whether it is ethically right to express one's opinion in a voice different from one's own. However, others expressed varying concerns such as privacy issues and the uncanny valley phenomenon and said they would not use it. P1 emphasized that using TTS with one's voice carries the risk of misappropriation, which can lead to fatal ethical issues. P13 pointed out that the recorded voices usually sound different from what people envisioned for themselves.

“I want to use my voice TTS once the security issues are resolved.” (P3)

If we record our voice and listen to it, it sounds different from what we know. I'm not a fan of my recorded voice. Maybe TTS too.” (P13)

The participants expected the capability to customize the voices. P15 and P17 suggested that personalized voice settings interfaces fine-tune the tone, speech rate, and mood of voice. P16 proposed a feature that calibrates the synthesized voice to suit users’ voices.

“Just like Photoshopping a selfie, we could edit its voice better than the real. I want to speak with a more deep, sonorous voice that still sounds like mine.” (P17)

6.2.7 Interfaces that do not interfere with user activities. The participants advised that the TTS speaking tool should not interfere with editing materials or web surfing. During these activities, the participants considered switching windows every time to type in the TTS tool tiresome. They thought that a floating interface could be appropriate so that it could be easily accessed and used only in desired cases.

“Like the spotlight feature on Mac, it would be nice if the type area pops up with hotkey.” (P4)

In addition, our probes required holding the “Tab” key to pause speech, and the participants said that they could not switch screens or edit documents while holding the button. They expected that the pausing interface would function as a toggle switch.

6.3 Reconfirming the potential of the TTS speaking tool

We have already confirmed the potential of the TTS speaking tool in Study 1, but we could also reconfirm it in Study 2. Despite the various empirical challenges and additional expectations, most participants agreed with the need for a TTS speaking tool in videoconferencing. The participants said that they have frequently encountered speaking-restrained spatial contexts in public or communal spaces and could not properly participate in videoconferencing. Uncontrollable noises and spaces where people concentrate or sleep are mainly mentioned as the surrounding contexts. P7 and P17 said that they had restrained their speech in videoconferencing because they were uncomfortable with the surrounding family hearing their speech. Our participants said that they had clear problems using text chats in those contexts. P4 said that the videoconferencing flow was repeatedly broken when someone spoke through a text chat.

“During lectures, the professor read all the chats one by one, and it was definitely a problem. It seemed so cumbersome.” (P12)

“Many people usually turn off the chat window or do not check it well. That's why I think using the tool is better.” (P9)

“When I delivered non-face-to-face university lectures last year, I mainly attended videoconferencing in a communal office due to my schedule. One time, screen sharing became turned off while a student was giving a presentation. Because the office was so noisy that day, I typed‘Please share your screen again’ in chat. But, it didn't work. In such cases, I can relate a lot to the lack of chatting.” (P8)

Half of the participants thought that our probe was still uncomfortable, whereas the other half rated it sufficiently useful. The participants who rated our probe as useful enough said that their experience were much better than expected. They said that communicating with the synthesized voice was initially awkward, but they quickly got used to it.

“I was wondering if it would work well, but it was really nice to be able to have a conversation.” (P4)

“It wasn't challenging for me. It is good to participate actively. I want it for my usual meetings.” (P10)

6.4 Other possible use cases for the TTS speaking tool

The participants also suggested other possible use cases in which the TTS speaking tool could be useful, in addition to the situations we have dealt with in this research. P2, P13, and P16 wanted to utilize the TTS tool to deliver private message for videoconferencing. P2 said that current videoconferencing has no way to get others to check private text messages when they have not yet checked them. P2 maintained that the TTS tool would allow voice messages to be delivered secretly without moving the lips. P13 and P16 thought the TTS tool would be useful for privately delivering urgent messages without interrupting the conversation flow while others were talking. P11 presented the idea of using the TTS tool with a translator when users wanted to speak an unfamiliar language. The participants agreed that it allows everyone to speak fluently, even when they do not know the correct pronunciation and accent. Diverse ideas for utilizing the ear-catching characteristics of synthesized voices were also presented. P2 said that it would be useful to deliver important information, and six participants suggested using the tool for freshening up the atmosphere. The idea of using AR avatars together was presented by P1 and P12. P3 and P13 proposed utilizing the TTS tool for ice breaking.

7 DISCUSSION

In this section, we first discuss the usefulness and potential of the TTS speaking tool in videoconferencing by summarizing the findings of the two user-centered studies. We then discuss the design considerations for a real-time TTS speaking tool.

7.1 Could a TTS speaking tool be a useful communication channel in videoconferencing?

Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, videoconferencing has become a new paradigm. As the post-COVID era begins, people have participated in videoconferencing in more diverse spaces. Through our participants, we confirmed that users actually experienced psychological burdens in speaking due to surrounding contexts during videoconferencing. Our participants often had to attend videoconferencing in a noisy or silent communal office. They also had to attend videoconferencing late at night when their family or roommates were sleeping. Our participants said they sometimes don't deliver what they want to say or say only part of it in such situations. Because being partially or completely speechless in a meeting could be detrimental to self-efficacy, and having fewer speakers could also negatively impact the experience of other interlocutors, videoconferencing systems should provide another channel that allows users to voice.

Currently, the only alternative for those users to deliver messages is text chat. However, in Study 1, we found that text chat users disrupted the conversation flow and could not actively engage. These findings mean that even people without speech impairments experience contextual speech restrictions in online communications, and current online communication methods cannot fully support their participation.

In this research, we considered the TTS speaking tool as a practical and feasible support for videoconferencing users in unable-to-speak contexts, as it is already commercialized in various fields, such as voice agents [ 46 ] or automated response systems. Through the two probe studies, we confirmed that the tool could support the active participation of the users. Even though participants in Study 2 experienced various empirical challenges, they were still very positive about the need for a TTS speaking tool. The participants emphasized that the challenges of using only text chat in speech constrained contexts far outweighed the empirical challenges of the TTS speaking tool. These findings mean that it is very important to have instant and synchronous communication in an online collaboration, which aligns with the background of the active introduction of voice communication in the online game [ 101 ]. Based on these findings, we claim that the TTS speaking tool is worth introducing into videoconferencing as an additional speaking channel to support the participation of users who have contextual speech restrictions.

7.2 Discussions on considerations for designing a real-time TTS speaking tool

Then, how should the TTS speaking tool be designed? We originally planned to develop and test the final probe with further improvements based on our findings. However, after conducting two probe studies, we found that its design can diverge in many ways, rather than converging on one optimal solution. Deriving the final probe design was more difficult as we found that its design might be heavily influenced by the meeting contexts and speech synthesizing methods.

We first recognized that actual applications should be accompanied by further contemplation of the domain and service context. For instance, the participants stated that it is relatively less problematic to intercept a turn in a casual meeting, but a defensive turn-taking aid for preventing interception should be required in a formal conference. The way subtitles are presented should also vary greatly depending on the type of meeting. For formal and record-critical meetings, the videoconferencing system can record the TTS voice and other interlocutors’ utterances in chronological order. However, in a casual meeting, it is more appropriate to volatilize the TTS users’ texts just like the other interlocutor's speech to ensure equal participation. Previous studies have reported that people prefer different voice types depending on the context [ 14 ] and platform [ 113 ]. Lee et al. revealed that workers’ perceptions of videoconferencing vary depending on their career field [ 60 ]. Because videoconferencing is being conducted in various domains [ 58 , 103 ], its actual design should reflect more observational user research considering the applied service domain.

We also confirmed that the design of the TTS speaking tool for videoconferencing might vary depending on the speech synthesis methods. We found that known speech synthesis techniques, such as voice adaptation [ 17 , 108 , 114 ] or streaming synthesis [ 69 , 93 , 96 ], could address some of the participants’ expectations and challenges. The user needs and expectations of controlling prosody automatically and reading the typed text more naturally might lead to the future agenda of speech synthesis technology based on previous discussions.

Because our studies were conducted under a specific context where four participants were generating ideas on casual themes, our findings could not guarantee meeting diversity. For example, because we presented a casual conversation theme, the user needs for reflection on voice synthesis of the emotional expression typed in text chat style may have been stronger. Therefore, we thought that diverging design considerations based on our findings would be more meaningful rather than converging to specific design guidelines. Thus, we decided to discuss wide design considerations for the TTS speaking tool to provide a better videoconferencing experience. We believe it would be a meaningful contribution to the HCI community considering the insufficient discussions on the TTS speaking tool. This section presents our discussions on the three aspects of design considerations for the TTS speaking tool.

7.2.1 Considerations on voice. “Voice customizing system” and/or“voice avatar” .

One of the most important considerations when designing a TTS speaking tool is how and what voice should be provided. Simply put, the tool can provide as many voice options as possible. Based on our findings that listeners felt awkward when the synthesized voice differs significantly from the users’, a nudge may be considered to induce the users to use a similar voice to their own. Systems that recommend voices similar to users’ real voices or help users find desirable voices can be implemented. For easy searching, each voice can be described by characteristic tags, which is consistent with the suggestion of Byeon et al. [ 14 ]. Another way to support personalization is to design filtering interfaces for fine-tuning the voice along with the user's taste. An interface that calibrates the chosen voice with the user's prosody is also possible. However, high autonomy over voice control may also be problematic. We need to remember that users ask for voice customizing systems because they want to have a voice that suits them. None of the participants changed TTS voices after choosing a voice they preferred. Therefore, in videoconferencing, there is less need to change the voice during a conversation. Because suddenly changing the voice during meetings may create unnecessary confusion for other interlocutors, the voice customizing system should be designed to avoid frequent changes.

A voice avatar system that manages voice sets as avatars is worthwhile because users wish to have their own voice sets. The system should be able to create and manage diverse variations so that users can respond to different contexts [ 14 ], and interlocutors can easily distinguish speakers. Here, we could also consider introducing voice cloning [ 4 ] and voice adaptation [ 17 , 108 , 114 ] technologies that use users’ voices. However, using these technologies is accompanied by social trust issues [ 63 ] and ethical issues [ 91 ]. The TTS speaking tool that uses a user's near-realistic voice could also threaten human uniqueness [ 35 ], as speech synthesis is a reproducible imitation of human characteristics by non-humans. As Apple's Memoji depicts a user's face, developing a model capable of synthesizing voices with low similarity to the users but with a good identity can be an alternative solution to these issues. Privacy issues and the uncanny valley [ 72 ] phenomenon are still considerations, and the TTS speaking tool should be sufficiently descriptive to allow users to take responsibility for learning their voice.

7.2.2 Considerations on the speech synthesis method. “Prosody control interface” and/or“real-time vocal entrainment” .

The participants tried manipulating the pitch and speech rate to make paralanguage expressions, but it was not easy to adjust them in real-time. Therefore, when designing a TTS speaking tool, it is encouraged to contemplate how users can quickly and easily set their speaking style. Currently, the TTS services of Microsoft Azure and Amazon Poli provide the ability to change the speaking style with keywords such as“cheerful,”“friendly,”“sad,” and“terrified.” However, given that the participants did not have sufficient room to control detail settings in real-time, reading and selecting keywords may not be the best way. Therefore, the TTS speaking tool should provide speech style control in more user-friendly interfaces. The voicesetting interface proposed by Fiannaca et al. [ 37 ] shows a direction in this regard, and our findings imply it is worth examining it for users without speech impairment.

The TTS tool users wanted to regulate the prosody of synthesized voices as similar emotions and moods of other interlocutors. As a way to automate this, applying speech accommodation [ 41 ] to the synthesis process may be considered. Speech accommodation theory (also known as communication accommodation theory) [ 40 ] is defined as the spontaneous entrainment of one speaker into the speaking style of another speaker. Entrainment appears in various areas such as vocabulary selection, speech rate, pitch, and sentence structure. This phenomenon occurs even in short conversations [ 66 ] and has been observed when humans communicate with artificial voices [ 21 , 79 , 90 , 92 , 111 ]. In regular voice conversations between people, the entrainment level directly affects the degree of collaboration and conversational efficiency [ 70 , 83 ], and pitch and voice quality adaptation is related to rapport formation [ 66 ]. In addition, a study showed that social presence is high when entrainment is applied [ 67 ]. Cong et al. [ 22 ] claimed that it is necessary to model this phenomenon and proposed the application of an acoustic context encoder for speech synthesis. Mitsui et al. [ 71 ] introduced a TTS system that predicts the speaking style of entrainment. Our findings reaffirm the need for these studies, and go further than those studies focusing only on acoustic spontaneous behaviors, imply that TTS systems should consider empathy and emotion in conversational contexts. Therefore, if the TTS tool with an automated speech rate and pitch entrainment is applied, the users’ experience could change notably. This should be investigated in detail because there is no understanding of how users experience automated speech accommodation of their speech synthesized by the TTS tool.

“Translating text chat style into spoken language style” or“reading text as is” .

The challenges and needs derived from our participants differ from those in previous AAC studies conducted on people with speech impairment. Our participants were frequent text chat users accustomed to writing chat-style texts and had no physical difficulties communicating with their voices. Therefore, they felt disappointed that TTS could not properly illustrate chat-style text expressions. Pullin et al. [ 84 ] stated that AAC for people with speech impairments needs to support a wider range of tones, and our participants emphasized that paralinguistic expressions beyond this are required. Therefore, it is important to consider how to interpret and synthesize chat-style expressions in written text. Luangrath et al. [ 65 ] defined textual paralanguage as a means to transmit incidental information to written language and summarized various types of paralanguage. Clark et al. [ 18 ] categorized errors and irregular languages as casual chat-style languages. A text normalization study that handles chat-style abbreviations was also conducted [ 48 ]. Based on these studies, it is possible to develop a speech synthesis system that interprets nuances typed in a chat-style and applies them to speech synthesis. If the user places wordplay such as“loooooong” [ 18 ], the system could play stretched speech according to the number of“o.” In other words, the auditory textual paralanguage proposed by Luangrath et al. [ 65 ] can be utilized for TTS. Because studies on developing a laughter synthesis system in the context of TTS were also recently conducted [ 54 , 68 , 98 ], we have the opportunity to synthesize texts for laughter into speech. If users type“lol,” the system could mix laughing sounds with their speeches. The higher the number of“lol,” the louder or faster laughing sounds could be applied.

However, these solutions are difficult to deploy and may not deliver outstanding performance. Given that chat styles vary according to language and culture and are constantly changing [ 43 , 80 , 110 ], interpreting and synthesizing chat styles well would not be achieved with a simple approach. In particular, it is difficult to easily recognize detailed emotional expressions [ 3 ]. Here, we could revisit the ways our participants used the probe. When participants typed a“ ∼ ” or“ㅋㅋ” (i.e., lol), TTS spoke them just as“tilde” and“kiki” (pronunciation of ㅋㅋ in Korean) as they were, but they continued to use those letters regardless. However, they gave up typing“!” because the system neither changed the tone satisfactorily nor read it as it was. P12 said that speaking“tilde” at the end of a speech was perceived as affectionately enough. These results suggest that just reading all written characters as it is could be a good cheap solution. Therefore, unless we could ensure user acceptance [ 26 ] of translating text chat style into spoken language style, the TTS speech tool should honestly read every character verbatim.

“Faster synthesis” and/or“streaming synthesis” .

The speech synthesis API used for our probe converted speech in near real-time, but the participants wanted a faster speech synthesis. Therefore, we should continue to explore ways to further shorten the speech synthesis time. Because the noise-free and flawless characteristics of the synthesized voice cause unnecessary attention and awkwardness, it is possible to consider lowering its quality and shortening the time. Streaming TTS (also known as incremental TTS) [ 69 , 93 , 96 ] can also be applied. It refers to a technology that proceeds with synthesis based on the part of a sentence, even if the entire sentence is not yet inputted [ 96 ]. This has been studied for the quick synthesis of text typed by AAC users [ 93 ] or robot-generated responses to human utterances [ 109 ]. When the participants sent text word by word or did not send the entire speech content at once, the utterances became more unnatural. We expect that streaming TTS will make it possible to build a faster and more natural TTS, even if the speech content is divided and transmitted. Despite the recent development of various TTS technologies, they are not applied to the AAC field [ 37 ], so the TTS APIs should actively review the provision of related technologies.

7.2.3 Considerations on the user interface design. “Excusing after active turn-taking” or“waiting to prevent turn snatching” .

Voice speakers take turns based on various visual or prosodic cues when uttering [ 24 , 107 ]. Because the cues are not available in the TTS speaking tool, the tool should indicate the users’ input status or provide clues to recognize the start and end of the speech. Furthermore, speaking with the TTS tool is surely slower than 200ms, which is a human to take a turn and utter [ 61 ]. Therefore, the tool should provide features that allow users to easily take turns or yield turns. For instance, if users unintentionally take another interlocutor's turn, the system suggests the user play“Sorry” or automatically plays it. It can also help users to quickly yield a turn by suggesting an“Oh, what was that?” button to the user. These may work well for casual meetings but may still be considered rude in more formal meetings. In formal meetings where defensive turn-taking is encouraged, the TTS tool should provide a waiting system in which synthesized speech automatically stays on standby when someone is speaking or is about to speak. That is, synthesized speeches could wait until users press the“Speak now” button or no one is speaking.

“Non-intrusive interfaces” and“automated interfaces” .

The participants argued that manipulating the TTS speaking tool should not interfere with their activities. Therefore, occupying many portions of screens or requiring repetitive and continuous operation is undesirable. Because the participants said that their typing behavior lacked room to control other interfaces, automating features was encouraged whenever possible. For instance, the system can automatically add filled pauses such as“um” or“uh” occasionally [ 25 ] when others wait in the silence during users’ typing. If complete automation is difficult, interfaces that actively suggest what users may need or expect should be considered.

8 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

All 32 videoconferencing users were between 19 and 31 years of age and recruited according to their conditions. Because they were the age groups used to text chats, different results may appear if the studies were conducted with users of different age groups. In particular, other age groups could not type what to say in text chat style. All the participants were Korean-speaking individuals, so we used the Korean TTS API. This condition was a limitation because we could not confirm various findings according to language and culture. All the participants were members of the same university or graduate school was also a cultural limitation. Because our studies were conducted in a laboratory environment, future work could include further investigation into various real-world meeting situations. For example, we guided all participants to turn on the camera in both studies, but users often turn off the camera in the real world. Study 1 was conducted with people who know each other. However, in reality, people sometimes conduct videoconferencing with strangers, so there could be more findings in speaking only through text chat in videoconferencing. The fact that the private conference room provided to the participants in Study 2 was an unfamiliar space to them may also have influenced the results. We observed a relatively casual meeting, but other forms of videoconferencing may have yielded different results. We created each team of four interlocutors, and only one participant used the TTS speaking tool. It would be interesting for future research to investigate cases involving more interlocutors or TTS speaking tool users. Hybrid meeting environments, where only a portion of the interlocutors join by videoconferencing, are also worth exploring. In hybrid meetings, speaking-restrained issues may become more important because equal participation of remote participants is more difficult [ 45 , 88 ]. Although we attempted to provide sufficient tutorials and time to get used to our probes, the initial effect may have affected the results. Therefore, our future work has a chance to include observations of longer-term effects and discussions of organizational trust. Owing to our resources and technical limitations, there was less than a second delay in synthesizing and transmitting each utterance. If this time can be significantly shortened, other user reviews can appear. Finally, we took advantage of the text input, which ensures minimal accessibility for most users and is readily available. Through the discussions on the input method of AAC [ 23 ] and the development of the SSI field, the problem related to the text input we presented could be solved in a completely different way.

9 CONCLUSIONS

To date, most videoconferencing studies have been conducted in laboratory environments or at home, but the experience of participating in videoconferencing in other spaces has not been sufficiently discussed. However, users participated in videoconferencing in spaces with others, such as cafés, libraries, communal offices, and communal living spaces. They sometimes experienced challenges in participating well because of the speaking-restrained contexts in these spaces. Although current users can use text chats as the only alternative, we identified in Study 1 that text chats are insufficient to support their active participation. The interlocutors who communicated mainly with their voices had difficulty checking text chats during videoconferencing. The users who spoke through text chats often interrupted the communication flow because the interlocutors checked the chats at different times. Through two probe studies, we confirmed the possibility of using the TTS speaking tool. We also collected data on empirical challenges and users’ expectations for using TTS tool in videoconferencing through the user-centered approach. Although our probe was structured as a basic prototype, the participants discovered its effectiveness and suggested other uses. Most participants agreed that it would be valuable to be improved and introduced for a better videoconferencing experience. Based on our findings, we discussed the usefulness of a TTS speaking tool in videoconferencing and proposed the three aspects of design considerations. We hope that this would contribute to supporting the active participation of videoconferencing users.
